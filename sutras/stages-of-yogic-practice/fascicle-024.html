Fascicle 24 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十四 · Wakeful Practice, Day and Night

Awake Through the Watches of the Night

Fascicle 24 continues the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation's long account of the fourteen path-provisions, picking up exactly where fascicle 23 left off: wakeful practice, the seventh provision, structured around the day and the four watches of the night, followed by clear awareness in conduct, the eighth. Fascicle 24 is now complete.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 24 complete Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 24 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Wakeful Practice Opens: What Counts as the First Watch and the Last Watch

Fascicle 24 opens directly onto the seventh of the fourteen provisions, wakeful practice — a discipline for structuring the entire day and night, day practice, first-watch practice, and last-watch practice, around sitting and walking meditation, with the middle watches reserved for sleep. The treatise begins by defining the night's four watches with technical precision.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之三……復次，初夜後夜常勤修習悎寤瑜伽者，云何初夜？云何後夜？云何悎寤瑜伽？云何常勤修習悎寤瑜伽？言初夜者，謂夜四分中，過初一分，是夜初分。言後夜者，謂夜四分中，過後一分，是夜後分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, the third part of the third chapter … Furthermore, as for one who constantly and diligently cultivates wakeful practice in the first watch and the last watch of the night: what is the first watch? What is the last watch? What is wakeful practice? What is constant, diligent cultivation of wakeful practice? The first watch means that, of the night's four divisions, the first division passed through is the night's first part. The last watch means that, of the night's four divisions, the last division passed through is the night's last part.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating this material precisely: fascicle 24 is the third of four installments covering the "provisions for detachment" within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation — fascicle 22 covered detachment itself and moral discipline, fascicle 23 covered restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating, and this fascicle covers wakeful practice through the first and last watches of the night, plus clear awareness in conduct; fascicle 25 will close out the remaining provisions.

He then explains the treatise's specific way of dividing a day: rather than the six-period scheme familiar from other sutras (dawn, midday, sunset, evening, midnight, cockcrow), this treatise splits both day and night into four three-hour periods each. The night's four periods run from 6 pm to 6 am; the "first watch" is 6–9 pm, and the "last watch" is 3–6 am. The two middle periods, 9 pm to 3 am, are simply sleep — six hours, which Cháng Bǎi notes is a genuinely workable amount even by modern standards, more realistic than what many retreat schedules actually allow. The structure this sets up is elegant: meditate in the evening, sleep six hours, wake and meditate again before dawn — with the daytime hours reserved for their own parallel practice, discussed next.

2. Wakeful Practice Defined: Walking and Sitting Around the Clock

Chinese Source

悎寤瑜伽者，謂如說言：於晝日分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心；於初夜分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心。淨修心已，出住處外洗濯其足，還入住處，右脅而臥，重累其足，住光明想、正念、正知，思惟起想，巧便而臥。至夜後分，速疾悎寤，經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wakeful practice means: it is said that, during the day, one purifies the mind of the obstructions and what accords with them through walking meditation and sitting meditation; in the first watch of the night, one purifies the mind of the obstructions and what accords with them through walking meditation and sitting meditation. Having purified the mind, one goes outside the dwelling to wash the feet, then returns inside, lies down on the right side, one leg resting upon the other, abiding in the perception of light, mindfulness, and clear comprehension, forming the intention to rise, and lying down skillfully. Upon reaching the last watch, one wakes swiftly, and again purifies the mind of the obstructions and what accords with them through walking meditation and sitting meditation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the entire twenty-four-hour cycle this defines. Throughout the day (6 am–6 pm) and the first watch (6–9 pm), the practitioner alternates walking meditation — pacing a straight line or a loop, east to west and back — with formal cross-legged sitting, using both postures to purify the mind of the five hindrances (desire, ill-will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-remorse, doubt) and of whatever sense-objects tend to provoke them. Before sleep, a precise protocol follows: washing the feet (bare feet in the ancient setting made this a practical necessity, not merely ritual), lying on the right side in the Buddha's traditional posture, legs stacked, and settling the mind on a "perception of light" — Cháng Bǎi identifies three versions: the natural luminosity of a divine body, the physical light of sun, moon, and stars, or the figurative light of Dharma teachings such as loving-kindness or the contemplation of non-self and emptiness. Any of these keeps the mind bright and wards off nightmares. Alongside this, the practitioner maintains mindfulness and clear comprehension — checking whether the mind has drifted toward sensual thoughts and pulling it back if so — and deliberately forms the intention to wake at a set hour, trusting the storehouse consciousness to register the resolve, before finally settling into sleep "skillfully," meaning with this whole structure in place rather than simply collapsing into unconsciousness. Waking in the last watch is to be swift, not lingering, immediately resuming the same walking-and-sitting purification used during the day and the first watch.

3. Constant Diligent Cultivation: Five Kinds of Energy

Chinese Source

常勤修習悎寤瑜伽者，謂如有一世尊弟子，聽聞悎寤瑜伽法已，欲樂修學，便依如是悎寤瑜伽，作如是念：我當成辦佛所聽許悎寤瑜伽。發生樂欲，精進勤劬，超越勇猛勢力發起、勇悍、剛決、不可制伏、策勵其心無間相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for one who constantly and diligently cultivates wakeful practice: it is like a disciple of the Blessed One who, having heard the teaching on wakeful practice, wishes to study and practice it, and, relying on this wakeful practice, thinks: I shall accomplish the wakeful practice permitted and taught by the Buddha. Such a one generates aspiration, strives with vigor, arises with a force that surpasses ordinary courage, is bold, resolute, unable to be overcome, and spurs the mind on in unbroken continuity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as a compressed portrait of genuine diligence, and maps its five phrases onto the five kinds of energetic effort taught elsewhere in the treatise (Compendium of Determinations, fascicle 89). "Arising with surpassing force" is armor-like energy — the resolve one puts on before battle, here aimed at one's own afflictions, born from intense aspiration. "Boldness" is applied energy — the courage to actually take up the practice once armored. "Resoluteness" is unyielding energy — refusing to belittle oneself or grow timid about reaching the goal. "Unable to be overcome" is unshakable energy — the capacity to bear cold, heat, hunger, and other hardships without the resolve wavering. And "spurring the mind on in unbroken continuity" is insatiable energy — never resting content with progress already made, always seeking the next, subtler stage of the path. Cháng Bǎi warns against the common failure mode: a meditator whose legs finally stop aching, who sits comfortably through one session, and mistakes that ease for arrival — genuine diligence keeps pressing forward rather than settling for a single good sitting. With this, the treatise's brief outline of wakeful practice — first watch, last watch, the practice itself, and diligent cultivation of it — is complete; a detailed exposition follows.

4. Walking and Sitting Meditation, in Technical Detail

Chinese Source

言晝日者，謂從日出時至日沒時。言經行者，謂於廣長稱其度量一地方所，若往、若來相應身業。言宴坐者，謂如有一，或於大床、或小繩床、或草葉座，結加趺坐，端身正願，安住背念。

Ru-Yi Translation

The day means the time from sunrise to sunset. Walking meditation means the bodily act of going and coming within a properly measured stretch of ground, back and forth. Sitting meditation means: it is like one who, on a large bed, a small rope bed, or a seat of grass and leaves, sits cross-legged, holds the body upright, forms a correct resolve, and abides turned away from unwholesome thought.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes that "day" simply means sunrise to sunset, adjusted for latitude and season, and that walking meditation is pacing a measured stretch of ground back and forth, mind fixed on a contemplation such as the four foundations of mindfulness — not aimless strolling; in group retreats, a bell or clapper signals when to stop, training the practitioner to feel impermanence in that abrupt halt.

He then unpacks sitting meditation's posture in real detail. The full lotus is described as reserved for men in the tradition he's drawing on, since a woman's differently-shaped pelvis makes full lotus physically risky over long practice; a half-lotus or even simple cross-legged sitting is the appropriate substitute, switched side to side to avoid spinal strain. Citing the treatise's own later list of five reasons the full lotus is recommended when possible — it settles the body toward tranquility and concentration more readily than other postures, it can be sustained longest without fatigue, it carries a dignity not shared by non-Buddhist ascetic postures of the time, its visual uprightness naturally inspires reverence in onlookers, and every Buddha's disciple across all three times has praised it — Cháng Bǎi treats the posture itself as already a kind of teaching. "Holding the body upright" means gently drawing the spine straight without rigid tension, letting the breath settle downward afterward rather than rising and scattering the mind; an upright spine also makes drowsiness harder, since sleep naturally slumps the head and curves the back. "Correct resolve" means sitting without pretense or performance — not for how it looks to others, but from a genuine wish to practice, free of the flattery or deceit that would otherwise leave the mind agitated by outer concerns. "Abiding turned away from unwholesome thought" means deliberately setting aside distorted attention and dwelling instead in correct reflection, turned against the whole class of unwholesome states.

5. Section: The Five Hindrances and Their Five Provoking Conditions

The treatise names the five hindrances that wakeful practice exists to purify, then names the specific perceptual triggers — one paired to each hindrance — that a practitioner must learn to recognize and not dwell upon.

Chinese Source

所言障者，謂五種蓋。順障法者，謂能引蓋、隨順蓋法。云何五蓋？謂貪欲蓋、瞋恚蓋、惛沈睡眠蓋、掉舉惡作蓋，及以疑蓋。云何順障法？謂淨妙相，瞋恚相，黑闇相，親屬、國土、不死尋思，追憶昔時笑戲喜樂承事隨念，及以三世或於三世非理法思。

Ru-Yi Translation

The obstructions are the five hindrances. What accords with the obstructions is whatever draws out a hindrance or conforms to it. What are the five hindrances? The hindrance of desire, the hindrance of ill-will, the hindrance of sloth and torpor, the hindrance of restlessness and remorse, and the hindrance of doubt. What accords with the obstructions? The mark of what is attractive; the mark of resentment; the mark of darkness; preoccupation with kin, homeland, and one's own mortality, together with recollecting past laughter, play, pleasure, and service rendered to elders; and incorrect reflection on the three times.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five hindrances as already familiar from the Ground of Meditative Equipoise, and briefly re-states each: desire is the endless pursuit of the five sense-pleasures' seemingly pure and lovely aspects; ill-will arises from being called out for a fault, from dwelling on a past unpleasant experience, or from plotting revenge; sloth-and-torpor follows from broken discipline, unrestrained senses, poor eating habits, laziness, lack of clear self-awareness, or half-hearted sitting; restlessness-and-remorse arises from preoccupation with family, travel, or mortality, or from replaying old amusements and regrets; and doubt is uncertainty about the teacher, the teaching, the training, or the guidance received, and about the very goal of concentration or nirvana one is seeking.

He then pairs each hindrance with the specific perceptual trigger that provokes it. Attractive marks — dwelling on the seemingly lovely aspects of form, sound, or a person's body — feed desire; the treatise's own earlier chapter on meditative equipoise singles out fixation on the eight alluring features of a woman's body as a specific danger for male practitioners here. Marks of resentment — toward oneself, loved ones, disliked people, past or present or future enemies and friends, displeasing situations, jealousy, old grudges, or others' views, ten categories in all — feed ill-will. Marks of darkness — a mind grown dull and unclear about its objects, confused or doubtful about dependent origination — feed sloth-and-torpor. Preoccupation with kin (their rise and fall, closeness and separation), homeland (imagining a more pleasant place to live, or resenting the present one), one's own mortality (busying the mind with plans as if death were far off), and nostalgic replay of old amusements or duties performed for elders, all feed restlessness-and-remorse. And incorrect reflection on the three times — confused speculation about self and non-self, and about oneself, other beings, and the world across past, present, and future — feeds doubt. Cháng Bǎi's point in laying all this out so precisely is practical: a practitioner who can recognize a hindrance's specific trigger the moment it arises has already gone most of the way toward not dwelling on it.

6. Purifying the Mind During Walking Meditation: The Perception of Light

Chinese Source

問：於經行時，從幾障法淨修其心？云何從彼淨修其心？答：從惛沈睡眠蓋，及能引惛沈睡眠障法，淨修其心。為除彼故，於光明相善巧精懇，善取善思、善了善達。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: During walking meditation, from how many obstructions is the mind purified, and how is it purified from them? Answer: The mind is purified from the hindrance of sloth and torpor, and from whatever draws out that hindrance. In order to remove it, one becomes skillful and earnest regarding the perception of light — skillfully grasping it, skillfully reflecting on it, skillfully understanding it, and skillfully penetrating it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that walking meditation is matched to exactly one hindrance — sloth-and-torpor, along with the "dark" perceptual trigger that feeds it — since physical movement is the most direct antidote to drowsiness. The remedy the treatise prescribes is cultivating a "perception of light," the same threefold light introduced earlier for the pre-sleep protocol: the antidote-light that directly counters darkness, the figurative light of Dharma teaching, and the natural light of a purified body. Cháng Bǎi glosses the four verbs describing how this perception should be developed: "skillful" means the reflection accords with correct principle rather than wandering; "earnest" means applying it without error and without interruption, seriously rather than half-heartedly; "grasping and reflecting well" means actually taking hold of the light as an object of attention and reasoning about it correctly; and "understanding and penetrating well" means using the Dharma-light specifically to see clearly into the nature of things. The walking practitioner who feels drowsiness creeping in is meant to turn toward this bright object of attention rather than simply pushing through on willpower alone.

7. "Light-with-Thought": The Six Recollections

Chinese Source

以有明俱心，及有光俱心。或於屏處、或於露處往返經行。於經行時，隨緣一種淨妙境界，極善示現，勸導、讚勵、慶慰其心。謂或念佛、或法、或僧、或戒、或捨、或復念天。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is done with a mind accompanied by brightness, and a mind accompanied by light. One paces back and forth in walking meditation, whether in a sheltered place or in the open. During walking meditation, taking as object any one pure and excellent condition, one calls it vividly to mind, and thereby exhorts, encourages, and comforts one's own mind — namely, by recollecting the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, moral discipline, generosity, or the divine realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes two ways of holding light in mind during walking meditation: a "mind accompanied by light" — actually visualizing sun, moon, lamplight, or a deity's own radiance, whether pacing in a shaded, sheltered spot or out in the open — and a "mind accompanied by brightness," which is the Dharma-light itself, called to mind through the six recollections. Each of the six is aimed specifically at breaking drowsiness by giving the mind something vivid and energizing to dwell on. Recollecting the Buddha means calling to mind his ten epithets and the qualities behind them, and resolving to walk the same path he did. Recollecting the Dharma means calling to mind that the teaching is good in the beginning, middle, and end, leads reliably to liberation, and is cooling to the very afflictions keeping the mind restless. Recollecting the Saṅgha means calling to mind the noble ones specifically — the eight types of stream-enterers through arhats, or great bodhisattvas — never ordinary companions, since only genuine attainment is worth aspiring to. Recollecting discipline means checking one's own conduct of body, speech, and mind for anything that might trouble others, and taking quiet satisfaction in what has been kept well. Recollecting generosity means calling to mind one's own past acts of giving, and the joy that comes from having helped. And recollecting the divine realms means calling to mind the five qualities — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, and wisdom — that lead toward rebirth there, and resolving to keep cultivating them. Cháng Bǎi stresses that this recollection must be so well-trained that it arises instantly and vividly the moment it's needed — during illness, before sleep, in any circumstance — not something one has merely read about once; only that level of familiarity actually counters drowsiness in the moment it appears.

8. More Antidotes to Drowsiness, and the Formula That Closes Them

Chinese Source

或於宣說惛沈睡眠過患相應所有正法，於此法中，為除彼故，以無量門訶責毀訾惛沈睡眠所有過失，以無量門稱揚讚歎惛沈睡眠永斷功德。……為除彼故，於此正法聽聞受持，以大音聲若讀、若誦，為他開示，思惟其義，稱量觀察。……或觀方隅，或瞻星月諸宿道度，或以冷水洗灑面目。由是惛沈睡眠纏蓋未生不生，已生除遣。如是方便，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, among the teachings that speak of the faults of sloth and torpor, one dwells on that teaching, and through countless approaches censures and condemns every fault of sloth and torpor, and through countless approaches praises and extols the merit of permanently ending it. …In order to remove it, one hears and retains this teaching, reciting or reading it aloud, explaining it to others, reflecting on its meaning, and weighing and examining it. …Or one gazes at the four directions, or looks up at the stars, moon, and their courses, or washes the face with cold water. By this, the entanglement of sloth and torpor does not arise if not yet arisen, and is removed if already arisen. Such are the skillful means by which the mind is purified of what accords with the obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise draws here on the twelve traditional categories of scriptural genre (discourses, verse restatements, prophecies, standalone verses, unprompted teachings, narratives of circumstance, parables, accounts of past disciples, birth-stories, extended Mahāyāna teachings, accounts of the marvelous, and analytical treatises) — all twelve, in their own way, censuring drowsiness and praising wakefulness. He mentions in passing a list from the Sutra of Awakening the Pure Mind cataloguing twenty distinct faults of excessive sleep — laziness, physical heaviness, dull skin, poor digestion, weakened faculties, and so on — meant to be genuinely unappealing to contemplate. The practical remedy is then very concrete: memorize passages on this theme and recite them aloud (a raised voice itself keeps the mind from drifting into sleep), or explain them to someone else, or simply reflect carefully on their reasoning. Beyond that, three more physical measures are offered — looking out at the four directions and their intermediate points, gazing up at the stars and tracking their slow movement across the night, or splashing cold water on the face. Cháng Bǎi adds a story from the Āgamas in which the Buddha gives a disciple eleven such methods and says plainly that if all eleven fail, the only honest option left is to actually sleep and resume practice once rested — there is no virtue in forcing an unproductive sit. The section then closes with the same formula used throughout the treatise: by these skillful means, sloth-and-torpor is prevented from arising, and dispelled once arisen, completing the account of purifying the mind during walking meditation.

9. Section: Purifying the Mind During Sitting Meditation — Four Remaining Hindrances

Walking meditation addressed sloth-and-torpor alone; sitting meditation is now matched to the four hindrances that remain — desire, ill-will, restlessness-and-remorse, and doubt. The treatise opens with desire's antidote: the classical contemplation of a corpse's decay, worked through nine stages.

Chinese Source

問：於宴坐時，從幾障法淨修其心？云何從彼淨修其心？答：從四障法淨修其心。謂貪欲、瞋恚、掉舉惡作、疑蓋，及能引彼法，淨修其心。……為令已生貪欲纏蓋速除遣故，為令未生極遠離故，結加趺坐，端身正願，安住背念；或觀青瘀、或觀膿爛、或觀變壞、或觀膨脹、或觀食噉、或觀血塗、或觀其骨、或觀其鎖、或觀骨鎖，或於隨一賢善定相作意思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: During sitting meditation, from how many obstructions is the mind purified, and how is it purified from them? Answer: The mind is purified from four obstructions — desire, ill-will, restlessness-and-remorse, and doubt — and from whatever draws them out. …In order to swiftly remove the hindrance of desire once arisen, and to keep it far away before it arises, one sits cross-legged, holds the body upright, forms a correct resolve, and abides turned away from unwholesome thought; and one contemplates a corpse turning livid, or festering, or decaying, or bloating, or being devoured, or smeared with blood, or reduced to bones, or reduced to bones held by sinew, or a scattered skeleton — or one reflects with the mind fixed on any one of these excellent, virtuous marks of concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the shift: where walking meditation had one job, sitting meditation carries four, since sitting still is when desire, anger, agitation, and doubt most readily surface. For desire specifically, the same posture already described — cross-legged, upright, resolved, mind turned from distorted attention — is now paired with the classical nine-stage contemplation of a corpse's decay: livid discoloration, festering, decomposition, bloating, being eaten by animals, smeared with blood, reduced to bare bone, bone held together by remaining sinew, and finally a scattered skeleton. He explains why this specific object is called an "excellent, virtuous mark of concentration": it is the one truth that applies equally to everyone, clever or foolish, wealthy or poor — every body ends this way, and no one, having genuinely seen it, would wish to lie down beside a corpse out of desire. Fascicle 26 will return to this contemplation in far more technical detail, addressing four distinct kinds of desire (for another's complexion, for their form, for the pleasure of touch, and for being served) each with its own antidote; here the outline is only sketched. Cháng Bǎi is careful to note the point isn't morbidity for its own sake — it's that seeing clearly through desire's most extreme case, a decaying body, loosens desire's grip on far subtler objects as well.

10. Desire's Antidote Completes: Scripture, Then the Closing Formula

Chinese Source

以無量門稱揚讚歎一切貪欲永斷功德。所謂契經、應頌、記別，乃至廣說。為斷貪欲，於此正法聽聞受持，言善通利、意善尋思、見善通達，即於此法，如是宴坐，如理思惟。由是因緣，貪欲纏蓋未生不生，已生除遣。如是方便，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through countless approaches, one praises and extols every merit of permanently ending desire — as set forth in discourses, verse restatements, prophecies, and so on at length. In order to cut off desire, one hears and retains this teaching, so that one's speech becomes fluent in it, one's mind investigates it well, and one's insight penetrates it thoroughly; and, relying on this very teaching, one sits in meditation and reflects correctly. By this cause, the entanglement of desire does not arise if not yet arisen, and is removed if already arisen. Such are the skillful means by which the mind is purified of what accords with the obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes desire's antidote the same way sloth-and-torpor's was closed: direct contemplation (here, the corpse's decay) paired with hearing, reciting, and reflecting on scriptural passages that expose desire's faults — craving, attachment, the deep self-love the treatise calls "storing," attachment to loved ones, and the reckless pursuit of pleasure regardless of consequence. He notes in passing that Mahāyāna practice diverges here: a bodhisattva doesn't merely extinguish desire and withdraw from saṃsāra the way this śrāvaka path aims to, but stays engaged with beings precisely through compassion, perfecting their own qualities in the process of benefiting others rather than through renunciation alone. With the formula "prevented if unarisen, removed if arisen" repeated once more, desire's antidote is complete, and the treatise moves to the second of sitting meditation's four hindrances.

11. Section: Ill-Will's Antidote — Boundless Loving-Kindness in All Directions

The treatise turns to ill-will, and prescribes the classical practice of radiating loving-kindness outward, direction by direction, until it fills the entire universe without limit or exception.

Chinese Source

於瞋恚蓋法有差別者，謂如是宴坐，以慈俱心，無怨無敵、無損無惱，廣大無量，極善修習；普於一方發起勝解，具足安住。如是第二、如是第三、如是第四，上下傍布，普遍一切無邊世界發起勝解，具足安住。餘如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the distinct method for the hindrance of ill-will: one sits in meditation with a mind accompanied by loving-kindness — free of resentment, free of hostility, free of the wish to harm, free of malice — vast and boundless, cultivated to the utmost, and, directed pervasively toward one direction, gives rise to a resolute understanding, abiding in it fully. So too toward the second direction, the third, the fourth, and above, below, and the intermediate points, pervading all boundless worlds, giving rise to that same resolute understanding, abiding in it fully. The rest is as explained before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as the classical loving-kindness meditation from the four immeasurables, worked through its familiar structure: a mind actively wishing benefit and ease on beings, free of every trace of enmity, conflict, or malice, directed first at one direction, then systematically extended to all four cardinal directions, the four intermediate points, and above and below — every possible direction, without gap or exception — until the resolve is vast, immeasurable, and settled, "pure and unbroken" the way the treatise elsewhere describes it. The identical structure, he notes, applies to compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, the other three immeasurables, each pervading all directions the same way.

He adds several supplementary Mahāyāna methods for dissolving anger specifically, drawn from later treatises: reflecting that every being has, across beginningless lifetimes, at some point been one's own parent or sibling, so there is no one who has not already shown one kindness; reflecting that beings arise and perish moment by moment, so no stable "harmer" or "harmed" can actually be found; reflecting that only the five aggregates exist, with no real self to be injured or to injure; reflecting with compassion that the offending person is already suffering, and does not need further pain added to their burden; and reflecting on all beings as one's own child, toward whom no parent wishes harm. For someone actively causing harm right now, a further contemplation reframes the relationship across lifetimes — perhaps this person was once poorly treated by oneself in the past, or was once a mother who cared for oneself and now suffers the karmic echo of one's own past ingratitude — dissolving resentment into something closer to grief at the whole tangled situation. Cháng Bǎi's practical suggestion: find whichever one of these reflections lands most naturally, memorize it, and bring it to mind the moment anger surfaces in sitting.

12. Restlessness-and-Remorse's Antidote: The Nine Stages of Mental Abiding

Chinese Source

於掉舉惡作蓋法有差別者，謂如是宴坐，令心內住，成辦一趣，得三摩地。餘如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the distinct method for the hindrance of restlessness and remorse: one sits in meditation causing the mind to abide inwardly, to become one-pointed, and to attain concentration. The rest is as explained before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats restlessness-and-remorse's remedy as the treatise's classical nine stages of mental abiding, only sketched here in three key moments: "abiding inwardly" is the first stage — deliberately pulling attention back from wandering thoughts of family, homeland, or old memories to a single object, such as the breath or a recitation; "becoming one-pointed" is the later stages, where the mind, once repeatedly redirected, settles and no longer needs forceful correction; and "attaining concentration" is the ninth and final stage, effortless absorption, where the object is held without any strain at all. Fascicle 30 will give all nine stages by name. He illustrates the shift from effortful to effortless attention with two stories he likes to use: a book on Japanese archery describing a student shown a target cluttered with a window, branches, birds, and the moon, who at first sees all of it, then gradually — through long training — sees only the target's rings, and finally only the bullseye, at which point every shot lands true because nothing else registers; and an anecdote about a thief caught stealing gold in broad daylight in front of a crowd, who explains "I saw only the gold — nothing else," illustrating the same one-pointed absorption turned to an unfortunate end. Ordinary minds, Cháng Bǎi notes, are scattered by habit, jumping from one half-finished thought to the next; nothing gets done well until this scattering is trained into genuine one-pointedness.

13. Section: Doubt's Antidote — Rejecting Speculation, Reflecting Rightly on Dependent Origination

The treatise closes sitting meditation's four antidotes with doubt, first naming the exact speculative questions about self across the three times that must be set aside, then prescribing correct reflection on dependent origination in their place.

Chinese Source

於疑蓋法有差別者，謂如是宴坐，於過去世，非不如理作意思惟；於未來世、於現在世，非不如理作意思惟。我於過去為曾有耶？為曾無耶？……今此有情從何而來？於此殞沒當往何所？於如是等不如正理作意思惟，應正遠離。如理思惟去來今世，唯見有法，唯見有事；知有為有，知無為無；唯觀有因，唯觀有果；於實無事不增不益，於實有事不毀不謗。於其實有，了知實有。謂於無常、苦、空、無我一切法中，了知無常、苦、空、無我。以能如是如理思惟，便於佛所無惑無疑。餘如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the distinct method for the hindrance of doubt: sitting in meditation, one does not engage in incorrect reflection about the past, the future, or the present. Did I exist in the past, or did I not? … Where does this being come from, and where will it go once it perishes here? Such incorrect reflection should rightly be avoided. Reflecting correctly on the past, future, and present, one sees only phenomena, only events; one knows what exists as existing, and what does not exist as not existing; one observes only causes and only effects; one neither exaggerates what is truly non-existent nor denies what is truly existent. Regarding what truly exists, one understands it as it truly is — namely, understanding impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self within all phenomena as impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self. By reflecting correctly in this way, one comes to have no confusion and no doubt regarding the Buddha. The rest is as explained before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the rejected questions as the classic speculative traps around a fixed self — did I exist before this life, what was I, what will I become, where did I come from, where am I going — all of them presupposing a stable self that dependent origination denies from the outset; dwelling on them is precisely what breeds doubt rather than resolving it. The corrective is to reflect instead on the four accepted modes of reasoning already used elsewhere in the treatise: the principle of dependence (seeing that suffering follows wherever conditions for it exist), the principle of function (seeing that sustained practice of this reflection itself dispels ignorance), the principle of proof (grounding the reflection in scriptural authority, direct realization, and valid inference together), and the principle of natural law (recognizing dependent origination as the fixed nature of phenomena, not a doctrine invented after the fact). Applied to the twelve links, this means seeing only the conditioned chain itself — ignorance conditioning formations, and so on through birth and death — with no separate self riding along inside it: causes and effects arising lawfully, nothing genuinely existent denied, nothing non-existent invented. Extended to the five aggregates, the same reflection yields the direct seeing of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self, and it is precisely this — not any argument about the self's existence — that Cháng Bǎi says removes all remaining doubt about the Buddha's teaching itself. With doubt's antidote given, all four hindrances addressed by sitting meditation — desire, ill-will, restlessness-and-remorse, and doubt — are now complete, alongside walking meditation's antidote to sloth-and-torpor from earlier in the fascicle.

14. Doubt's Antidote Closes, and "Purifying the Mind by the Power of the Teaching" Concludes

Chinese Source

於法、於僧、於苦、於集、於滅、於道、於因，及因所生諸法無惑無疑。……又於瞋恚蓋應作是說：為斷瞋恚及瞋恚相，於此正法聽聞受持，乃至廣說。於掉舉惡作蓋應作是說：為斷掉舉惡作及順彼法，於此正法聽聞受持，乃至廣說。於其疑蓋應作是說：為斷疑蓋及順彼法，於此正法聽聞受持，乃至廣說。如是方便，從貪欲、瞋恚、惛沈睡眠、掉舉惡作、疑蓋，及順彼法，淨修其心。是故說言：經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the Dharma, the Saṅgha, suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path, cause, and the phenomena arising from cause, one has no confusion and no doubt. …And regarding the hindrance of ill-will, it should likewise be said: in order to cut off ill-will and its mark, one hears and retains this teaching, and so on at length. Regarding the hindrance of restlessness and remorse, it should likewise be said: in order to cut off restlessness and remorse and what accords with them, one hears and retains this teaching, and so on at length. Regarding the hindrance of doubt, it should likewise be said: in order to cut off doubt and what accords with it, one hears and retains this teaching, and so on at length. By such skillful means, the mind is purified of desire, ill-will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-remorse, doubt, and what accords with them. It is therefore said: through walking and sitting meditation, the mind is purified of what accords with the obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes doubt's antidote widens beyond just the three-times speculation already covered, to include confidence in the Dharma, the noble Saṅgha, and the Four Noble Truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, the path — plus cause and effect generally; genuine understanding of dependent origination dissolves uncertainty about all of these together, not the self-question alone. The treatise then briefly notes that ill-will, restlessness-and-remorse, and doubt each also receive the same "hearing and retaining scripture that censures the fault and praises its elimination" treatment already given in full for desire and sloth-and-torpor — stated here only in brief, since the pattern is now established. With this, the whole first major approach to purifying the mind during walking and sitting meditation — "by the power of the teaching," meaning direct contemplation plus scriptural reflection, applied individually to all five hindrances — is complete.

15. Section: A Second Power — Purifying the Mind Through One's Own Conscience

Having covered purification "by the power of the teaching," the treatise now opens a second and third approach: purifying the mind through one's own inner conscience, and through concern for how one appears to others.

Chinese Source

復有由自增上及世增上，從順障法淨修其心。云何名為由自增上？謂如有一，於諸蓋中隨起一種，便自了知此非善法，於所生蓋不堅執著，速疾棄捨、擯遣、變吐。又能自觀此所生蓋，甚可羞恥，令心染惱，令慧羸劣，是損害品。如是名為由自增上，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, there is purifying the mind of what accords with the obstructions through one's own conscience and through concern for the world. What is meant by one's own conscience? It is like one who, when any one of the hindrances arises, immediately recognizes it as unwholesome, does not cling firmly to the hindrance that has arisen, and swiftly abandons it, casts it out, and expels it. Such a one further observes that the hindrance which has arisen is truly shameful — it defiles the mind and weakens wisdom, and is thus a harmful thing. This is called purifying the mind of what accords with the obstructions through one's own conscience.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies these as two more of the treatise's classic mental factors: self-respecting conscience (慚) and shame before others (愧). Self-respecting conscience means catching a hindrance the instant it appears, recognizing it immediately as unwholesome, and — crucially — not dwelling on it or elaborating it, simply letting it go and returning at once to the object of meditation, followed by a genuine sense of shame that such a mental state arose at all, since it defiles the mind and weakens the very wisdom the practice is meant to cultivate.

He illustrates the mechanics of "recognize and release" with a story about Milarepa and a shepherd who, though constantly busy tending his flock, longed to practice and asked for instruction. Milarepa told him: let the mind be like space — clinging to nothing; like a mountain — utterly unmoved; like a windless lake — undisturbed by any of the eight worldly concerns (gain and loss, praise and blame, and so on); and like the full moon — luminous and clear. When the shepherd objected that clouds still drift through the sky of his mind no matter how he tries, Milarepa answered simply: clouds are still part of space; don't chase after them, and the sky reappears once they pass. Applied to sitting meditation, a hindrance arising is exactly such a cloud — the point is not to follow it, but to return, again and again, to the original object, until the mind can rest there on its own. The shepherd, Cháng Bǎi notes, eventually succeeded through nothing more than this patient, repeated returning.

16. A Third Power: Purifying the Mind Through Concern for Others' Regard

Chinese Source

云何名為由世增上，從順障法淨修其心？謂如有一，於諸蓋中隨一已生，或將生時，便作是念：我若生起所未生蓋，當為大師之所訶責，亦為諸天及諸有智同梵行者以法輕毀。彼由如是世增上故，未生諸蓋能令不生，已生諸蓋能速棄捨。如是名為由世增上，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by purifying the mind of what accords with the obstructions through concern for the world? It is like one who, when any one of the hindrances has arisen or is about to arise, thinks: if I allow a hindrance not yet arisen to arise, I will be reproached by the Teacher, and will be rightly disdained by the gods and by wise companions in the spiritual life. Through this concern for the world, hindrances not yet arisen are prevented from arising, and hindrances already arisen are swiftly abandoned. This is called purifying the mind of what accords with the obstructions through concern for the world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes this third power as the natural counterpart to the second: where self-respecting conscience looks inward at what the hindrance does to one's own mind, concern for the world looks outward, to the imagined judgment of the Buddha, the devas, and wise fellow practitioners who would rightly think less of one for succumbing to a hindrance already named and warned against. The mechanism is the same either way — the hindrance is prevented if not yet arisen, and swiftly abandoned if already arisen — but the two together give a practitioner a genuinely complete motivational structure, one rooted in self-respect and one in accountability to a wider community, so that a lapse in either direction still has something to catch it.

17. Section: Proper Sleep — Why the Lion King's Posture

The treatise turns from the first watch's purification practice to the middle watches' sleep itself, treating even sleeping posture as part of practice: feet washed, body lying on the right side like a lion king, deliberately distinguished from three inferior postures.

Chinese Source

又為護持諸臥具故，順世儀故。盡夜初分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心。……洗濯足已，還入住處，如法寢臥，為令寢臥長養大種。……問：以何因緣右脅而臥？答：與師子王法相似故。……如師子王，一切獸中，勇悍堅猛，最為第一。苾芻亦爾，於常修習悎寤瑜伽，發勤精進，勇悍堅猛，最為第一。由是因緣，與師子王臥法相似。非如其餘鬼臥、天臥、受欲者臥。由彼一切懶惰懈怠，下劣精進，勢力薄弱。又法應爾，如師子王右脅臥者，如是臥時，身無掉亂，念無忘失，睡不極重，不見惡夢。異此臥者，與是相違，當知具有一切過失。是故說言：右脅而臥，重累其足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, to protect one's bedding, and in accordance with worldly custom, one washes the feet after walking and sitting meditation through the whole of the first watch. …Having washed the feet, one returns to the dwelling and lies down properly, so that lying down nourishes the great elements of the body. …Question: For what reason does one lie on the right side? Answer: Because it resembles the posture of the lion king. …Just as the lion king is, among all beasts, the most courageous, firm, and mighty, so too the monk, in constantly cultivating wakeful practice, generates diligence that is most courageous, firm, and mighty; for this reason it is said to resemble the lion king's posture — unlike the posture of a hungry ghost, a god, or one given to lust, all of which come from laziness, inferior diligence, and feeble strength. It is simply so by nature that lying as the lion king does, on the right side, the body does not thrash about, mindfulness is not lost, sleep is not excessively heavy, and no bad dreams appear. Lying otherwise brings the opposite — know that it carries every one of these faults. It is therefore said: one lies on the right side, one leg resting upon the other.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats even sleep as an extension of practice rather than a break from it. Washing the feet before bed protects the bedding — barefoot walking made this a practical necessity in the ancient setting — and proper lying-down actually nourishes the body's four great elements, so that health itself supports the "unbroken, ever-diligent cultivation of wholesome practice" the treatise keeps returning to; a weak, sickly body spends its time on doctors and medicine rather than practice.

He explains the lion-king comparison as a comparison of vigor, not literal posture: the lion, fiercest and most powerful of beasts, stands for the monk's own courageous, unrelenting diligence in wakeful practice — the posture is simply named after the quality of mind it's meant to express. Three inferior postures are named by contrast: lying face-down like a hungry ghost (associated with a downward, degraded rebirth), lying face-up like a god (associated with heedless ease), and lying on the left side like one absorbed in lust — all three, Cháng Bǎi says, arising from and reinforcing laziness and weak resolve. The right-side lion posture, by contrast, genuinely produces four benefits: the body does not toss and turn through the night, mindfulness is not lost even in sleep, sleep does not become excessively heavy or oversleep, and nightmares do not arise — benefits Cháng Bǎi notes he has heard corroborated informally by long-time meditators who switched postures and noticed the difference.

18. Sleeping with the Perception of Light Still Present

Chinese Source

云何名為住光明想巧便而臥？謂於光明相善巧精懇，善取善思、善了善達，思惟諸天光明俱心，巧便而臥。由是因緣，雖復寢臥，心不惛闇。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by lying down skillfully while abiding in the perception of light? It means being skillful and earnest regarding the mark of light — skillfully grasping it, skillfully reflecting on it, skillfully understanding it, skillfully penetrating it — reflecting on the light of the celestial beings with a mind that holds it, and thereby lying down skillfully. By this cause, even while asleep, the mind does not become dark and dull.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this passage specifically names the light of celestial beings' own radiant bodies, though the same three-fold light discussed earlier for walking meditation — the antidote-light of sun, moon, and lamp; the figurative light of Dharma teaching; and the natural light of a purified body — applies equally well here. The point is continuity: rather than letting the mind go slack and dark the moment the body lies down, the practitioner deliberately carries a bright object of attention into sleep itself, so that even unconscious rest doesn't become a total blank the way it does for someone who simply collapses into it. This, Cháng Bǎi says, is why a practitioner who sleeps this way wakes more readily and more clearly than one who doesn't — the mind was never fully extinguished to begin with.

19. Lying Down with Mindfulness: Dharma Follows the Mind into Sleep

Chinese Source

云何正念巧便而臥？謂若諸法，已聞、已思、已熟修習，體性是善，能引義利。由正念故，乃至睡夢亦常隨轉；由正念故，於睡夢中亦常記憶，令彼法相分明現前，即於彼法心多隨觀；由正念故，隨其所念，或善心眠、或無記心眠。是名正念巧便而臥。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is lying down skillfully with mindfulness? It means that phenomena already heard, already reflected upon, and already thoroughly cultivated in practice — wholesome in nature, capable of bringing genuine benefit — continue to accompany the mind, through the power of mindfulness, even into sleep and dreams. Through mindfulness, these phenomena are also remembered during sleep and dreams, appearing clearly before the mind, which then dwells upon them repeatedly. Through mindfulness, depending on what is held in mind, one sleeps with a wholesome mind, or with a neutral mind. This is called lying down skillfully with mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the natural fruit of genuine hearing, reflection, and cultivation (聞思修): a teaching absorbed deeply enough doesn't switch off when the body falls asleep, but keeps quietly running in the background, surfacing in dreams and remaining available to the mind even in unconsciousness. Practically, this means sleep itself becomes either wholesome-minded (dreaming of loving-kindness, emptiness, or whatever contemplation one has truly internalized) or at minimum neutral (something like counting the breath, neither wholesome nor unwholesome) — but never given over to idle, unwholesome mental chatter. This is why, Cháng Bǎi notes, a lifetime devotee of a single recitation practice — reciting the Buddha's name, for instance — is said to be able to continue that recitation through sleep and even through severe illness, and why such steadiness in ordinary sleep is considered real evidence of readiness for the moment of death itself, when an untrained mind is most likely to be swept away by whatever surfaces.

20. Lying Down with Clear Comprehension: Catching Afflictions Even in Sleep

Chinese Source

云何正知巧便而臥？謂由正念而寢臥時，若有隨一煩惱現前，染惱其心。於此煩惱現生起時，能正覺了，令不堅著，速疾棄捨，既通達已，令心轉還。是名正知巧便而臥。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is lying down skillfully with clear comprehension? It means that, while lying down with mindfulness, if any affliction should appear and defile the mind, one correctly recognizes it the moment it arises, does not cling to it firmly, swiftly abandons it, and, having thoroughly understood it, brings the mind back. This is called lying down skillfully with clear comprehension.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as mindfulness's necessary companion: mindfulness keeps a wholesome or neutral object present through sleep, but clear comprehension is the ability to notice the moment that object slips and an affliction — a stray thought of past pleasure, an old resentment — has taken its place, and to return to the original object at once rather than drifting further into it. He treats a practitioner's dream life as a genuinely useful diagnostic: chaotic, disturbing, or threatening dreams suggest the Dharma hasn't yet penetrated deeply enough to hold through unconsciousness, and are worth responding to on waking — going before the Buddha to acknowledge the lapse honestly and returning to hearing, reflection, and practice with renewed effort — rather than dismissed as meaningless. The eventual goal, held through both mindfulness and clear comprehension together, is a mind so steady that even the final moment of death — traditionally the most vulnerable point for the mind to be swept away by whatever last thought arises — is met without confusion.

21. Section: Forming the Intention to Rise — The Deer's Alertness

The fourth and final component of lying down skillfully closes the entire teaching on proper sleep: resolving, before ever falling asleep, to rise promptly and resume practice — with the wakeful alertness of a wild deer rather than the abandon of ordinary sleep.

Chinese Source

云何名為思惟起想巧便而臥？謂以精進策勵其心，然後寢臥。於寢臥時，時時覺寤，如林野鹿，不應一切縱放其心，隨順、趣向、臨入睡眠。復作是念：我今應於諸佛所許悎寤瑜伽，一切皆當具足成辦。……過今夜分至明清旦，倍增發起勤精進住，起發具足。當知此中，由第一思惟起想，無重睡眠，於應起時速疾能起，終不過時方乃悎寤。由第二思惟起想，能於諸佛共所聽許師子王臥如法而臥，無增無減。由第三思惟起想，令善欲樂常無懈廢。雖有失念，而能後後展轉受學，令無斷絕。如是名為思惟起想巧便而臥。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by lying down skillfully while forming the intention to rise? It means spurring on the mind with diligence before lying down. While lying down, one wakes readily again and again, like a deer in the wild forest, and should not let the mind go slack, following, tending toward, and falling into heavy sleep. One further thinks: I should now fully accomplish every part of the wakeful practice permitted by all Buddhas. …Once this night has passed into the bright morning, I shall redouble my diligence, generating it in full. Know that through the first such intention, sleep is not heavy, and one rises swiftly and promptly when it is time, never oversleeping. Through the second, one lies as all Buddhas together permit — the lion king's posture — properly, neither adding to it nor falling short of it. Through the third, the aspiration toward wholesome states never grows slack; even if mindfulness is momentarily lost, one takes it up again and again afterward, so that it is never truly broken. This is called lying down skillfully while forming the intention to rise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the deer image directly: a wild deer's hearing is so sharp that the faintest sound wakes it instantly, and the practitioner is meant to sleep the same way — light, alert, ready to rise the moment it's time, never sinking into the heavy, difficult-to-leave sleep that comes from indulging drowsiness rather than merely resting the body. Three specific intentions are formed before sleep: first, resolving to fully accomplish every element of wakeful practice as the Buddhas themselves have taught it; second, delighting in the practice itself, generating the strongest possible enthusiasm for carrying it out; and third, resolving in advance to redouble effort the moment the last watch begins, refusing laziness room to take hold. The brief summary then maps a specific fruit to each: the first intention yields sleep that is never heavy, so rising is swift and prompt, never overslept; the second sustains the lion-king posture faithfully through the night, neither more nor less than prescribed; and the third keeps the aspiration toward practice from ever truly lapsing — even when mindfulness slips, as it inevitably sometimes does for an unenlightened mind, the resolve simply picks itself back up rather than staying lost. With this, the treatise's four components of skillful lying down — the perception of light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and the intention to rise — are all now complete, closing the entire teaching on how to sleep through the night's middle watches as part of wakeful practice itself.

22. Waking Swiftly in the Last Watch

Chinese Source

彼由如是住光明想、正念、正知、思惟起想巧便而臥，於夜中分，夜四分中，過於一分，正習睡眠。令於起時，身有堪能應時而起，非為上品惛沈睡眠纏所制伏，令將起時闇鈍、薄弱、懶惰、懈怠。由無如是闇鈍、薄弱、懶惰、懈怠，暫作意時，無有艱難，速疾能起。從諸障法淨修心者，如前應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because one properly sleeps through the middle watch — the second of the night's four divisions — abiding in the perception of light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and the intention to rise, the body is capable of rising promptly when it is time, not overcome by heavy sloth-and-torpor, and not dull, weak, lazy, or idle upon rising. Being free of such dullness, weakness, laziness, and idleness, the moment one turns attention to rising, there is no difficulty at all, and one rises swiftly. Purifying the mind of the obstructions from that point on should be understood as explained before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the direct payoff of the four-part sleep discipline just completed: a practitioner who slept with light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and the intention to rise wakes cleanly, without the heavy grogginess ("dullness"), lack of resolve ("weakness"), craving for more sleep ("laziness"), or general inertia ("idleness") that plague an ordinary waking. He compares the difference to a car whose ignition is already on — the moment attention turns to rising, the body simply responds, rather than needing to be forced awake against real resistance. Once up, walking and sitting meditation resume exactly as described earlier in the fascicle, purifying the mind of whichever hindrance surfaces using the same methods already given in full. With this, the detailed exposition (廣辨) of wakeful practice — day, first watch, sleep, and last watch, each addressed in turn — is complete, and the treatise moves to its brief summary.

23. Section: The Brief Summary — Four Correct Tasks, and Wakeful Practice Closes

The treatise closes its entire teaching on wakeful practice with a compact summary: four tasks that define what "constantly and diligently cultivating wakeful practice" actually means, each then matched back to the specific scriptural phrase that taught it.

Chinese Source

謂常勤修習悎寤瑜伽所有士夫補特伽羅，略有四種正所作事。何等為四？一者、乃至悎寤，常不捨離所修善品，無間常委修善法中，勇猛精進。二者、以時而臥，不以非時。三者、無染汙心而習睡眠，非染汙心。四者、以時悎寤，起不過時。是名四種常勤修習悎寤瑜伽所有士夫補特伽羅正所作事。依此四種正所作事，諸佛世尊為聲聞眾宣說修習悎寤瑜伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for any person who constantly and diligently cultivates wakeful practice, there are, in brief, four correct tasks. What are the four? First, up until sleep, one never abandons the wholesome practices one cultivates, applying oneself to them without interruption, earnestly and diligently. Second, one lies down only at the proper time, not at an improper time. Third, one sleeps without a defiled mind, never with a defiled mind. Fourth, one wakes at the proper time, never rising later than one should. These are the four correct tasks of any person who constantly and diligently cultivates wakeful practice. Based on these four correct tasks, the Buddhas, the Blessed Ones, teach the assembly of hearer-disciples the cultivation of wakeful practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi shows how each of the four tasks maps directly onto a specific phrase already taught in full. The instruction to practice walking and sitting meditation through the day and the first watch teaches the first task: unbroken, vigorous diligence right up until sleep. The instruction to wash the feet and lie in the lion king's posture teaches the second task: sleeping only at the proper time — the middle watch, never during the day or otherwise out of season. The instruction to lie down abiding in the perception of light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and the intention to rise teaches the third task: sleeping without a defiled mind, since all four of those supports keep the mind wholesome or at least neutral through the night. And the instruction to wake swiftly in the last watch and resume walking and sitting meditation teaches the fourth task: waking at the proper time, never oversleeping past it. Cháng Bǎi's summary point is that everything taught across the entire fascicle so far — every posture, every antidote to every hindrance, every recollection — ultimately reduces to these four simple commitments, and it is specifically in these terms, he notes, that the Buddha himself is said to have taught wakeful practice to the assembly of hearer-disciples. With this brief summary given, wakeful practice (悎寤瑜伽), the seventh of the fourteen path-provisions, is now fully complete — its brief outline, detailed exposition, and summary all given in turn, exactly as restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating were in the two fascicles before it.

24. Wakeful Practice's Harder Points Clarified, and the Whole Teaching Closes

Chinese Source

此中所說住光明想、正念、正知、思惟起想巧便臥者，顯由二緣，無染汙心而習睡眠，非染汙心。謂由正念，及由正知。復由二緣以時悎寤，起不過時。謂由住光明想，及由思惟起想。……由正念故，於善所緣攝斂而臥；由正知故，於善所緣若心退失，起諸煩惱，即便速疾能正了知。……由住光明想，及思惟起想，無重睡眠，非睡眠纏能遠隨逐。如是宣說常勤修習悎寤瑜伽所有略義，及前所說廣辯釋義，總說名為初夜後夜常勤修習悎寤瑜伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is said here — lying down skillfully abiding in the perception of light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and the intention to rise — reveals that sleeping without a defiled mind depends on two conditions: mindfulness, and clear comprehension. And waking at the proper time, never oversleeping, likewise depends on two conditions: the perception of light, and the intention to rise. …Through mindfulness, one lies down with the mind drawn in toward a wholesome object; through clear comprehension, if the mind should slip from that wholesome object and an affliction arise, one correctly recognizes it at once. …Through the perception of light and the intention to rise, sleep is not heavy, and one is not pursued far by the grip of sleep. Thus has been explained the brief summary of constantly and diligently cultivating wakeful practice, which, together with the detailed exposition given before, is together called the constant, diligent cultivation of wakeful practice in the first watch and the last watch of the night.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi resolves a natural question — why did the treatise list four supports (light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, intention to rise) but only two conditions govern each of the two remaining outcomes? — by pairing them precisely: undefiled sleep depends on mindfulness (which holds a wholesome object in place through the night) and clear comprehension (which catches and corrects any lapse into affliction); timely waking depends on the perception of light (which by itself prevents sleep from ever growing heavy) and the intention to rise (which keeps the resolve to wake from being overtaken by sleep's pull). With this final clarification, the treatise formally closes the entire teaching on wakeful practice — brief outline, detailed exposition, and brief summary together — as constant, diligent cultivation of wakeful practice in the first and last watches of the night, provision seven of the fourteen provisions for the path.

25. Section: Clear Awareness in Conduct Opens — Nine Everyday Contexts

Fascicle 24 continues directly into the eighth provision, clear awareness in conduct — a discipline of maintaining full, deliberate awareness through every ordinary activity of a monastic's day, named across nine broad contexts.

Chinese Source

云何名為正知而住？謂如有一，若往、若還正知而住，若覩、若瞻正知而住，若屈、若伸正知而住，持僧伽胝及以衣鉢正知而住，若食、若飲、若噉、若嘗正知而住，若行、若住、若坐、若臥正知而住，於悎寤時正知而住，若語、若默正知而住，解勞睡時正知而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by clear awareness in conduct? It is like one who maintains clear awareness whether going or returning; maintains clear awareness whether looking directly or glancing about; maintains clear awareness whether bending or extending the limbs; maintains clear awareness while carrying the great robe, the other robes, and the bowl; maintains clear awareness while eating, drinking, chewing, or tasting; maintains clear awareness whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down; maintains clear awareness while awake; maintains clear awareness whether speaking or silent; and maintains clear awareness even while resting from fatigue in sleep.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames clear awareness in conduct as wakeful practice's natural extension: where the previous provision structured an entire day and night around meditation and disciplined sleep, this provision asks that the same deliberate, unclouded awareness carry into every ordinary physical act along the way. The nine contexts named cover essentially the full range of a monastic's activity: traveling to and from a place (alms round, visiting relatives, visiting another monastery); the simple acts of looking directly at something versus glancing around; the physical motions of bending and stretching the limbs; the correct handling of the great ceremonial robe, the other two robes, and the alms bowl; the various stages of taking a meal; the four basic postures of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down; ordinary waking activity generally; speaking as opposed to remaining silent; and even the act of resting or napping to relieve fatigue outside the formal sleep periods already covered. Nothing in a practitioner's day, in other words, is considered too mundane to be done with full awareness — the treatise is about to work through each of these nine in turn, starting with the first and richest: going and returning.

26. Going and Returning: Knowing "I Go, I Return"

Chinese Source

所言往者，謂如有一，往詣聚落，往聚落間；往詣家屬，往家屬間；往詣道場，往道場間。所言還者，謂如有一，從聚落還，聚落間還；從家屬還，家屬間還；從道場還，道場間還。所言往還正知住者，謂於自往，正知我往；及於自還，正知我還。

Ru-Yi Translation

Going means: it is like one who journeys to a village, or among villages; journeys to visit relatives, or among relatives' homes; journeys to a monastery, or among monasteries. Returning means: it is like one who returns from a village, or from among villages; returns from visiting relatives, or from among relatives' homes; returns from a monastery, or from among monasteries. Clear awareness in going and returning means: in one's own going, clearly knowing "I go"; and in one's own returning, clearly knowing "I return."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the basic definitions of "going" and "returning" as simply the ordinary range of a monastic's travel — into a village or between villages for the alms round, to visit relatives, or to visit another monastery to consult a teacher — with "returning" as their exact mirror. The clear awareness itself, at its most basic level, is nothing more than knowing that one is going or returning at all: not walking on autopilot, lost in random thought, but aware in the moment "I am going," "I am returning," with the mind's activity actually tracking the body's rather than drifting elsewhere. Cháng Bǎi frames this as the necessary first layer beneath everything more specific that follows — knowing where one is going before asking whether one should be going there in the first place.

27. Section: Which Households to Avoid, and When Not to Visit

The treatise names, with striking specificity, exactly which households a practitioner should never visit, and exactly which circumstances make an otherwise appropriate visit inappropriate.

Chinese Source

於所應往及非所往，能正了知；於所應還及非所還，能正了知。……或有居家我不應往，何等居家？謂唱令家，或酤酒家，或婬女家，或國王家，或旃荼羅、羯恥那家；或復有家一向誹謗，不可迴轉。……於應往時及非往時，能正了知……又有居家我雖應往，不應太早太晚而往。若施主家有遽務時，亦不應往；若戲樂時、若有營構嚴飾事時、若為世間弊穢法時、若忿競時，亦不應往。

Ru-Yi Translation

One correctly discerns which places should be visited and which should not; one correctly discerns when one should return and when one should not. …There are households one should not visit — which households? Those of criers who announce slaughter, or wine-sellers, or courtesans, or the king, or executioners and jailers; or households that persistently slander the Triple Gem without any prospect of change. …One correctly discerns the proper time to go and the improper time. …There are households one may rightly visit, yet one should go neither too early nor too late. If the host's household is occupied with urgent affairs, one should not go; if they are engaged in revelry, or in construction or decoration, in worldly self-indulgence, or in anger and dispute, one should likewise not go.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the households worth actively visiting — the homes of respected nobility, learned brahmins, devout lay disciples, officials, wealthy patrons, elders of good character, or established merchants — as places where alms rounds, teaching, and mutual support between monastic and lay community naturally happen. Against these, six categories are named as places to avoid entirely: households of criers whose trade is publicly announcing an animal about to be slaughtered; wine-sellers, given the Brahmajāla-style tradition that even facilitating others' drinking carries serious karmic consequence; brothels; the royal palace, illustrated with a cautionary story of a monk whose frequent visits to a king led a demoted minister to suspect the monk of having influenced his downfall — proximity to power being politically dangerous even when entirely innocent; households of executioners or jailers, whose trade is bound up with killing or punishment; and households of the inveterately faithless, so committed to denigrating the Triple Gem that a visit could accomplish nothing but reinforce their disdain.

Beyond the household itself, timing matters just as much: visiting an otherwise appropriate home too early or too late is inconsiderate, as is visiting a monastery without notice — Cháng Bǎi notes specifically that a nun should never enter a community of monks unannounced. A household genuinely busy with an urgent matter should not be visited even if visits are normally welcome; nor should one visit while the household is caught up in entertainment or celebration, mid-renovation or otherwise absorbed in worldly display, indulging in sensual pleasure, or in the middle of a quarrel — each of these makes the visit itself, however well-intentioned, an intrusion rather than a support.

28. Going and Returning Closes: A Fourfold Pattern That Recurs Throughout

The treatise closes "going and returning" by naming four dimensions of clear awareness — the act itself, the place, the time, and the manner befitting a monastic's appearance — a fourfold structure that will recur, essentially unchanged, across all nine contexts of clear awareness still to come.

Chinese Source

彼由成就此正知故，自知而往，自知而還；往所應往，非非所往，還所應還，非非所還；以時往還，不以非時；如其色類動止軌則、禮式、威儀，應往應還，如是而往，如是而還。是名正知。如是名為若往、若還正知而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having accomplished this clear awareness, one knowingly goes and knowingly returns; one goes where one should go, not where one should not, and returns where one should return, not where one should not; one goes and returns at the proper time, not at an improper time; and, in keeping with the deportment, etiquette, and demeanor proper to one's appearance as a monastic, one should go and should return exactly so. This is clear awareness. This is called clear awareness in going and returning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies four distinct dimensions folded into this closing formula, which the treatise will apply again and again to every remaining context of clear awareness: awareness of the act itself (simply knowing "I go," "I return"); awareness of place (the specific households and locations already named as appropriate or inappropriate); awareness of time (neither too early nor too late, and not when a household is occupied); and awareness of manner — a monastic's shaved head, plain-dyed robes, and deliberately unadorned appearance are themselves, Cháng Bǎi notes (citing the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation), meant to be reflected on with real awareness rather than taken for granted, so that the humble outward form continually reinforces the inward resolve of genuine renunciation. Once this fourfold pattern — act, place, time, manner — is recognized here, the same analysis will simply be applied to each remaining activity in turn, so later sections can move more quickly through what is structurally the same territory.

29. Looking and Gazing: Incidental Sight Versus Deliberate Attention

Chinese Source

所言覩者，謂於如前所列諸事，若往、若還，先無覺慧、先無功用、先無欲樂，於其中間眼見眾色，是名為覩。所言瞻者，謂於如前所列諸事，若往、若還，覺慧為先、功用為先、欲樂為先，眼見眾色。謂或諸王，或諸王等，或諸僚佐，或諸黎庶，或婆羅門，或諸居士，或饒財寶長者商主，或餘外物、房舍、屋宇、殿堂、廊廟，或餘世間眾雜妙事。觀見此等，是名為瞻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Incidental sight means: in the course of going or returning on the affairs already listed, without any prior deliberation, effort, or intention, one's eyes happen to fall on various forms along the way — this is called incidental sight. Deliberate gazing means: in the course of going or returning on those same affairs, with deliberation, effort, and intention leading the way, one's eyes take in various forms — kings, or those of equal rank, or ministers, or common people, or brahmins, or householders, or wealthy elders and merchants, or other external things such as dwellings, rooftops, halls, and galleries, or various other marvelous things of the world. Observing such things is called deliberate gazing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a clean distinction between the two Chinese terms: incidental sight (覩) is whatever the eye happens to register without any prior intention to look — a person, a building, a stranger passed on the road, simply seen in passing; deliberate gazing (瞻) is looking with actual purpose, the eye directed on purpose at something specific, whether a person of note or an object of genuine interest, such as visiting a doctor's office and deliberately looking at the doctor while incidentally also seeing other patients in the waiting room. Both, the treatise insists, require clear awareness — not suppressing sight altogether, which is impossible, but knowing in each case which kind of seeing is happening, whether the object is one worth dwelling on or one better left unexamined (something likely to provoke greed, aversion, or delusion), and, if it is worth attending to, doing so for exactly as long as is fitting and no longer. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise elsewhere identifies visible objects as ultimately just color and shape provisionally construed as things — recognizing this doesn't require looking away from the world, only looking at it without being pulled in by it. The same fourfold structure just established for going and returning — awareness of the act, the object, the time, and the manner — governs looking and gazing in exactly the same way, closing this section with the identical formula.

30. Bending and Stretching: Clear Awareness Down to Each Limb

Chinese Source

謂彼如是覩時、瞻時，若往為先、若還為先，或屈伸足、或屈伸臂、或屈伸手、或復屈伸隨一支節，是名屈伸。若於屈伸所有自相，能正了知；若所屈伸，能正了知；若屈伸時，能正了知；若如是屈及如是伸，能正了知；是名正知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether while thus looking or gazing, or leading into going or returning, one bends or extends the foot, or bends or extends the arm, or bends or extends the hand, or bends or extends any other limb — this is called bending and stretching. Correctly knowing the mark of the bending or stretching itself; correctly knowing what part is bent or stretched; correctly knowing when it is bent or stretched; correctly knowing that it is bent or stretched exactly so — this is clear awareness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as clear awareness extended down to the smallest physical gestures — every bend of a finger, wrist, arm, or foot done with actual knowledge of doing it, rather than as an unconscious fidget. He gives concrete monastic etiquette as the practical content behind the abstraction: never stretching the legs out toward a Buddha image in a shrine hall, never sitting with the knees splayed wide in another's presence, never swaying the body, arms, or head absentmindedly — deportment the treatise elsewhere sums up as never letting any part of the body move without purpose. The same fourfold clear-knowing already established — the act itself, the specific limb involved, the appropriate time, and the appropriate manner — applies here exactly as it did to going, returning, looking, and gazing, and closes this section with the same formula.

31. Section: Carrying the Robes and Bowl With Clear Awareness

The treatise turns to a monastic's most basic possessions — the great robe, the other robes, and the alms bowl — defining each precisely and specifying the etiquette of actually maintaining and wearing them.

Chinese Source

謂有大衣，或六十條、或九條等、或兩重刺，名僧伽胝。被服受用，能正將護，說名為持。若有中衣，若有下衣，或持為衣，或有長衣，或應作淨，或已作淨，如是一切說名為衣。若堪受持或鐵、或瓦乞食應器，說名為鉢。現充受用，能正將護，說名為持。若於如是或僧伽胝、或衣、或鉢所有自相，能正了知；於所應持或僧伽胝、或衣、或鉢或淨不淨，能正了知；若於此時或僧伽胝、或衣、或鉢已持應持，能正了知；若於如是或僧伽胝、或衣、或鉢應如是持，能正了知；是名正知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The great robe — whether of sixty strips, or nine strips, or sewn with double stitching — is called the saṅghāṭī. Wearing and using it, properly protecting it, is called holding it. There is also the middle robe and the lower robe; there may be robes held as substitute robes, or extra robes, robes yet to be formally consecrated, or robes already consecrated — all of these together are called robes. A begging-bowl of proper measure, capable of being maintained, whether of iron or clay, is called the bowl. Keeping it presently fit for use, properly protecting it, is called holding it. Correctly knowing the specific nature of the great robe, the robes, or the bowl; correctly knowing which one should be held, and whether it is consecrated or not; correctly knowing, at this time, which is already held and which should be held; correctly knowing that the great robe, the robes, or the bowl should be held exactly so — this is clear awareness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the traditional three robes: the great robe (saṅghāṭī), worn when entering a village or royal court, sewn from strips of cloth in nine graded patterns (nine, eleven, or thirteen strips for the lowest grade, up to twenty-five for the highest) using a specific "reversed stitch" meant to prevent the whole seam from unraveling if one thread breaks; the middle robe, worn for assemblies and teaching; and the lower robe, worn for daily labor. He notes that Chinese Buddhism eventually adapted these Indian garments into the jackets and long robes still worn today, reserving the original three robes mainly for ceremonial use over them. Beyond the three robes proper, a monastic might hold an extra or substitute robe under specific allowances — formally "consecrated" through a brief ritual declaration to a fellow monastic that prevents an extra possession from becoming an object of attachment — and the bowl itself must be sized to one's actual appetite, neither so small that it leaves one hungry nor so large that food goes to waste.

He then draws out the practical etiquette folded into "holding" properly: knowing which robe is called for and whether it has been consecrated, wearing it correctly buttoned rather than left open, neither hiked up too short nor allowed to tear or hang loosely agape, and — for the bowl specifically — never setting it down in a filthy or unclean spot, nor in a ditch, nor near a cliff edge where it could be knocked over and broken. Every physical detail of managing these basic possessions, Cháng Bǎi stresses, is itself an occasion for the same fourfold clear awareness — the object itself, which one is called for, the correct time, and the correct manner — already established for going, returning, looking, gazing, bending, and stretching.

32. Section: Eating, Drinking, Chewing, and Tasting With Clear Awareness

The treatise distinguishes four modes of taking nourishment by their specific character, before applying the same fourfold clear awareness — the food itself, what's appropriate, the right time, the right manner — to all of them together.

Chinese Source

謂噉餅麨、或飯、或糜、或羹、或臛，或有所餘造作轉變可噉可食，能持生命。如是等類皆名為噉，亦名為食。云何為嘗？謂嘗乳、酪、生酥、熟酥、油、蜜、沙糖、魚、肉、醯、鮓，或新果實，或有種種咀嚼品類。如是一切總名為嘗，亦名為食。云何為飲？謂沙糖汁、或石蜜汁、或飯漿飲、或鑽酪飲、或酢為飲、或抨酪飲，乃至於水，總名為飲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Chewing means eating flatbread, roasted flour, rice, gruel, soup, or meat broth, or anything else processed and transformed into something chewable and edible, capable of sustaining life — all such things are called chewing, and also called eating. What is tasting? Tasting milk, curd, fresh butter, clarified butter, oil, honey, sugar, fish, meat, vinegar, pickled foods, fresh fruit, or various things taken in small bites — all of this together is called tasting. What is drinking? Sugarcane juice, or rock-sugar syrup, or rice-water, or a churned buttermilk drink, or a vinegar drink, or a whey drink, down to plain water — all of this together is called drinking.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the terms mainly by function: chewing covers the staple foods proper — bread, rice, porridge, and soups, whatever actually sustains the body; tasting covers the accompaniments — dairy products, oils, sweeteners, and fresh produce, taken in smaller amounts alongside the meal; and drinking covers liquids generally, from flavored juices down to plain water. He notes the treatise elsewhere reminds the reader that even something as solid-seeming as bread is, on examination, a composite of flour, water, and whatever else went into it — a "provisionally real thing," not some single, findable essence called "bread," the same analysis already applied to food back in fascicle 23's teaching on moderation in eating. The same fourfold clear awareness that has governed every context so far — knowing the specific nature of what's being eaten or drunk, knowing what's appropriate to one's own condition (a weak stomach calls for solid rice rather than thin gruel, for instance), knowing the correct time, and eating or drinking in the correct manner — closes this section exactly as it closed every context before it, down to the specific utensils (a fork for fruit, a knife for cutting, a spoon for liquids) fitting the specific food at hand.

33. The Four Postures and Wakeful Activity, Briefly Redefined

Chinese Source

謂如有一，於經行處來往經行，或復往詣同法者所，或涉道路。如是等類，說名為行。復如有一，住經行處，住諸同法阿遮利耶、鄔波陀耶，及諸尊長、等尊長前。如是等類，說名為住。復如有一，或於大床……結加趺坐，端身正願，安住背念。如是等類，說名為坐。復如有一，出住處外洗濯其足，還入住處……右脅而臥，重疊其足。如是等類，說名為臥。復如有一，於晝日分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心；於初夜分、於後夜分經行、宴坐，從順障法淨修其心；說名悎寤。

Ru-Yi Translation

Walking means pacing back and forth in a place set aside for walking meditation, or going to visit a fellow practitioner, or traveling a road. Standing means standing still in a place of walking meditation, or standing before a fellow practitioner's teacher of discipline, one's own preceptor, or other venerable elders. Sitting means sitting cross-legged on a large bed, small rope bed, grass seat, spread mat, or sitting-cloth, holding the body upright, forming a correct resolve, and abiding turned away from unwholesome thought. Lying down means, having washed the feet outside the dwelling and returned inside — whether on a bed, a grass seat, in a forest dwelling, under a tree, or in a quiet room — lying on the right side, one leg resting upon the other. Wakeful activity means practicing walking and sitting meditation through the day, and through the first and last watches of the night, purifying the mind of the obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes these five definitions largely recap what the treatise already established in far more detail earlier in the fascicle — sitting posture matches the description given for the day's practice, lying posture matches the lion-king posture given for proper sleep, and wakeful activity is simply a one-line restatement of the entire wakeful-practice teaching just closed. What's new here is mainly the context for "standing": specifically standing before one's discipline teacher, one's preceptor, or other respected elders — a posture of attentive respect distinct from merely pausing during walking meditation. Applied here, the same fourfold clear awareness — the posture itself, what's appropriate, the right time, the right manner — governs all four postures and wakeful activity exactly as it has governed every context so far; having established the pattern this many times, the treatise moves more quickly through familiar ground before turning to genuinely new material: speech, silence, and rest.

34. Section: Three Occasions for Speech

The treatise names exactly three occasions when a practitioner devoted to silence should nonetheless speak: reciting what has been learned, teaching others, and offering mutual encouragement.

Chinese Source

復如有一，於未受法正受正習，令得究竟。……即於如是已所受法，言善通利，謂大音聲若讀、若誦。或復為他廣說開示。……於時時間與諸有智同梵行者，或餘在家諸賢善者，語言談論，共相慶慰，為欲勸勵及求資具。如是等類，說名為語。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like one who properly receives and practices a teaching not yet received, so as to bring it to completion … and, regarding a teaching already received, becomes fluent in its language, reciting or reading it aloud. Or one speaks in order to explain it fully to others. …And from time to time, one converses with wise fellow practitioners, or with virtuous lay people, mutually encouraging one another, so as to exhort and inspire, and also to request the necessities of life. All such things are called speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three legitimate reasons to break silence: first, reciting scripture aloud while first learning it, or reviewing what's already been memorized, since speaking it out loud strengthens retention in a way silent reading doesn't; second, actually teaching it to someone else, which — as any teacher discovers — clarifies one's own understanding even further than solitary review; and third, periodic conversation with fellow practitioners or supportive lay people, exchanging encouragement about genuine progress in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, or requesting the basic supports of practice from a patron. Each, Cháng Bǎi notes, is speech in service of the path rather than idle talk, and the same fourfold clear awareness governing every context so far applies here too: knowing what one is saying, to whom, at what time, and in what manner.

35. Two Occasions for Silence: Solitary Reflection and Meditation

Chinese Source

復如有一，隨先所聞、隨先所習言善通利究竟諸法，獨處空閑，思惟其義，籌量觀察。或處靜室，令心內住、等住、安住，及與近住、調伏、寂靜、最極寂靜、一趣、等持。或復於彼毗鉢舍那修瑜伽行。如是等類，說名為默。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is also like one who, regarding a teaching already fluently mastered through prior hearing and practice, dwells alone in solitude, reflecting on its meaning, weighing and examining it. Or, in a quiet room, one settles the mind inwardly, evenly, and firmly, drawing it near, taming it, calming it, calming it utterly, unifying it, and holding it in balance. Or one cultivates yogic practice through insight meditation. All such things are called silence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names two silent activities as the counterpart to the three occasions for speech: solitary reflection on a teaching already learned — turning it over privately, weighing its meaning, rather than merely having memorized the words — and formal meditation itself, both calming practice (restated here through its nine familiar stages: settling the mind inward, evening it out, steadying it, drawing it close, taming it, calming it, calming it utterly, unifying it in one-pointed absorption, and finally holding it in effortless balance) and insight meditation built on that foundation. Both, he notes, genuinely require silence — the deepening of the teaching's meaning and the actual work of concentration and insight happen internally, not through further speech. Together with the three occasions for speech, this closes the treatise's account of when a practitioner should talk and when they should stay silent.

36. Section: Rest From Fatigue, and All Eight Remaining Contexts Close Together

The treatise permits genuine rest when heat or exhaustion make it necessary, then closes walking, standing, sitting, lying down, wakeful activity, speech, silence, and rest — all eight remaining contexts — with the same fourfold clear awareness used throughout.

Chinese Source

復如有一，於其熱分極炎暑時，或為熱逼、或為劬勞，便生疲倦，非時惛寐，樂著睡眠，是名勞睡。若復於行廣說乃至於解勞睡所有自相，能正了知。於所應行，乃至於所應解勞睡，能正了知。於應行時，乃至於應解勞睡時，能正了知。如所應行，乃至如所應解勞睡，能正了知。是名正知。如是名為於行、於住、於坐、於臥、於其悎寤、於語、於默、於解勞睡正知而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is also like one who, in the hottest part of the hot season, oppressed by heat or worn out from exertion, grows fatigued and, though it is not the proper time, becomes drowsy and takes to sleep — this is called rest from fatigue. Correctly knowing the specific nature of walking, and so on up through rest from fatigue; correctly knowing what is appropriate in each; correctly knowing the proper time for each; correctly knowing that each should be done exactly so — this is clear awareness. This is called clear awareness in walking, standing, sitting, lying down, wakeful activity, speech, silence, and rest from fatigue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "rest from fatigue" as the treatise's realistic concession: during the hottest part of the year, or after genuinely demanding labor, brief sleep outside the formal rest periods is not indulgence but simple good sense — pushing through exhaustion produces poor practice, not better practice. He notes the same principle from the antidotes to sloth-and-torpor earlier in the fascicle: if every remedy has been tried and drowsiness still wins, the honest response is to actually rest, then return to practice once genuinely refreshed. With this, all eight remaining contexts of clear awareness — walking, standing, sitting, lying down, wakeful activity, speech, silence, and rest — close together under the identical fourfold structure already established for going and returning: awareness of the specific activity itself, of what's appropriate to it, of its proper timing, and of the correct manner of carrying it out. Every one of the nine contexts named at this section's opening — going/returning, looking/gazing, bending/stretching, robe and bowl, eating and drinking, the four postures and wakeful activity, speech and silence, and rest — has now been individually unpacked in full.

37. Section: A Second, Deeper Pass — Travel Safety and the Moon Simile

The treatise now revisits all nine contexts a second time, asking not just what clear awareness means for each but what sequence and purpose underlie it. Going and returning reopens first, adding real-world safety guidance and a memorable simile: approaching a patron's house the way the moon waxes toward fullness.

Chinese Source

又如所往，如是應往。不與暴亂惡象俱行，不與暴亂眾車、惡馬、惡牛、惡狗而共同行，不入鬧叢，不蹈棘刺，不踰垣牆，不越坑塹，不墮山崖，不溺深水，不履糞穢。應隨月喻往施主家，具足慚愧，遠離憍傲，盪滌身心，不求利養，不希恭敬。如自獲得所有利養，心生喜悅，如是於他所得利養，心亦喜悅。不自高舉，不輕懱他，心懷哀愍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, one should go exactly as one ought to go: not alongside a rampaging, vicious elephant; not alongside a runaway cart, a vicious horse, a vicious ox, or a vicious dog; not into a chaotic crowd; not treading on thorns; not climbing over a wall; not crossing a ditch or pit; not falling from a cliff; not drowning in deep water; not walking through filth. One should approach a patron's house as the simile of the moon suggests — fully possessed of conscience and shame, free of arrogance, with body and mind cleansed, seeking neither gain nor reverence. Just as one feels joy on receiving some gain oneself, one should feel the same joy when another receives it. One should neither exalt oneself nor belittle others, but hold compassion in the heart.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the physical safety guidance plainly: a monastic walking to a village or a patron's home is instructed to steer clear of dangerous animals, unruly crowds, and hazardous terrain — brambles, walls, ditches, cliffs, deep water, filth — not out of timidity, but because a practitioner has a responsibility to protect the body that carries out the practice.

The moon simile carries the real weight, though: just as the moon waxes steadily from new to full, growing only brighter and never regressing, a practitioner approaching a patron's home should carry a mind that has only grown purer over time — conscience and shame intact, free of arrogance, cleansed of the very neediness that might otherwise color the visit, seeking neither material gain nor the patron's personal esteem. Cháng Bǎi draws out the generosity this implies: genuine joy at a companion's gift is just as real as joy at one's own, with no jealousy if a fellow monastic receives more, no superiority if one receives more oneself, and — above all — compassion held for the patron as a fellow being worth caring about, not merely a source of support. He frames this as the emotional discipline underlying an otherwise simple errand: an alms round done without any of this inner cultivation is just begging, while one done this way is itself part of the path.

38. Four Points of Self-Restraint When Receiving Support

Chinese Source

又應如是自持其心，往施主家。豈有出家往詣他所，要望他施非不惠施，廣說乃至要當速疾而非遲緩。又作是心：我於今假往施主家，所受施物應知其量。又我不應利養因緣，矯詐虛誑、現惑亂相、以利求利。得利養已，無染、無愛，亦不耽嗜、饕餮、迷悶、堅執、湎著而受用之。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should also restrain the mind in this way when going to a patron's house: how could one who has gone forth visit another expecting to be given something, as though it were owed rather than freely offered — and so on, expecting it to come quickly rather than slowly? One should further think: I go now, for a time, to the patron's house, and should know the proper measure of what is received. And I should not, for the sake of gain, resort to deceit, false pretense, or the display of confusing appearances, using gain to seek further gain. Having received support, one should use it without attachment, without craving, and without indulgence, gluttony, infatuation, rigid grasping, or heedless abandon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names four distinct disciplines of mind for receiving lay support. First, never treating a gift as owed — no silent expectation that a visit must produce a donation, must come quickly, must be offered respectfully, must be generous, or must be of fine quality; a patron's freedom to give or not give must be genuinely respected. Second, knowing the proper measure of what to accept — taking only what's actually needed for practice, not accumulating out of habit or anxiety. Third, never resorting to improper means to increase what's received: no false claims of spiritual attainment or supernatural power, no theatrical display of exaggerated serenity, no wearing a patron down through persistent pressure "the way one grinds an inkstone," and no comparing one gift to another to shame a patron into giving more — Cháng Bǎi treats all of these as forms of manipulation dressed up as spiritual practice. And fourth, once support has actually been received, using it free of the same six degrees of attachment named earlier in the treatise's teaching on food: no craving, infatuation, gluttony, confusion, rigid possessiveness, or reckless indulgence. Together these four disciplines govern the entire economic relationship between a monastic and their lay supporters — protecting both the patron's freedom to give and the practitioner's own freedom from becoming subtly dependent on, or manipulative toward, the gifts received.

39. What Not to Look At: Entertainment and Alluring Forms

Chinese Source

何色類色所不應觀？謂諸妓樂戲笑歡娛，或餘遊戲所作歌舞音樂等事；如是復有母邑殊勝，幼少盛年，美妙形色；或復有餘所見眾色，能壞梵行、能障梵行、能令種種諸惡不善尋思現行。如是色類所有眾色，不應觀視。

Ru-Yi Translation

What class of forms should not be observed? Musical performance, laughter and revelry, and other entertainments involving song, dance, and music; likewise, women of striking beauty, youthful and in the prime of life, of lovely form; or any other sights capable of undermining pure conduct, obstructing pure conduct, or provoking various unwholesome deliberations. Forms of this class should not be looked upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as simple, practical guidance for someone walking through a public street: performances, revelry, and music tend to stir up restlessness and craving, and are best passed by without lingering attention; the same goes for any strikingly attractive person, or any advertisement, image, or display specifically designed to provoke desire or agitation. None of this requires physically averting the eyes in an exaggerated way — simply not choosing to dwell on or seek out such sights, letting them pass as incidental rather than deliberate seeing, in line with the distinction already drawn earlier between the two.

40. Section: What Should Be Looked At — Old Age, Illness, and Death

Against what should be avoided, the treatise names exactly what a practitioner should deliberately look at when the opportunity arises: the visible reality of aging, sickness, and death — described here in unflinching, vivid detail as genuine supports for the path.

Chinese Source

何色類色是所應觀？謂諸所有衰老朽邁上氣者身、傴僂憑杖戰掉者身。或諸疾苦重病者身，腳腫、手腫、腹腫、面腫、膚色萎黃、瘡癬、疥癩、眾苦逼迫、身形委頓、身形洪爛、諸根闇鈍。或有夭喪，死經一日、或經二日、或經七日，被諸烏鵲、餓狗、鵄鷲、狐狼、野干種種暴惡傍生禽獸之所食噉。或命終已，出置高床，上施幰帳，前後大眾或哀或哭，以其灰土塵坌身髮，生愁、生苦、生悲、生怨、生憂、生惱。如是等類所有眾色，我應觀察。觀是眾色，能順梵行、能攝梵行、能令諸善尋思現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What class of forms should be observed? The bodies of those aged and worn down, gasping for breath, bent double, leaning on a staff, trembling as they move. Or the bodies of those gravely ill — feet swollen, hands swollen, abdomen swollen, face swollen, skin sallow and withered, covered in sores, scabies, or leprous lesions, oppressed by manifold suffering, the body wasted, or the body's flesh decayed and rotting, the faculties dulled. Or those who have died before their time — a body one day dead, or two days, or seven, devoured by crows and magpies, starving dogs, kites and vultures, foxes, wolves, and jackals, all manner of fierce and savage beasts and birds. Or, once life has ended, the body carried out and placed on a high bier beneath a canopy, the assembled crowd before and behind either grieving in silence or wailing aloud, ashes and dust scattered over the body and hair, giving rise to sorrow, suffering, grief, resentment, distress, and anguish. All such forms as these I should observe. Observing these forms accords with pure conduct, supports pure conduct, and gives rise to wholesome deliberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as deliberately unflinching, and reads each of the three stages as its own contemplation. Old age is meant to be looked at directly — the bent back, the trembling hands, the labored breath, the dependence on a cane — precisely so a practitioner recognizes their own future in it; he tells of a well-known teacher who admitted only in his own old age that he finally understood why elderly people groaned so long before managing to sit down, having wondered about it impatiently in his youth. Illness is meant to be looked at with the same directness — swollen limbs, sallow skin, sores, wasting, the confusion of a mind dulled by suffering — the kind of sight readily available in any hospital ward, and one that naturally provokes both compassion and urgency about one's own practice. Death completes the sequence in two stages: first the body itself, left exposed and within days set upon by carrion birds and scavenging animals — the same unsparing physical realism as the nine-stage corpse contemplation given earlier in the fascicle — and then, just as pointedly, the grief of the mourners themselves, ash and dust scattered over hair and clothing in the old Indian mourning custom, open weeping in the crowd, real sorrow that needs no exaggeration to make its point. Cháng Bǎi's reading is that all three are meant to be looked at, not merely known about in the abstract: direct sight, more than intellectual acknowledgment, is what actually loosens attachment to the idea of a stable, permanent, attractive self — and it is precisely this looking, done deliberately rather than avoided, that the treatise calls supportive of pure conduct.

41. Entering a Patron's House: Posture, Seating, and Deportment

Chinese Source

不應搖身、搖臂、搖頭、跳躑、攜手、叉腰、竦肩入施主家，不應輒坐所不許座，不應不審觀座而坐，不應放縱一切身分，不應翹足、不應交足、不太狹足、不太廣足，端嚴而坐。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should not enter a patron's house swaying the body, swinging the arms, shaking the head, hopping about, holding hands with another, hands on the hips, or shoulders hunched up. One should not sit in a seat one has not been offered; one should not sit down without first examining the seat carefully; one should not let any part of the body go slack. One should not cross one leg over the other, nor sit with the legs crossed, nor with the feet too close together, nor too far apart — one should sit upright and dignified.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as concrete deportment for entering someone else's home: no swaying gait, swinging arms, restless head movements, hopping, hand-holding, hands-on-hips posture, or hunched shoulders — all of it reading as undignified or overly casual. Seating carries its own etiquette: a host's own household has seats of different rank and significance, and sitting in an unoffered seat, or a seat clearly reserved for someone senior, is a real breach of manners; checking a seat before sitting is also simply practical, since something could easily have been left on it. Once seated, the posture matters as much as the seat itself — no crossed legs, no legs held unnaturally close together or splayed too wide, roughly shoulder-width being the natural, dignified middle; Cháng Bǎi adds the practical monastic instruction to sit toward the front third of a seat rather than sinking fully back into it, which naturally keeps the spine upright and the feet planted rather than lounging. All of it, he notes, is simply the "bending and stretching" clear awareness already introduced earlier, now applied to the specific setting of a lay household.

42. Wearing the Robe and Handling the Bowl in a Patron's Home

Chinese Source

不應開紐、不軒、不磔，亦不褰張而被法服；所服法衣并皆齊整，不高、不下、不如象鼻；非如多羅樹間房穗、非如龍首、非如豆摶而被法服。不應持鉢預就其食，不應持鉢在飲食上，不應置鉢在雜穢處、若坑澗處、若崖岸處。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should not wear the Dharma robe with its fastenings undone, hiked up, torn, or hanging open. The robe worn should be entirely even — neither too high nor too low, not drooping like an elephant's trunk; not uneven like the gaps between palm-tree fronds, not bulging like a dragon's head, not puffed out like a lump of bean paste. One should not hold the bowl and approach the food ahead of time; one should not rest the bowl on top of food or drink; one should not set the bowl down in a filthy place, a ditch, or the edge of a cliff.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the same robe-and-bowl clear awareness given earlier in the fascicle, now made specific to visiting a lay household: the robe properly fastened rather than left open, draped evenly rather than sagging on one side like a trailing elephant's trunk or bulging unevenly like ill-fitting cloth bunched at the collar — small details, but ones that reflect real attentiveness rather than carelessness. The bowl carries its own etiquette of patience and care: not reaching for it before the community has actually begun eating, not resting it carelessly on top of food or drink, and never setting it down somewhere it could be knocked over, fall into a ditch, or slide off a cliff edge — practical concern for a genuinely important possession, not fussiness for its own sake.

43. Taking a Meal With Real Care

Chinese Source

又應次第受用飲食。不應以飯覆羹臛上，不以羹臛覆其飯上，不應饕餮受諸飲食，不應嫌恨受諸飲食，不太麤食、不太細食，不應圓摶食，不應舐手、不應舐鉢，不振手食、不振足食，不應齧斷而食其食。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should also take food in the proper order. One should not cover the soup with rice, nor cover the rice with soup; one should not receive food with gluttonous greed, nor with resentment; one should not eat too coarsely, nor too fastidiously; one should not roll food into large balls; one should not lick the hand, nor lick the bowl; one should not shake the hand or the foot while eating; one should not tear food apart with the teeth while eating it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "proper order" as seating and serving by seniority — those with more years in the monastic life eating first — and then works through a list of small table manners that add up to real consideration: not hiding rice under soup or soup under rice, presumably to avoid either concealing spoiled food or wasting what's underneath; taking food without gluttonous grasping and, just as importantly, without resentment if a patron happens not to give anything at all, since — as the treatise on moderation in eating already established — no one owes a monastic a gift. Neither picking at food too fussily nor eating too crudely, not rolling food into unwieldy balls, not licking the hand or the bowl clean, not fidgeting hands or feet at the table, and cutting rather than tearing food apart with the teeth — Cháng Bǎi treats all of it as simply making a shared meal pleasant for everyone present, monastic and lay host alike, rather than treating table manners as beneath a serious practitioner's attention.

44. Walking Meditation Revisited: Where, When Not To, and How

Chinese Source

從施主家還歸住處，於晝夜分，在自別人所經行處往反經行；非於他處、非不委處、非不恣處、非不與處而輒經行。非身劬勞、非身疲倦、非心掉舉所制伏時而習經行。為修善品、為善思惟，內攝諸根、心不外亂而習經行，不太馳速、不太躁動，亦非一向專事往來而習經行，時時進步、時時停住而習經行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having returned from a patron's house to one's dwelling, one paces back and forth, by day and by night, in a walking-meditation space specifically one's own — not in another's space, not in an unfamiliar space, not in a space where one cannot move freely, and not in a space the community has not allotted. One does not practice walking meditation when overcome by bodily exertion, bodily fatigue, or mental restlessness. For the sake of wholesome cultivation and correct reflection, drawing the faculties inward with the mind undistracted, one practices walking meditation neither too fast nor too agitated, and not simply pacing back and forth continuously, but advancing and pausing by turns.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi specifies the walking space itself must be one's own — sized to one's own build and stride, distinct from a space shared with or belonging to someone else — familiar enough not to need constant caution about its terrain, open enough to pace freely back and forth without feeling hemmed in, and properly assigned by the community rather than simply claimed. Three states rule out walking meditation entirely: bodily exhaustion, bodily fatigue, and a mind already caught in restlessness, since none of these conditions can actually benefit from the practice. Done correctly, the pace is neither hurried nor agitated, and — distinctively — not simply continuous back-and-forth pacing: the practitioner periodically stops to check whether the mind has wandered from its object, then resumes, alternating movement and pause. Cháng Bǎi contrasts this with two familiar styles he's observed: Chan-style walking meditation, often fast-paced with an abrupt stop signaled by a clapper, and the Theravāda style of very slow walking with close attention to each footfall — neither, he notes, quite matches this treatise's specific instruction to alternate walking and brief stillness as its own technique.

45. Sitting and Sleep, Briefly Recapitulated

Chinese Source

復於大床，或小繩床，或草葉座，或尼師壇，或阿練若、樹下、冢間，或空閑室，結加趺坐，端身正願，安住背念，而習宴坐。於夜中分如法寢息，於晝日分及夜初分，修諸善品不應太急。如是寢時，應如前說，住光明想、正念、正知、思惟起想。於夜後分速疾悎寤。

Ru-Yi Translation

Then, on a large bed, a small rope bed, a grass seat, a sitting-cloth, in a forest dwelling, under a tree, near a charnel ground, or in a quiet room, one sits cross-legged, holds the body upright, forms a correct resolve, and abides turned from unwholesome thought — this is sitting meditation. In the middle watch of the night, one sleeps properly; through the day and the first watch, one cultivates wholesome practices without undue haste. When lying down, one should, as explained before, abide in the perception of light, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and the intention to rise. In the last watch, one wakes swiftly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this simply restates sitting posture and the full sleep discipline already given in complete detail earlier in the fascicle — the same cross-legged posture, upright body, and correct resolve for sitting; the same light-perception, mindfulness, clear comprehension, and intention to rise for sleep; the same swift waking in the last watch. What's genuinely new is the added instruction not to rush through daytime and first-watch practice — cultivating wholesome states "without undue haste," since forcing urgency tends to produce agitation rather than genuine progress, even while the overall diligence described throughout the fascicle remains earnest and unbroken.

46. Section: Speech and Silence, First Meaning — Setting Aside Worldly Writing for Genuine Reflection

The treatise returns to speech and silence a second time, now distinguishing two complete frameworks for understanding them. The first centers on what a practitioner should read and think about in solitude: scripture that leads toward liberation, never worldly writing that merely entertains.

Chinese Source

或於語論、或於讀誦，勤修加行。或為修斷，閑居宴默思惟法時，應當遠離順世典籍，綺字、綺句、綺飾文詞，能引無義，不能令證神通等覺、究竟涅槃。復於如來所說正法，最極甚深、相似甚深空性相應，隨順緣性及諸緣起，殷重無間善攝善受，令堅、令住、令無失壞，為成正行，不為利養恭敬稱譽。又於是法言善通利、慧善觀察，於諠雜眾不樂習近、不樂多業、不樂多言，於時時間安住正念。

Ru-Yi Translation

Either in discussion or in recitation, one diligently applies effort. Or, for the sake of cutting off the afflictions, dwelling alone in quiet silence to reflect on the teaching, one should avoid worldly writings, ornate words, ornate phrases, and ornate expressions — these lead only to what is meaningless, and cannot bring about the realization of higher knowledge, awakening, or final nirvana. One should instead, regarding the true Dharma spoken by the Tathāgata — the utterly profound, and the seemingly profound teaching connected with emptiness, in accordance with conditionality and dependent origination — earnestly and without interruption take it up well and hold it well, making it firm, making it abide, never letting it be lost, so as to accomplish correct practice, not for the sake of gain, honor, or praise. One should further become fluent in the words of this teaching, examine it with wisdom, take no pleasure in mixing with noisy crowds, take no pleasure in excessive activity, take no pleasure in excessive talk, and abide in mindfulness from time to time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as guidance for a practitioner's private study time: speech is legitimate for discussion and recitation, but solitary silence should be spent on genuine reflection, never on worldly literature — however elegantly written — since ornate language aimed at pleasure or persuasion cannot itself produce the direct realization, awakening, or final nirvana the practice is actually for.

In its place, the treatise names two depths of the Dharma worth this kind of sustained attention. The "utterly profound" teaching is the direct realization of emptiness itself — dependent origination seen so clearly that no separate self, agent, or experiencer can be found anywhere in the causal chain, a realization language itself cannot fully carry. The "seemingly profound" teaching is that same truth as it can actually be put into words: the twelve links of dependent origination, impermanence, suffering, and non-self, expressed through concepts and doctrine that point toward but don't themselves constitute the direct realization. Cháng Bǎi frames genuine "practicing the teaching in accordance with the teaching" (法隨法行) as sustained, earnest engagement with both — holding the teaching firmly in mind until it becomes stable and unshakeable — motivated purely by the wish to accomplish the path, never by a wish for material support, respect, or reputation. Practically, this also means avoiding noisy crowds who have no real interest in practice, avoiding unnecessary activity, and avoiding excessive talk — since talking drains energy and scatters the mind exactly when silence is needed to look inward — while returning again and again to mindfulness: checking, in each moment, whether self-grasping or attachment has crept back into how one is experiencing an otherwise-illusory world.

47. Section: Nine Things Not to Be Thought About

The treatise closes the first meaning of speech and silence with genuine meditation practice, naming nine categories of speculation a practitioner should deliberately set aside as unanswerable and unproductive.

Chinese Source

於自所證，離增上慢；於少下劣差別證中，不生喜足；於上所證中無退屈；善能遠離不應思處，時時修習止觀瑜伽，樂斷樂修，無間修習、殷重修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding one's own realization, one is free from overestimation; regarding a lesser, inferior degree of realization, one does not become complacent; regarding a higher realization yet to be attained, one does not lose heart. Skillfully avoiding what should not be thought about, one cultivates the yoga of calming and insight from time to time, delighting in cutting off the afflictions and delighting in cultivation, practicing without interruption and with earnest seriousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes the three attitudes genuine meditation requires: not mistaking a lesser attainment for a greater one, not settling complacently for whatever small progress has been made, and not losing heart when a higher realization still lies ahead. He then lists, citing the Xianyang Shengjiao Lun, nine categories of speculation the treatise says should be set aside entirely rather than pursued in thought: the self (since no fixed, independent self can actually be found); sentient beings (a concept that only arises dependent on the idea of a self, and dissolves along with it); the world (basic facts like mountains being high or water being wet, self-evident and not worth debating); karmic retribution (too subtle for an unenlightened mind to trace with any accuracy); the states of accomplished meditators (not meaningfully imaginable by someone who hasn't reached them); the states of Buddhas themselves (beyond words and conceptual thought entirely); the fourteen classical unanswerable questions (whether the world is eternal, non-eternal, both, or neither; finite, infinite, both, or neither; whether a Tathāgata exists after death, doesn't, both, or neither; and whether the soul and the body are identical or different — the Buddha's own silence on these questions, since they rest on false premises no direct answer can resolve); anything that isn't genuine Dharma; and anything that simply feeds the afflictions, such as replaying a grievance against someone.

Dwelling on any of these nine, Cháng Bǎi explains, produces three specific harms: it agitates the mind, it actively erodes merit (since even a single unwholesome thought can undercut what took real effort to build), and it fails to accomplish anything wholesome at all — idle speculation of this kind is simply wasted motion. Set against this list of nine, what should be thought about is comparatively simple: moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom, and good company that supports cultivating them. This entire "silence" side of the first meaning closes on cultivating meditation through what the treatise calls the four yogas — faith (trusting that calming and insight practice genuinely leads where the Buddhas said it would), aspiration (actually wanting to purify the mind through it), diligence (translating that aspiration into sustained effort), and skillful means (the specific techniques of calming and insight themselves) — practiced without interruption and without ever settling for less than full completion.

48. Speech and Silence, Second Meaning: Fellowship, Teaching, and Genuine Retreat

Chinese Source

與諸有智同梵行者，語言談論，共相慶慰，樂興請問，樂求諸善，無違諍心。言詞稱量、言詞合理、言詞正直、言詞寂靜，樂勤為他宣說正法。又應宴默，於惡不善所有尋思不樂尋思，又於非理所有諸法不樂思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conversing with wise fellow practitioners, mutually encouraging one another, delighting in asking questions, delighting in seeking wholesome qualities, free of any contentious mind. One's words are measured, one's words accord with reason, one's words are orderly, one's words are calm — delighting in diligently teaching the true Dharma to others. One should also retreat into silence, taking no pleasure in unwholesome deliberation, and taking no pleasure in reflecting on what is unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this second framework as complementary to the first, organized simply around who is present rather than what's being studied. Speech among wise companions should be genuinely collegial — mutual encouragement, real questions asked without hesitation, a shared pursuit of wholesome qualities, and no trace of argumentative one-upmanship. Teaching others carries its own four-part standard: words measured against what the Buddha actually taught rather than personal opinion, words that hang together with sound reasoning and fitting analogy, words delivered in clear order rather than scattered or jumbled, and words spoken calmly, without any wish to win or outshine the listener — Cháng Bǎi notes that even correct teaching, delivered with a competitive edge, loses this last quality entirely.

Silence, in this second framework, is simply the negative space around all of that: no pleasure taken in dwelling on unwholesome trains of thought, and none taken in mulling over whatever doesn't actually accord with reason. Together, the two meanings of speech and silence given across this section cover both the content of a practitioner's inner and outer life — what to read, reflect on, and avoid thinking about — and its social dimension — how to speak with companions, how to teach, and what silence is actually meant to protect.

49. Resting From Fatigue, Done Discreetly and Briefly

Chinese Source

又於熱分極炎暑時，勇猛策勵，發勤精進，隨作一種所應作事，勞倦因緣，遂於非時發起惛睡。為此義故，暫應寢息，欲令惛睡疾疾除遣，勿經久時，損減善品、障礙善品。於寢息時，或關閉門，或令苾芻在傍看守，或毗奈耶隱密軌則，以衣蔽身，在深隱處須臾寢息，令諸勞睡皆悉除遣。如是名為正知而住先後次第。謂依行時及依住時。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, during the hottest part of the hot season, having courageously exerted oneself in whatever task is called for, one may grow fatigued from the exertion and, though it is not the proper time, become drowsy. For this reason, one should briefly rest, so as to swiftly dispel the drowsiness — but not for long, lest it diminish or obstruct wholesome practice. While resting, one should close the door, or have a fellow monastic keep watch nearby, or, following the Vinaya's discreet provisions, cover the body with the robe and rest briefly in a secluded spot, so that the fatigue is fully dispelled. This is called the sequence of clear awareness in conduct, according to activity and according to dwelling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this final context as the Vinaya's own realistic accommodation: a brief nap during the hottest part of the year, taken discreetly — door closed, or a companion keeping watch, body covered — for only a short while (traditionally around forty-eight minutes) so it dispels fatigue without eating into practice time or becoming an indulgence in its own right. With this, the treatise closes the entire sequence of clear awareness in conduct, organized under two headings: awareness while active (going, returning, looking, gazing, bending, stretching, robe and bowl, eating and drinking) and awareness while settled in one's dwelling (the four postures, wakeful activity, speech, silence, and rest) — the complete architecture underlying all nine contexts named at the section's very start.

50. Section: What "Clear Knowing" and "Abiding" Actually Mean

Having worked through every context in exhaustive detail, the treatise steps back to define the two words at the heart of the entire teaching — what "clear knowing" itself is, and what it means to genuinely "abide" in it.

Chinese Source

又於善品先未趣入，心興加行如理作意俱行妙慧，說名正知。即此正知，行時、住時，一切成辦無所減少，如是名為正知而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, regarding wholesome states not yet entered into, the subtle wisdom that arises together with applied effort and correct attention is called clear knowing. This very clear knowing, whether in activity or in dwelling, accomplishing everything without any diminishment — this is called clear awareness in conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise cutting to the essence after an extraordinarily long list of concrete applications: clear knowing is not merely a checklist of correct behaviors, but a genuine, subtle wisdom — arising together with real effort and correct attention — that a practitioner brings to bear on a wholesome state not yet fully realized. It is, in other words, intelligence in service of the path, not mere rule-following. "Abiding" in this clear knowing simply means sustaining it completely through both halves of a practitioner's life: the active half (going out, encountering the world, handling possessions, eating) and the settled half (postures, practice, speech and silence, rest) — with nothing lost or diminished in either. Cháng Bǎi notes this passage is effectively the treatise's own definition of the eightfold path's factor of "right action" fully unpacked: every bodily, verbal, and mental act, correctly aligned with the Dharma, at every single moment of an ordinary day. Cultivated this thoroughly at the foundational stage of practice, it becomes the direct groundwork for the calming and insight meditation the entire path is ultimately building toward.

51. Section: The Brief Summary — Five Activities, Five Dwellings, Four Governing Factors

The treatise closes clear awareness in conduct with a compact numerical summary, sorting the nine contexts already covered into five "acts" performed while active, five performed while settled in one's dwelling, and four factors that govern both.

Chinese Source

謂於行時有五種業，於其住時有五種業，行時、住時正知而住有四種業，如是名為正知而住所有略義。云何行時有五種業？一者、身業，二者、眼業，三者、一切支節業，四者、衣鉢業，五者、飲食業。云何名為住時五業？一者、身業，二者、語業，三者、意業，四者、晝業，五者、夜業。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are five acts while active, five acts while dwelling, and four factors governing clear awareness in conduct whether active or dwelling — this is the brief summary of clear awareness in conduct. What are the five acts while active? First, the act of the body; second, the act of the eyes; third, the act of all the limbs; fourth, the act involving robe and bowl; fifth, the act of eating and drinking. What are the five acts while dwelling? First, the act of the body; second, the act of speech; third, the act of mind; fourth, the act of the day; fifth, the act of the night.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the five active acts directly onto contexts already covered in full: the body's act is going and returning; the eyes' act is looking and gazing; the limbs' act is bending and stretching; the robe-and-bowl act is carrying and wearing them correctly; and the food act is eating, drinking, chewing, and tasting. The five acts while dwelling map similarly: the body's act is walking, standing, and sitting; speech's act is speaking; the mind's act is lying down, staying silent, and resting from fatigue — since all three, done rightly, are fundamentally mental disciplines (holding the perception of light and mindfulness through sleep, avoiding unwholesome deliberation in silence, resting only as much as genuinely needed); and the day-act and night-act together are simply wakeful practice itself, spanning both body and speech across the full daily cycle. Cháng Bǎi notes this is one traditional way of organizing the material just covered — a second commentator's scheme groups things slightly differently — but the underlying point both agree on is the same: nothing in a practitioner's day falls outside this fivefold-and-fivefold structure of clear awareness.

52. The Four Factors: Mindfulness, Correct Action, Freedom From Fault, Accumulated Provisions

Chinese Source

云何名為行時、住時正知而住所有四業？謂初依彼行業、住業起如是業，即於彼業安守正念，不放逸住。當知此業，正念所攝、不放逸攝。若於是事、是處、是時，如量、如理、如其品類所應作者，即於此事、此處、此時，如量、如理、如其品類正知而作。彼由如是正知作故，於現法中無罪無犯，無有惡作，無變無悔；於當來世亦無有罪，身壞死後不墮惡趣，不生一切那落迦中。為得未得，積習資糧。如是名為正知而住所有略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the four factors governing clear awareness in conduct, whether active or dwelling? First, based on the acts of activity and dwelling just described, one gives rise to those very acts while firmly maintaining mindfulness and dwelling without negligence — know that this factor is comprised of mindfulness and freedom from negligence. Second, regarding a given matter, place, and time, one acts exactly as is proper in measure, in reason, and in kind. Because one acts with such clear knowing, in this present life one is without fault, without transgression, without any wrongdoing, without inner corruption, and without regret; in the life to come, one is likewise without fault, and upon the body's dissolution at death, one does not fall into a miserable rebirth, nor is one born into any hell. And, for the sake of attaining what has not yet been attained, one accumulates the provisions for the path. This is the brief summary of clear awareness in conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes commentators have organized this passage's "four factors" somewhat differently, but converge on the same substance. One influential reading (Master Kuiji's) names four distinct factors: first, maintaining mindfulness and freedom from negligence throughout every act described; second, doing exactly what should be done — in the right matter, place, time, measure, and manner, correctly and without excess or deficiency; third, remaining free of fault in both this present life and lives to come; and fourth, accumulating the very provisions — worldly and world-transcending — not yet attained, building steadily toward the path's fulfillment. Cháng Bǎi walks through the vivid, concrete illustration the treatise itself offers: someone going out to beg for alms correctly knows the right place (one that supports rather than undermines pure conduct), the right time (morning, not too early or late), the right measure (only as much food as genuinely needed), and the right manner (dignified, free of craving or resentment, respectful of both worldly custom and monastic discipline) — and, doing all of this together, the alms round itself becomes an act of practice rather than a mere errand. The reward for this thoroughgoing clear awareness, Cháng Bǎi stresses, is concrete rather than abstract: freedom from the kind of subtle inner corruption that comes from acting carelessly, freedom from the regret that surfaces precisely at the moment of death when it can no longer be undone, and — most significantly — protection from an unfortunate rebirth, since a mind this consistently disciplined simply doesn't generate the causes for one.

53. Section: Clear Awareness in Conduct Closes, and Fascicle 24 Reaches Its True End

With its brief summary complete, the treatise formally closes clear awareness in conduct — the eighth of the fourteen path-provisions — and, with it, fascicle 24 itself reaches the exact end of its source text.

Chinese Source

前廣分別，今此略義，一切總名正知而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

The detailed exposition given before, together with this brief summary given now, are all together called clear awareness in conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes with the treatise's own simple formula: everything covered across this long teaching — the nine contexts individually unpacked, the second deeper pass through going and returning, looking and gazing, bending and stretching, robes and bowl, eating, the four postures, speech and silence, and rest, and finally this compact five-five-four summary — is, taken together, simply what "clear awareness in conduct" means. Provision eight of the fourteen provisions for the path is now complete, joining restraint of the faculties, moderation in eating, and wakeful practice as fully unpacked teachings within this short run of fascicles. And with this closing line, fascicle 24 itself reaches the exact end of its source text — moral discipline, restraint of the faculties, moderation in eating, wakeful practice, and clear awareness in conduct now all stand complete, with only the remaining path-provisions — good companionship, hearing and reflecting on the true teaching, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the ornaments of a genuine renunciant — left for fascicle 25 to unpack.
