Fascicle 44 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十四 · Veneration, Association, and the Immeasurables

Venerating the Three Jewels, Approaching Genuine Friends

Having closed the entire six-perfections and four-means-of-gathering-beings system across fascicles 39 through 43, the treatise opens the sixteenth chapter of the Bodhisattva Stage's foundational section: how a bodhisattva venerates the three jewels, approaches and relies on genuine spiritual friends, and cultivates the four immeasurable states of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 44 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Chapter Sixteen Opens — Veneration, Association, and the Immeasurables

Fascicle 44 opens the sixteenth of the Bodhisattva Stage's eighteen chapters: how a bodhisattva venerates the three jewels, approaches genuine spiritual friends, and cultivates the four immeasurables. A summary verse names the chapter's entire structure in advance — three great sections, venerating the three jewels first, approaching good friends second, and cultivating the immeasurables placed deliberately last.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處供養親近無量品第十六。云何菩薩供養、親近、修習無量？嗢柁南曰：供三寶　親善友　修無量　最為後。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter sixteen: veneration, association, and the immeasurables. How does a bodhisattva who has generated the supreme mind of awakening properly venerate and make offerings to the three jewels, approach and rely on genuine spiritual friends, and cultivate the immeasurable states of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity? A summary verse states: "Venerating the three jewels; approaching good friends; cultivating the immeasurables, placed last of all." The verse first names its topic in outline — three great sections: venerating the three jewels, approaching good friends, and, placed deliberately last, cultivating the four immeasurables.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi marks this chapter's arrival as a genuine turning point in the treatise's overall structure. Fascicles 39 through 43 carried the entire architecture of the six perfections and the four means of gathering beings — generosity, discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, wisdom, and then kind speech, beneficial conduct, and working alongside others — through to their complete karmic fruit in the person of a fully awakened buddha. Chapter sixteen now steps back from that six-and-four structure to address something more foundational still: the concrete devotional and relational practices that actually sustain a bodhisattva's own practice day to day — venerating the three jewels that provide the entire path's object of refuge, relying on genuine teachers and companions who can actually guide that practice correctly, and cultivating the four immeasurable states that keep a bodhisattva's own heart properly oriented toward every being without exception.

He notes the deliberate placement of the four immeasurables at the very end of the verse, despite appearing to be, in some sense, the emotional core of everything a bodhisattva does: the treatise's own compositional logic moves from the most external object of devotion — the three jewels, encountered largely through the senses in worship and offering — inward through relationship — spiritual friends, encountered through actual living association — and finally to the most interior cultivation of all — the four immeasurables, a purely internal transformation of the bodhisattva's own heart. This closes the chapter's opening statement of scope; the treatise turns next to venerating the three jewels, opening with the buddha as the first and most senior of the three.

2. Milestone: The Ten Kinds of Veneration Named

The treatise's first great section, venerating the three jewels, opens by asking how a bodhisattva venerates the Buddha specifically, then names ten kinds of veneration in outline: relic veneration, stupa veneration, present veneration, absent veneration, self-performed veneration, teaching-others veneration, wealth-and-respect veneration, vast veneration, undefiled veneration, and proper-practice veneration.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩於如來所供養如來？當知供養略有十種。一、設利羅供養，二、制多供養，三、現前供養，四、不現前供養，五、自作供養，六、教他供養，七、財敬供養，八、廣大供養，九、無染供養，十、正行供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does a bodhisattva venerate the Tathāgata in the Tathāgata's own presence? One should understand that veneration is, in brief, of ten kinds: first, relic veneration; second, stupa veneration; third, present veneration; fourth, absent veneration; fifth, self-performed veneration; sixth, teaching-others veneration; seventh, wealth-and-respect veneration; eighth, vast veneration; ninth, undefiled veneration; tenth, proper-practice veneration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "Tathāgata" through its three classical bodies before turning to veneration itself: the dharma body, coming from nowhere and going nowhere, as the Diamond Sūtra states; the reward body, "thus" naming ultimate truth itself and "come" naming genuine awakening to it; and the response body, arriving by riding the very path of genuine reality to accomplish complete awakening. Veneration itself, he notes, carries a double sense — toward what is senior it means drawing near, attending upon, and honoring; toward what is junior it means sympathy, compassion, and care — and its methods are many, which is why the treatise organizes them here into ten specific kinds before explaining each one in turn.

He flags the list's own internal logic in advance, worth holding in mind across the ten explanations to come: the first nine are all, in one way or another, forms of material or ritual offering, while the tenth, proper-practice veneration, stands apart as dharma-offering — actually practicing the teaching itself as the highest form of veneration a bodhisattva can render. This closes the naming of the ten kinds; the treatise turns next to explaining each one, opening with relic veneration and stupa veneration.

3. Milestone: Relic Veneration and Stupa Veneration — Honoring the Buddha's Body, and Every Structure Built to Hold It

The first two kinds of veneration: relic veneration, meaning direct veneration of the Buddha's own physical body, whether living or, after his passing, his bodily relics; and stupa veneration, meaning veneration offered at any structure built to house those relics — reliquary mound, niche, platform, or shrine, whether newly built or long-standing.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，親現供養如來色身，是名設利羅供養。若諸菩薩，於為如來所造一切若窣堵波、若龕、若臺、若故制多、新制多所設諸供養，是名制多供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who directly and personally venerate the Tathāgata's own physical body — this is called relic veneration. Whatever veneration bodhisattvas offer at any reliquary mound, niche, platform, old shrine, or new shrine built for the Tathāgata's sake — this is called stupa veneration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that "relic," śarīra in the original Sanskrit, literally means "body" — so this first kind of veneration names direct devotion to the Buddha's own physical form, whether the living body during his lifetime or the bodily relics remaining after his passing. Stupa veneration then covers every kind of structure built afterward specifically to house those relics: the reliquary mound proper, a niche built as a small separate chamber resembling a room, and a platform on which an image is enthroned. He notes a genuine terminological looseness in the tradition here — strictly, a structure containing actual relics is called a stupa while one without relics is called a shrine, but later usage largely blurred this distinction, some texts calling any such structure a shrine regardless, others extending "shrine" specifically to the four sites of the Buddha's birth, awakening, first teaching, and final passing.

He treats these first two kinds together as naming the most basic, most universally recognized forms devotion to the Buddha can take — bowing before an actual relic, or before a structure raised specifically to hold one — the entry point from which the remaining eight, progressively more refined kinds of veneration will unfold. This closes relic and stupa veneration; the treatise turns next to present and absent veneration, distinguishing them by whether the object of devotion is actually before one's eyes.

4. Milestone: Present Veneration — One Buddha's Dharma-Nature Is Every Buddha's Own

The third kind, present veneration, simply names devotion offered directly before the Buddha's body or a shrine actually seen and encountered. But the treatise then adds a striking contemplation available specifically within present veneration: since one buddha's own true nature is identical to every buddha's, past, present, and future, venerating the one Buddha genuinely before one's eyes is understood to reach every buddha and every shrine across the ten directions at once.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於如來身、或制多所親面對前，現矚現見而設供養，是名現前供養。若諸菩薩，於如來所、若制多所，現前施設供養具時，發起增上意樂俱心、淨信俱心，作是思惟：若一如來法性，即是去來今世一切如來法性；若一如來制多法性，即是十方無邊無際一切世界所有如來制多法性。是故我今供現如來，即是供養其餘三世一切如來；供現制多，即是供養其餘十方無邊無際一切世界若窣堵波、若龕、若臺、若故制多、若新制多。當知是名菩薩俱供現不現前一切如來及以制多。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who set out veneration directly facing the Tathāgata's own body or a shrine, genuinely seen and beheld before their own eyes — this is called present veneration. When bodhisattvas set out veneration's own implements directly before the Tathāgata or a shrine, they raise a mind joined with heightened aspiration and a mind joined with pure faith, contemplating in this way: the true nature of this one Tathāgata is precisely the true nature of every Tathāgata across past, future, and present; the true nature of this one shrine is precisely the true nature of every shrine belonging to every Tathāgata across every world throughout the ten directions, boundless and without limit. Therefore, in venerating the Tathāgata now present before me, I venerate every other Tathāgata across the three times as well; in venerating this shrine now present, I venerate every other reliquary mound, niche, platform, old shrine, and new shrine across every boundless world in the ten directions as well. One should understand this to be called a bodhisattva jointly venerating every Tathāgata and every shrine, present and absent alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the contemplation embedded here as the passage's real point rather than a decorative aside. A bodhisattva setting out incense, flowers, lamps, unguents, fruit, tea, food, jewels, pearls, or robes before one Buddha or one shrine deliberately raises two minds together: a heightened, joyful aspiration wholly devoted to the act, and a pure faith grounded in genuine reflection — reflection specifically on the fact that any one buddha's true suchness-nature carries no separate, private identity distinguishing it from any other buddha's own suchness-nature. Buddhas differ in the particular causes and conditions that shaped their own path to awakening, but at the level of dharma-body and its defining qualities, they are utterly without distinction — "buddha and buddha, the same path," as the classical phrase has it.

From this single insight the entire present-absent distinction dissolves in terms of actual efficacy: because the nature venerated is never private to the one buddha physically present, an act of present veneration automatically extends itself, without any additional effort, to the entirety of the three times and the ten directions. He treats "increased aspiration joined with mind" as simply naming the felt joy of venerating, present or absent, without discrimination, and "pure faith joined with mind" as the specific confidence, itself irreversible once genuinely established, that every buddha's qualities — the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the eighteen unique qualities — are held equally and without variance by every buddha alike. This closes present veneration; the treatise turns next to absent veneration, the practice available once the Buddha has already passed from the world.

5. Milestone: Absent Veneration — Visualization After the Buddha's Passing, and Boundless Merit From Raising Even a Single Image

The fourth kind, absent veneration, covers devotion offered when no actual relic or shrine stands before one's eyes: forming, in the mind alone, the thought "this is the Tathāgata" toward every buddha and shrine not physically present, and thereby venerating universally. The treatise then adds a second form — after the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, actually building images, stupas, niches, or platforms, however many one's own capacity allows, from one to countless millions — promising immeasurable merit and freedom from lower rebirth as its fruit.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於不現前一切如來及以制多作如來想，普為三世一切如來、一切十方如來制多施設供養，當知是名菩薩唯供不現前佛及以制多。若諸菩薩，佛涅槃後，為如來故造立形像，若窣堵波、若龕、若臺，隨力隨能或一或二，或復眾多，乃至百千俱胝等數；如是菩薩於如來所，設不現前弘廣供養，當獲無量大福德果，攝受無量廣大梵福。菩薩由此，能於無量劫大劫中，不墮惡趣。由是因緣，非不圓滿無上正等菩提資糧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who form the thought "this is the Tathāgata" toward every Tathāgata and shrine not presently before them, thereby setting out veneration universally toward every Tathāgata across the three times and every shrine throughout the ten directions — one should understand this to be called a bodhisattva venerating only the absent buddhas and shrines. After the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, bodhisattvas who, for the Tathāgata's sake, build images, or reliquary mounds, or niches, or platforms, whether one or two according to their own strength and ability, or a great many, even reaching hundreds of thousands or millions in number — such bodhisattvas, by setting out this vast, absent veneration in the Tathāgata's presence, will obtain the fruit of immeasurable, great merit and virtue, gathering in immeasurable, vast Brahmā-realm blessing. Through this cause, bodhisattvas are able, across immeasurable eons of great eons, to avoid falling into the lower realms. Through this very cause and condition, they are, without exception, one step further toward the complete fulfillment of the requisite provisions for supreme, complete awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses a point easy to miss in this passage: absent veneration does not require an actual physical object at all. Once the Buddha has passed beyond the world, a bodhisattva can set out genuine offerings — incense, flowers, lamps — while simply holding the visualized thought "the Buddha is here," and the treatise explicitly does not treat this as a lesser, merely symbolic gesture; the offering, joined to genuine visualization, is complete veneration in its own right, extended universally across the three times and ten directions precisely because it was never tied to one particular physical location to begin with.

He treats the second form, image-building after the parinirvāṇa, as this section's own practical instruction for an age with no living Buddha to venerate directly: whatever a person's actual means allow, from a single humble image to hundreds of thousands, the merit produced is described as immeasurable specifically because it secures freedom from the three lower realms across countless future eons and steadily completes the provisions needed for full awakening — a deliberately encouraging promise meant to invite even the most materially modest act of image-making, rather than reserving genuine merit only for the wealthy or powerful. This closes absent veneration; the treatise turns next to comparing the karmic fruit of present versus absent veneration directly.

6. Milestone: Present, Absent, or Both — Ranking the Karmic Fruit; Then Self-Performed and Teaching-Others Veneration

The treatise ranks the karmic fruit of the first four kinds directly: veneration limited to what is present yields a vast fruit; veneration limited to what is absent yields a still greater fruit; but veneration joining both together yields the single greatest fruit of all, unsurpassed. The fifth and sixth kinds then turn from the object venerated to the manner of venerating: self-performed veneration, done entirely with one's own hands without delegating to servants or dependents; and teaching-others veneration, in which a bodhisattva without means gives what little they have to the genuinely poor so that they too may make an offering.

Chinese Source

此中菩薩，唯供現前佛及制多，應知獲得廣大福果。若唯供養不現前佛及以制多，應知獲得大大福果。若俱供養現不現前佛及制多，應知獲得最大福果，為無有上。若諸菩薩，於如來所、若制多所欲設供養，唯自手作，不使奴婢、作使、朋友、僚庶、親屬，不依懈惰諸放逸處而設供養，是名菩薩自作供養。若諸菩薩，現有少分可供財物，興悲愍心，故思施與貧苦少福無力有情，令於如來、若制多所持用供養，願彼當來多受安樂。彼得此物，供養如來及以制多，菩薩於斯自無所供，當知是名菩薩唯教他設供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, bodhisattvas who venerate only present buddhas and shrines should be understood to obtain a vast fruit of merit. Those who venerate only absent buddhas and shrines should be understood to obtain a greater, still vaster fruit. Those who venerate both present and absent buddhas and shrines together should be understood to obtain the single greatest fruit, unsurpassed by any other. Bodhisattvas who, wishing to set out veneration in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine, do so entirely with their own hands, without employing servants, hired hands, friends, retainers, or relatives, and without any trace of laziness or heedlessness in setting it out — this is called a bodhisattva's self-performed veneration. Bodhisattvas who presently have only a small amount of offerable wealth, and who, moved by a compassionate mind, deliberately give it to suffering beings of little merit and little means so that they may use it to venerate the Tathāgata or a shrine, wishing them much future happiness as a result — when such beings receive this gift and venerate the Tathāgata and the shrine with it, while the bodhisattva themselves offers nothing directly — one should understand this to be called a bodhisattva's veneration solely through teaching others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this ranking of present versus absent veneration as directly continuous with the previous milestone's own insight: because absent veneration already rests on the identical universal contemplation available within present veneration, while also reaching every buddha not physically encountered, its fruit naturally exceeds present veneration alone; joining the two together loses nothing available to either while adding the concrete, sensory devotion unique to present veneration, hence the combined fruit as the greatest of the three.

Turning to self-performed veneration, he stresses this is not a rule against ever allowing others to help — the treatise's own next passage in fact praises encouraging one's whole household and community to venerate together — but a rule specifically against veneration delegated out of laziness, where a bodhisattva capable of making the offering personally instead assigns the task to a servant or dependent purely to avoid the effort themselves. Teaching-others veneration, by contrast, describes a bodhisattva without sufficient means of their own choosing compassion over pride: rather than withholding the little they have or feeling ashamed at their own poverty, they pass that small amount to someone with even less, letting that person become the one who actually makes the offering — an act of generosity that is, in its own right, complete veneration, even though the bodhisattva's own hands never touch the shrine. This closes the comparison of present and absent veneration and opens self-performed and teaching-others veneration; the treatise turns next to combining self-performed veneration with encouraging an entire community to venerate together.

7. Milestone: Rallying an Entire Community to Venerate Together — From King and Brahmin Down to the Lowest Outcaste

The treatise expands self-performed veneration into its fullest form: a bodhisattva who not only venerates personally but actively urges parents, spouse, servants, friends, relatives, kings, ministers, wealthy householders, merchants, and literally every person down to the poorest outcaste, together with teachers, fellow disciples, monastics, and even non-Buddhist practitioners, to venerate the Tathāgata according to each one's own capacity — naming this the highest form of veneration, jointly performed by self and others together.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於如來所、若制多所欲設供養，非唯自作，亦勸父母、妻子、奴婢、作使、朋友、僚庶、親屬，及他國王、王子、大臣、長者、居士、若婆羅門、國邑聚落饒財商主，下至一切男女大小、貧匱苦厄旃荼羅等，及以親教、軌範諸師，共住、近住一切弟子，同梵行者、諸出家者、外道等眾，令於如來、若制多所，隨力隨能作諸供養，當知是名菩薩自他咸共供養。此中菩薩，若唯自供佛及制多，應知獲得廣大福果。若唯教他，應知獲得大大福果。若能自他俱共供養，應知獲得最大福果，為無有上。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, wishing to set out veneration in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine, do so not only themselves but also urge their parents, spouse, servants, hired hands, friends, retainers, and relatives, as well as other kings, princes, ministers, elders, householders, brahmins, and wealthy merchants of the country's cities and villages, down to every man and woman, old and young, and even the destitute, suffering, and outcaste, together with their own preceptors and instructing teachers, fellow disciples living together or nearby, companions in the pure conduct, monastics, and even followers of other paths, to make whatever veneration each one's own strength and ability allows in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine — one should understand this to be called a bodhisattva's veneration jointly performed by self and others together. Among these, bodhisattvas who venerate the Buddha and the shrine only by themselves should be understood to obtain a vast fruit of merit. Those who only teach others to venerate should be understood to obtain a greater fruit. Those able to venerate jointly, both by themselves and through others, should be understood to obtain the single greatest fruit, unsurpassed by any other.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the deliberate comprehensiveness of the list of people a bodhisattva is urged to include — moving in one unbroken sweep from the most intimate relations (parents, spouse) through household dependents, social peers, and political authority, all the way down to those with the lowest social standing in the ancient Indian caste system, and finally outward again to religious companions and even practitioners of rival traditions. The point, he stresses, is that veneration recognizes no social boundary whatsoever: a bodhisattva actively invites literally anyone within reach, regardless of station, faith, or means, since the merit of venerating the three jewels is available to any being capable of the act, and a bodhisattva's own compassion should never quietly exclude anyone from that opportunity out of prejudice or convenience.

He treats the final ranking here as a precise mirror of the previous milestone's ranking of self-performed against teaching-others veneration, now scaled up from an individual act of teaching one poor person to a bodhisattva's capacity to mobilize an entire community: doing it oneself alone is good, teaching others to do it is better, but a bodhisattva who does both — personally venerating while also actively rallying everyone within reach to do the same — produces the single greatest fruit, since neither one's own direct devotion nor the multiplied devotion of everyone one has brought along is lost in the combination. This closes self-performed, teaching-others, and jointly-performed veneration; the treatise turns next to wealth-and-respect veneration, the concrete gestures and material offerings a bodhisattva actually makes.

8. Milestone: Wealth-and-Respect Veneration — Bowing, Circling, and Every Material Offering Named in Full

The seventh kind, wealth-and-respect veneration, names two components in vivid detail: respectful veneration through bodily gesture — inquiry, prostration, welcome, joined palms, incense of every kind, garlands, music, banners, canopies, lamps, chanted praise, five-limbed prostration, and circumambulation to the right — and wealth veneration through material offering — inexhaustible wealth, every precious substance from pearl to coral to amber, ornamental jewelry, bells, and scattered treasures.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於如來所、若制多所，以諸衣服、飲食、臥具、病緣醫藥供身什物，敬問、禮拜、奉迎、合掌，種種熏香、末香、塗香、華鬘、妓樂、幢蓋、旛燈，歌頌稱讚，五輪歸命，趨繞右旋，而為供養。或復奉施無盡財供，或復奉施末尼、真珠、琉璃、螺貝、璧玉、珊瑚、硨磲、瑪瑙、琥珀、金銀、赤珠、右旋如是等寶，或復奉施末尼、環釧、寶璩、印等諸莊嚴具，乃至奉施種種寶鈴，或散珍奇、或纏寶縷而為供養。是名菩薩於如來所、若制多所財敬供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine, offer robes, food and drink, bedding, and medicine for illness, along with every article supporting the body; who respectfully inquire, prostrate, welcome, and join their palms; who offer every kind of fragrant incense, powdered incense, and fragrant unguent, garlands of flowers, music and dance, banners, canopies, and lamps; who chant praises, prostrate with all five limbs in total self-surrender, and circle in reverence to the right — all of this constitutes veneration. Or again, bodhisattvas offer inexhaustible wealth as an offering; or offer maṇi jewels, pearls, lapis lazuli, conch shells, alabaster, coral, giant clam shell, agate, amber, gold and silver, and right-spiraling red pearls, and every jewel of this kind; or offer maṇi jewels, bracelets, jade ornaments, and seals as ornamental articles, down to offering every kind of jeweled bell, or scattering rare and marvelous objects, or offering robes woven through with jeweled thread. This is called a bodhisattva's wealth-and-respect veneration in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi divides this seventh kind cleanly into two halves the treatise itself distinguishes: respectful veneration, meaning the entire physical vocabulary of devotion — inquiry after the Buddha's own well-being, full prostration, ceremonial welcome, joined palms, every category of incense, garlands, music, banners and canopies, lamplight, chanted verses of praise, prostration touching all five limbs to the ground in complete self-surrender, and circumambulating a shrine keeping it to one's right hand as a mark of reverence — and wealth veneration proper, meaning actual material wealth offered outright: gemstones named individually and at length, precious metal, ornamental jewelry, and woven cloth threaded through with jewels.

He treats the sheer specificity of this catalogue — naming particular gemstones, particular gestures, particular ritual objects one by one rather than simply saying "offer valuable things respectfully" — as itself instructive: veneration is not a vague inner attitude alone but a concrete, embodied practice with its own vocabulary of gesture and substance, worth learning in some detail precisely because genuine reverence naturally seeks expression through the body and through whatever material means are actually available, rather than remaining only an abstract inward feeling. This closes wealth-and-respect veneration; the treatise turns next to vast veneration, the eighth kind, unfolding this same material offering across seven further dimensions of scale.

9. Milestone: Vast Veneration's Sevenfold Structure — a Privilege Reserved for Bodhisattvas of the First Ground

The eighth kind, vast veneration, takes the wealth-and-respect offerings just described and multiplies them across seven distinctions: many offerings or exquisite offerings; present or absent; self-performed or taught to others; and, as the seventh and most demanding condition, offered with a genuinely pure mind and vivid, forceful conviction — a condition the commentary ties specifically to bodhisattvas who have already reached the extremely-joyous first ground and can literally behold buddhas appearing before them.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於如來所、若制多所，長時施設即上所陳財敬供養，若多供具、若妙供具、若現在前、不現在前、若自造作、教他造作、若淳淨心猛利勝解現前供養；即以如是所種善根，迴向無上正等菩提，如是七種說名菩薩廣大供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine, set out the wealth-and-respect veneration already described above over a long period of time — whether through many offerings or exquisite offerings, whether present or absent, whether self-performed or taught to others, or whether offered with a genuinely pure mind and vivid, forceful conviction directly before the object of veneration — and then dedicate whatever wholesome root has thereby been planted toward supreme, complete awakening: these seven together are called a bodhisattva's vast veneration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the seven distinctions in pairs before isolating the seventh as the passage's genuine center of interest: many offerings names sheer inexhaustible quantity, exquisite offerings names the finest, most precious substances, present versus absent and self versus taught-to-others simply repeat the categories already established earlier in the chapter — but the seventh condition, offering with a pure mind and vivid, forceful conviction, he ties directly to the treatise's own account, elsewhere in the Bodhisattva Stage's chapter on the ten grounds, of the extremely-joyous first ground: a bodhisattva who has entered that ground can, through two specific causes, literally behold buddhas appearing directly before them and, upon seeing them, spontaneously offer every kind of exalted, joyful material gift.

He identifies those two causes as, first, a relatively coarse purity of faith — the confidence, arising from hearing the bodhisattva canon's own account of the Buddha's inconceivable qualities, that countlessly many buddhas exist under countlessly many names throughout the ten directions, joined with a genuine wish to behold them directly — and, second, a bodhisattva's own correct and vast aspiration to liberate beings and spread the true teaching; it is specifically this coarse purity of faith, sought with vivid, forceful conviction, that the treatise names here as vast veneration's seventh and most demanding condition. He closes by noting the treatise's own two-part rationale for why exactly these seven distinctions, taken together, earn the name "vast" — either because sheer quantity and exquisite quality, sustained over time with pure conviction and dedicated to awakening, are vast in themselves, or because all seven of the first seven kinds of veneration already covered, if practiced this way, together deserve to be called vast — with the underlying requirement, either way, being genuine duration, genuine purity of conviction, and genuine dedication of the resulting merit toward awakening. This closes vast veneration; the treatise turns next to undefiled veneration, the ninth kind.

10. Milestone: Undefiled Veneration's Six Marks — No Contempt, No Pretense, and No Impure Substance

The ninth kind, undefiled veneration, names six marks that keep an offering genuinely free of taint: performing it oneself without contempt; letting others perform it too; never with heedlessness, laziness, or disrespect; never carelessly discarded or offered with a distracted or defiled mind; never staged falsely before kings or nobles merely to win their favor; and never made with foul or unsuitable substances such as bitter orpiment, ghee-washing, malodorous incense, or thorned flowers.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於如來所、若制多所，自手供養不懷輕慢；令他供養；不住放逸、懈惰、不敬而為供養；不輕棄擲、不散漫心、無雜染心而為供養；不於信佛國王、大臣、諸貴勝前，為財敬故，詐設種種虛事供養；不雌黃塗，不酥灌洗，不以種種局崛羅香、遏迦華等餘不淨物而為供養。如是六種說名菩薩無染供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, in the Tathāgata's presence or at a shrine, venerate with their own hands without any trace of contempt; who also let others venerate; who never venerate while heedless, lazy, or disrespectful; who never venerate by carelessly discarding or scattering the offering, and never with a distracted or defiled mind; who never stage false, elaborate displays of veneration before kings, ministers, or other noble patrons who have faith in the Buddha, purely to win wealth or esteem for themselves; and who never venerate using orpiment paste, ghee-washing, or foul substances such as costus incense or thorned flowers as an offering — these six together are called a bodhisattva's undefiled veneration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi separates the first five marks, all concerning the state of the venerating mind, from the sixth, concerning the physical purity of the offering itself. The first mark rules out a bodhisattva secretly regarding their own personal veneration as somehow superior to how others venerate, or looking down on offerings less elaborate than their own; the second actively affirms letting others take part rather than monopolizing the act; the third and fourth together rule out every variety of careless, halfhearted, or emotionally compromised offering, whether from simple laziness or from an unsettled, defiled state of mind; and the fifth names a particularly pointed form of hypocrisy — staging an elaborate show of devotion specifically in front of a powerful patron in order to win that patron's own admiration, wealth, or favor, using apparent piety as a tool for worldly gain.

The sixth mark, he explains item by item, concerns substances genuinely unfit for an offering regardless of the venerator's own intention: orpiment, a bitter, pungent mineral pigment, is toxic and thus impure however it is applied; ghee used to bathe an image, rather than pure water, likewise counts as impure; costus incense, also called benzoin, is described in the Yogācārabhūmi's own commentarial tradition as producing an acrid smell resembling burning glue when lit; and a certain thorned white flower, though fragrant, carries barbs unsuitable for a Buddha image. The Buddha, being the most venerable object of devotion imaginable, deserves only genuinely clean and fitting substances — offering anything impure, unsuitable, or toxic, however well-intentioned, still constitutes a defiled offering by the treatise's own strict standard. This closes undefiled veneration's six marks; the treatise turns next to the sources of wealth that make all this veneration possible, and to a striking account of veneration performed by emanation bodies at the eighth ground.

11. Milestone: Emanation-Body Veneration — the Eighth-Ground Bodhisattva Who Venerates With a Hundred Thousand Multiplied Hands

The treatise names three sources of the wealth used in veneration — earned by one's own labor, obtained by asking others, or, for bodhisattvas who have attained mastery over requisites, simply willed into being — before turning to a vivid account of this last, most advanced possibility: an eighth-ground bodhisattva emanating up to hundreds of thousands of manifestation-bodies, each sprouting hundreds or thousands of arms, each arm bearing offerings finer than anything found even among the gods, to bow, scatter incense, and sing praises before the Buddha all at once.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，如是財敬、廣大、無染供養如來及制多時，或自臂力所集財寶，或從他求所獲財寶，或得眾具自在財寶，能為如是種種供養。已得眾具自在菩薩，化作化身，或一或二，或復眾多，乃至百千俱胝等數。此一切身皆於如來及制多所恭敬禮拜。復從如是一一化身，化出多手，或百或千，或過是數。此一切手皆持無量出過諸天上妙華香、殊勝可愛種種珍寶，奉散如來及制多所。復從如是一切化身，化出無量上妙音聲，歌讚如來廣大甚深真實功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, when bodhisattvas make these offerings of wealth-and-respect veneration, vast veneration, and undefiled veneration to the Tathāgata and to shrines, the wealth used may come from wealth gathered through their own arms' labor, or wealth obtained by asking it of others, or the wealth of one who has attained mastery over requisites, all equally capable of accomplishing every kind of veneration described. A bodhisattva who has already attained mastery over requisites transforms and emanates manifestation-bodies, one, two, or many, even reaching hundreds of thousands or millions in number. Every one of these bodies bows and prostrates in reverence before the Tathāgata and every shrine. From each and every one of these manifestation-bodies in turn, many arms are further emanated, a hundred, a thousand, or more. Every one of these arms holds immeasurable, exquisite flowers and incense surpassing anything found even among the gods, and rare, beloved jewels of every kind, and scatters them in offering before the Tathāgata and every shrine. From all of these manifestation-bodies together, immeasurable, exquisite sounds are further emanated, singing the Tathāgata's vast, profound, and genuinely real qualities in praise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "mastery over requisites" as a specific attainment of the eighth bodhisattva ground, drawn from the treatise's own later chapter on the grounds: a bodhisattva abiding in effortless, unconditioned equanimity at this stage need only briefly turn their attention toward any needed article of provision — food, incense, flowers — and it is instantly accomplished exactly as wished, one facet among the ten kinds of mastery such a bodhisattva possesses, alongside mastery over worldly crafts and mastery over whatever object of devoted conviction they turn their mind toward.

He treats the vivid imagery here — a single bodhisattva emanating up to millions of bodies, each body sprouting up to thousands of arms, each arm bearing offerings finer than the gods' own, filling the air with song — not as poetic exaggeration but as the treatise's own literal description of a genuinely available capacity at this stage of the path, meant to convey just how far veneration can expand once a bodhisattva's own mastery over the physical world has matured completely. He closes this milestone noting the treatise's own explanation for why such a bodhisattva no longer needs to wait for a living Buddha to appear in order to venerate one directly: having already entered the stage of non-regression, such a bodhisattva can travel to any buddha-land at will and venerate any buddha there without the slightest obstruction, which is why the passage states plainly that a bodhisattva at this stage no longer longs for the Tathāgata's own appearance in this particular world specifically. This closes the account of wealth's three sources and eighth-ground emanation veneration; the treatise turns next to veneration for the bodhisattva who has no wealth of any of these three kinds at all.

12. Milestone: Veneration Without Wealth — Rejoicing as the Great Equalizer, Available to Every Bodhisattva Regardless of Means

The treatise closes its account of material veneration with the case of a bodhisattva possessing none of the three kinds of wealth just described — no wealth earned by their own labor, none obtained from others, and none of the eighth ground's effortless mastery. Such a bodhisattva can still venerate every buddha across every world in the ten directions by simply, and wholeheartedly, rejoicing in every offering anyone anywhere is making right now — a practice the treatise urges every bodhisattva to sustain constantly, regardless of how much or little wealth they actually possess.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若無自力所集財寶，亦無從他求得財寶，及無菩薩所獲眾具自在財寶，可設供養；然於所有或贍部洲、或四大洲、或千世界、二千世界、或復三千大千世界，乃至十方無邊無際諸世界中，下中上品供養如來一切供具，菩薩於彼以淨信俱勝解俱心，周遍思惟，一切隨喜。如是菩薩少用功力，而興無邊廣大供養，攝受菩提廣大資糧。菩薩於此恆常無間起真善心、起歡喜心，當勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas who possess neither wealth gathered by their own labor, nor wealth obtained by asking others, nor the effortless wealth of a bodhisattva who has attained mastery over requisites, and thus have nothing available to offer — such bodhisattvas may nonetheless, with respect to every offering of every quality, lesser, middling, and superior, being made to the Tathāgata throughout Jambudvīpa, or the four great continents, or a thousand worlds, or two thousand worlds, or a great trichiliocosm of three thousand worlds, or indeed throughout every boundless, limitless world in the ten directions, join a mind of pure faith with a mind of genuine conviction, contemplate this universally, and rejoice in all of it without exception. Such bodhisattvas, expending very little effort, thereby raise up boundless, vast veneration, and gather in the vast provisions for awakening. Bodhisattvas should, without any interruption whatsoever, constantly raise this genuinely wholesome mind, this mind of joy, and should diligently train in doing so.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as one of the chapter's most quietly consequential teachings: it removes wealth entirely as a precondition for the merit of veneration. A bodhisattva with genuinely nothing to offer forms the thought that offerings are being made, right now, to every buddha throughout every conceivable scale of world — Jambudvīpa alone, then the four continents, then a thousand worlds, then a great trichiliocosm, then the entirety of the boundless ten directions — and simply rejoices, deeply and genuinely, in every one of those offerings as though it were their own. He glosses "universal contemplation" as the deliberate mental act of picturing offerings being made to every buddha everywhere, and "rejoicing in all of it" as the resulting deep gladness and joy this contemplation produces — not a passive, abstract wish but an actively cultivated delight.

He treats the closing instruction — to raise this mind constantly and without interruption, and to train in it diligently — as placing this practice on entirely equal footing with the elaborate material veneration described in every prior milestone: precisely because it costs nothing and requires no particular capacity, it has no excuse for being neglected, and a bodhisattva sincerely committed to the path should be sustaining this rejoicing mind continuously, not merely on occasions when material offering happens to be unavailable. This closes the entire account of material veneration's many varieties — wealthy and wealthless, present and absent, self-performed and communal; the treatise turns next to the tenth and final kind, proper-practice veneration, which it will go on to call the highest offering of all.

13. Milestone: Proper-Practice Veneration Opens — Five Practices Compressed Into the Time It Takes to Milk a Cow

The tenth and final kind of veneration, proper-practice veneration, is dharma-offering itself: actually practicing the teaching, even for the briefest possible stretch of time — as short as it takes to draw a single pull of milk from a cow — through five compressed practices: cultivating the four immeasurables toward every restless being; contemplating impermanence, suffering, and non-self across all conditioned phenomena; contemplating nirvāṇa's own genuine benefit; recollecting the three jewels and the perfections; and, deepest of all, beginning to settle the mind in a state without conceptual discrimination or fixed characteristic.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，少時少時、須臾須臾，乃至下如𣪬牛乳頃，普於一切蠢動有情修習慈悲喜捨俱心，於一切行修無常想、無常苦想、苦無我想，於其涅槃修勝利想，於佛法僧、波羅蜜多修習隨念；少時少時、須臾須臾，於一切法發生少分下劣忍智，信解離言法性真如，起無分別無相心住；何況於此若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, for however brief a stretch of time, moment by moment, even for as short a span as it takes to draw a single pull of milk from a cow, cultivate throughout that span a mind joined with loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity toward every restlessly stirring being; who cultivate, toward all conditioned phenomena, the perception of impermanence, the perception of impermanence's own suffering, and the perception of suffering's own selflessness; who cultivate, toward nirvāṇa, the perception of its genuine benefit; who cultivate mindful recollection of the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, and the perfections; and who, for however brief a stretch of time, moment by moment, generate toward all phenomena even a small measure of provisional patient acceptance and understanding, coming to have faith and comprehension in the true suchness of reality's own nature beyond all verbal expression, and thereby begin to settle the mind in a state free of conceptual discrimination and free of fixed characteristic — how much more so, then, should one expect of practicing this even further and even more intensively.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks all five compressed practices named here in turn. The first, the four immeasurables, is explained through the same threefold structure already introduced earlier in the treatise's account of meditative concentration: loving-kindness and the rest may take sentient beings themselves as their object, universally wishing well-being to every being desiring happiness throughout the ten directions; or take mere phenomena as their object, resting in the correct view that beings are simply provisional labels applied to phenomena, without a fixed self; or take no object at all, the most advanced mode, resting free of all conceptual discrimination whatsoever. The second, contemplating impermanence, suffering, and non-self across the five aggregates, can proceed either by the śrāvaka path's own step-by-step reasoning — observing moment-to-moment arising and ceasing as impermanence, observing the suffering inherent in painful sensation, the disintegration inherent in pleasant sensation, and the unsettled quality inherent even in neutral sensation, and finally observing that this very inability to command one's own experience at will proves there is no controlling self behind it — or by the great vehicle's own doctrine of the three natures.

The third, contemplating nirvāṇa's genuine benefit, draws on the classical fourfold account of nirvāṇa in the great vehicle: intrinsically pure nirvāṇa, present in all beings' own true nature regardless of affliction; nirvāṇa with residue, where affliction's causes are cut off but the present body still remains; nirvāṇa without residue, where even that remaining body has ceased; and non-abiding nirvāṇa, dwelling in neither saṃsāra nor mere extinction, benefiting beings endlessly into the future — this last kind, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, is said to possess the three bodies of dharma, wisdom, and liberation, or the four virtues of permanence, bliss, self, and purity, or the eight flavors including permanence, ease, and freedom from decay, since its own essence is never mere blank cessation but the very substance of ultimate reality itself. The fourth, recollecting the three jewels and the perfections, is explained as a direct antidote to two families of meditative fault — dullness and drowsiness on the calming side, restlessness, craving, and aversion on the insight side — while the fifth, generating a small measure of provisional patient acceptance toward all phenomena, is tied to the treatise's own account of the path of preparation: the very first taste of confidence, still humble in scale, in the truth that all phenomena are ultimately without fixed verbal essence, which matures step by step into the actual path of seeing. This closes the five compressed practices; the treatise turns next to naming the remaining forms of practice, disciplined conduct, and the perfections themselves as equally valid proper-practice veneration, and to ranking proper-practice veneration as the very highest offering of all.

14. Milestone: Proper-Practice Veneration Closes — Guarding the Precepts and Training in Every Perfection, Named as the Highest Offering of All

Proper-practice veneration's scope widens beyond the five compressed contemplations to include simply guarding the bodhisattva precepts one has received, and diligently training in calming, insight, the factors of awakening, every perfection, and every means of gathering beings. The treatise then delivers its verdict: this dharma-offering ranks first, highest, most excellent, most sublime, and unsurpassed — exceeding even every kind of wealth-and-respect veneration already described, by a hundred times, a thousand times, and beyond calculation.

Chinese Source

如是守護菩薩所受尸羅律儀，於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那菩提分法精勤修學，亦於一切波羅蜜多及諸攝事正勤修學。是名菩薩於如來所正行供養。如是供養為最第一、最上、最勝、最妙、無上。如是供養，過前所說具一切種財敬供養，百倍、千倍，乃至鄔波尼殺曇倍。由此十相，應知是名具一切種供養如來。如供養佛，如是供養若法、若僧，隨其所應，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Likewise, guarding the bodhisattva precepts and vows one has received, diligently training in calming and insight practice and in the factors of awakening, and also correctly and diligently training in every perfection and every means of gathering beings — this too is called a bodhisattva's proper-practice veneration in the Tathāgata's presence. Veneration of this kind is first, highest, most excellent, most sublime, and unsurpassed. Veneration of this kind exceeds every kind of wealth-and-respect veneration already fully described, by a hundred times, a thousand times, even by an incalculable multiple beyond counting. Through these ten marks, one should understand this to be called complete veneration of the Tathāgata in every one of its kinds. Just as one venerates the Buddha, so too one venerates the Dharma and the Saṅgha, each according to what is fitting — one should understand this in just the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this final widening of proper-practice veneration as removing any suspicion that the five compressed contemplations named earlier are somehow the entire content of dharma-offering: guarding one's precepts faithfully and training diligently in calming, insight, the thirty-seven factors of awakening, and the full six or ten perfections together with the four means of gathering beings are, each in their own right, already complete instances of venerating the Buddha through genuine practice — the actual living of the teaching is itself the offering, regardless of which particular practice is being sustained at a given moment.

He treats the ranking that follows — first, highest, most excellent, most sublime, unsurpassed, exceeding material veneration by a factor beyond calculation — as the treatise's own deliberate closing statement on the entire ten-fold structure: material offering matters and produces genuine merit, as milestone after milestone in this chapter has shown, but nothing compares to actually practicing the teaching the Buddha taught, since that alone repays the Buddha's own compassion in the coin the Buddha himself valued most — beings genuinely walking the path toward liberation. The closing line then extends the entire ten-fold structure, without needing to repeat it, to the Dharma and the Saṅgha as well: whatever form of veneration applies to the Buddha applies, suitably adjusted, to the teaching and the community of practitioners equally, since all three jewels are, in the end, a single object of refuge. This closes the treatise's complete account of venerating the Buddha, extended to the Dharma and Saṅgha; the treatise turns next to the six kinds of heightened aspiration a bodhisattva should raise whenever venerating any of the three jewels.

15. Milestone: Six Kinds of Heightened Aspiration Open — the Buddha as an Unsurpassed Field of Merit, and a Debtor's Own Great Benefactor

The treatise names six kinds of heightened aspiration a bodhisattva should raise toward the Buddha whenever venerating any of the three jewels, opening with two: first, reflecting on the Buddha as an unsurpassed field of great merit, where even a small act of genuine faith and reverence yields boundless merit, and where offering without any attachment to self, Buddha, or gift at all yields the undefiled fruit of great awakening itself; second, reflecting on the Buddha as one who holds an unsurpassed, great debt of kindness toward every being, having undertaken immeasurable difficult practices and austerities purely in order to teach the true dharma that lets beings attain every genuine benefit.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩於三寶所，由十種相興供養時，應緣如來發起六種增上意樂。一者、無上大功德田，增上意樂；二者、無上有大恩德，增上意樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

When bodhisattvas of this kind, in the presence of the three jewels, raise veneration through the ten marks already described, they should, taking the Tathāgata as their object, give rise to six kinds of heightened aspiration. First, the heightened aspiration of reflecting on the Tathāgata as an unsurpassed field of great merit; second, the heightened aspiration of reflecting on the Tathāgata as one holding an unsurpassed, great debt of kindness toward beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "field of merit" through the classical agricultural metaphor the tradition uses for every object of giving: sowing a gift into salted, barren ground yields nothing and even ruins the seed, exactly as offering to a teacher of wrong view produces no merit and actively harms genuine faith; sowing into an ordinary virtuous layperson's field yields a thin harvest; sowing into one's own parents yields a rich harvest reaching the Brahmā heavens, though parents, being ordinary beings rather than noble ones, cannot themselves lead the giver out of saṃsāra; sowing into an arhat or solitary realizer yields more still, though the field itself remains narrow, its harvest correspondingly limited; sowing into a bodhisattva yields immeasurable merit, yet even this falls short of the Buddha, an utterly boundless field of merit spanning both worldly and transcendent good — sincere faith and reverence toward this field alone already produces boundless merit, and once that offering is made free of any clinging to a self who gives, a Buddha who receives, or a gift being given at all, what ripens is nothing less than the undefiled fruit of great awakening itself.

He treats the second aspiration, the debt of kindness, as calling to mind the concrete cost of the Buddha's own path: countless lifetimes of difficult, painful practice undertaken for no other purpose than to be able to teach the true dharma afterward, so that beings who could never have discovered it unaided might attain every genuine benefit — worldly, spiritual, and ultimate — through that teaching alone. Holding this debt in mind while venerating, he stresses, turns an outwardly simple ritual gesture into something closer to genuine gratitude actually felt, rather than mere formal politeness. This closes the first two heightened aspirations; the treatise turns next to the third, reflecting on the Buddha as supreme among literally every kind of being that exists.

16. Milestone: Supreme Among Every Footed and Footless Being — the Buddha as Cream Skimmed From Cream Itself

The third heightened aspiration: reflecting that the Buddha is supreme among every possible kind of being — footless, two-footed, four-footed, or many-footed; possessing form or formless; possessing perception, lacking it, or dwelling in neither — a supremacy the treatise illustrates with a classical image from the Ekottarika Āgama: just as ghee refined from butter is the finest product in the entire chain from milk to cream to butter to ghee, so the Tathāgata stands supreme among literally every being across every possible category of existence.

Chinese Source

三者、一切無足、二足及多足等有情中尊，增上意樂。攝異門分說：無足有情者，如蛇等。二足有情者，謂人等。四足有情者，如牛等。多足有情者，如百足等。如來於此一切有情三種第一，謂由蠢動故、由依止故、由心故。是名一切有情中尊。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the heightened aspiration of reflecting that the Tathāgata is supreme among beings without feet, with two feet, and with many feet, and every being of this kind. The Compendium of Synonyms explains: footless beings are such as snakes; two-footed beings are such as humans; four-footed beings are such as cattle; many-footed beings are such as centipedes. The Tathāgata is, among all these beings, first in three respects: by way of restless stirring, by way of what one's body depends upon, and by way of mind. This is called being supreme among every kind of being.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the taxonomy named here in full: footless beings such as snakes and earthworms; two-footed beings such as humans, celestial beings, and birds; four-footed beings such as cattle, dogs, pigs, and sheep; and many-footed beings such as centipedes, cockroaches, and spiders — with "and the rest" further folding in beings with form and beings without form across the form and formless realms, and beings with perception, without perception, or in the subtlest state of neither perception nor its absence, spanning every conceivable mode of existence across all three realms.

He treats the sūtra passage's own ghee image as doing real interpretive work rather than merely decorating the point: milk yields cream, cream yields butter, butter yields ghee, and at each stage the product refines further — the Buddha's supremacy among beings works the identical way, not a supremacy imposed from outside but the natural, inevitable culmination of a process, each stage of being subtly refined into the next until arriving at what is simply, straightforwardly finest. Because the Buddha manifests in every realm of being in the very form appropriate to that realm — a heavenly form among the gods, a human form among humans — while remaining supreme within each and every one of those forms, beings across the entire spectrum of existence are drawn to follow the Buddha's own guidance specifically because the Buddha appears to them as one of their own kind, yet unmistakably foremost among it. This closes the third heightened aspiration; the treatise turns next to the fourth, fifth, and sixth together — the Buddha's own rarity, singular presence in this world-system, and complete perfection of both worldly and transcendent good.

17. Milestone: Rare as the Udumbara Flower, Alone in This World-System, the Complete Basis of Every Benefit — the Sixth Aspiration Closes the Set

The fourth, fifth, and sixth heightened aspirations close the set: reflecting that meeting the Buddha is as vanishingly rare as the legendary udumbara flower said to bloom only once in three thousand years; that out of the Buddha's own perfectly equal dharma-body pervading countless worlds, the Buddha chose to appear alone in this particular world-system out of special compassion for its especially sharp-minded yet especially suffering-laden beings; and that the Buddha's own worldly and transcendent virtues have reached total completion, becoming the complete basis on which every genuine benefit for beings ultimately rests.

Chinese Source

四者、猶如鄔曇妙華極難值遇，增上意樂；五者、獨一出現三千大千世界，增上意樂；六者、一切世出世間功德圓滿一切義依，增上意樂。由是六種增上意樂，於如來所，若於如來法所、僧所，少分思惟而興供養，尚獲無量大功德果，何況其多。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the heightened aspiration of reflecting that the Tathāgata is as extraordinarily difficult to encounter as the exquisite udumbara flower; fifth, the heightened aspiration of reflecting that the Tathāgata appears alone within this great trichiliocosm of three thousand worlds; sixth, the heightened aspiration of reflecting that the Tathāgata's every worldly and transcendent virtue has reached complete perfection, becoming the very basis of every genuine benefit. Through these six kinds of heightened aspiration, even a small measure of reflection while raising veneration in the presence of the Tathāgata, or of the Tathāgata's Dharma, or of the Saṅgha, already obtains an immeasurable fruit of great merit and virtue — how much more so, then, if that reflection is sustained abundantly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth aspiration's udumbara image as pointing to a genuine, deliberate pattern rather than mere cosmic accident: the Buddha, present without exception wherever conditions allow, nonetheless withdraws from view when beings lack the karmic connection to actually benefit from a direct encounter, so that when the causes finally do ripen and the Buddha does appear, that rarity itself becomes part of what makes the encounter precious — the "difficulty" is pedagogical, cultivating in beings a genuine longing rather than casual familiarity, exactly as the fabled udumbara flower, said to bloom once only when the rare wheel-turning king arises in the world, becomes treasured precisely for how seldom it is actually seen.

He treats the fifth aspiration as making a related but distinct point about the Buddha's own compassionate choice: since the Buddha's dharma-body is genuinely equal and pervades countless world-systems without preference, the Buddha's singular appearance specifically in this present, suffering-laden world reflects a deliberate compassionate response to beings here being simultaneously sharp-minded and unusually beset by suffering — precisely the combination of capacity and need that calls for direct intervention. The sixth aspiration, he treats as this set's natural summation: the same worldly virtue named earlier under the ten good deeds of ordinary human and celestial merit, and the transcendent virtue named under the three vehicles' own liberating teaching, both brought by the Buddha to total completion, becoming literally the ground on which the Lotus Sūtra's own claim rests — that everything the Buddha teaches ultimately arrives at the single ground of complete wisdom. He closes by noting the treatise's own final, almost understated remark: even a brief moment's reflection on these six aspirations while venerating already produces boundless merit, which he treats as a deliberate invitation rather than a minimum requirement — an encouragement to actually hold these six thoughts in mind, however briefly, every single time veneration is offered, rather than letting the ten material and practice-based kinds of veneration already described become empty ritual gesture divorced from genuine reflection. This closes the entire first great section of the chapter, venerating the three jewels; the treatise turns next to its second great section, approaching genuine spiritual friends.

18. Milestone: Approaching Good Friends Opens — Seven Questions Frame an Entire Section

The chapter's second great section opens with seven questions that frame everything to follow: by what marks does a bodhisattva qualify to be a genuine spiritual friend to others; by what marks is that friendship never empty or false; by what marks does a spiritual friend become a genuinely trustworthy support; what should a spiritual friend actually do for those they guide; how should a disciple properly approach such a friend; with what attitude should a disciple listen to the teaching received from them; and on what points should a disciple never second-guess the teacher delivering it.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩成就幾相，能為善友？由幾種相，善友不虛？成就幾相，令善友性作信依處？復有幾種善友菩薩，於所化生為善友事？菩薩幾種親近善友？由幾種想，於善友所聽聞正法？由幾種處，於善友所聽聞法時，於說法師不作異意？

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore: by how many marks does a bodhisattva succeed in being a genuine spiritual friend? By how many marks is such friendship never empty or false? By how many marks does the very nature of being a spiritual friend become a genuinely trustworthy support? By how many further ways does a bodhisattva who is a spiritual friend actually act on behalf of the beings they are guiding? By how many ways does a bodhisattva properly approach a spiritual friend? By how many kinds of reflection does one listen to the true teaching in a spiritual friend's presence? And on how many points, when listening to the teaching in a spiritual friend's presence, should one never form a contrary opinion of the teacher delivering it?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā's own observation that this relationship runs in both directions at once: the one teaching is genuinely a spiritual friend to the one listening, but the one listening — attentive, sincere, genuinely putting the teaching into practice — is, in a real sense, equally a spiritual friend to the one teaching, since a devoted student sustains and encourages a teacher's own continued effort exactly as a good teacher sustains a student's. Maitreya's treatise, he notes, organizes this entire mutual relationship into seven distinct topics, moving from what qualifies someone to teach, through what makes that teaching trustworthy and effective, to how a student should actually receive it — a structure that treats "approaching good friends" as a genuinely two-sided discipline rather than a matter of the student's conduct alone.

He flags this section's own significance within the larger arc of the treatise: having spent chapter fifteen and the whole preceding stretch of fascicles on what a bodhisattva does — the six perfections, the four means of gathering beings, veneration of the three jewels — the treatise now turns to a foundational question usually assumed rather than examined: where does a bodhisattva's own guidance in all of this actually come from, and how does a bodhisattva recognize, approach, and rightly learn from the teachers capable of providing it? This closes the section's opening sevenfold question; the treatise turns next to the first of the seven, the eight marks that qualify someone to be a genuine spiritual friend.

19. Milestone: The Eight Marks of a Complete Spiritual Friend Open — Unbroken Discipline, Genuine Learning, and Real Realization

The first of the seven topics names eight marks completing a genuine spiritual friend, opening with three: dwelling excellently and correctly within the bodhisattva precepts, without lapse or breach; possessing genuine learning, an innate discerning intelligence matured through study into real understanding of the teaching's own letter and meaning; and possessing genuine realization, having actually attained calming or insight practice through the cultivated wisdom born of that learning, not merely theoretical familiarity with the teaching.

Chinese Source

當知菩薩成就八支，能為善友眾相圓滿。一者、住戒。於諸菩薩律儀戒中妙善安住，無闕無穿。二者、多聞。覺慧成就。三者、具證。得修所成隨一勝善，逮奢摩他、毗鉢舍那。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should understand that bodhisattvas who accomplish eight elements are able to be spiritual friends whose every mark is completely fulfilled. First, dwelling in discipline: excellently and correctly abiding within the bodhisattva precepts and vows, without lapse and without breach. Second, genuine learning: the accomplishment of a discerning intelligence. Third, genuine realization: having attained, through cultivated wisdom, any one of the excellent, wholesome states, reaching calming practice and insight practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "dwelling in discipline" through the treatise's own broader account of complete precept-keeping — guarding the individual liberation vows appropriate to one's own status among the seven classes of Buddhist practitioner, from the two hundred fifty rules of a monk to the five precepts of a lay follower, maintaining complete ritual and behavioral propriety, feeling genuine dread even at minor faults, and undertaking every training precept — with "no lapse" meaning no violation of a major precept and "no breach" meaning no violation of a minor one, or no habitual violation, and, whenever a violation does occur, prompt and genuine confession restoring the precept's purity.

He treats "genuine learning" as naming two capacities working together: an innate, discerning intelligence a person is simply born with, called awakening, and the further clarity that intelligence gains through diligent study and reflection, called wisdom proper — together allowing someone to receive the Buddha's own twelve categories of teaching with full attention, retain it accurately, recite it fluently, reflect on its meaning skillfully, and see through to its actual intent, thereby developing a genuine, structured understanding of the entire path rather than scattered fragments of doctrine. "Genuine realization" he treats as the crucial further step beyond mere learning: a spiritual friend worth relying on has not merely studied calming and insight practice in the abstract but has actually attained some excellent state through them — the same three miraculous teaching methods named earlier in this fascicle's account of meditative concentration make their appearance again here as one mark of what such realization can produce, since only someone who has genuinely walked the path themselves can reliably guide someone else along it. This closes the first three marks; the treatise turns next to the fourth through sixth — compassion willing to forgo one's own comfort, fearlessness in teaching, and patience under provocation.

20. Milestone: Compassion, Fearlessness, and Patience — the Sixteen Classical Reflections That Let a Teacher Absorb Any Insult

The fourth through sixth marks: genuine compassion, willing to set aside one's own settled meditative ease and diligently work for others without weariness; fearlessness, teaching the true dharma without ever losing mindfulness or eloquence to fear; and patience, able to endure mockery, ridicule, coarse language, and outright hostility without resentment — a capacity the tradition supports with sixteen distinct classical reflections, from contemplating impermanence and emptiness to recalling that even buddhas and great arhats have suffered abuse without complaint.

Chinese Source

四者、哀愍。內具慈悲，能捨自己現法樂住，精勤無怠饒益於他。五者、無畏。為他宣說正法教時，非由恐怖忘失念辯。六者、堪忍。於他輕笑、調弄、鄙言、違拒等事，非愛言語、種種惡行皆悉能忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, compassion: possessing genuine loving-kindness and compassion within, able to set aside one's own present ease dwelling in meditative absorption, diligently and tirelessly benefiting others. Fifth, fearlessness: when proclaiming the true teaching for others' sake, never losing mindfulness or eloquence through fear. Sixth, patience: able to bear entirely with others' mockery, ridicule, coarse and contemptible speech, and open defiance, and with every kind of unwelcome word or ill conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats compassion here as directly echoing a quality already named twice earlier in this fascicle — willingness to leave meditative ease and return to the desire realm's turbulence — now applied specifically to the role of teacher: a spiritual friend's compassion is proven not by feeling sympathy in the abstract but by concretely and continually choosing to spend effort on students rather than resting in personal attainment. Fearlessness he ties to the treatise's own account, in the Śrāvaka Stage, of freedom from the very fear that could otherwise make a teacher's mind seize up mid-explanation — a teacher genuinely free of this fear finds dharma teachings in literally any object, a single flower or blade of grass, because the mind is never gripped by anxiety about performing correctly.

He treats patience as this mark's richest, giving the full classical sixteen reflections the Chengshi lun recommends for absorbing verbal abuse without resentment: contemplating that both the one who insults and the one insulted arise and perish moment by moment, leaving no fixed target for anger; contemplating emptiness, so that no real "insulter" or "insulted" can be found at all; recognizing that a true accusation deserves acceptance rather than anger, while a false one will bring its own karmic consequence on the speaker regardless; recalling one's own past karma as the actual cause of receiving abuse now, so the debt is simply being repaid; recognizing the body itself as an instrument fit to receive suffering; recognizing one's own part, through the very act of hearing and interpreting the insult, in generating one's own distress; noticing that the anger belongs to the deluded mind of the other person rather than to that person as such, exactly as a healer treats the disease afflicting a patient rather than raging at the patient themselves; recalling that even the Buddha and great disciples such as Śāriputra endured slander and abuse without complaint; observing that mere words, unlike physical harm or the loss of life, are a genuinely light thing to bear; recognizing abuse as valuable confirmation of one's own progress in cultivating renunciation; recalling that failing to bear it now only stores up future suffering, so patience now is the lighter path; and feeling deep shame at the thought of behaving in body or speech unworthy of a genuine disciple of the World-Honored One. He treats the full sixteen, taken together, as this section's own proof that patience under provocation is not simply willpower gritted through but a skill built from specific, learnable habits of reflection — precisely the kind of resource a spiritual friend needs on hand, since teaching inevitably invites exactly this kind of provocation from at least some listeners. This closes compassion, fearlessness, and patience; the treatise turns next to the seventh and eighth marks, tirelessness in teaching and skillful speech's own eight distinct qualities.

21. Milestone: Tirelessness Before Four Assemblies, and Skillful Speech's Eight Qualities — Even Reaching "Ninety Days on a Single Word"

The seventh and eighth marks complete the set: tirelessness, teaching fluently and without fatigue before all four Buddhist assemblies no matter how long the session runs; and skillful speech, whose eight component qualities the commentary unpacks at length — from language that leads listeners toward the highest goal, through sound as lovely as the legendary kalaviṅka bird's song, to an inexhaustible eloquence capable, as the tradition remembers of Master Zhiyi, of spending ninety full days expounding the single word "wondrous."

Chinese Source

七者、無倦。其力克彊，能多思擇，處在四眾說正法時，言無謇澀，心不疲厭。八者、善辭。語具圓滿，不壞法性，言辭辯了。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, tirelessness: possessing genuinely strong physical and mental capacity, able to reflect and discern extensively over a long time, so that when teaching the true dharma before the four assemblies, one's speech never stumbles and one's mind never grows fatigued or weary. Eighth, skillful speech: language that is fully complete in every one of its qualities, never damaging the true nature of reality, and eloquent, clear, and fully understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats tirelessness as simply the physical and mental stamina genuine teaching requires — strong enough in both body and mind to sustain hours of careful doctrinal reflection before monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen alike without the speech faltering or the mind flagging, a mark easy to overlook next to the more obviously "spiritual" qualities on this list but no less necessary for a teacher who may need to speak at length whenever the occasion calls for it.

He unpacks skillful speech's eight components in full, following the Śrāvaka Stage's own list: language that leads a listener toward the highest goal — nirvāṇa for the lesser vehicle, supreme awakening for the great vehicle; language as exquisitely beautiful in sound as the kalaviṅka bird's own song, clear and delightful to hear; language thoroughly clear, using words and phrases skillfully chosen to convey the teaching vividly and accurately; language easy to understand, explaining profound truths through accessible means; language listeners actually want to hear, drawing out meaning that genuinely delights and sustains real enthusiasm for continued listening; language that never overstays or falls short, matched correctly in duration and pacing to its audience; language entirely free of any hidden agenda — no craving for reputation or gain, no wish merely to win the listener's personal allegiance, only the wish that the listener actually benefit; and language of truly inexhaustible range, able to draw on limitless skillful means and wisdom, exemplified, he notes, by Master Zhiyi's own legendary ninety days spent expounding the single word "wondrous" in the title of the Lotus Sūtra. He then explains "never damaging the true nature of reality" as avoiding both of two classical extremes — treating conventionally-labeled things as though they possessed some fixed, independent essence, and treating even the provisional basis for those labels, or ultimate emptiness itself, as utterly nonexistent — steering a genuinely middle course between over-affirming and over-denying, and closes by unpacking "eloquent, clear, and fully understood" into its own seven supporting qualities: proper sequence and correspondence to the four kinds of reasoning, cohesion between successive points, faithful accordance with the intended meaning, transparency that lets a listener see straight through to the teaching's own root, a phrasing that skillfully draws the listener's mind toward genuine accord with liberation, address genuinely fitted to each of five kinds of audience, and timing and manner in keeping with the occasion — together forming what he calls the resource base for the thirty-seven factors of awakening themselves, since skillfully delivered teaching is what actually moves a listener to cultivate them. This closes all eight marks of a genuinely complete spiritual friend; the treatise turns next to the second of the seven topics — the five marks by which such a friend's guidance is never empty or in vain.

22. Milestone: Guidance That Is Never Empty — the Five Marks Behind Genuinely Effective Teaching

The second topic names five marks that keep a spiritual friend's guidance genuinely effective rather than empty gesture: wanting to benefit and bring ease to others before they even ask; correctly and without distortion knowing what actually constitutes real benefit and real ease as opposed to its counterfeit; teaching with the right skillful means and manner suited to the audience's own capacity; never growing weary however difficult a student proves; and holding a genuinely equal great compassion, showing no favoritism between the most gifted and the least capable among those being taught.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩具五種相，眾德相應，能為善友所作不虛。一者、於他先欲求作利益安樂；二者、於彼利益安樂如實了知，無顛倒覺；三者、於彼善權方便順儀說法，隨眾堪受調伏事中，有能有力；四者、饒益心無厭倦；五者、具足平等大悲，於諸有情劣中勝品心無偏黨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who possess five marks, joined together with many virtues, are able to be spiritual friends whose guidance is never empty or in vain. First, wishing beforehand to bring benefit and ease to others. Second, correctly and without distorted understanding knowing what genuinely constitutes that benefit and ease. Third, possessing both the capacity and the strength to teach the dharma through skillful and fitting means, suited to whatever degree of discipline the audience can actually receive. Fourth, a mind that benefits others without ever growing weary. Fifth, possessing a genuinely equal great compassion, with no partiality of mind between the lesser and the more excellent among the various kinds of beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first mark as naming a specifically anticipatory compassion — wishing to help before the student has even voiced the need, benefit understood here as change genuinely aligned with the three trainings of discipline, concentration, and wisdom, and ease understood as the resulting settledness of body and mind, or alternatively benefit as future good and ease as present good. The second mark he ties directly to the treatise's own earlier chapter on benefiting self and others: a genuine spiritual friend never confuses a student's momentary comfort with their real, lasting benefit, and correctly, without any distortion, discerns which pleasures actually help and which merely feel good while doing harm — gently redirecting a student away from the latter according to the student's own capacity to hear it.

The third mark he glosses through the treatise's own chapter on the power of one's spiritual lineage, which specifies concrete rules of decorum for teaching — never speaking from a high seat to someone seated below without genuine cause, never standing while the listener sits, never speaking to someone walking ahead while the teacher trails behind, never addressing someone with their head covered — since genuinely skillful teaching includes not only correct content but correct manner and posture as well. The fourth and fifth marks he treats as a matched pair: tirelessness in the face of difficult students, and an entirely even compassion refusing to play favorites between the most promising and the least promising among them — together ensuring that a spiritual friend's guidance actually reaches every student capable of receiving it, rather than concentrating effort only on the easiest or most talented. This closes the five marks of guidance that is never empty; the treatise turns next to the third topic, the five marks that make a spiritual friend a genuinely trustworthy basis of faith.

23. Milestone: A Trustworthy Basis of Faith Opens — Dignified Deportment So Complete Even Its Absence Is Never Seen

The third topic names five marks that let a spiritual friend's very nature become a genuine basis of faith — so that beings hearing of such a teacher from far away already feel pure faith arising, let alone those who actually meet them. The first two: dignified deportment, every posture of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down fully composed and utterly free of restless movement in any limb; and grave sincerity, body, speech, and mind alike free of any trace of agitation or disturbance in their outward activity.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩成就五相，令善友性作信依處，令他遠聞極生淨信，何況親覩。一者、勝妙。威儀圓滿、威儀寂靜、威儀具足，一切支分皆無躁動。二者、敦肅。三業現行無掉無擾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who accomplish five marks make the very nature of being a spiritual friend into a genuine basis of faith, such that others, merely hearing of them from far away, already give rise to the purest faith — how much more so, then, upon actually beholding them in person. First, dignified deportment: complete, tranquil, and fully accomplished deportment, with not a single limb or part of the body showing any restless movement. Second, grave sincerity: body, speech, and mind alike showing, in their actual activity, neither agitation nor disturbance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "dignified deportment" through the Śrāvaka Stage's own threefold breakdown: complete deportment means every posture of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down follows correct convention, in harmony with ordinary social custom without violating it and in harmony with monastic discipline without violating that either; tranquil deportment means the body of one free from craving remains genuinely settled, the sense faculties never restlessly darting toward their objects, able to hold a single posture for a long stretch of time without anxious fidgeting or the need to keep shifting position — he notes the traditional account of Master Xuánzàng receiving high officials for an entire morning without once changing his seated posture, appearing entirely at ease throughout, as the classical example of exactly this quality; and fully accomplished deportment means every action of the body while walking or sitting is undertaken with clear, attentive awareness, so that nothing is left careless or diminished — down to concrete details the tradition specifies explicitly: never swaying the body while walking, never shaking the arms or head, never leaping about, never holding hands with a companion, never resting a hand on the hip or hunching the shoulders when entering a donor's home, never sitting carelessly without first checking the seat, and sitting upright with the legs neither crossed, nor pressed too closely together, nor spread too far apart. He notes Master Hóngyī's well-known habit of tapping a chair before sitting and shaking out his shoes before putting them on, precisely to avoid crushing any insect that might be resting there — offered as a living illustration of exactly this attentive, undisturbed deportment a genuinely dignified teacher embodies.

He treats "grave sincerity" as simply naming the same freedom from agitation extended explicitly across all three doors of action — body settled and unmoving as already described, speech soft, quiet, disinclined toward noise or crowds, and mind that, upon seeing a form, hearing a sound, smelling a scent, tasting a flavor, or feeling a touch, registers the bare sensation itself without any craving arising on top of it, remaining fearless, profoundly wise, expansively at ease, inwardly composed, free of greed, free of anger, genuinely patient, and undisturbed by any of the unwholesome trains of thought — desire, hostility, harm — that could otherwise agitate an ordinary mind. This closes dignified deportment and grave sincerity; the treatise turns next to the third and fourth marks — freedom from pretense, and freedom from envy.

24. Milestone: No Pretense, No Envy, No Excess — Closing the Marks of a Trustworthy Basis of Faith

The remaining three marks: freedom from pretense, so that dignified bearing flows naturally from genuine inner virtue rather than being staged to win others' trust; freedom from envy, never resenting another teacher's own gains in offerings and respect but actively inviting them to teach and rejoicing in their success even more than in one's own; and frugality, keeping few possessions and readily giving away whatever comes, so that generosity toward others is matched by genuine austerity toward oneself.

Chinese Source

三者、無矯。不為誑他，故思詐現嚴整威儀。四者、無嫉。終不於他說法所得利養恭敬生不堪忍，而常自勵請他說法；復恆勸餘於彼廣施利養恭敬，無諂偽心。又常於他其心純淨，見彼說法及得財敬，深生隨喜。如自所獲利養恭敬，心生歡喜，見他所得利養恭敬，其心歡喜復過於是。五者、儉約。尠儲器物，隨得隨捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, freedom from pretense: never deliberately staging a falsely dignified, orderly bearing simply in order to deceive others into trusting oneself. Fourth, freedom from envy: never once unable to bear another's gain in offerings and respect from teaching the dharma, but instead constantly urging oneself on to invite them to teach, and constantly encouraging others to give that teacher generous offerings and respect, entirely free of any flattering or false intent. Moreover, one's mind remains genuinely pure toward others at all times, so that upon seeing another teach and receive offerings and respect, one feels deep, genuine rejoicing — exactly as one feels joy upon receiving offerings and respect oneself, and, upon seeing another receive them, feeling a joy that surpasses even that. Fifth, frugality: keeping very few stored possessions, giving away at once whatever one happens to receive.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats freedom from pretense as the necessary safeguard behind the first two marks: dignified deportment and grave sincerity could, in principle, both be counterfeited by someone merely performing composure to win a following, so the treatise names this third mark specifically to insist that the composure must arise genuinely from within, from actual inner virtue, never staged for the sake of others' trust or admiration. Freedom from envy he treats as this section's most demanding mark, since three of its own five poisons — greed, hatred, and delusion — are precisely what envy toward a rival teacher's success would otherwise engage: a spiritual friend who has genuinely overcome these no longer experiences another teacher's popularity as any kind of threat, and instead actively works to increase that other teacher's following, taking joy in their success that the passage explicitly says exceeds even the joy felt at one's own success — a striking reversal of the ordinary competitive instinct.

He treats frugality as this list's most practically visible mark: a genuine spiritual friend accumulates little beyond what circulates immediately back out to others, austere toward the self in exact proportion to how generous they are toward everyone else. Taken together, he notes, these five marks — dignified, sincere, unpretentious, unenvious, and frugal — explain why the treatise can claim that even hearing of such a person from a great distance already produces genuine faith: each mark closes off one specific way a teacher's own outward reputation could otherwise be hollow, leaving nothing left to doubt once all five are genuinely present together. This closes the entire third topic, becoming a trustworthy basis of faith; the treatise turns next to the fourth topic, the five concrete duties a spiritual friend actually performs for the beings they guide.

25. Milestone: Five Duties of a Spiritual Friend — Correcting, Reminding, Instructing, Admonishing, and Teaching

The fourth topic names the five concrete things a spiritual friend actually does for the beings under their guidance: correcting faults through respectful admonishment; reminding students of both forgotten faults and forgotten teaching; instructing them in the yogic attention proper to bodily and mental withdrawal and the removal of meditative obstruction; admonishing them, evenhandedly applying the Buddha's own teaching and discipline whenever a genuine violation occurs; and teaching the dharma itself, pitched to each student's own actual capacity.

Chinese Source

善友菩薩由五種相，於所化生為善友事。一、能諫舉，二、能令憶，三、能教授，四、能教誡，五、能說法。如是諸句廣辯應知如聲聞地。教授教誡，廣說如前力種性品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who are spiritual friends, through five marks, perform the actual work of a spiritual friend for the beings they are guiding. First, correcting through admonishment; second, restoring memory; third, instructing; fourth, admonishing according to discipline; fifth, teaching the dharma. One should understand every one of these in full detail as already explained in the Śrāvaka Stage. Instruction and admonishment according to discipline are explained in full detail, as before, in the chapter on the power of spiritual lineage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the five in turn: correcting through admonishment means pointing out and urging correction of a student's actual fault, done respectfully rather than harshly; restoring memory covers two related tasks, reminding a student of a fault they have committed and reminding them of dharma teaching they have simply forgotten; instructing means guiding a student, again and again, toward the correct sequence of the noble path — withdrawing the body from unfavorable conditions, withdrawing the mind from distraction, and removing the five meditative hindrances — through undistorted teaching genuinely matched to calming and insight practice; admonishing according to discipline means applying the Buddha's own teaching and monastic discipline evenhandedly, and, whenever a student is found to have genuinely transgressed in some respect, correctly and appropriately reproving, penalizing, or, in serious cases, formally expelling them in order to bring about their own correction; and teaching the dharma means simply delivering instruction pitched, whether more introductory or more advanced, to the actual capacity of whichever being is being taught.

He unpacks the cross-reference to the chapter on spiritual lineage in some detail: instruction there names eight distinct objects of practice matched to a student's own particular affliction — contemplating the body's impurity for the strongly desirous, cultivating loving-kindness for the strongly hostile, contemplating dependent origination for the deeply confused, mindfulness of breathing for the easily distracted, and analysis of the elements for the arrogant, together with guidance away from the two extremes of eternalism and nihilism and away from excessive spiritual pride; while admonishment according to discipline names five distinct responses — restraining a student from committing a fault, permitting and encouraging faultless conduct such as the perfections themselves, gently correcting a first, minor lapse, formally reproving or expelling a student who repeatedly and disrespectfully violates the teaching (done, crucially, without any trace of resentment and with an affection that never wavers before or after the correction), and warmly praising and encouraging a student who has genuinely followed the guidance given. This closes the five duties of a spiritual friend; the treatise turns next to the fifth topic, the four marks of correctly approaching such a friend.

26. Milestone: Approaching a Spiritual Friend Correctly — Serving Through Illness and Health, and Genuine Obedience Without Wavering

The fifth topic names four marks of a disciple's own complete approach to a spiritual friend: attending on them at all times whether sick or well, with constant love, respect, and pure faith; greeting, prostrating, welcoming, and serving them with genuine warmth; supplying them with properly obtained robes, food, bedding, and medicine; and, most demandingly, genuinely following their guidance without wavering — accepting what is sanctioned, releasing what is proscribed, confessing any lapse honestly, and approaching them again and again in a spirit of pure obedience rather than mere formal courtesy.

Chinese Source

當知菩薩由四種相，方得圓滿親近善友。一、於善友有病無病隨時供侍，恆常發起愛敬淨信。二、於善友隨時敬問、禮拜、奉迎、合掌、殷勤，修和敬業而為供養。三、於善友如法衣服、飲食、臥具、病緣醫藥資身什物隨時供養。四、於善友若正依止，於如法義若合若離，隨自在轉，無有傾動，如實顯發，作奉教心，隨時往詣恭敬、承事、請問、聽受。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should understand that bodhisattvas, through four marks, come to approach a spiritual friend in a genuinely complete way. First, attending on a spiritual friend at all times, whether they are sick or well, constantly raising love, respect, and pure faith. Second, respectfully greeting, prostrating before, welcoming, and joining palms toward a spiritual friend at all times, earnestly cultivating harmonious, respectful conduct as an offering. Third, supplying a spiritual friend at all times with properly, lawfully obtained robes, food and drink, bedding, and medicine for illness, and every article supporting the body. Fourth, taking a spiritual friend as one's genuine basis of reliance, freely and correctly acting in accordance with the true dharma's own meaning, whether accepting what accords with it or releasing what does not, without any wavering; genuinely disclosing any fault as it actually is; holding a mind of pure obedience to the teaching; and going again and again to respectfully attend upon, ask questions of, and receive what is taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first three marks as together naming the concrete, embodied side of discipleship already familiar from the earlier account of venerating the three jewels — constant personal service through illness and health alike, ritual gestures of respect, and material support through properly, honestly obtained provisions — before the fourth mark shifts to the deeper, internal work of genuine reliance: taking the spiritual friend as one's actual guide toward transformation from ordinary being to noble one, correctly and without distortion putting into practice whatever that friend sanctions, correctly and without distortion releasing whatever that friend proscribes, and doing so freely, not grudgingly or under mere external pressure.

He treats "without any wavering" as ruling out being drawn away by some rival, non-Buddhist teaching once genuine reliance has been established, and "genuinely disclosing any fault as it actually is" as requiring immediate, honest confession the moment any lapse occurs, rather than concealment or excuse-making. "A mind of pure obedience" he glosses as a mind entirely free of any wish to trouble or provoke the teacher, wanting nothing but genuine understanding of the teaching itself — the disposition, he stresses, that actually makes every other mark on this list possible, since service and material support offered with a resentful or self-interested mind would not constitute genuine approach at all. This closes the four marks of properly approaching a spiritual friend; the treatise turns next to the sixth topic, the five attitudes with which a disciple should actually listen once the teaching begins.

27. Milestone: Listening to the Dharma Opens — Regarding It as a Rare Jewel, and as the Eye That Will See Everything Knowable

The sixth topic names five attitudes a disciple should hold while actually listening to the teaching. The first, regarding the dharma as a rare jewel, rests on the sheer rarity of encountering a buddha, a genuine teaching, or the karmic opportunity to actually hear it at all — treasured precisely because it is so seldom available. The second, regarding it as an eye, looks toward the future: hearing correct teaching now plants the cause for the vast, spontaneously arising wisdom of complete awakening itself, the eye by which a buddha sees everything knowable without exception.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩欲聽法時，作五種想，應從善友聽聞正法。一、作寶想。難得義故。二、作眼想。能得廣大俱生妙慧因性義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who wish to listen to the dharma should form five kinds of thought when listening to the true teaching from a spiritual friend. First, regarding it as a jewel, because of how genuinely difficult it is to obtain. Second, regarding it as an eye, because it forms the cause for obtaining the vast, spontaneously arising, wondrous wisdom of complete awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the jewel-comparison across three layers: a genuine spiritual friend is rare, since a buddha's own appearance in the world is exceedingly hard to encounter even though the dharma-body itself never ceases, and even short of an actual buddha, teachers who genuinely embody discipline, concentration, and wisdom remain scarce, especially in a degenerate age; the teaching itself is rare, whether the great vehicle's own account of the middle way and the emptiness of both self and phenomena, or the lesser vehicle's four noble truths and dependent origination, since either requires a buddha's own appearance to be taught in the first place; and, rarest of all, the actual karmic opportunity to hear it, since encountering genuine dharma requires having cultivated sufficient merit and wholesome roots in the past, a circumstance the tradition calls difficult to encounter across countless eons — precisely why, he notes, the customary verse recited before any dharma talk begins exists specifically to remind the listener of this rarity, rather than functioning as empty ritual formality.

He treats the eye-comparison as pointing specifically toward the future fruit of listening: correct teaching, properly received now, becomes the seed for the vast, unprompted wisdom a buddha alone possesses — vast because it comprehends every knowable object without exception, spontaneous because in a future life it will require no teacher to arise, unfolding entirely from its own prior cultivation. He notes the treatise's own fourfold account of what this "great awakening" fruit actually consists in — being freed forever from what once compelled rebirth, no longer subject to any further compelled rebirth at all, correctly discerning every knowable object, and reaching the utterly purified expanse of reality itself — treating listening now as already planting the specific cause for all four. This closes the first two attitudes toward listening; the treatise turns next to the third through fifth, regarding the teaching as light, as a great and excellent fruit, and as a faultless, deeply satisfying joy.

28. Milestone: Listening to the Dharma Closes — As Light Illuminating Every Phenomenon Equally, As the Cause of Nirvāṇa, and as Faultless Joy Drawn From the Seven Noble Treasures

The remaining three attitudes: regarding the teaching as light, since it will one day open the very eye of wisdom that illuminates every phenomenon exactly as it truly is; regarding it as leading to a great and excellent fruit, since it forms the cause for nirvāṇa's own liberation and complete awakening's own wisdom; and regarding it as a faultless, deeply satisfying joy even now, since correctly discerning the teaching through calming and insight practice already produces a boundless, undefiled happiness — the very happiness the tradition names among the seven noble treasures of faith, discipline, conscience, shame, learning, generosity, and wisdom.

Chinese Source

三、作明想。已得廣大俱生慧眼，於一切種如實所知等照義故。四、作大果勝功德想。能得涅槃及三菩提無上妙跡因性義故。五、作無罪大適悅想。於現法中未得涅槃及三菩提，於法如實揀擇止觀無罪大樂因性義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, regarding it as light, because it forms the cause for the vast, spontaneously arising eye of wisdom already obtained, illuminating every kind of knowable phenomenon exactly as it is, equally and without distinction. Fourth, regarding it as a great and excellent fruit, because it forms the cause for nirvāṇa and complete awakening's own unsurpassed, wondrous path. Fifth, regarding it as a faultless, deeply satisfying joy, because, even in this present life, before nirvāṇa and complete awakening have yet been attained, it forms the cause for the faultless, great happiness of correctly discerning the dharma through calming and insight practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the light-attitude from the earlier eye-attitude as a matter of emphasis: the eye names wisdom itself as a still-developing organ, the mere capacity to see, while light names that same wisdom already functioning, actively illuminating both the way things conventionally appear and the way things ultimately are, "equally" meaning that this illumination plays no favorites among phenomena, all dharmas resting equally empty of any fixed, independent essence once genuinely seen through. The fourth attitude he ties to the treatise's own fourfold account of nirvāṇa already discussed earlier in this fascicle's proper-practice veneration passage — nirvāṇa itself as the fruit of cutting off affliction, complete awakening as the fruit of complete wisdom — hearing genuine dharma now, he stresses, is already the seed of both.

The fifth attitude he treats as the treatise's own reminder that dharma-listening is not solely deferred to some future payoff: even now, prior to any final attainment, correctly discerning the teaching through calming and insight already produces real, tangible happiness — specifically the happiness born of the seventh of the seven noble treasures, wisdom, listed alongside faith, discipline, learning, generosity, conscience, and shame in the treatise's own earlier account of the noble path's stages: faith's own treasure producing a pure happiness unshaken by any rival teaching, discipline's treasure producing happiness born in a favorable rebirth, conscience and shame together producing a happiness free of later regret, learning's treasure producing a happiness joined with correct understanding, generosity's treasure producing a happiness of never lacking resources in future lives, and wisdom's treasure producing, at last, a genuinely unprecedented happiness of real liberation — boundless and entirely without taint, hence "faultless great joy." This closes the entire sixth topic, the five attitudes for listening to the dharma; the treatise turns next to the seventh and final topic of this section, the five points on which a disciple should never form a critical opinion of the teacher delivering the teaching.

29. Milestone: Never Judging the Teacher — Broken Precepts, Low Birth, and an Unlovely Face Are Never Grounds to Refuse the Teaching

The seventh and final topic of this section names five points on which a disciple should never think ill of a teacher and thereby refuse to listen: the teacher's own imperfect precept-keeping, so long as their view remains correct and the lapse is not a root violation; their humble family origin, since even a person of low birth may have genuinely attained the noble path; and their unattractive physical appearance, since a teacher's face has nothing whatsoever to do with the value of what they teach.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩欲從善友聽聞法時，於說法師由五種處不作異意，以純淨心屬耳聽法。一、於壞戒不作異意。謂不作心：此是破戒，不住律儀，我今不應從彼聽法。二、於壞族不作異意。謂不作心：此是卑姓，我今不應從彼聽法。三、於壞色不作異意。謂不作心：此是醜陋，我今不應從彼聽法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who wish to listen to the dharma from a spiritual friend should never form a contrary opinion of the teacher on five points, but should instead listen to the teaching with a wholly pure mind, ears genuinely attentive. First, never forming a contrary opinion regarding broken discipline: that is, never thinking, "this teacher has broken the precepts and does not abide in proper discipline; I ought not listen to the teaching from them now." Second, never forming a contrary opinion regarding humble lineage: that is, never thinking, "this teacher is of a lowly family; I ought not listen to the teaching from them now." Third, never forming a contrary opinion regarding an unlovely appearance: that is, never thinking, "this teacher is unsightly; I ought not listen to the teaching from them now."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first point with real precision rather than blanket permission: a listener should not refuse teaching from someone with an imperfect precept record so long as that teacher's actual view remains correct and the lapse in question is a lesser fault rather than one of the root violations that would sever the very basis of monastic standing — citing Tsongkhapa's own commentary on this exact passage, which distinguishes faults that still leave some remainder of the precept intact from the root violations that leave none. The second point he treats as a direct application of the earlier teaching, in this same fascicle, on the Buddha's own supremacy transcending every social category: if birth alone determined spiritual worth, an outcaste who genuinely attains arhatship would remain unworthy of being heard, which the entire thrust of the Buddhist path explicitly denies.

The third point he treats as needing barely any argument once stated plainly: a teacher's physical appearance carries no bearing whatsoever on the truth or value of the dharma being taught, and refusing instruction merely because the teacher is unattractive to look at would be rejecting the entire substance of the teaching over something wholly beside the point. Taken together, he notes, these first three points systematically strip away the most common superficial excuses a listener might unconsciously reach for — moral record, social status, physical appearance — each one tempting precisely because it offers an easy, surface-level reason to dismiss a teacher without engaging with what they actually have to say. This closes the first three points; the treatise turns next to the fourth and fifth — unrefined language and blunt, harsh speech — and to the section's closing warning against squandering the opportunity to learn.

30. Milestone: Rely on Meaning, Not on Rhetoric — Approaching Good Friends Closes With a Warning Against Squandering Wisdom Through Pettiness

The fourth and fifth points: never dismissing a teacher merely for using unpolished, inelegant language, since the dharma should always be relied upon for its meaning rather than for the beauty of its phrasing; and never dismissing a teacher merely for harsh or blunt speech born of correcting real faults, since here too meaning outweighs manner. The treatise then closes the entire section with a sharp warning: a listener of weak wisdom who looks down on a teacher and refuses to listen on any such superficial ground is not genuinely seeking their own benefit at all, but is instead actively losing the very wisdom they could have gained.

Chinese Source

四、於壞文不作異意。謂不作心：此於言辭不善藻飾，我今不應從彼聽法。但依於義，不應依文。五、於壞美不作異意。謂不作心：此語麤惡，多懷忿恚，不以美言宣說諸法，我今不應從彼聽法。如是菩薩欲聽法時，於是五處不應作意，但應恭敬攝受正法，於說法師未嘗見過。若有菩薩其慧微劣，於說法師心生嫌鄙，不欲從其聽聞正法，當知此行不求自利，退失勝慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, never forming a contrary opinion regarding unrefined phrasing: that is, never thinking, "this teacher's words are not skillfully embellished; I ought not listen to the teaching from them now." One should rely upon the meaning, not upon the phrasing. Fifth, never forming a contrary opinion regarding blunt speech: that is, never thinking, "this teacher's words are coarse, harboring much indignation, and this teacher does not proclaim the teaching in agreeable language; I ought not listen to the teaching from them now." Bodhisattvas who wish to listen to the dharma should never dwell on any of these five points, but should instead respectfully take up the true teaching, never once looking for fault in the teacher delivering it. Should any bodhisattva of weak, inferior wisdom form a contemptuous, disdainful opinion of a teacher and refuse to listen to the true teaching from them, one should understand that such conduct does not genuinely seek one's own benefit at all, but instead causes one's own excellent wisdom to be lost.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth and fifth points as two faces of the same underlying error: judging teaching by its surface style rather than its substance, whether that surface fails by being too plain and unpolished or too rough and blunt. He cross-references the treatise's own earlier account, from the Stage With Both Thought and Reflection, of the eight qualities genuinely skillful speech possesses — leading toward liberation, aesthetically pleasing, clearly explained, vividly illustrated, genuinely beneficial, free of self-interest, appropriately paced, and coherently sustained — noting that harsh or blunt speech, lacking these qualities on the surface, is named their precise opposite there, yet even so a listener must not let that surface roughness obscure whatever genuine benefit the underlying meaning still carries; a teacher correcting a real fault bluntly, out of genuine concern rather than cruelty, has not thereby forfeited the value of what they are actually saying.

He treats the closing warning as the entire section's real point, delivered with unusual bluntness for the treatise's own normally measured tone: a listener who lets any of these five superficial impressions — imperfect discipline, humble birth, plain looks, unpolished words, blunt delivery — become grounds for refusing instruction is not thereby protecting their own dignity or standards, but actively squandering wisdom that was genuinely available to them, since wisdom, as he puts it, never reaches a point of being satisfied — every teacher, however seemingly ordinary, humble, or rough around the edges, has something a sincere listener stands to actually gain, and refusing that opportunity over a surface impression is a loss with no compensating benefit whatsoever. With this warning, the treatise closes the entire second great section of the chapter — approaching good friends, spanning all seven of its topics from what qualifies someone to teach through how a disciple should listen without prejudice; the treatise turns next to its third and final great section, cultivating the four immeasurables.

31. Milestone: Cultivating the Four Immeasurables Opens — Three Depths From Sentient Beings to Mere Phenomena to No Object at All

The chapter's third and final great section opens by asking how a bodhisattva cultivates loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, then names three progressively deeper ways of doing so: taking sentient beings themselves as the object, wishing them well directly; taking mere phenomena as the object, recognizing that "beings" are only a provisional label for the aggregates of form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness; and taking no object at all, resting free of every conceptual discrimination whatsoever while continuing to wish all beings well.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩修四無量慈、悲、喜、捨？謂諸菩薩略有三種修四無量。一者、有情緣無量，二者、法緣無量，三者、無緣無量。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do bodhisattvas cultivate the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity? Bodhisattvas cultivate the four immeasurables, in brief, in three ways: first, the immeasurables taking sentient beings as their object; second, the immeasurables taking mere phenomena as their object; third, the immeasurables taking no object at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three as a single graduated path rather than three unrelated methods: taking sentient beings as the object means precisely what it sounds like, wishing well directly to the boundless beings of the ten directions with no further philosophical refinement yet applied; taking mere phenomena as the object means gradually shifting that same wish from the conventional truth of "beings" toward the ultimate truth that what is actually present is only the ever-changing aggregates of form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness, "being" itself no more than a provisional label laid over that shifting process, so that cultivating the immeasurables this way slowly opens into a genuine understanding of dependent origination; and taking no object at all means going one step further still, recognizing that even the aggregates themselves, and the practitioner's own sense of "now I am practicing this," are equally without any fixed, independent essence, so that the cultivation becomes genuinely free of the three false notions of a giver, a receiver, and a gift, resonating directly with ultimate truth.

He notes that this same threefold structure was already introduced once earlier in this very fascicle, in the account of virtuous-person meditation's own four immeasurables — proof, he stresses, that the treatise regards this graduated structure as the single correct way of approaching every one of the four immeasurables, not a special technique reserved for meditative concentration specifically. This closes the section's opening statement of method; the treatise turns next to defining loving-kindness itself across these same three depths, before doing the same for compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity in turn.

32. Milestone: Loving-Kindness Defined Across Three Depths, Then the Same Structure Extended to the Remaining Three

Loving-kindness is defined first at the level of sentient beings: raising the heightened aspiration to give happiness to whoever desires it, universally, across the ten directions, resting in an undistorted, correct understanding of beings whether friend, enemy, or stranger. It then deepens into the phenomena-based and objectless levels already outlined, and the treatise notes this same threefold pattern applies equally to compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — before defining each of those three in turn through its own most basic, sentient-being-focused form.

Chinese Source

於其最初欲求樂者，發起與樂增上意樂，普緣十方，安住無倒有情勝解，修慈俱心，當知是名有情緣慈。若諸菩薩，住唯法想增上意樂，正觀唯法假說有情，修慈俱心，當知即此名法緣慈。若諸菩薩，復於諸法遠離分別，修慈俱心，當知即此名無緣慈。如有情緣、法緣、無緣三慈差別，悲、喜、捨三，當知亦爾。若諸菩薩，於有苦者，發起除苦增上意樂，普緣十方修悲俱心，是名為悲。若諸菩薩，於有樂者，發起隨喜增上意樂，普緣十方修喜俱心，是名為喜。若諸菩薩，即於如是無苦無樂、有苦、有樂三種有情，隨其次第，發起遠離癡瞋貪惑增上意樂，普緣十方修捨俱心，是名為捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward those who, from the very outset, wish for happiness, raising the heightened aspiration to give happiness, universally extended across the ten directions, resting in an undistorted, correct understanding of beings, and cultivating a mind joined with loving-kindness — one should understand this to be called loving-kindness taking sentient beings as its object. Bodhisattvas who abide in the heightened aspiration of regarding beings as mere phenomena, correctly observing that "sentient beings" is only a provisional label for mere phenomena, and who cultivate a mind joined with loving-kindness on this basis — one should understand this to be called loving-kindness taking mere phenomena as its object. Bodhisattvas who, further still, remain entirely free of conceptual discrimination toward all phenomena while cultivating a mind joined with loving-kindness — one should understand this to be called loving-kindness taking no object at all. Just as loving-kindness differs across these three — sentient beings, mere phenomena, and no object — one should understand that compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity are exactly the same. Bodhisattvas who, toward those in suffering, raise the heightened aspiration to remove suffering, universally extended across the ten directions, cultivating a mind joined with compassion — this is called compassion. Bodhisattvas who, toward those who have happiness, raise the heightened aspiration to rejoice with them, universally extended across the ten directions, cultivating a mind joined with sympathetic joy — this is called sympathetic joy. Bodhisattvas who, toward these same three kinds of being — those with neither suffering nor happiness, those with suffering, and those with happiness — raise, in proper sequence, the heightened aspiration to release delusion, hostility, and craving, universally extended across the ten directions, cultivating a mind joined with equanimity — this is called equanimity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats equanimity's own definition here as carrying a genuinely distinct structure worth pausing over: rather than a single uniform wish extended equally to every being, as with the other three, equanimity matches a specific antidote to each of three different conditions — toward beings caught in the deceptively peaceful equanimous feeling of the higher meditative absorptions themselves, wishing them free of the underlying delusion that mistakes that peace for something ultimate; toward beings actually suffering, wishing them free of the hostility that painful sensation so easily provokes; and toward beings actually enjoying happiness, wishing them free of the craving that pleasant sensation so easily provokes. He explains why each pairing holds: neutral, neither-painful-nor-pleasant sensation is itself a subtle form of suffering-through-conditioning that delusion mistakes for genuine peace, hence its link to delusion; painful sensation reliably breeds resentment in nearly everyone who experiences it, hence its link to hostility; and pleasant sensation reliably breeds attachment in nearly everyone who experiences it, hence its link to craving — three distinct diagnoses matched to three distinct conditions, all gathered under the single heading of equanimity because each one aims at releasing beings from whichever affliction their present feeling-tone is quietly compounding.

This closes the basic, sentient-being-focused definition of all four immeasurables; the treatise turns next to comparing this fourfold cultivation against how outsiders and the two lesser vehicles cultivate loving-kindness and the rest, before turning to the vast object of compassion specifically — the full catalogue of suffering a bodhisattva must genuinely see before compassion can become truly boundless.

33. Milestone: Uncommon Even Among Śrāvakas — What the Bodhisattva's Four Immeasurables Share With Outsiders, With the Two Vehicles, and With No One Else at All

The treatise draws a sharp comparison: the sentient-being-focused level of the four immeasurables is shared with non-Buddhist outsiders, who cultivate them too but only to be reborn in the Brahmā heavens, still bound by craving for further existence; the phenomena-based level is shared with śrāvakas and solitary realizers, who likewise see through the illusion of a fixed self, but not with outsiders; and the objectless level belongs to bodhisattvas alone, resting on the understanding of emptiness that only the great vehicle teaches. A closing note groups the four differently again: loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy fall under the aspiration for happiness, while equanimity alone falls under the aspiration for genuine benefit.

Chinese Source

此中菩薩慈等無量有情緣者，當知其相與外道共。若法緣者，當知其相與諸聲聞及獨覺共，不共外道。若無緣者，當知其相不共一切聲聞、獨覺，及諸外道。又諸菩薩三種無量，應知安樂意樂所攝，謂慈、悲、喜。一種無量，應知利益意樂所攝，是謂為捨。如是菩薩一切無量，名為哀愍。以諸菩薩成就此故，名哀愍者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, one should understand that the bodhisattva's loving-kindness and the rest, when taking sentient beings as their object, share their character with outsiders. When taking mere phenomena as their object, one should understand that their character is shared with śrāvakas and solitary realizers, but not with outsiders. When taking no object at all, one should understand that their character is shared with none of these — not śrāvakas, not solitary realizers, and not outsiders. Furthermore, one should understand that three of the bodhisattva's immeasurables — loving-kindness, compassion, and sympathetic joy — are gathered under the aspiration for happiness, while the remaining one, equanimity, is gathered under the aspiration for genuine benefit. Bodhisattvas cultivating every one of the immeasurables in this way are called ones of genuine compassion; it is precisely because bodhisattvas accomplish this that they are named ones of genuine compassion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi quotes at length from the Maitreya-paripṛcchā's own commentary to sharpen exactly what separates the bodhisattva's cultivation from every rival version: outsiders who cultivate the four immeasurables still do so soaked through with craving, and so ripen only into a form-realm rebirth, never touching liberation; śrāvakas and solitary realizers cultivate every wholesome root for their own sake alone, motivated by dread of affliction's own heat rather than genuine concern for other beings, and take up the immeasurables not for beings' sake but to subdue their own residual fetters; bodhisattvas alone hold beings continuously in mind, dedicate every practice back to those beings, take up discipline and generosity out of loving-kindness and compassion specifically to benefit them, willingly endure the world's own worst afflictions rather than abandon beings to escape into private peace, work out concrete means to relieve each being's own particular form of suffering one by one, and are able to behold nirvāṇa without needing to enter it immediately, precisely so as to remain among suffering beings and accomplish boundless buddha-qualities on their behalf — this, he stresses, is the actual reason the treatise can call a bodhisattva's version of the four immeasurables "immeasurable" in the fullest sense, while an outsider's or a śrāvaka's version, however sincerely practiced, remains bounded by comparison.

He treats the closing grouping — three immeasurables under the aspiration for happiness, one under the aspiration for genuine benefit — as distinguishing what is wished for directly (pleasant feeling, freedom from pain, delight in others' good fortune, all straightforwardly desirable) from what is wished for because it is actually good even when it does not feel immediately pleasant (freedom from the three poisons themselves, which most beings do not consciously crave the way they crave happiness, yet which alone constitutes their real, lasting benefit) — precisely the same benefit-versus-ease distinction the treatise drew earlier in its account of what makes guidance from a spiritual friend genuinely effective. This closes the section's overview of how the four immeasurables compare across traditions; the treatise turns next, at far greater length, to compassion's own vast object — the full catalogue of one hundred and ten kinds of suffering a bodhisattva must genuinely see in order for compassion to reach its fullest, truest scope.

34. Milestone: One Hundred and Ten Kinds of Suffering Open — From the Single Suffering of Endless Wandering Through the Sting of Two Arrows to the Three Classical Sufferings of Pain, Conditioning, and Reversal

The treatise turns to compassion's vast object: one hundred and ten kinds of suffering observed throughout the world of sentient beings, the first fifty-five arranged as a triangular sequence — one kind, then two, then three, and so on up through ten. The single suffering is simply undifferentiated wandering through birth and death itself, afflicting every being without exception. The two are desire as the root of all suffering, and delusion's own suffering at the very moment of painful ripening, when self-clinging turns physical pain into a second, mental wound — being struck, as the text vividly puts it, by two arrows rather than one. The three are the classical threefold suffering of intrinsic pain, of restless conditioning even in neutral feeling, and of pleasure's own inevitable collapse.

Relying on the eight worldly conditions, there are eight kinds of suffering: first, the suffering of decline, when what is subject to decline actually declines; second, the suffering of exhaustion, when what is subject to exhaustion actually runs out; third, the suffering of aging, when what is subject to aging actually ages; fourth, the suffering of sickness, when what is subject to sickness actually sickens; fifth, the suffering of death, when what is subject to death actually dies; sixth, the suffering of lacking benefit; seventh, the suffering of lacking reputation; eighth, the suffering of open criticism. These are called the eight sufferings. Ninth, the suffering of mere wanting. All of this together is called suffering that relentlessly follows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties the first five of these eight directly to the aggregates' own basic nature: everything conditioned is, by its very constitution, subject to decline and eventual exhaustion, and a living body specifically is subject to the further three of aging, sickness, and death — he cross-references the treatise's own later chapter cataloguing seventeen distinct marks of aging and nine distinct marks of death, treating "old age" and "death" here as compressed pointers to that much fuller analysis elsewhere. The remaining three of the eight worldly conditions — lacking benefit, lacking reputation, and open criticism — he treats as suffering built specifically into social existence: material want when the basic resources of clothing, food, bedding, and medicine run short; the bitter frustration of failing to achieve the recognition one hoped for, or worse, actively losing whatever reputation one once had; and the sting of being openly mocked or disdained to one's face, which he distinguishes from slander spoken behind one's back as a related but separate injury.

The ninth suffering, mere wanting, he treats as this category's own quiet capstone: all eight worldly conditions already named are themselves specific instances of a single underlying structure — wherever there is wanting, of any kind whatsoever, suffering has already, in some sense, begun, since wanting inherently opens the possibility of frustration the moment its object fails to cooperate. Taken together, he notes, these nine sufferings earn the name "relentlessly following" precisely because no ordinary being, however fortunate their present circumstances, ever fully escapes every one of the eight worldly conditions across an entire lifetime, and the ninth, simple wanting, follows a being into literally every waking moment. This closes suffering that relentlessly follows; the treatise turns next to the ninth and final category, suffering of every kind, closing the entire second fifty-five and, with it, the complete catalogue of one hundred and ten sufferings.

41. Milestone: Suffering of Every Kind Closes the Entire Catalogue — the Shadow Cast by Five Kinds of Genuine Happiness, and the Coarse Affliction-Seeds That Make the Mind Unfit for Practice

The ninth and final category, suffering of every kind, names ten sufferings in two groups of five: the first five as the precise shadow cast by the five kinds of genuine happiness named elsewhere in the treatise — causal happiness, resultant happiness, happiness as the antidote to pain, happiness of cessation, and the fourfold happiness free of affliction's harm (renunciation, seclusion, stillness, and awakening) — and the second five as the suffering of being oppressed, of lacking life's basic requisites, of the body's elements falling out of balance, of what is loved changing or being destroyed, and of the coarse affliction-seeds carried from all three realms that leave body and mind unfit for genuine practice. A closing tally then confirms the total: fifty-five plus fifty-five make exactly one hundred and ten, the complete object upon which a bodhisattva's compassion is grown, strengthened, and finally perfected.

一切種苦中，復有十苦。謂如前說五樂所治有五種苦。一、因苦，二、受苦，三、唯無樂苦，四、受不斷苦，五、出離、遠離、寂靜、菩提樂所對治，家、欲界結、尋、異生苦。是名五苦。復有五苦。一、逼迫苦，二、眾具匱乏苦，三、界不平等苦，四、所愛變壞苦，五、三界煩惱品麤重苦。是名五苦。前五此五，總十種苦，當知是名一切種苦。前五十五，今五十五，總有一百一十種苦，是菩薩悲所緣境界。緣此境故，諸菩薩悲生起、增長、修習圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within suffering of every kind, there are, further, ten kinds. As already explained, the five kinds of happiness each have their own corresponding suffering as what they overcome: first, the suffering that is itself a cause; second, the suffering that is itself a felt sensation; third, the suffering of simply lacking happiness; fourth, the suffering of sensation never being cut off; fifth, the suffering overcome respectively by the happiness of renunciation, of seclusion, of stillness, and of awakening — namely, the suffering of household life, the suffering of the desire realm's own fetters, the suffering of discursive thought, and the suffering of an ordinary, unawakened being. These are called the five sufferings. There are, further, five sufferings: first, the suffering of being oppressed; second, the suffering of lacking life's requisites; third, the suffering of the body's elements falling out of balance; fourth, the suffering of what is loved changing or being destroyed; fifth, the suffering of the coarse affliction-seeds belonging to the three realms. These are called the five sufferings. The previous five together with these five make ten kinds of suffering in all; one should understand this to be called suffering of every kind. The previous fifty-five together with this fifty-five make one hundred and ten kinds of suffering in all — this is the very object upon which a bodhisattva's compassion rests. By taking this as its object, a bodhisattva's compassion arises where it had not yet arisen, grows where it had already arisen, and is brought to complete cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own earlier chapter on benefiting self and others for the five kinds of happiness this final grouping mirrors in reverse: causal happiness, meaning every present or future favorable cause capable of eventually producing a pleasant sensation; resultant happiness, the actual felt pleasure that ripens from it; happiness as pain's own antidote, the relief that arrives once a specific pain, such as hunger, is actually addressed; happiness of cessation, available only to noble ones who have attained cessation-absorption and thereby temporarily suspend sensation altogether, since, from the standpoint of ultimate truth, every ordinary sensation whatsoever is, in the end, a form of suffering; and the fourfold happiness free of affliction's harm — renunciation's own happiness in leaving the entangling household behind, seclusion's happiness in the first absorption's freedom from desire-realm affliction, stillness's happiness in the second absorption's freedom even from discursive thought, and awakening's happiness in realizing reality directly through the śrāvaka, solitary-realizer, or supreme path. Each of these five happinesses, he notes, has its own exact negative counterpart named here as suffering — lacking the cause, lacking the resulting sensation, lacking any relief from present pain, never escaping sensation's own restlessness even for a moment, and remaining, respectively, bound to household life, to the desire realm's own fetters, to discursive thought, or to an unawakened being's ordinary condition.

He treats the second five as this list's most concretely physical cluster — sheer oppressive pressure, material lack, elemental imbalance, and the loss of what is loved, all already familiar in substance from earlier categories — before closing on the coarse affliction-seeds belonging to all three realms, which the treatise's own earlier chapter on the five-sense-consciousness-accompanied ground defines as the dormant seeds of both afflictive obstruction and cognitive obstruction lodged within a being's own continuum, rendering body and mind rigid, unyielding, and genuinely unfit for the settled practice of calming and insight until specifically addressed. He closes this milestone on the treatise's own explicit tally — fifty-five plus fifty-five equals one hundred and ten — treating this exhaustive catalogue not as an academic exercise in classification but as the concrete curriculum through which a bodhisattva's own compassion is deliberately built: compassion that has not yet arisen is caused to arise by genuinely contemplating this list; compassion already present is caused to grow larger by continued contemplation of it; and compassion is finally brought to full, complete cultivation only once every one of these one hundred and ten sufferings has been genuinely seen and held in mind at once. This closes the entire catalogue of one hundred and ten sufferings; the treatise turns next to a second, more compressed lens on the same territory — nineteen kinds of suffering distilled from the hundred and ten just completed.

42. Milestone: Nineteen Kinds of Suffering — the Same Territory Distilled Into a Second, Sharper Lens for Arousing Great Compassion

Having laid out the full one hundred and ten sufferings, the treatise offers a second, more compressed pass across the identical territory: nineteen kinds of suffering selected specifically for how deep, how subtle, and how genuinely difficult to fully grasp they are — the first eight drawn from the earlier fifty-five, the remaining eleven drawn from the later fifty-five — precisely because sufferings this deep and subtle are what actually arouses a bodhisattva's own great compassion, as opposed to the more obvious sufferings an ordinary person notices without any particular training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps every one of these nineteen back to its exact source within the prior catalogue, confirming that nothing new is being introduced here, only a deliberate re-selection: the first eight are drawn from the earlier fifty-five — delusion at ripening from the twofold suffering's second item, conditioning-suffering from the threefold suffering's second item, the ultimate suffering from the fourfold suffering's fourth item, causal suffering from the sixfold suffering's first item, birth-suffering from the sevenfold suffering's first item, self-inflicted distress from the eightfold suffering's sixth item, and declining discipline and declining view from the ninefold suffering's sixth and seventh items — while the remaining eleven are drawn from the later fifty-five, mapped with equal precision back to their own specific categories and positions.

He treats the deliberate selectivity of this list as its real point: rather than repeating the entire hundred and ten, the treatise isolates nineteen specifically chosen for being genuinely deep, subtle, and difficult to fully appreciate without real reflection — ignorance's own suffering, for instance, or the suffering of coarse affliction-seeds lying dormant beneath ordinary awareness, are not the kind of pain an untrained person notices at a glance, unlike more obvious items such as hunger or bereavement. It is precisely this deeper, less visible tier of suffering, he stresses, that calls forth great compassion specifically — a compassion that sees past the obvious surface of pain into its subtler, more pervasive roots — as distinct from the ordinary sympathy any decent person might feel on witnessing someone else's visible distress. This closes the treatise's entire double accounting of suffering as compassion's object, one hundred and ten items and then nineteen; the treatise turns next to the four specific causes and conditions by which an ordinary compassion actually becomes worthy of the name "great compassion."
