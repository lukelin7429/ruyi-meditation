Fascicle 60 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十 · Karma Defilement's Weight Examined

The Mormon Passenger Who Said Killing Was Guiltless

Continuing directly from fascicle 59's exhaustive working-out of the ten unwholesome karma-paths, the treatise now turns to a further question every practitioner eventually asks: given that killing, or any unwholesome deed, has already been shown complete, what makes one instance of it graver than another? Five factors decide the answer, illustrated throughout with vivid stories drawn from Master Cháng Bǎi's own life and teaching experience.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 60 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Five Factors That Decide Whether a Killing Becomes Grave

Fascicle 59 closed having worked out all ten unwholesome karma-paths in exhaustive detail, their defining factors, their concrete methods, and their underlying motives, down to why one particular denial alone is singled out and named wrong view. The treatise now turns to a further, closely related question: given that a karma-path has already been shown complete, what determines whether that same deed counts as grave or light? Taking killing as its example once more, the treatise names five deciding factors: motivation, method, the absence of any antidote, wrong clinging, and the object itself. Motivation decides gravity first: killing driven by intense, forceful greed, intense, forceful hatred, or intense, forceful delusion is grave killing, while killing arising instead from some milder, more passing impulse, carried out almost thoughtlessly under the sway of a lesser circumstance rather than deliberate malice, is by contrast light killing. Method decides gravity as well, and unfolds through many further particulars: rejoicing inwardly at the very thought, I shall do this, I am doing this, I have done this; carrying out the deed personally or urging another to do it, and praising the act either way; feeling genuine delight upon meeting a fellow killer, as though celebrating a shared accomplishment; harboring the intention for a long time, nursing resentment before finally acting; carrying the deed out without interruption and with real thoroughness; killing many different kinds of beings all at once; maintaining a fixed, ongoing intention to kill as a matter of livelihood; killing a being already terrified and left with nowhere to flee; and killing someone already suffering, impoverished, grieving, or in tears — every one of these particulars, the treatise concludes, makes killing grave through the very method by which it is carried out.

Chinese Source

復次，由五因緣，殺生成重。何等為五？一、由意樂，二、由方便，三、由無治，四、由邪執，五、由其事。若由猛利貪欲意樂所作，猛利瞋恚意樂所作，猛利愚癡意樂所作，名重殺生。與此相違，名輕殺生。若有念言：我應當作、正作、已作。心便踊躍，心生歡悅；或有自作，或復勸他，於彼所作稱揚讚歎，見同法者意便欣慶，長時思量、長時蓄積怨恨心已方有所作，無間所作、殷重所作；或於一時頓殺多類；或以堅固發業因緣而行殺害；或令恐怖無所依投，方行殺害；或於孤苦、貧窮、哀慼、悲泣等者而行殺害。如是一切，由方便故，名重殺生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, killing becomes grave through five causes and conditions. What are the five? First, through motivation. Second, through method. Third, through the absence of antidote. Fourth, through wrong clinging. Fifth, through the object. If carried out through forceful greed, forceful hatred, or forceful delusion, it is called grave killing. Opposite to this is called light killing. If one thinks: I shall do this, am doing this, have done this, and the mind leaps with joy; whether done personally or urged upon another, praised either way; delighting upon meeting a fellow practitioner of the same deed; harbored and accumulated as resentment over a long time before finally being carried out; carried out without interruption and thoroughly; killing many kinds all at once; carried out through a firm, ongoing intention; carried out upon one left terrified with nowhere to turn; or carried out upon one already solitary, impoverished, grieving, or weeping — all of this, through method, is called grave killing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening question, what makes one instance of the same wrongdoing graver than another, as one of the treatise's own most practically important refinements, since it moves the discussion beyond the simple binary of complete versus incomplete karma-path toward something far closer to how any thoughtful person actually weighs moral responsibility in real life, recognizing that intention, premeditation, and the vulnerability of the victim all genuinely matter. He highlights the particular weight given to killing the already-terrified or already-suffering as this section's own quiet insistence that cruelty toward the defenseless carries its own distinct and heavier burden, beyond whatever weight the killing itself already carries on its own.

2. Section: A Chicken's Struggle, and a Fellow Passenger's Wrong View

The treatise names its third factor, the absence of any antidote: one who does nothing but kill, unable even to keep a single precept for even a single day, unable to observe the fasting days on the eighth, fourteenth, and fifteenth of the month or at the half-month, unable to give even occasional charity, cultivate merit, pay respects, or practice the six points of reverent harmony with fellow practitioners, unable to feel forceful shame or genuine remorse over the wrong already done, and who neither attains worldly freedom from desire through meditation nor genuine realization of the noble truths through the transcendent path — such a person's killing, entirely unmitigated by any of these countervailing practices, becomes grave killing precisely through this total absence of antidote. The fourth factor is wrong clinging: certain ascetics and brahmins, continuing a long tradition of improper sacrifice, hold the view that ritual killing is itself righteous conduct and act on this very conviction, and others hold that killing a sheep, for instance, carries no fault at all, since such animals exist purely as provisions, created by the lord of the world specifically to be consumed — killing carried out on the strength of such wrong views is likewise grave killing.

Chinese Source

若唯行殺，不能日日乃至極少持一學處；或亦不能於月八日、十四、十五及半月等，受持齋戒；或亦不能於時時間惠施作福、問訊禮拜、迎送合掌和敬業等；又亦不能於時時間獲得猛利增上慚愧，悔所惡作；又不證得世間離欲，亦不證得真法現觀。如是一切，由無治故，名重殺生。若諸沙門、或婆羅門，繼邪祠祀，隨忍此見，執為正法而行殺戮。由邪執故，名重殺生。又作是心：殺羊無罪，由彼羊等為資生故，世主所化。諸如是等依止邪見而行殺害，皆邪執故，名重殺生。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one only kills, unable to keep even a single precept even briefly, unable to observe the fasting days on the eighth, fourteenth, and fifteenth and at the half-month, unable at any time to give charity, cultivate merit, pay respects, or practice reverent harmony, unable at any time to feel forceful, heightened shame or remorse for what was done wrong, and neither attains worldly freedom from desire nor genuine realization of the true dharma — all of this, through the absence of antidote, is called grave killing. Certain ascetics or brahmins, continuing wrongful sacrifice, accepting this view, holding it as correct dharma, carry out killing. Through wrong clinging, this is called grave killing. Also thinking: killing a sheep carries no fault, since such creatures exist as provisions, transformed by the lord of the world for this purpose. All such killing carried out relying on wrong view is, through wrong clinging, called grave killing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recalls his own first flight to San Diego, seated beside a Mormon layperson who asked why he kept a vegetarian diet; when he explained it was to avoid harming living beings, his fellow passenger replied that such creatures were made by God for human consumption, and that killing them therefore carried no guilt at all — a view he did not yet, at that time, recognize as a textbook instance of exactly the wrong clinging this passage describes. He recalls as well a fellow monastic who entered monastic life after being asked, as a child, to kill a chicken for his mother, and who was so shaken by the sight of the half-killed bird still convulsing after an incomplete cut to its throat that he begged never to be asked to do such a thing again, his own natural compassion, still uncorrupted by any wrong view rationalizing the act as harmless, becoming the very seed of his eventual renunciation.

3. Section: The Object Itself Decides Gravity — And Theft's Own Weighing Begins

The treatise names its fifth factor, the object of the deed itself: killing a large-bodied being is graver than killing a small one, since a human being, for instance, carries far greater weight than an ant. Killing a human being, or a fetus already bearing the human form, whether father, mother, or any other deeply respected benefactor; killing one who has come seeking refuge or entrusted themselves to one's own care; killing a noble learner, a bodhisattva, an arhat, or a solitary awakened one; or forming the malicious intent to harm a Tathāgata and actually drawing blood, as when Devadatta rolled a boulder down a mountain hoping to harm the Buddha and succeeded only in wounding his foot, a deed made all the graver still by the very fact that a Tathāgata's own nature makes him genuinely impossible to kill — every one of these, through the object involved, constitutes grave killing, and killing under circumstances opposite to everything now described is, by contrast, light killing. With this, the treatise completes its detailed account of killing's own gravity, and turns to theft, first by way of the object's own value: theft of items worth as little as five coins is already reckoned grave, according to the Buddhist standard, and every other unwholesome deed should be understood to follow a similar object-based principle, just as with killing. The treatise then details theft's own nine circumstances of gravity in turn: theft committed frequently and repeatedly, through sheer habituation turning even a light offense grave; theft of especially fine, precious, or valuable goods; and theft from one who has entrusted their own confidence to the thief.

Chinese Source

若有殺害大身眾生，此由事故，名重殺生。或有殺害人或人相，或父或母，及餘尊重；或有殺害歸投委信，或諸有學、或諸菩薩、或阿羅漢、或諸獨覺；或於如來作殺害意惡心出血，如來性爾不可殺故。如是一切，由其事故，名重殺生。與如上說因緣相違而殺生者，名輕殺生。復次，當說不與取等，由其事故，輕重差別。餘隨所應，如殺應知。復次，若多劫盜，名重不與取。

Ru-Yi Translation

Killing a large-bodied being is, through the object, called grave killing. Killing a human being or one bearing the human form, a father, a mother, or another deeply respected one; killing one who has sought refuge or entrusted themselves; killing a noble learner, a bodhisattva, an arhat, or a solitary awakened one; or forming the malicious intent to harm a Tathāgata and drawing blood, since a Tathāgata's own nature makes him impossible to kill — all of this, through the object, is called grave killing. Killing under circumstances opposite to what has been described is called light killing. Moreover, the gravity of theft and the rest, through the object, shall now be explained. The rest should be understood accordingly, just as with killing. Moreover, frequent plundering is called grave theft.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the observation that a Tathāgata's own nature makes him genuinely impossible to kill as a remarkable theological point tucked inside an otherwise straightforward ethical catalogue, Devadatta's own malicious act condemned as grave precisely because of its intent and its violence, even though its actual object could never, in the end, be destroyed at all. He highlights the five-coin threshold for grave theft as an example of the tradition's own consistent preference for concrete, specific standards over vague moral generalities, giving practitioners and monastic communities alike a genuinely workable rule rather than leaving the line between light and grave theft to individual judgment alone.

4. Section: Stealing From the Poor, Deceiving the Trusted, and Splitting the Sangha

The treatise continues theft's nine circumstances of gravity: theft from one already destitute and impoverished; theft from the Buddhist monastic community, whose very possessions were offered in good faith by devoted followers throughout the world; plundering a village, including the burning of homes, dwellings, and possessions out of pure hatred; and theft from a noble learner, an arhat, a solitary awakened one, or property belonging to the monastic community as a whole or to a Buddhist shrine or monastery, whether the community's own dwellings and provisions or the temple's own sacred possessions. Turning to sexual misconduct's own grave circumstances: approaching one's mother or a mother-like figure, the wife of one who has entrusted their own confidence to oneself, a woman observing the precepts, a nun, a novice, or a female probationer; engaging in the wrong bodily part, specifically the face; engaging at the wrong time, while precepts are being observed, at the completion of pregnancy, or during serious illness; and engaging in the wrong place, within a Buddhist shrine or monastery, itself meant for cultivating freedom from desire — every one of these, through the object involved, makes sexual misconduct grave. Turning finally to false speech's own grave circumstances: lying in order to deceive another out of especially fine or valuable wealth; lying to one who has entrusted their confidence, to one's own father or mother, or, as already explained, to any other deeply respected being, a noble learner, a bodhisattva, an arhat, a solitary awakened one, or even the Buddha himself; lying in a way that actually succeeds in causing another to commit grave killing, grave theft, or grave sexual misconduct, the resulting gravity of those deeds compounding the gravity of the lie that caused them; and, gravest of all forms of false speech without exception, a lie capable of splitting the harmonious monastic community itself apart.

Chinese Source

如是若劫盜妙好，劫盜委信，劫盜孤貧，劫盜佛法出家之眾；若入聚落而行劫盜，劫盜有學、或阿羅漢、或諸獨覺、或復僧祇、或佛靈廟所有財物。如是一切，由其事故，名重不與取。復次，行不應行中，若母母親委信他妻，或住禁戒、或苾芻尼、或勤策女、或復正學。如是一切，由其事故，名重欲邪行。非支行中，若於面門。由其事故，名重欲邪行。非時行中，若受齋戒、若胎圓滿、若有重病。由其事故，名重欲邪行。非處行中，若佛靈廟、若僧伽藍。由其事故，名重欲邪行。復次，若為誑惑多取他財，若妙若勝而說妄語。由事重故，名重妄語。若於委信、若父若母，廣說如前乃至佛所而說妄語。由事重故，名重妄語。或有妄語令他殺生，損失財物及與妻妾，此若成辦極重殺生、重不與取、重欲邪行。此由事重，名重妄語。或有妄語能破壞僧，於諸妄語此最尤重。

Ru-Yi Translation

Stealing especially fine goods, stealing from one who has entrusted their confidence, stealing from the destitute, stealing from the Buddhist monastic community, plundering a village, or stealing property belonging to a noble learner, an arhat, a solitary awakened one, the monastic community, or a Buddhist shrine, is, through the object, all called grave theft. Moreover, approaching one's mother, a mother-like figure, the wife of one who has entrusted their confidence, one observing the precepts, a nun, a novice, or a probationer, is all, through the object, called grave sexual misconduct. Engaging in the face rather than the proper part is, through the object, called grave sexual misconduct. Engaging while precepts are observed, at the completion of pregnancy, or during serious illness is, through the object, called grave sexual misconduct. Engaging within a Buddhist shrine or monastery is, through the object, called grave sexual misconduct. Moreover, lying in order to deceive and take especially fine or excellent wealth from another is, through the gravity of the object, called grave false speech. Lying to one who has entrusted their confidence, to one's father or mother, or, as already explained, even to the Buddha, is, through the gravity of the object, called grave false speech. Lying in a way that causes another to commit grave killing, or the loss of great wealth and of wife and concubines, if this succeeds and brings about grave killing, grave theft, or grave sexual misconduct, is, through the gravity of the object, called grave false speech. Lying capable of splitting the monastic community apart is the gravest of all false speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the singling out of schism-causing lies as the single gravest form of false speech as a fitting bookend to the treatise's own earlier discussion, back in fascicle 59, of the six points of harmony that make communal spiritual life possible in the first place, since a lie capable of tearing that very harmony apart attacks not merely one relationship but the entire structure that allows many practitioners to support one another's progress at all. He highlights the object-based principle running consistently through killing, theft, sexual misconduct, and false speech alike, greater harm to a more vulnerable, more trusted, or more significant object producing a graver offense, as the treatise's own coherent ethical logic, treating the karma-path's own completion and its ultimate gravity as two related but genuinely distinct questions throughout.

5. Section: Cursing One's Own Parents — Harsh Words at Their Gravest

The treatise completes divisive speech's own grave circumstances: destroying another's genuine good friends, parents, sons, or daughters, or splitting the harmonious monastic community itself apart; and divisive speech that actually succeeds in provoking grave killing, grave theft, or grave sexual misconduct in its wake, is likewise grave through the very gravity of what it causes. Turning to harsh speech, the treatise names its own gravest form: directing coarse, wounding words at one's own parents or at any other teacher, precisely because these are the very people owed the deepest respect, makes such speech an especially grievous wrong; and scolding, reviling, or reproaching another to their face using an accusation that is not even true compounds the offense of harsh speech with the offense of falsehood at once, making it graver still. Turning to idle chatter, the treatise explains that its own gravity generally follows the same pattern already established for false speech; idle chatter arising specifically from dispute, litigation, or rivalry is likewise grave; and with a mind already defiled by affliction, enthusiastically reciting, praising, and broadly expounding to others the very heretical texts capable of leading only to greed, hatred, and delusion, when the Buddhist tradition's own teachings ought to take precedence instead, makes such idle chatter grave as well, as does mocking or ridiculing one's own parents, family, or teachers with words entirely lacking in reason or respect.

Chinese Source

或破壞他，令離善友、父母、男女，破和合僧。若離間語，能引殺生、或不與取、或欲邪行。如前所說道理應知。如是一切，由事重故，名重離間語。復次，若於父母及餘師長發麤惡言。由事重故，名重麤惡語。或以不實不真妄語，現前毀罵訶責於他。由事重故，名重麤惡語。復次，凡諸綺語隨妄語等，此語輕重，如彼應知。若依鬥訟、諍競等事而發綺語，亦名為重。若以染汙心，於能引無義外道典籍承誦、讚詠、廣為他說。由事重故，名重綺語。若於父母、眷屬、師長調弄輕笑，現作語言不近道理。亦由事重，名重綺語。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or destroying another, causing them to be parted from good friends, parents, sons, or daughters, or splitting the harmonious community, or divisive speech capable of provoking killing, taking what is not given, or sexual misconduct, should be understood according to the principle already explained. All of this, through the gravity of the object, is called grave divisive speech. Moreover, directing coarse speech at one's parents or other teachers is, through the gravity of the object, called grave harsh speech. Reviling and reproaching another to their face with an untrue, unreal falsehood is, through the gravity of the object, called grave harsh speech. Moreover, idle chatter generally follows false speech in its gravity, as should be understood accordingly. Idle chatter arising from dispute and rivalry is likewise called grave. With a defiled mind, reciting, praising, and broadly expounding to others heretical texts capable of leading only to no benefit is, through the gravity of the object, called grave idle chatter. Mocking and ridiculing one's parents, family, or teachers with words far from reason is likewise, through the gravity of the object, called grave idle chatter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights harsh words directed at one's own parents as a fitting bookend to the treatise's own repeated insistence, across killing, theft, and now speech alike, that the very same act carries different weight depending on who receives it, the deepest bonds of gratitude and dependency making betrayal within them correspondingly graver than betrayal of a stranger. He notes that idle chatter's gravity being explicitly tied to false speech's own already-established pattern shows the treatise's own economy of method, refusing to repeat an entire analysis from scratch once the underlying principle, that greater harm to a more significant object makes for a graver offense, has already been thoroughly established.

6. Section: Wanting Recognition From Kings — And the Worst of All Wrong Views

The treatise turns to covetousness's own gravest forms: coveting property belonging to the monastic community as a whole or to a Buddhist shrine is grave precisely because of the significance of what is coveted; and a practitioner who, out of an overreaching conceit, mistakes their own accomplishment for genuine attainment, believing themselves wise and realized, and on this false basis actively seeks recognition and material support from kings, ministers, the wealthy and powerful, respected teachers, or wise fellow practitioners in spiritual life, generates grave covetousness through the very significance of those they seek to impress. Turning to malice, the treatise names harboring the wish to harm one's own parents, family, or teachers as grave, precisely because of who they are; harboring the wish to harm someone entirely blameless, someone already poverty-stricken, orphaned, or genuinely pitiable, who deserves compassion rather than cruelty, is likewise grave; and harboring the wish to harm someone who has sincerely come seeking refuge, or someone to whom one is personally indebted for a genuine kindness once received, is grave for the very same reason. And turning finally to wrong view, the treatise names the gravest of all wrong views as the one capable of denying every single real thing there is, cause, function, result, giving, loving care, reverent offering, and the entire moral order these depend upon, all at once; and the view that denies that any true arhat, any noble one who has genuinely reached the destination, or any noble one of upright conduct exists anywhere in the world at all is likewise grave, through the very significance of what it denies. Killing, and every karma-path following it, carried out under circumstances opposite to everything now described, is, by contrast, light.

Chinese Source

復次，若於僧祇、佛靈廟等所有財寶，起貪欲心。由事重故，名重貪欲。若於己德起增上慢，自謂智者，乃於國王、大臣、豪貴、所尊、師長及諸聰叡同梵行等，起增上欲，貪求利養，名重貪欲。復次，若於父母、眷屬、師長，起損害心。由事重故，名重瞋恚。又於無過、貧窮、孤苦、可傷愍者，起損害心。由事重故，名重瞋恚。又於誠心來歸投者及有恩所，起損害心。由事重故，名重瞋恚。復次，若於一切餘邪見中，諸有能謗一切邪見，此謗一切事門轉故，名重邪見。又若有見，謂無世間真阿羅漢、正至、正行，乃至廣說。如是邪見，由事重故，名重邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, coveting property belonging to the monastic community or a Buddhist shrine is, through the gravity of the object, called grave covetousness. Harboring an overreaching conceit about one's own virtue, considering oneself wise, and seeking recognition and support from kings, ministers, the powerful, respected teachers, and wise fellow practitioners, is called grave covetousness. Moreover, harboring the wish to harm one's parents, family, or teachers is, through the gravity of the object, called grave malice. Harboring the wish to harm one who is blameless, poor, orphaned, or pitiable is, through the gravity of the object, called grave malice. Harboring the wish to harm one who has sincerely sought refuge or one to whom a debt of kindness is owed is, through the gravity of the object, called grave malice. Moreover, among all wrong views, the view capable of denying every single real thing turns upon every matter at once, and is called grave wrong view. The view holding that there is no true arhat, no one who has reached the destination, and no one of upright conduct in the world is, through the gravity of the object, called grave wrong view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the warning against seeking recognition from kings and ministers on the false basis of an overreaching conceit as a pointed continuation of fascicle 59's own earlier caution about craving a reputation for spiritual virtue, now sharpened further by adding the specific temptation of worldly power and prestige into the mix, a combination the tradition clearly regarded as especially corrosive for a sincere practitioner's own path. He notes that with wrong view's own gravest form now named, denying every real thing at once, the treatise has completed its full accounting of gravity across all ten unwholesome karma-paths by way of the object, closing this entire stretch of ethical analysis with the same object-based principle running consistently from killing all the way through to wrong view.

7. Section: Done Without Increasing, Increased Without Being Done

With killing's own circumstances of gravity now fully closed, every case not already described being reckoned light, the treatise turns to a further distinction: the unwholesome karma driven by killing may be committed without being habitually increased, increased without ever actually being committed, both committed and increased together, or neither committed nor increased at all. Killing committed without increase includes an act carried out by a child too young to genuinely discriminate right from wrong; an act committed only in a dream, carrying little real force; an act committed thoughtlessly, without real intention; an act committed only because another compelled it against one's own wish; and an act committed once, immediately followed by forceful regret and genuine revulsion, sincere self-reproach, and the formal undertaking of the precept against killing, so that the deed's own force grows faint, and, before its karmic fruit has even ripened, followed further still by a worldly path of meditative detachment weakening the very seed behind it, and finally by a transcendent path of genuine realization severing that seed entirely, leaving nothing whatsoever remaining. Killing increased without being committed belongs instead to one who, over a long stretch of time, repeatedly turns the thought of harming another over and over in the mind, growing the very unwholesome force of killing through sheer rumination alone, even while never actually carrying the deed out. Killing both committed and increased together includes every remaining case, an act carried out and then left entirely unaddressed, with no remorse, no precept taken, and no antidote of meditation or realization ever applied to weaken it. And killing neither committed nor increased is simply everything falling outside these three. Every remaining karma-path follows this same fourfold pattern, though greed, hatred, and wrong view, being purely mental karma-paths, have no equivalent of increase without commission at all, since forming the very thought itself already constitutes the deed.

Chinese Source

復次，殺生所引不善諸業，或有是作而非增長，或有增長而非是作，或有亦作亦復增長，或有非作亦非增長。初句，謂無識別童稚所作，或夢所作，或不思而作，或自無欲他逼令作。或有暫作，續即還起猛利悔心及厭患心，懇責遠離，正受律儀，令彼微薄，未與果報，便起世間離欲之道，損彼種子；次起出世永斷之道，害彼種子令無有餘。增長而非作者，謂如有一，為害生故，於長夜中數隨尋伺。由此因緣，彼遂增長殺生所引惡不善法，然不能作殺生之業。於貪欲、瞋恚、邪見中，無有第二增長而非作句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, the unwholesome karma drawn forth by killing may be committed without increase, increased without being committed, both committed and increased, or neither committed nor increased. The first phrase refers to an act done by a discriminating-incapable child, done in a dream, done without intention, or done only because another, against one's own wish, compelled it. Or committed once, immediately followed by forceful regret and revulsion, sincere self-reproach and distancing, correctly undertaking the precept, weakening it, and before the fruit has ripened, giving rise to a worldly path of freedom from desire that damages its seed, and next a transcendent path of permanent severing that destroys that seed, leaving nothing remaining. Increased without being committed refers to one who, for the sake of harming a being, repeatedly examines and reflects on it through a long night of time; by this cause and condition, the unwholesome dharma drawn forth by killing increases, yet the karma of killing itself is never actually committed. Among greed, hatred, and wrong view, there is no second phrase of increase without commission.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the description of committing killing once and then immediately following it with forceful regret, precept-taking, and eventually meditative severing as this section's own genuinely hopeful account of what real repentance actually accomplishes, showing that a single unwholesome act, however grave in itself, need not become an ever-deepening pattern if it is met quickly and thoroughly with its own proper antidote. He highlights the exclusion of greed, hatred, and wrong view from the "increased without committed" category as a precise and important clarification: unlike killing, where thought and deed remain genuinely separable, these three purely mental karma-paths collapse thought and deed into the very same event, so that dwelling on them at length is not merely a step toward the offense but is already, in full, the offense itself.

8. Section: Three Kinds of Fruit — A World Grown Barren by Its Own Killing

The treatise turns to a further question: what fruit does each karma-path actually draw forth? Killing, when closely cultivated, habitually practiced, and repeatedly engaged in, draws forth a ripening fruit of rebirth in the hells. Should one then emerge from that hell and take rebirth here among humankind, killing's own outflow fruit, resembling its own cause, is a short and truncated lifespan, cut off before its natural end. And killing's dominant fruit shapes the very world such beings come to inhabit: the fruits, food, and medicines found there carry little vitality or luster, their own strength and potency feeble and diminished, prone to spoiling once consumed and producing bodily imbalance, so that sickness spreads freely, and countless beings, their natural lifespan not yet exhausted, die well before their time. The remaining nine karma-paths' own ripening and outflow fruits follow a similar pattern, each in turn: theft yields poverty and scarcity of resources; sexual misconduct yields an unfaithful spouse; false speech yields being widely slandered by others; divisive speech yields estrangement from one's own friends and family; harsh speech yields constantly hearing disagreeable sounds; idle chatter yields words that carry no authority and win no one's trust; and covetousness, hatred, and wrong view each yield, in turn, an ever more forceful recurrence of that very same affliction, greed compounding into greater greed, hatred into greater hatred, delusion into greater delusion, each left unchecked only deepening further with every passing life. Turning to the dominant fruit specifically, theft shapes a barren world in which fruit is scarce, fails to ripen, rots easily, tastes of nothing genuine, and where drought leaves the very orchards withered, or bearing no fruit at all; and sexual misconduct shapes a world thick with filth, foul-smelling refuse, and unclean things crowding every corner, nothing in such a place ever genuinely pleasant to encounter.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the dominant fruit's own shaping of the shared physical world, not merely an individual's own personal circumstances, as the treatise's own striking claim that karma operates collectively as well as individually, an entire environment's own barrenness, filth, or fertility ultimately traceable back to the aggregate unwholesome or wholesome conduct of the beings who come to inhabit it together. He highlights the outflow fruit's own precise, like-for-like correspondence, a habitual killer reborn with a shortened life, a repeated thief reborn into poverty, as the tradition's own consistent insistence that karma is never arbitrary, each result recognizably growing from the very same seed that produced it, however long the interval between cause and its eventual, unmistakable fruit.

9. Section: When Even the Finest Flowers Vanish From the World

The treatise completes its dominant fruit for the remaining karma-paths. False speech shapes a world where farming, seafaring, and every ordinary worldly enterprise fail to flourish, bringing little genuine advantage, rarely working out as hoped, and thick with occasions for real fear and dread. Divisive speech shapes a world of hills and ravines cutting one place off from another, treacherous, difficult terrain, thick likewise with fear and danger. Harsh speech shapes a world of bare, withered stumps, thorns, poisonous brambles, tiles, gravel, and sand, dried out and unnourished, without ponds, its rivers and springs run dry, its very soil turned salty and barren, its hills and gorges dangerous, thick with fear throughout. Idle chatter shapes a world where fruit trees bear at the wrong season or fail to bear at all, or bear fruit that looks ripe yet never truly is, rooted so shallowly that it cannot long endure, its gardens and pools unpleasant and overgrown. Covetousness shapes a world whose every flourishing thing, year by year, day by day, gradually diminishes, its every flavor only ever fading, never growing richer. Hatred shapes a world thick with plague, calamity, affliction, hostility, and terror, roamed by lions, tigers, wolves, and every manner of fierce beast, by serpents and venomous creatures, by malevolent spirits, and by violent bandits. And wrong view shapes a world where the very finest flowers and fruits vanish entirely, where impure things come to look pure and miserable things come to look pleasant, offering no genuine dwelling place, no genuine protection, and no genuine refuge at all. With this, the treatise closes its complete account of the dominant fruit belonging to every one of the ten unwholesome karma-paths.

10. Section: For Meat, For Robbery, For a False Friend's Sacrifice

The treatise turns to a new question, drawn directly from the Buddha's own teaching that killing arises in three ways, generated by greed, by hatred, and by delusion, and that this same threefold pattern holds true all the way through to wrong view as well, asking how this distinction should actually be understood. Killing generated by greed takes four forms: killing for the sake of flesh and blood to consume; killing with the prior intention of then plundering the victim's own wealth; killing instigated by another, whether hired to do it, repaying a debt of past kindness, drawn in by a friend's own influence, hoping to win friendship through the very favor of killing, driven by need for food and clothing, or simply obeying a master's command; and killing driven by gain and loss, believing another capable of causing one's own decline, or capable of obstructing one's own profit, a pattern extending as well to every one of the eight worldly winds, gain and loss, disrepute and repute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain, whenever any of these becomes the actual motive behind the deed. Killing generated by hatred follows the ninefold pattern of ill will already explained at length, directed at oneself across past, present, and future, at one's own loved ones, and at one's enemies in turn. And killing generated by delusion begins with computing killing itself as righteous conduct, as when one believes, I am this other being's true friend, and because I kill them, their body destroyed and their life ended, they will surely be reborn in the heavens — killing carried out on the strength of this very belief is generated by delusion.

He highlights the extension of greed-generated killing across the full eight worldly winds, gain, loss, disrepute, repute, praise, blame, pleasure, and pain, as the treatise's own reminder that the underlying motive behind violence is rarely a single simple craving, but is instead woven through with an entire web of ordinary human concerns about status, reputation, and comfort that a careful practitioner must learn to recognize in their own subtler forms as well.

11. Section: Abandoning the Dying, and Theft's Own Three Roots

The treatise continues delusion-generated killing: killing carried out because one's own elder or teacher commanded it, believing this alone makes it righteous conduct; killing directed at anyone who slanders the heavens, Brahmā, the lord of the world, or brahmins themselves, believing such killing is likewise sanctioned by righteous law; and encouraging another to kill by teaching them that killing generates no ripening result at all, so that the one so persuaded goes on to kill with an untroubled mind, the very teacher who persuaded them thereby incurring the offense of killing generated by delusion in their own right. A further delusion holds that placing one's own aging parents or beloved family into fire, starving them to death, casting them from a cliff, or abandoning them in the wilderness is itself righteous conduct, a practice the tradition recalls certain ancient societies actually codified into law, elderly and dying relatives cast into a pit of fire or left in open wilderness to die alone once judged too old to be of further use — the same custom, disturbingly, recorded among certain peoples even into more recent times. True filial devotion, the tradition insists, means caring for one's own parents and family through to their very last moment, never abandoning them so long as life remains, and all of these delusions together, from sacrificial killing through the abandonment of the elderly, constitute the full karma-path of killing generated by delusion. Turning to theft, killing's own remaining discussion having closed, greed generates its first form through sheer gluttonous grasping, snatching at another's wealth or food the way a starving animal devours its prey, unable to restrain the craving itself.

12. Section: Burning a Village Out of Pure Spite

Theft generated by greed continues: theft undertaken for hire, in return for a kindness already received, in hope of a kindness still to come, in obedience to a master's command for the sake of food and clothing, for the sake of being praised, or simply for the sake of one's own comfort — a pattern the tradition warns can begin innocently enough in a child's own small, uncorrected pilfering, only to deepen, unchecked, into something capable of costing that same child their life in later years. Theft generated by hatred follows instead the ninefold pattern of ill will already explained, carried out purely to torment another rather than out of any actual craving for their wealth, not even truly desiring the goods taken at all; burning down an entire village, its homes, possessions, and treasures, purely out of hatred toward its inhabitants, an act the treatise regards as at least equal to theft in its own gravity, if not graver still; and commanding another to plunder and scatter an enemy's own wealth, the one who gives such a command thereby incurring the very offense of theft generated by hatred, even without lifting a hand themselves. Theft generated by delusion includes believing that stealing to support one's own elders is itself righteous conduct; believing it righteous to confiscate the property of anyone who slanders the heavens, Brahmā, or brahmins; and believing it righteous to plunder wealth needed for ritual sacrifice, or the very implements the sacrifice requires, on the mistaken conviction that killing a sacrificial animal in this way ensures its own rebirth in the heavens.

13. Section: A Parent's Command, and the Cow-Worshippers' Origin Story

Sexual misconduct generated by greed includes acting to please a friend, obeying a master's command out of need for food and clothing, or pursuing the act as a kind of trade for one's own livelihood. Sexual misconduct generated by hatred includes carrying out the act purely in retaliation, following the ninefold pattern of ill will, without any actual desire having preceded it at all, driven only by anger at some prior offense; and, out of hatred for another, arranging for that person's own wife or concubine to be shamed by inducing someone else to violate her, an offense the instigator incurs even without acting personally, and one the treatise treats as potentially graver still than direct action. Sexual misconduct generated by delusion includes believing that one's own mother, father, or another woman commanding the act has thereby made it a source of great merit rather than a wrong requiring correction, mistaking what is genuinely improper for what is genuinely righteous purely because a trusted elder demanded it. Turning to false speech, greed generates it for the sake of profit, out of fear that another might otherwise damage one's own wealth, for the sake of praise, or for the sake of comfort; hatred generates it according to the same ninefold pattern of ill will already explained; and delusion generates it in several distinct forms, lying for the sake of an elder, or lying in service of the so-called cow-precept, a practice certain outsiders held could bring purification, tracing back to a belief that in an age of great scarcity, when the earth's own nourishing essence had vanished and beings faced starvation, the heavens transformed themselves into cattle to plow the fields and sustain humankind, and that cattle, being heaven's own emanation, are themselves pure, their very dung and urine considered sacred, certain outsiders even adopting bovine habits, grazing on grass with lowered head and closed eyes, believing this itself constitutes the very discipline capable of leading to heaven.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the cow-precept's own origin story, cattle as heaven's own compassionate emanation sent to plow the fields of a starving world, as a genuinely poignant example of how gratitude toward a real, remembered kindness can, across generations, calcify into an entire system of ritual practice detached from any of the original ethical reasoning that might have justified it. He highlights the instigator of another's sexual shaming as bearing the very same offense as the one who physically commits it, treating this consistent principle, that arranging wrongdoing carries the wrongdoing's own full weight, as one of the treatise's most frequently repeated and evidently most important safeguards against moral evasion by proxy.

14. Section: Craving an Enemy's Ruin, and Longing for Rudra's Own World

Delusion continues to generate false speech through further forms: believing that lying against ascetics or brahmins who oppose the heavens, Brahmā, or brahmins themselves is thereby righteous, since such opponents are deemed unworthy of truthful treatment; and believing, through a kind of self-deceiving concealment, that a lie is genuinely lawful and disciplined, and that even a lie capable of splitting the monastic community apart is therefore no wrongdoing at all. Divisive speech and harsh speech, the treatise notes, should be understood to arise from greed, hatred, and delusion according to this very same pattern already explained for false speech. Idle chatter generated by greed includes speaking for the sake of amusement, to display one's own cleverness, or for profit, praise, and comfort; idle chatter generated by hatred follows the ninefold pattern of ill will; and idle chatter generated by delusion includes the mistaken belief that such speech genuinely serves truth, stability, liberation, or the pursuit of the Dharma itself. Turning to covetousness, greed generates it toward the wealth of one who is not even an enemy, first taking careful note of what they possess and then craving that it become one's own; hatred generates it instead toward an enemy's own wealth specifically, not because that wealth is genuinely desirable in itself, but purely through the force of the nine grounds of ill will, wishing everything the enemy possesses might somehow belong to oneself instead, simply to deny them any advantage; and delusion generates covetousness through longing for rebirth in the exalted worlds of Rudra, of Viṣṇu, of Indra, or of the lord of the Brahmā realm, believing that fixing the mind upon such a rebirth and dwelling there in thought secures great blessing and protection, even though this very longing remains, in the end, still entirely bound within the cycle of birth and death.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats hatred-generated covetousness, craving an enemy's wealth not because it is genuinely wanted but purely to deny them its benefit, as this section's own sharpest illustration of how thoroughly ill will can distort even so ordinary a craving as wanting money, the wealth itself becoming almost incidental to the real underlying wish, which is simply that the other person suffer some loss. He highlights delusion-generated covetousness, longing for rebirth among the exalted gods, as a fitting closing example for this entire threefold analysis, since it shows that even a desire aimed at something conventionally regarded as sacred or elevated remains, examined honestly, still just another form of craving, still fully bound within the very cycle of birth and death it might have seemed, on the surface, to transcend.

15. Section: All Ten Roots Now Traced — Which Poison Actually Completes Each Path

Malice generated by greed arises when harming another actually serves one's own profit, reputation, praise, or comfort, without any genuine hatred toward that being at all, malice here simply being craving's own instrument. Malice generated by hatred follows the ninefold pattern of ill will already explained in full. And malice generated by delusion belongs to certain ascetics and brahmins, whether within this teaching or among outside traditions, who despise anyone holding a different view, harboring the wish to harm fellow ascetics or brahmins purely on account of that difference. Turning finally to wrong view, greed generates it when one who denies the existence of giving and its fruit does so specifically to justify seeking great offerings and clothing from kings and the wealthy, the wrong view itself functioning as a rationalization for craving; hatred generates it when one who resents those holding the correct view that giving and loving care do bring real results, refusing to share any view with a rival, therefore deliberately adopts the opposite, denying position purely out of spite; and delusion generates it through simple unreasoned thinking, faulty reflection, and mistaken examination of reality in its own right, without any further craving or resentment driving it at all. With every one of the ten unwholesome karma-paths now traced back to its own threefold root in greed, hatred, and delusion, the treatise turns to a further decisive discernment in eight parts, the first asking which affliction actually completes each karma-path, even though all three may serve as its method: killing, harsh speech, and malice are each completed specifically by hatred; theft, sexual misconduct, and covetousness are each completed specifically by greed; false speech and divisive speech may each be completed equally by any of the three; and wrong view is completed specifically by delusion.

He highlights greed-generated wrong view, denying karmic fruit specifically to justify seeking royal patronage, as this whole extensive analysis's own final, sobering demonstration that even a belief about ultimate reality can be quietly manufactured to serve an entirely mundane craving underneath it.

16. Section: Four Groundings for Ten Karma-Paths

The treatise turns to its second decisive discernment, the basis or locus each karma-path actually depends upon. Killing, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, and malice, six karma-paths in all, arise specifically with respect to other sentient beings, requiring some other being as their very object. Taking what is not given and covetousness arise instead with respect to wealth and material possessions. Idle chatter arises with respect to the name-body, the inner realm of language and conceptual proliferation from which meaningless speech is drawn. And wrong view arises with respect to conditioned formations themselves, the five aggregates and the entire fabricated world these formations comprise, since it is precisely this very reality that wrong view goes on to deny. The treatise then turns to a third decisive discernment, asking through what three further conditions and causes an unwholesome karma-path becomes so fully, thoroughly evil in its own unwholesome nature that it inevitably draws forth a ripening result in the evil destinies to come.

He notes that wrong view's own locus, conditioned formations themselves, fittingly closes the list, since it names not some particular being or possession but reality as a whole, the very ground every other karma-path already depends upon without exception.

17. Section: A Victim's Own Suffering Measures the Crime

The treatise answers its own question: three causes and conditions make an unwholesome karma-path fully complete in its evil, unwholesome nature. The intrinsic-nature fault, the volition drawn forth by killing and, in turn, by every one of the remaining nine karma-paths down through wrong view, is itself, in its own essential character, defiled and unwholesome, and it is precisely this intrinsic fault that first earns the name evil. The causal-condition fault arises when this same volition is driven by forceful, intense greed, hatred, or delusion, and it is this forceful driving condition that turns the deed into a truly grave, superior grade of unwholesome action, capable of drawing forth a genuinely undesirable result. And the contamination fault arises once the deed has actually reached its own completion, and here the treatise offers a striking clarification: the true measure of this fault is not whether the victim ever curses the perpetrator or wishes some terrible fate upon them, but simply the actual, felt suffering that victim genuinely experiences, since a defiled mind capable of inflicting real unwanted anguish upon another thereby draws forth a vast offense purely through the force of that suffering itself, entirely apart from whatever the victim does or does not think or wish in return — the size of the crime measured, in the end, by the size of the pain actually caused, and by nothing else besides. The treatise illustrates this natural, automatic quality of contamination through two vivid images: a lodestone need not consciously will that iron come and cling to it, and yet whatever iron lies nearby naturally clings regardless, simply by virtue of the stone's own inherent nature; and a sun-crystal need not consciously will that fire spring forth from it, and yet fire naturally springs forth the instant sunlight touches it, again simply by virtue of the crystal's own inherent nature — offense clings to a defiled deed in precisely the same natural, unwilled way.

He highlights the lodestone and sun-crystal images together as an unusually elegant way of expressing something otherwise difficult to state plainly, that unwholesome consequence is not a matter of divine bookkeeping applied from outside, but something built directly into the very nature of a defiled act, arising as naturally and inevitably as iron drawn to a magnet or fire drawn from crystal by sunlight.

18. Section: Māra's Corruption, and the Fire Already Latent in Every Deed

The treatise offers two further analogies. Just as the four great elements' own inherent force gives rise to solidity, wetness, warmth, and motion, these very qualities never existing as something separate from the elements themselves, but simply being what the elements' own force naturally transforms into, so too does an unwholesome deed's own transformation into a seed capable of drawing an evil result occur through nothing other than volition's own inherent force, exactly as a practitioner's own accumulated power of meditative accomplishment transforms, through its own inherent force, into visible wonders. Even Māra, the tradition recalls, corrupted the hearts of countless brahmin elders and householders against the Blessed One not by implanting anything genuinely new within their minds at all, but purely through his own scheming power, which simply triggered the very seeds of affliction already dormant within them, turning their hearts hostile without adding a single foreign element beyond what was already latently there. In just this same way, the treatise concludes, an unwholesome deed's own contamination is nothing added from outside, but simply the natural unfolding of a defiled volition's own inherent power, already carrying within it, from the very moment of its own completion, everything needed to eventually ripen into its own evil-destiny result. With its full account of what makes an unwholesome karma-path extremely evil now complete, the treatise turns to a further decisive discernment: the distinction between karma whose result is certain to be experienced and karma whose result remains uncertain.

19. Section: Stealing Then Killing — One Act or Two?

The treatise closes its account of certain and uncertain karma: karma satisfying the five factors already explained, motivation, method, absence of antidote, wrong clinging, and object, becomes extremely grave and is called certain karma, definitely to be experienced; karma opposite to this is called uncertain karma. Karma may further be classified into four kinds: certain in its ripening result alone, certain in its timing alone, certain in both, or uncertain in both. Even an arhat may still carry certain, definitely-to-be-experienced unwholesome karma from a past life or from their own earlier, unawakened days in this very life, yet since no further nourishing condition remains to feed such karma once affliction has been permanently severed, its result, when it does ripen, arrives only lightly, a grave offense borne as no more than a minor discomfort, this same karma finally and permanently ceasing to ripen at all once the transformed basis of full buddhahood is achieved, its every seed exhausted for good. Turning to a further question: if someone, in one continuous motion, steals a creature and then kills it, should this be counted as a single unwholesome act or as two? The answer is two, even though the speed of the whole sequence creates the mistaken impression, an overreaching conceit born of sheer rapidity, that only one act has occurred. The very moment one forms the thought, I shall take this creature, a first volition of theft has already arisen; and the very moment one then forms the thought, I shall now kill it, a second, entirely distinct volition of killing arises in turn — the mind, moving too quickly to notice its own two separate turnings, mistakes what are genuinely two complete unwholesome acts for merely one.

20. Section: Five Marks of a Field Vast Enough to Ripen Karma in This Very Life

The treatise turns to a further decisive discernment: what makes karma ripen immediately, within this very life, rather than only in some life to come. In brief, three causes and conditions produce such immediately-ripening karma: a vast field, a vast intention, and unbroken purity. A field becomes vast through five distinguishing marks. First, one who abides in the absorption of loving-kindness, having risen from a powerful, superior aspiration to bring the very finest benefit and ease to every being without exception; offering to one who has just emerged from such an absorption is, in effect, offering to every being at once, since that very absorption's own object was every being at once. Second, one who abides in the absorption free of contention, guarding every being's own mind so carefully that no affliction is ever stirred up in them, as the venerable Subhūti was famed for cultivating so thoroughly that, foreseeing any encounter likely to provoke another's own affliction, he would simply decline to leave his dwelling that day at all. Third, one who has just emerged from the attainment of cessation, a state closely resembling nirvana's own still peace, reached only by noble ones from the third fruit onward. Fourth, a stream-enterer, already possessing the fourfold unbreakable faith and the very discipline of never again committing any unwholesome deed, permanently protected from ever falling into the evil destinies again. And fifth, an arhat, a Buddha, or the great community of monks headed by the Buddha himself, whose own continuity has reached the most thoroughly purified completion possible. Offerings made to any of these five kinds of field, the treatise explains, are vast enough in themselves to ripen their own karmic result within this very life.

21. Section: A Weak Thread, a Trickle of Water, a Single Drop of Vinegar

Vast intention, the treatise explains, is giving with a deep, earnest, wholly sincere heart toward beings who are suffering, impoverished, and without any refuge or protector, offering wealth that was itself cleanly and lawfully obtained. Unbroken purity is never having obstructed another's own giving in a past life, neither in body, speech, nor mind, and never having harbored a defiled thought of attachment toward what was given, thereby leaving one's own continuity free, in this present life, from the four obstructions that otherwise hinder giving: unfamiliarity, never having practiced giving before and so finding it difficult now; insufficiency, having too little to give in the first place; attachment to some especially fine or pleasing possession one cannot bear to part with; and giving only in eager anticipation of a future reward of wealth, a hope-driven generosity that is itself already a subtle obstruction. When all three conditions, a vast field, a vast intention, and unbroken purity, are genuinely present together, the resulting wholesome karma is certain to be experienced not only in this very life, but in the next life, and in lives further still beyond that. And precisely the same threefold pattern applies equally to unwholesome karma: harm done against an equally vast field, driven by an equally forceful and deliberate mind, and carried out with an equally unbroken pattern of prior similar conduct, likewise becomes certain to ripen immediately, within this very life. The treatise illustrates the varying reach of a single moment's karma across the three times through three vivid images: a weak thread able to hold only a single flower before its strength gives out corresponds to karma ripening only in this present life; a somewhat stronger thread able to hold two flowers corresponds to karma ripening in this life and the next; and a genuinely strong thread able to hold many flowers before finally giving way corresponds to karma ripening across this life, the next, and lives further still.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the naming of hope-driven generosity itself as a subtle obstruction to unbroken purity as a genuinely searching standard, since it suggests that even outwardly generous giving can remain quietly incomplete if performed with one eye already on its own future payoff, true unbroken purity requiring a giving mind entirely undistracted by what it might itself receive in return. He highlights the three graduated images, thread, water, and vinegar, as the treatise's own way of making an abstract question, how far does a single act's own consequence actually reach across time, vivid and intuitively graspable, karma's own strength, like a thread's own tensile limit, simply running out at different points depending on how forcefully and how purely it was originally generated.

22. Section: How Bodhisattvas Sever Unwholesome Karma Without Severing Affliction

The treatise turns to a further decisive discernment: bondage and freedom from bondage. All ten unwholesome karma-paths are bound solely to the desire realm, and can ripen only into desire-realm results, falling far more often into the evil destinies than into any wholesome one. Karma bound for the evil destinies is permanently and entirely severed the moment the fruit of stream-entry is attained; ordinary beings who merely achieve worldly freedom from desire through meditation, or who are reborn into the higher realms on that same strength, have only suppressed such karma temporarily, never yet severing it for good. Karma still bound to the desire realm itself is permanently severed by a non-returner, whether still dwelling here or already reborn into the pure abodes above, and by an arhat, every remaining unwholesome karma entirely exhausted. And a bodhisattva who has already entered the ground of genuinely pure, superior aspiration likewise permanently severs every unwholesome karma-path entirely, though through an entirely different mechanism from the hearers' own path: not through severing the underlying affliction-seed itself, which such a bodhisattva may still deliberately retain in subtle form precisely in order to keep taking rebirth for the sake of guiding other beings, as when a bodhisattva of the very first ground vows to become a wheel-turning monarch within the desire realm itself, but purely through the sustained power of never losing mindfulness, a vigilance so thoroughly maintained that discrimination-arisen unwholesome conduct simply never has the opportunity to arise again at all.

If ordinary beings become worldly free of desire, or are reborn into the higher realms, all such karma is only suppressed, not yet permanently severed. If a bodhisattva has already entered the ground of pure, superior aspiration, all unwholesome karma is likewise permanently severed. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this distinction between the hearer's path, severing unwholesome karma by uprooting affliction itself, and the bodhisattva's path, severing the very same karma through sustained mindfulness alone while deliberately retaining subtle affliction, as one of the treatise's own clearest articulations of what actually separates the two vehicles at a structural level, not a difference in how thoroughly each frees a being from wrongdoing, since both genuinely succeed, but a difference in why each does so, the hearer aiming at personal liberation from affliction itself, the bodhisattva aiming instead at remaining connected enough to ordinary beings, through retained affliction, to keep taking rebirth among them for their own sake. He highlights the first-ground bodhisattva's own vow to become a wheel-turning king within the desire realm as a vivid, concrete image of this very principle in action, spiritual attainment here expressed not as an escape from worldly rebirth but as a freely chosen return to it.

23. Section: Karma Defilement's Own Closing Verse — And Rebirth Defilement Opens

The treatise closes its distinction between karma and karma-path: killing through idle chatter, the seven bodily and verbal deeds, are each both karma and karma-path at once, since volition itself, expressed through body and speech, is genuinely karma, while its own unwholesome character, capable of drawing forth a ripened result, makes it equally a karma-path. Greed, hatred, and wrong view, by contrast, are karma-path but not karma in their own right, since, on the hearers' own account, karma proper belongs to a subtle form generated by bodily and verbal action, while these three purely mental states remain simply the affliction accompanying volition rather than volition's own active expression, even though the Great Vehicle's own account treats the matter somewhat differently, volition itself being a mental dharma throughout. With this, the treatise notes that no further elaboration on these karma-paths will appear, and offers its own closing verse, gathering together the seven gates by which karma defilement has now been fully examined: its own self-nature, in brief and in full detail; its method; its gravity; its increase or lack of increase, and its association with the very afflictions that generate it; the fruit it draws forth; and the decisive discernment gathering these threads together in eight further parts. With this verse, the deciding discernment of karma defilement has now been fully explained. The deciding discernment of rebirth defilement, the third and final kind of defilement framing this entire stretch of the treatise, shall now be explained in turn, beginning with eleven kinds of existence in brief: an exclusively pleasant existence, belonging to a portion of the gods, those dwelling in the first three meditative absorptions; an exclusively painful existence, belonging to the beings of hell; a mixed existence of both pleasure and pain, belonging to a portion of the gods, humans, spirits, and animals, the six heavens of the desire realm, some humans, some spirits, and even certain fortunate animals, pampered household pets dressed and groomed and housed as though royalty, or celebrated creatures kept in vast, climate-controlled enclosures with their own attendants; and an existence neither painful nor pleasant, belonging to a portion of the gods dwelling in the fourth meditative absorption and the four formless attainments beyond it.

24. Section: Ten Kinds of Mastery, and a Bodhisattva's Journey Through Every Heaven

The treatise continues its eleven kinds of existence: an exclusively impure existence belongs to ordinary beings of the desire realm, their own dwelling place and their own body and mind alike unclean; an exclusively pure existence belongs to a bodhisattva who has already attained mastery, one who, effortlessly and without any need for deliberate striving, abides in a signless state and has thereby attained ten kinds of mastery, over life, over mind, over possessions, over karma, over rebirth, over aspiration, over resolve, over miraculous power, over the Dharma, and over wisdom, taking rebirth for the most part as the great Brahmā king himself, in a place and body both genuinely pure. Such bodhisattvas, ground by ground, take rebirth throughout the very heavens and worlds they mean to guide: a bodhisattva of the first ground vows for the most part to become a wheel-turning monarch ruling a single continent; of the second ground, a golden-wheeled monarch ruling all four continents; of the third ground, though capable of the form realm's own higher rebirth, chooses instead to be reborn in the desire realm by the very force of their own vow; of the fourth ground, the king of the Yāma heaven; of the fifth, the king of Tuṣita heaven; of the sixth, the king of the heaven of transformed delights; of the seventh, the king of the heaven of mastery over others' transformations; of the eighth, a great Brahmā king ruling a thousand world-systems; of the ninth, a great Brahmā king ruling two great-thousand world-systems; and of the tenth ground, Maheśvara himself, sovereign of the very highest of the pure abodes, where this treatise itself records that a tenth-ground bodhisattva, for the most part, finally attains complete buddhahood. Pure-and-impure existence belongs instead to ordinary beings of the form and formless realms, their own dwelling place genuinely pure even while their own minds remain unpurified.
