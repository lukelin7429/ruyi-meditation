Matters of Monastic Discipline Begins

Fascicle 99 opens an entirely new great division: matters of monastic discipline (調伏事), the treatise's second great topic within the embrace of the bases (攝事分), following directly after matters of the sūtras (素怛纜事), which occupied fascicles 85 through 98 in their entirety. Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture transcript notes that fascicles 99 through the first half of 100 explain matters of discipline, and the remainder of fascicle 100 explains matters of the foundational treatises (摩怛履迦事) — together, these three (sūtra, discipline, and foundational treatise) embrace the whole of the Buddha's teaching. This fascicle opens with the summary verse naming nine matters — benefit, classes of offense, embrace, conduct, contrary and accordant practice, tranquility, thorough knowledge, faith and its absence, and the powers and what follows — and closes the first two of these nine entire: the ten benefits of the precepts, and the five classes of offense together with their fifteen underlying faults. It continues into the third matter, the fivefold embrace of the vinaya through its own nature, prohibition, restriction, and allowance.

1. Section: Matters of Monastic Discipline Begins — The Treatise's Second Great Division

Within the embrace of the bases, the general survey embrace of matters of monastic discipline, the first part.

Chinese Source

攝事分中調伏事總擇攝第五之一

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the embrace of the bases, the general survey embrace of matters of monastic discipline, the first part.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This title marks the opening of **matters of monastic discipline (調伏事)**, the second of the embrace of the bases' three great topics. The embrace of the bases spans fascicles 85 through 100 — sixteen fascicles altogether — explaining sūtra matters (素怛纜事), discipline matters (毗奈耶事), and foundational-treatise matters (摩怛履迦事), which together embrace the whole of the Buddha's teaching. Fascicles 85 through 98 explained sūtra matters in their entirety (now closed, per fascicle 98's own completion); fascicles 99 through the first half of 100 explain discipline matters; the remainder of fascicle 100 explains foundational-treatise matters. Discipline (毗奈耶) carries the sense of taming — through the practice of the precepts, unwholesome states are removed and the mind is tamed; discipline matters chiefly explain the substance of the precepts, which the treatise elsewhere organizes under five kinds of establishment: establishing the training, establishing transgression, establishing renunciation, establishing cessation, and establishing formal proceedings. This general survey embrace gathers together the essential principles bound up with the precepts as a whole, to be considered and resolved in turn.

如是已說素怛纜事摩怛理迦。云何名為毗奈耶事摩怛理迦？

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus has sūtra matters' foundational treatise already been explained. What is called discipline matters' foundational treatise?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens discipline matters' own foundational treatise (毗奈耶事摩怛理迦4), the second of four sections explaining discipline matters, beginning with concluding what came before and giving rise to what follows (戊一結前生後) — as fascicles 85 through 98 have already explained sūtra matters by way of treatise-discourse, this now asks what it means to discuss the matters belonging to discipline in the same way.

3. Section: Explaining How This Name Is Obtained

It means that, transmitted onward from these four sūtra-external individual-liberation sūtras, all their broadly stated foundational treatise, spoken by the Tathāgata, manifested by the Tathāgata, praised by the Tathāgata — this is called discipline's foundational treatise.

Chinese Source

謂即從此四種經外別解脫經，所有廣說摩怛理迦展轉傳來，如來所說、如來所顯、如來所讚，名毗奈耶摩怛理迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

It means that, transmitted onward from these four sūtra-external individual-liberation sūtras, all their broadly stated foundational treatise, spoken by the Tathāgata, manifested by the Tathāgata, praised by the Tathāgata — this is called discipline's foundational treatise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

This opens explaining how this name is obtained (戊二釋其得名) — beyond the four āgamas already discussed as sūtra matters (the mixed, middle, long, and increasing-by-one āgamas), the individual-liberation sūtras further expound the principle of discipline, and this, together with all the treatise-discourse found throughout the broad vinaya concerning the precepts, transmitted onward, spoken and manifested and praised by the Buddha himself, is called discipline's foundational treatise.

4. Section: Briefly Explaining the General Marks — The Summary Verse Marks and Lists

This general mark of discipline's foundational treatise, in a small part, I shall now explain. A summary verse says: benefit, classes, embrace, conduct in accordance; contrary and accordant, able to still; thorough knowledge, faith and its absence; the powers and the rest, coming after.

Chinese Source

此毗奈耶摩怛理迦總相少分，我今當說。嗢柁南曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

This general mark of discipline's foundational treatise, in a small part, I shall now explain. A summary verse says:

Chinese Source

利聚攝隨行　逆順能寂靜　遍知信不信　力等為其後

Ru-Yi Translation

Benefit, classes, embrace, conduct in accordance; contrary and accordant, able to still; thorough knowledge, faith and its absence; the powers and the rest, coming after.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens briefly explaining the general marks (戊三略說總相2), the third of discipline matters' foundational treatise's four sections, beginning with the summary verse's own marking and listing (己一嗢柁南標) — nine matters unfold in the long-form explanation to come: (1) benefit, the ten benefits of the precepts; (2) classes, the five classes of offense, commonly called the five categories; (3) embrace, the fivefold embrace of discipline through its own nature, prohibition, restriction, allowance, and conduct; (4) conduct in accordance, how one conducts oneself day to day in accordance with the precepts — inward intention, dwelling place, requisites relied upon, and how monastic business is formally conducted; (5) contrary and accordant, practice that accords with or runs contrary to the noble path's own cultivation, explaining in particular what counts as a semblance of the true dharma; (6) able to still, how the great assembly stills its afflictions and dwells in harmony without dispute, and how disputes, once arisen, are extinguished; (7) thorough knowledge, thoroughly knowing offense and non-offense, and, where there is offense, its gravity, the means of repentance, and the raising and troubling of charges; (8) faith and its absence, how a monastic's own conduct can give rise to faith in those without it, or to its opposite; (9) the powers and the rest, the five powers of one cultivating the noble path — the power of preparatory practice, of resolve, of clear exposition, of right wisdom, and of uprightness — together with two further points, the five kinds of persons and the wayward practice of the threefold training in precepts, samādhi, and wisdom. The Notes on the Yogācārabhūmi divide this into eleven gates, but the layman Hán Qīngjìng divides it into nine, folding "the powers and the rest" three-fold into a single gate — the nine-gate division this fascicle follows.

5. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — Benefit Begins — Pointing to the Prior Mark

The Tathāgata, observing ten kinds of supreme benefit, established the disciples' training-grounds within discipline. Namely: gathering in the saṅgha, causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest, and so on, broadly stated — this should be known in its marks as in the embrace of the exegesis.

Chinese Source

如來觀見十種勝利，於毗奈耶中，為諸弟子制立學處。謂攝受僧伽，令僧精懇，乃至廣說。如攝釋分應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata, observing ten kinds of supreme benefit, established the disciples' training-grounds within discipline. Namely: gathering in the saṅgha, causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest, and so on, broadly stated — this should be known in its marks as in the embrace of the exegesis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the embrace of the exegesis, fascicle 82, and the 披尋記

This opens the long-form explanation (己二長行釋9), a ninefold structure matching the verse's nine matters, beginning with benefit (庚一利2), named by the verse's own first word, itself divided in two, beginning with pointing to the prior mark (辛一指前相) — the ten supreme benefits the Buddha saw in establishing the training-grounds are: gathering in the saṅgha; causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest, free of attachment to pleasure; causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease, free of extreme asceticism; causing those without pure faith to give rise to it; causing those with pure faith to increase it; taming those hard to tame; causing those with a sense of shame to dwell at ease; guarding against outflows in the present life; destroying outflows in future lives; and causing the true dharma to abide long in the world — all previously set out in full in the embrace of the exegesis, fascicle 82.

6. Section: Explaining the Distinctions Begins — Briefly Embracing in Phrases Begins — Gathering in the Saṅgha

If one is able to gather in those of the four great lineages and the rest who, with pure faith, go forth from the household into homelessness, one should know this is called gathering in the saṅgha.

Chinese Source

若能攝受四大姓等正信出家趣非家眾，當知說名攝受僧伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one is able to gather in those of the four great lineages and the rest who, with pure faith, go forth from the household into homelessness, one should know this is called gathering in the saṅgha.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

This opens explaining the distinctions (辛二釋差別2), the second of benefit's two sections, beginning with briefly embracing in phrases (壬一略攝句4) — the ten benefits are here gathered into four: gathering in the saṅgha, causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest, causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease, and causing the true dharma to abide long — beginning with gathering in the saṅgha (癸一) — the precepts allow anyone of pure faith, of any of the four great lineages (the ruling warrior class, the priestly brāhmaṇa class, the merchant class, and the servant class) or any other kind of being, to go forth equally, without discrimination by caste.

7. Section: Causing the Saṅgha to Be Diligent and Earnest Begins — Marking What Is Said

Having thus gone forth from the household into homelessness, when it is explained to them that there is cause and condition, there is renunciation, there is what is relied upon, there is valor, there are miraculous transformations, and other profound teachings — one should know this is called causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest.

Chinese Source

如是出家趣非家已，為其宣說有因緣、有出離、有所依、有勇猛、有神變等甚深法教，當知說名令僧精懇。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus gone forth from the household into homelessness, when it is explained to them that there is cause and condition, there is renunciation, there is what is relied upon, there is valor, there are miraculous transformations, and other profound teachings — one should know this is called causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest (癸二令僧精懇2), beginning with marking what is said (子一標說) — five deep teachings are marked: cause and condition, for the Buddha establishes the precepts only when occasion truly calls for it; renunciation, meaning either never having transgressed since ordination, or having transgressed and then repented to purity; what is relied upon, the robes, food, bedding, and medicine on which monastic life depends, to be used with contentment regardless of quality; valor, the courage to press forward toward the path without retreating; and miraculous transformations, the three kinds of wonder-working (of mind-reading, of psychic power, and of instruction) by which the Buddha and the arhats teach most fittingly.

8. Section: Pointing to the Explanation, Closing "Causing the Saṅgha to Be Diligent and Earnest" Entirely

The distinctions among these phrases — there is cause and condition, and the rest — should be known in their marks as already discussed in the bodhisattva grounds.

Chinese Source

有因緣等諸句差別，如菩薩地已辯其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The distinctions among these phrases — there is cause and condition, and the rest — should be known in their marks as already discussed in the bodhisattva grounds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the bodhisattva grounds, fascicle 43, and the 披尋記 at length

With this, pointing to the explanation (子二指釋) closes entirely, closing **causing the saṅgha to be diligent and earnest (癸二令僧精懇2: 標說/指釋) entirely** — the bodhisattva grounds explain each of the five terms in turn: a teaching is said to have cause and condition when it is established as a training-ground in response to genuine occasion; it is said to have renunciation when, for one who has transgressed what was undertaken, a means of return to purity is provided; it is said to have what is relied upon when it establishes undistorted dharma-and-discipline conduct embraced by the four reliances; it is said to have valor (勇決 — "valiant resolve," glossing 勇猛) when it rightly shows the path of never turning back from all suffering; and it is said to have miraculous transformation when, accompanied by the three wonders, nothing it teaches is ever spoken in vain. Master Cháng Bǎi surveys several traditional glosses of "the four reliances" here — as the four requisites of monastic life (per the 披尋記); as the four noble lineages of contentment (per the Notes on the Yogācārabhūmi and Master Kuījī — begging for food, wearing discarded-cloth robes, sitting beneath trees, delighting in cutting off affliction and in cultivation); and, differently again, per Master Shéntài, as relying on the dharma rather than the person, on the meaning rather than the words, on wisdom rather than consciousness, and on the definitive teaching rather than the provisional.

9. Section: Causing the Saṅgha to Dwell at Ease Begins — Marking and Listing

By five marks, one should know this is called causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease. First, causing the requisites that accord with the path to be without lack; second, causing one of a divergent teaching to be expelled; third, causing what wrongdoing has arisen to be well removed; fourth, causing every entangling affliction to be well subdued; fifth, causing the underlying tendencies of affliction to be well permanently extinguished.

Chinese Source

由五種相，應知說名令僧安樂。一者、令順道具無所匱乏；二者、令擯異法補特伽羅；三者、令善除遣所生惡作；四者、令善降伏諸煩惱纏；五者、令善永滅隨眠煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

By five marks, one should know this is called causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease. First, causing the requisites that accord with the path to be without lack; second, causing one of a divergent teaching to be expelled; third, causing what wrongdoing has arisen to be well removed; fourth, causing every entangling affliction to be well subdued; fifth, causing the underlying tendencies of affliction to be well permanently extinguished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease (癸三令僧安樂2), beginning with marking and listing (子一標列) — of the ten original benefits, the third itself absorbs the sixth through ninth (taming those hard to tame; causing those with shame to dwell at ease; guarding against present outflows; destroying future outflows), so five marks together constitute causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease.

10. Section: Correlating and Explaining Begins — Departing from the Extreme of Self-Affliction

One should know that, here, by the power of the first ease, those without pure faith are caused to give rise to pure faith, and those with pure faith are caused to increase it.

Chinese Source

應知此中，最初安樂增上力故，未淨信者令生淨信，已淨信者令其增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, here, by the power of the first ease, those without pure faith are caused to give rise to pure faith, and those with pure faith are caused to increase it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens correlating and explaining (子二配釋5), the second of causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease's two sections, matching each of the five marks back to the original ten benefits, beginning with departing from the extreme of self-affliction (丑一離自苦邊) — the first ease, requisites without lack, embraces two of the original benefits at once: causing faithless newcomers to find faith and enter the teaching, and causing the already-faithful to deepen their practice and advance toward realization.

11. Section: Taming What Is Hard to Tame

By the power of the second ease, ill-natured persons are subdued and gathered in.

Chinese Source

第二安樂增上力故，調攝鄙惡補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the power of the second ease, ill-natured persons are subdued and gathered in.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens taming what is hard to tame (丑二調伏難伏) — the second ease, expelling one of a divergent teaching, corresponds to appropriate remedy for the offending, gauged by the gravity of the transgression: a mild fault reproved gently, a middling fault more sharply, a grave fault with severe rebuke, and, where warranted, temporary or final expulsion — the vinaya's own graduated response to disciples unwilling to honor their teachers or wary companions.

12. Section: Shame and Freedom from Regret

By the power of the third ease, those with a sense of shame are caused to dwell at ease.

Chinese Source

第三安樂增上力故，令慚愧者得安樂住。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the power of the third ease, those with a sense of shame are caused to dwell at ease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens shame and freedom from regret (丑三慚愧無悔) — the third ease corresponds to removing what wrongdoing has arisen: one who feels shame delights in keeping the precepts purely, and on any lapse repents at once, thereby dwelling at ease without lingering regret.

13. Section: Guarding Against Outflows in the Present Dharma

By the power of the fourth ease, the present dharma's outflows are well guarded against.

Chinese Source

第四安樂增上力故，令善防護現法諸漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the power of the fourth ease, the present dharma's outflows are well guarded against.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens guarding against outflows in the present dharma (丑四防現法漏) — the fourth ease corresponds to subduing every entangling affliction: purity of precepts brings an ease of mind capable of subduing affliction's own present activity, guarding one against attachment and aversion arising from form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness here and now.

14. Section: Harming Outflows in the Future Dharma, Closing "Correlating and Explaining" and "Causing the Saṅgha to Dwell at Ease" Entirely

By the power of the fifth ease, one is able to permanently extinguish every future outflow.

Chinese Source

第五安樂增上力故，能令永滅當來諸漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the power of the fifth ease, one is able to permanently extinguish every future outflow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, harming outflows in the future dharma (丑五害後法漏) closes entirely, closing **correlating and explaining (子二配釋5: 離自苦邊/調伏難伏/慚愧無悔/防現法漏/害後法漏) entirely**, closing **causing the saṅgha to dwell at ease (癸三令僧安樂2: 標列/配釋) entirely** — the fifth ease corresponds to permanently extinguishing the underlying tendencies of affliction: relying on the precepts, dwelling harmoniously through the six points of reverent concord, body and mind at ease, one gains a power strong enough to cultivate calm-abiding and clear-seeing to the point of permanently severing the seeds of craving and views across the three realms, ending saṃsāra's own round.

15. Section: Wishing the Many to Abide Long in the Holy Life, and So On, Broadly Stated, Closing "Briefly Embracing in Phrases" Entirely

Having thus attained dwelling at ease, in order that those not yet entered may enter with ease, wishing the many to abide long in the holy life, and so on, broadly stated — all this should be known.

Chinese Source

如是獲得安樂住已，未得入者令易入故，欲令多人梵行久住，乃至廣說，皆應了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus attained dwelling at ease, in order that those not yet entered may enter with ease, wishing the many to abide long in the holy life, and so on, broadly stated — all this should be known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Four-Part Vinaya and the Ten Recitations Vinaya at length

With this, wishing the many to abide long in the holy life and so on (癸四) closes entirely, closing **briefly embracing in phrases (壬一略攝句4: 攝受僧伽/令僧精懇/令僧安樂/欲令多人梵行久住) entirely** — the tenth benefit, causing the true dharma to abide long, follows naturally once the saṅgha dwells at ease: those not yet within the teaching find it easier to enter, and the holy life abides long in the world. Master Cháng Bǎi traces this to the Four-Part Vinaya's five causes of the true dharma's swift decline (reciting the vinaya carelessly and teaching it incomplete; senior monastics themselves failing to keep the precepts, setting a bad example; the learned dying without having taught others; those hard to instruct being abandoned rather than corrected; and mutual quarreling over faults) and the Ten Recitations Vinaya's ten further causes (disciples of dull faculties and little zeal who recite without truly receiving the teaching; failure to understand the text; conduct that fails to inspire reverence; teaching by contentious wrangling rather than in accordance with the dharma; distaste for solitude; disregard for the teaching; impatience; disrespect toward elders; discouraging later generations from learning the precepts; and heedlessness driven by the pursuit of material gain) — this is why the vinaya's own survival in the world is what allows the holy life to abide long.

又此一切以要言之，謂正顯示最初攝受、次正攝受；既攝受已，令安樂住。及顯未來未攝受者易入方便。如是名為第二差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, to state all of this in essence: it rightly shows the very first gathering-in, then the proper gathering-in; having been gathered in, one is caused to dwell at ease. And it shows, for those not yet gathered in the future, the easy means of entry. This is called the second distinction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, raising the essential statement (壬二舉要言) closes entirely, closing **explaining the distinctions (辛二釋差別2: 略攝句/舉要言) entirely**, closing **benefit (庚一利2: 指前相/釋差別), the first of the long-form explanation's nine matters, entirely** — the ten benefits reduce, in essence, to four moments: first gathering in through going forth, proper gathering-in through diligent training, dwelling at ease through the precepts' own protection, and, finally, an easy door left open for whoever has not yet entered.

17. Section: Classes Begins — Setting Forth the Classes of Offense Begins — Briefly Marking and Listing

Further, one should know there are, in brief, five classes of offense that embrace every offense. What are the five? First, the class of defeat; second, the class of the remainder; third, the class of downfall; fourth, the class of individual confession; fifth, the class of wrongdoing.

Chinese Source

復次，應知略有五種罪聚攝一切罪。何等為五？一者、他勝罪聚，二者、眾餘罪聚，三者、隕墜罪聚，四者、別悔罪聚，五者、惡作罪聚。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, one should know there are, in brief, five classes of offense that embrace every offense. What are the five? First, the class of defeat; second, the class of the remainder; third, the class of downfall; fourth, the class of individual confession; fifth, the class of wrongdoing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Sarvāstivāda Treatise, and the 披尋記

This opens **classes (庚二聚2), the second of the nine matters**, named by the verse's second word, beginning with setting forth the classes of offense (辛一出罪聚3), beginning with briefly marking and listing (壬一略標列) — the class of defeat (parājika, the root offenses of killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech, four for monks and eight for nuns) is the gravest, admitting no repentance and severing one from monastic status, likened by the Sarvāstivāda Treatise to a scorched seed that, however well tended, can never sprout; the class of the remainder (saṅghāvaśeṣa) is grave but curable, like a beheading that has not quite severed the throat; the class of downfall (duṣkṛta leading to the lower realms through unrepented lapse) and the class of individual confession are lighter, resolved through confession to a fellow practitioner; and the class of wrongdoing embraces the training rules and idle speech, resolved through confession before another or self-reproach alone.

18. Section: Clarifying Accordant Placement

Gathering-coarse offenses and indeterminate offenses, as is fitting, enter into these very classes of offense.

Chinese Source

集麤不定，如其所應，即入如是諸罪聚中。

Ru-Yi Translation

Gathering-coarse offenses and indeterminate offenses, as is fitting, enter into these very classes of offense.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 100 and the 披尋記

This opens clarifying accordant placement (壬二明隨應) — a "gathering-coarse" offense is a preparatory offense, committed in the course of moving toward a graver root offense (such as attempted killing) without completing it; its own gravity is not fixed but varies according to which class of root offense it approaches, falling as a downfall-class or wrongdoing-class offense depending on the case — a distinct usage from the "indeterminate" offenses named elsewhere in the monastic code.

19. Section: Distinguishing Restoration to Purity, Closing "Setting Forth the Classes of Offense" Entirely

Further, there are four classes of offense restored to purity. What are the four? Namely, apart from defeat, the remaining classes of offense may all be restored to purity — hence the four classes of restoration. The very first class, though it too may be restored to purity, relies only on two kinds of persons, not indiscriminately upon all — for this reason, defeat is not established among the classes uniformly restored to purity.

Chinese Source

復有四種還淨罪聚。何等為四？謂除他勝，所餘罪聚皆可還淨，故有四種還淨罪聚。最初罪聚雖可還淨，然唯依二補特伽羅，非為一切無有差別皆可還淨。是故他勝，不立一向還淨聚中。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are four classes of offense restored to purity. What are the four? Namely, apart from defeat, the remaining classes of offense may all be restored to purity — hence the four classes of restoration. The very first class, though it too may be restored to purity, relies only on two kinds of persons, not indiscriminately upon all — for this reason, defeat is not established among the classes uniformly restored to purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

With this, distinguishing restoration to purity (壬三簡還淨) closes entirely, closing **setting forth the classes of offense (辛一出罪聚3: 略標列/明隨應/簡還淨) entirely** — the two exceptional persons for whom even a defeat-class offense may be restored are the earliest disciples who transgressed before the training-ground was ever established, and one who commits the offense but confesses it immediately, without a single moment's concealment; all others who commit defeat carry both the gravity of the deed itself and the fierce, shameless affliction behind it, and for them no restoration is possible.

應知此中，初修業者，於四他勝雖有事重過失，而無猛利纏過失，由彼意樂無勃惡故，謂於沙門無所顧戀。

This opens explaining (壬四釋14), the fourth of revealing the faults' five sections, a fourteen-part structure, beginning with the fault of a grave deed and the fault of a fierce entanglement taken together (癸一2), beginning with only the first fault (子一唯初過失2), beginning with generally marking and distinguishing (丑一總標簡) — this refers to certain early disciples who, prior to the Buddha's formal promulgation of a training-ground, transgressed one of the four root offenses without the fierce, malicious intent that ordinarily accompanies such a breach.

24. Section: Explaining That Mark Begins — Clarifying Non-Offense

If a newly ordained one, knowing that this deed obstructs renunciant status, does not transgress it even for the sake of preserving life — their intention's power is strong, not resting on the deed alone, and so they are without offense. The establishing of an offense committed must be by reason of intention's own heightened power.

Chinese Source

若初業者，了知此法能障沙門，為命因緣，亦不違犯。意樂力彊，不唯依事，故彼無犯。制立所犯，要由意樂增彊力故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a newly ordained one, knowing that this deed obstructs renunciant status, does not transgress it even for the sake of preserving life — their intention's power is strong, not resting on the deed alone, and so they are without offense. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining that mark (丑二釋彼相2), beginning with clarifying non-offense (寅一明無犯) — whether an act constitutes an offense is never decided by the deed's outward form alone but requires every condition to be complete; taking the precept against killing as an example, five conditions must all be present — a human victim, the perception of a human, the intent to kill, the effort to kill, and the actual severing of life — and offense is established chiefly through the strength of an underlying malicious intention.

25. Section: Distinguishing Offense, Closing "Explaining That Mark" and "Only the First Fault" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

If, though there is a transgression, there is not a single thought of concealment arising, that person may still emerge from it, and remains capable with regard to the renunciant fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, distinguishing offense (寅二簡有犯) closes entirely, closing **explaining that mark (丑二釋彼相2: 明無犯/簡有犯) entirely**, closing **only the first fault (子一唯初過失2: 總標簡/釋彼相) entirely** — even one who has genuinely transgressed remains capable of repenting and of realizing the renunciant fruit, so long as there is not a single moment's concealment; concealment alone is what forecloses restoration.

26. Section: Possessing Both Faults, Closing "The Fault of a Grave Deed and the Fault of a Fierce Entanglement" Entirely

Chinese Source

其餘一切犯他勝者，亦有事重過失，亦有猛利無慚無愧諸煩惱纏過失。當知彼由二皆重故，成不可出法及不般涅槃法。

Ru-Yi Translation

All others who commit defeat likewise have both the fault of a grave deed and the fault of fierce, shameless entangling afflictions. One should know that, because both are grave for them, they become incapable of emerging from this and incapable of full nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, possessing both faults (子二具二過失) closes entirely, closing **the fault of a grave deed and the fault of a fierce entanglement (癸一2), the first of fourteen faults, entirely** — apart from the two exceptional cases already distinguished, anyone else who commits a defeat-class offense carries both the deed's own grave weight and the fierce, unashamed affliction driving it, and for such a person neither restoration nor final liberation remains possible.

27. Section: The Fault of Lack and Discontent, Closing the Second Fault Entirely

If robes and bowls and the rest, permitted by the World-Honored One, should be received, made pure, and used, yet one entirely discards all of these, or uses them without making them pure — such offenses are established by reason of the fault of lack and discontent.

Chinese Source

若衣鉢等世尊開許，應持作淨而受用之。於彼一切悉皆棄捨，或不作淨而輒受用。如是等罪，由依匱乏不喜足過，制立所犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

If robes and bowls and the rest, permitted by the World-Honored One, should be received, made pure, and used, yet one entirely discards all of these, or uses them without making them pure — such offenses are established by reason of the fault of lack and discontent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

With this, **the fault of lack and discontent (癸二), the second of fourteen faults**, closes — "making pure" means formally relinquishing claim on any excess robe to a fellow practitioner, a practice meant to check attachment even to what is properly permitted; discarding even the three robes and bowl one is meant to keep courts real physical harm through excessive austerity, while using unpurified excess without restraint courts the opposite fault of greed and accumulation — the mean between the two is what the precept protects.

28. Section: The Fault of Others' Reproach, Closing the Third Fault Entirely

Chinese Source

若非親屬苾芻尼所，受衣、與衣；或共彼等獨在一處；或復非時，諸苾芻僧不同忍許，輒往教授；或除餘時，與諸母邑共道路行。如是等類，當知是名他所譏嫌過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, not at the hands of a kinswoman bhikṣuṇī, one receives a robe or gives a robe; or dwells alone together with such a one in a single place; or, at an improper time, unauthorized by the assembly of monks, goes to instruct her; or, save at other times, travels the road together with women — such and the like should be known as the fault of others' reproach.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing extensive vinaya narratives

With this, **the fault of others' reproach (癸三), the third of fourteen faults**, closes — each clause traces to a specific vinaya incident: a nun's gift of her own fine robe to a shabbily dressed monk, prompting the Buddha to restrict robe-exchange to kin; the monk Kālodāyī and the nun Sthūlanandā, both attractive and both harboring desire, seen sitting together and likened by onlookers to a married couple; the household elder Mahāprajāpatī's request that monks be permitted to instruct nuns, later abused when unauthorized monks used the occasion to discuss worldly affairs instead of dharma; and the elder Aniruddha, nearly beaten to death by a jealous husband after unwittingly escorting a woman home along the road — each incident becoming the occasion for a precept guarding against the mere appearance of impropriety, whatever the monk's actual intention.

29. Section: The Fault of Doubling Disbelief and Unsettling Faith, Closing the Fourth Fault Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

If, without proper deportment, one enters a village and the like to beg for alms and partake; if one sits improperly; if, in washing the hands or rinsing a vessel, and so forth, one enters another's dwelling uninvited before mealtime; or, without regard for the hour, wanders about the settlement after the meal — such and the like should be known as the fault of doubling the unfaithful's disbelief and unsettling the faithful.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of doubling disbelief and unsettling faith (癸四), the fourth of fourteen faults**, closes — improper posture while seated, water splashed carelessly while washing hands or bowls, entering another's home uninvited before a meal, and wandering the streets after the midday meal instead of returning to sit in stillness all alike drive away the trust of those without faith and unsettle the resolve of those who already have it.

30. Section: The Fault of Much Wealth and Much Enterprise, Closing the Fifth Fault Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

If one grasps at gold, silver, and other treasures, buying and selling of every kind, engaging in enterprise, planting groves and trees, or keeping fine bedding of Kauśeya silk and the like — one should know this is called the fault of much wealth and much enterprise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of much wealth and much enterprise (癸五), the fifth of fourteen faults**, closes — a renunciant's requisites exist only to sustain the body for the sake of practice and teaching; accumulating gold and silver, trading in antiques, running a business, cultivating orchards for profit, or keeping luxurious silk bedding all belong to worldly livelihood, leaving no real time left for the cultivation of precepts, samādhi, and wisdom.

31. Section: The Fault of Defiled Attachment, Closing the Sixth Fault Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

If one deliberately emits semen; or grasps and touches a woman's hand and the like; or acts as a matchmaker, thereby entering a defiled and changeable mind; or, for the sake of adornment, goes to the home of kin to seek fine, excess robes and the like — one should know this is called the fault of defiled attachment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記 and the grounds attained through cultivation, fascicle 20

With this, **the fault of defiled attachment (癸六), the sixth of fourteen faults**, closes — serving as matchmaker for a lay wedding is itself the beginning-point of saṃsāra's own round, easily stirring a defiled mind that should instead remain fixed on the path; seeking finer robes from kin betrays outward vanity in place of the true adornment of an inwardly afflictionless mind. The 披尋記 traces this to failing to abide by one's own vowed acceptance of a lowly form, deportment, and requisites — the grounds attained through cultivation, fascicle 20, sets out twenty-two marks across four areas by which a practitioner, having attained samādhi but not yet severed the subtler afflictions of relish, pride, views, doubt, and ignorance that samādhi itself can breed, examines whether shaved head, discarded-color robes, and unadorned form; unhurried, unassuming deportment in walking, sitting, and begging; and reliance on requisites received from others rather than hoarded, are all genuinely being kept.

32. Section: The Fault of Troubling Others, Closing the Seventh Fault Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

If, by a groundless and false charge belonging to another category, one defames a fellow monk, or engages in divisive speech and the like — one should know this is called the fault of troubling others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of troubling others (癸七), the seventh of fourteen faults**, closes — this precept traces to the elder Dabba Mallaputta, an arhat who diligently arranged lodging and meal-turns for the community, whom the resentful monk Chabbaggiya, given inferior quarters and food, falsely accused of misconduct through his own sister, a nun, out of pure malice with no basis in fact — the paradigm case of baseless slander the precept was framed to prevent.

33. Section: The Fault of Giving Rise to Illness, Closing the Eighth Fault Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

If one personally carries wool beyond three yojanas; or bears a heavy load; or climbs a tree taller than a person — one should know this is called the fault of giving rise to illness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of giving rise to illness (癸八), the eighth of fourteen faults**, closes — traced to the monk Upananda, who strung gathered wool on a staff and carried it a long distance, drawing lay accusations of trading in goods; carrying such burdens too far invites both social reproach and, per the treatise's own extension here, real physical strain — work beyond one's own bodily capacity is said elsewhere to injure even the faculty of mind.

34. Section: The Fault of Obstructing Passage to a Good Destiny and to Renunciant Status Begins — Obstructing a Good Destiny

This opens **the fault of obstructing passage to a good destiny and to renunciant status (癸九2), the ninth of fourteen faults**, beginning with obstructing a good destiny (子一障善趣) — deliberately, forcefully engineering a schism within the harmonious saṅgha is itself grave enough to obstruct rebirth in a good destiny.

35. Section: Obstructing Renunciant Status, Closing the Ninth Fault Entirely

If one does something such that others will no longer speak with one — one should know this is called the fault of obstructing renunciant status.

If one does something such that others will no longer speak with one — one should know this is called the fault of obstructing renunciant status.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 攝事分, fascicle 94, and the 披尋記

With this, obstructing renunciant status (子二障沙門) closes entirely, closing **the fault of obstructing passage to a good destiny and to renunciant status (癸九2: 障善趣/障沙門), the ninth of fourteen faults, entirely** — when the noble ones fall silent and decline to speak with someone, refusing to instruct or admonish them further, it is because that person, through repeated wayward conduct against the higher training in precepts, has already made themselves unfit to receive teaching — a condition traced earlier, in the embrace of the bases, fascicle 94, to neglect of practice, careless guarding of the senses, closeness with bad companions, and disrespect toward the three treasures.

36. Section: The Fault of Failing to Rightly Avoid and Guard, Closing the Tenth Fault Entirely

Chinese Source

若有棄擲僧祇臥具，置迥露處，捨而去等；或邪受用等。當知是名於應避護不正避護過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one discards the saṅgha's communal bedding, leaving it exposed in the open and abandoning it; or makes wrongful use of things — one should know this is called the fault of failing to rightly avoid and guard what should be avoided and guarded.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of failing to rightly avoid and guard (癸十), the tenth of fourteen faults**, closes — traced to seventeen young monks who left the saṅgha's own bedding spread out in the open while going off to receive a meal invitation, letting it be ruined by wind, dust, and vermin; communal property deserves the same care one would give one's own, whether or not it belongs to oneself personally.

37. Section: The Fault of Wrongly Serving as Reliance, Closing the Eleventh Fault Entirely

If one dwells together with a monk of wrong view, or a novice under probation, and the like, serving as their reliance — one should know this is called the fault of wrongly serving as reliance where one should not.

Chinese Source

若與邪見苾芻、勤策共居住等，為依止等，當知是名不應為依反與為依過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one dwells together with a monk of wrong view, or a novice under probation, and the like, serving as their reliance — one should know this is called the fault of wrongly serving as reliance where one should not.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of wrongly serving as reliance (癸十一), the eleventh of fourteen faults**, closes — traced to the monk Ariṣṭha, who clung to a wrong view even after repeated correction and was formally expelled by the saṅgha through a motion-and-three-announcements proceeding, barred from joint recitation and formal business; the group of six monks nonetheless continued to support him and to conduct business jointly with him, prompting the Buddha's rebuke and this precept — once formally expelled, no one should shelter or serve as reliance for such a person.

38. Section: The Fault of Failing to Revere, Closing the Twelfth Fault Entirely

If, toward the venerable teaching, one lightly gives offense and complains, glaring with an angry, hostile gaze, and does not reverently listen to and receive the individual-liberation sūtra and the like — one should know this is called the fault of failing to revere what should be revered.

Chinese Source

若於尊教輕觸怨咎，怒睛惡視，不恭敬聽受別解脫經等，當知是名於應恭敬而不恭敬過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, toward the venerable teaching, one lightly gives offense and complains, glaring with an angry, hostile gaze, and does not reverently listen to and receive the individual-liberation sūtra and the like — one should know this is called the fault of failing to revere what should be revered.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of failing to revere (癸十二), the twelfth of fourteen faults**, closes — traced to the group of six monks, who, fearing that fellow monks diligently studying and reciting the sūtras, vinaya, and treatises would grow able to expose their own faults, urged those students to stop, arguing that only the root offenses needed reciting and the rest was needless fuss — a scheme exposed and rebuked by the Buddha; to discourage others from studying the teaching is to blind them, and oneself, to the path.

39. Section: The Fault of Failing to Conceal and Wrongly Concealing, Closing the Thirteenth Fault Entirely

If, before a person not yet fully ordained, one declares that one has truly attained a superhuman state; or, again, conceals a fellow monk's committed grave offense and the like — one should know this is called the fault of failing to conceal what should be concealed, or wrongly concealing what should not be.

Chinese Source

若於未受具戒補特伽羅前，宣示實得勝過人法；或復覆藏苾芻所犯麤惡罪等。當知是名於應覆藏而不覆藏、不應覆藏而反覆藏過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, before a person not yet fully ordained, one declares that one has truly attained a superhuman state; or, again, conceals a fellow monk's committed grave offense and the like — one should know this is called the fault of failing to conceal what should be concealed, or wrongly concealing what should not be.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of failing to conceal and wrongly concealing (癸十三), the thirteenth of fourteen faults**, closes — one's own attainment of even samādhi, let alone the noble path, should never be announced, still less before those unordained; conversely, the monk Upananda's request that a close friend conceal his own repeated grave offenses, and that friend's later disclosure once their friendship soured, illustrates the opposite fault — a genuine grave offense in a fellow practitioner should never be shielded, but raised promptly so that repentance can follow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, **the fault of wrongly drawing near (癸十四), the fourteenth of fourteen faults**, closes, closing **explaining (壬四釋14: 事重過失及猛利纏過失/匱乏不喜足過失/他所譏嫌過失/不信變異過失/多諸財寶事業過失/染著過失/惱他過失/發起疾病過失/障往善趣沙門過失/於應避護不正避護過失/不應為依反與為依過失/於應恭敬而不恭敬過失/於應覆藏不覆藏等過失/不應習近而反習近過失) entirely** — the group of six monks, drinking unfiltered water containing living creatures at Śrāvastī, drew lay accusations of lacking compassion for the sentient beings so harmed; requisites of every kind should be examined and used only when lawfully clean, whether the impurity is physical or born of an underlying greed, aversion, or attachment in how they are used.

42. Section: Embrace Begins — Marking and Listing the Five Factors

Further, there are, in brief, five factors that embrace discipline. What are the five? First, the offense by nature; second, the prohibited offense; third, restriction; fourth, allowance; fifth, conduct.

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are, in brief, five factors that embrace discipline. What are the five? First, the offense by nature; second, the prohibited offense; third, restriction; fourth, allowance; fifth, conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens **embrace (庚三攝2), the third of the nine matters**, named by the verse's third word, beginning with marking and listing the five factors (辛一標列五法) — the offense by nature, the four grave wrongs of killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech, wrong by their own inherent nature whether or not the Buddha ever prohibited them; the prohibited offense, established by the Buddha specifically for renunciants (such as the precept against alcohol, which by clouding the mind makes every other offense easier to commit); restriction, the Buddha's own five reasons for establishing precepts, embracing both the offense by nature and the prohibited offense; allowance, circumstances of legitimate exception; and conduct, the threefold pattern of offense, non-offense, and restoration to purity.

43. Section: Separately Explaining Each Mark Begins — The Offense by Nature

云何性罪？謂性是不善，能為雜染損惱於他，能為雜染損惱於自。雖不遮制，但有現行便往惡趣；雖不遮制，但有現行能障沙門。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the offense by nature? It means that its nature is unwholesome, able to defile and injure another, and able to defile and injure oneself. Even without prohibition, its mere occurrence leads to a lower destiny; even without prohibition, its mere occurrence obstructs renunciant status.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

This opens separately explaining each mark (辛二別釋其相5), beginning with the offense by nature (壬一性罪) — the four root offenses of killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech are themselves unwholesome in their very substance, arising from craving, aversion, or delusion; whether or not any buddha ever prohibits them, anyone who commits them, believer or not, falls to a lower destiny and forfeits any hope of the renunciant fruit — the 披尋記 identifies the offense by nature more broadly with the full ten courses of unwholesome action.

44. Section: The Prohibited Offense

Wherever such a matter occurs, it is called the prohibited offense.

Chinese Source

云何遮罪？謂佛世尊觀彼形相不如法故，或令眾生重正法故，或見所作隨順現行性罪法故，或為隨順護他心故，或見障礙善趣、壽命、沙門性故，而正遮止。若有現行如是等事，說名遮罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the prohibited offense? It means that the World-Honored One, observing that its outward form is unlawful, or wishing that beings hold the true dharma in high regard, or seeing that the deed accords with and gives rise to an offense by nature, or for the sake of according with and protecting others' minds, or seeing that it obstructs a good destiny, obstructs the lifespan, or obstructs renunciant status — rightly prohibits it. Wherever such a matter occurs, it is called the prohibited offense.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the prohibited offense (壬二遮罪) — unlike the offense by nature, this class of offense exists only because the Buddha specifically prohibited it, for any of seven reasons: unbecoming outward conduct; deepening reverence for the true dharma; a lesser act that tends toward a graver offense by nature; guarding the feelings of others so as to avoid their reproach; obstructing a good future destiny; shortening the lifespan (such as failing to avoid known dangers or plague-ridden lands); and obstructing renunciant status outright.

45. Section: Restriction

It means that there are deeds able to lead to a lower destiny, or that obstruct a good destiny, or that obstruct lawfully obtained gain, or that obstruct the lifespan, or that obstruct renunciant status. Such and the like the Tathāgata restricts and prohibits from ever occurring — this is therefore called restriction.

Chinese Source

云何名制？謂有所作能往惡趣，或障善趣，或障如法所得利養，或障壽命，或障沙門。如是等類，如來遮制不令現行，故名為制。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called restriction? It means that there are deeds able to lead to a lower destiny, or that obstruct a good destiny, or that obstruct lawfully obtained gain, or that obstruct the lifespan, or that obstruct renunciant status. Such and the like the Tathāgata restricts and prohibits from ever occurring — this is therefore called restriction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens restriction (壬三制) — the Buddha established both offenses by nature and prohibited offenses for five underlying reasons taken together: deeds that lead to a future lower rebirth, that obstruct rebirth in a good destiny, that obstruct lawfully received support (as jealousy toward a fellow practitioner's own lawful gain does), that shorten the lifespan (as an intemperate or vengeful mind does), or that directly obstruct realization of the renunciant fruit.

46. Section: Allowance

Contrary to this, one should know, is called allowance.

Chinese Source

與此相違，應知名開。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contrary to this, one should know, is called allowance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens allowance (壬四開) — beyond the general prohibition, the Buddha also set out specific circumstances of legitimate exception: wherever a deed does not, in fact, obstruct a good destiny, renunciant status, the lifespan, or lawful gain, allowance may be given even where the general rule would otherwise forbid it.

48. Section: The Second Meaning, Closing "Distinguishing" Entirely

This opens **by intention (午三由意樂), the third of five grading causes** — the grade tracks directly to the intensity of the underlying affliction active at the moment of transgression, from a faint stirring up through a coarse, forceful one.

This opens **by the object (午四由事故3), the fourth of five grading causes**, beginning with marking the meaning (未一標義) — two people may act from what looks like the same underlying affliction, yet because the object of the deed differs, the resulting offense's own gravity differs — the treatise's own careful, case-by-case method of judgment rather than a single blanket rule.

53. Section: Explaining the Mark

This opens explaining the mark (未二釋相) — the very same aversion, acted on identically, yields a merely downfall-class offense against an animal, a grave defeat-class offense against a human being (or against what the Five-Part Vinaya calls "human in form" — a fetus past forty-nine days in the womb, once the six sense faculties have formed), and both a defeat offense and the gravest offense of immediate retribution against one's own father or mother, or, as noted further, against a teacher or preceptor — the object's own weight as a field of kindness or of reverence directly determines the offense's own gravity.

56. Section: Becoming a Middle-Grade Offense

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Four-Part Vinaya's ordination chapter and the Compendium of Formal Proceedings
