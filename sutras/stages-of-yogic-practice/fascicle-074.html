Fascicle 74 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十四 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

The Three Natures Finished, and What Follows

This is the third part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. It carries on from the first verse on the three natures given in the previous fascicle with a second and a third, and with them brings the long Chapter on the Real to a close; the fascicle then goes on to determine the chapters on awakening, on the lineage of powers, and on giving. The second verse takes the three natures up from nine angles: which of the five things includes each nature, how each is said to be without own nature, what is to be fully known and what abandoned and what realized, the hidden intent behind the forbearance of non-arising and the three doors of liberation, which knowledge ranges over each, how penetration differs with and without characteristics, what one enters upon when one enters them, their differences, and what they rest upon.

1. Section: A Second Verse on the Three Natures, and Which of the Five Things Includes Each

This is the third part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. Continuing from the first verse on the three natures given in the previous fascicle, a second summary verse says: what includes them, their being without nature, what is to be known and the rest; the hidden intent and the rest, what they are ranged over by; penetration, and entering upon; their differences, and what they rest upon, last of all. That is nine topics: which of the five things each of the three natures is included in; how three kinds of absence of own nature are set up upon the three natures; what among the three natures is to be fully known, what abandoned forever, what realized; the hidden sense of the three natures with regard to the forbearance of the non-arising of phenomena and the three doors of liberation; which kind of knowledge ranges over each; how penetrating the three natures differs as having or not having characteristics; how, on entering the three natures, one enters upon the perfectly accomplished and thereby sweeps away the dependently arisen; the differences among the three natures; and what the three natures rest upon in arising. Of the three natures and the five things beginning with characteristic — how many of the five things is the first nature included in? None of them at all, since the pervasively imputed nature has neither substance nor characteristic. How many is the second nature included in? Four — characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge — of which characteristic, name, and discrimination are the defiled portion of the dependently arisen and correct knowledge is its purified portion; characteristic and name are what is taken as object, discrimination and correct knowledge are what takes, and all four are conditioned things arisen from conditions. How many is the third nature included in? One: suchness. Then an objection. If the dependently arisen nature is also included in correct knowledge, why was it said earlier that the dependently arisen nature is to be known through the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature? The answer: that passage meant only the defiled portion of the dependently arisen nature, not the purified portion. As for the purified portion, it should be known that it is known through there being no clinging to that. The ordinary person's dependently arisen mind arises and ceases together with the imputing, which is why it is known through that clinging; whereas the noble one's correct knowledge — fundamental knowledge, which is apart from the spoken nature, and the knowledge subsequently attained, which takes up body and world knowing them to be like an illusion — no longer clings to what words set up, and is known through precisely that absence of clinging.

2. Section: How Each Nature Is Without Own Nature, What Is to Be Known, Abandoned, and Realized, and Three Forbearances of Non-Arising

Further, upon the three natures there are three kinds of absence of own nature: the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic, in respect of arising, and in respect of the ultimate. By the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic, the pervasively imputed nature is said to be without own nature — for what naming sets up is established as a characteristic by provisional naming and not by any characteristic of its own. By the absence of own nature in respect of arising, and by the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate, the dependently arisen nature is said to be without own nature: because it does not exist by any nature of its own, and because it is not what purity takes as its object. It arises only by the force of conditions, mind and mental factors from four and form from two, so that what would be arising of itself is simply not there; and when fundamental knowledge realizes suchness, the defiled portion of the dependently arisen does not appear at all, faculties, objects, consciousness, the receptacle world, grasper and grasped, and speech all being absent, since that portion is not what purified knowledge takes. And only by the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate is the perfectly accomplished nature said to be without own nature. Why so? Because this nature is itself the ultimate, and is also what the absence of own nature in all phenomena reveals. Of the three natures, how many are to be fully known? All of them, since anyone who would train must penetrate what is present and what absent in all three. How many are to be abandoned forever? One — the defiled portion of the dependently arisen, the whole chain from ignorance conditioning formations to birth conditioning aging and death, which must be severed for good if one is to leave birth and death and turn the eight consciousnesses into the four knowledges. How many are to be realized? One — the perfectly accomplished, without which no one turns from ordinary person into noble one. Further, by these three natures all the hidden senses of every scripture whose meaning is not drawn out should be settled. That is, the secret speech of the tathāgatas, and the essential meaning of the secret speech that bodhisattvas use throughout the measureless teachings, should all be settled accordingly by these three natures. And by way of example: as the scriptures speak of the forbearance of the non-arising of phenomena, how is it established? It is established by the three natures. By the pervasively imputed nature is established the forbearance that it is unarisen in its very nature; by the dependently arisen nature is established the forbearance that it does not arise of itself; and by the perfectly accomplished nature is established the forbearance that afflictions and the stain of suffering are unarisen. It should be known that this forbearance does not fall back. Take the rope in the dark: the darkness is ignorance, the rope is what conditions produced, and the snake is what imputation adds — there never was a snake, and that is the first forbearance; the rope itself was not there of its own, being made of hemp, and that is the second; and when the darkness lifts, not only is there no snake but the rope too is no longer found, and afflictions and suffering are seen to have never arisen in the suchness that stands revealed, which is the third. Further, the three doors of liberation are likewise established by the three natures: by the pervasively imputed nature is established the door of emptiness, since one takes as object the emptiness of everything words set up; by the dependently arisen nature is established the door of wishlessness, since knowing what conditions produce, one no longer wishes for the dependently arisen of a future life; and by the perfectly accomplished nature is established the door of signlessness, since one takes as object the selflessness of phenomena itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi answers the obvious objection that the previous fascicle said suchness is not called without own nature by any absence of own nature at all, while here the perfectly accomplished is said to be so called by the ultimate one. The two statements are about different things and do not conflict: there the reference was to the perfectly accomplished as the nature things already have, present whether realized or not, which no absence of own nature accounts for; here the reference is to it as what a noble one's knowledge realizes, which shows itself precisely where the imputed has fallen away from the dependently arisen, and so is indeed what the absence of own nature reveals. He also notes that of everything the world offers, only this is finally secure — every fortune turns and every undertaking ends, whereas one who has reached these forbearances does not fall back to being an ordinary person again.

3. Section: Which Knowledge Ranges Over Each Nature, What Penetration Enters, and What It Sweeps Away

The pervasively imputed nature — what kind of knowledge ranges over it? An ordinary person's knowledge, or a noble one's? No knowledge at all ranges over it, since it has no characteristic. Having neither substance nor characteristic, and being just what the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic covers, the nature that words set up is not the objective range of anyone's knowing. The dependently arisen nature — what kind of knowledge ranges over it? It is ranged over by two knowledges, but not by transcendent noble knowledge. It is the objective range of the ordinary person's knowledge, who by naming takes up the aggregates and the receptacle world and every characteristic, name, and discrimination within them; and it is equally the objective range of the knowledge subsequently attained, which takes up the same aggregates and world but, the seeds of affliction having been severed, knows all these conditioned things to be like an illusion and no longer holds name or characteristic to be real. Only when transcendent correct knowledge realizes suchness do the characteristics not appear at all, and so it is not ranged over by that. The perfectly accomplished nature — what kind of knowledge ranges over it? Only a noble one's knowledge ranges over it. When practitioners of contemplation penetrate the pervasively imputed nature, should it be said that their knowledge operates upon characteristics, or without characteristics? If they penetrate it by worldly knowledge, it should be said to operate upon characteristics; if by transcendent knowledge, it should be said to operate without characteristics. An ordinary person contemplating the emptiness of what naming sets up still has the characteristic of emptiness standing before him; and the subsequently attained knowledge, contemplating that same emptiness, still approaches it carrying a characteristic, though it no longer clings to the characteristic of emptiness. Only correct knowledge directly realizing suchness operates without characteristics. As with the pervasively imputed nature, so should it be known of the dependently arisen and the perfectly accomplished. If a practitioner of contemplation truly enters the pervasively imputed nature, what nature should be said to be entered upon? The perfectly accomplished. To enter it truly is to know that the spoken nature of everything is finally empty, and in that very moment correct knowledge is directly realizing suchness. And if the practitioner enters upon the perfectly accomplished nature, what nature should be said to be swept away? The dependently arisen — for when correct knowledge directly realizes suchness, the defiled dependently arisen, characteristic and name and discrimination, no longer appears, like a sky with no cloud in it. How many kinds does the pervasively imputed nature have? Whatever the measure of the own natures and differences set up and established within the dependently arisen nature, just that is the measure of the pervasively imputed nature as well. Therefore it should be known that the pervasively imputed nature has measureless differences — for what conditions produce runs through the sentient and the insentient and fills the ten directions without limit, and naming keeps pace with it exactly. And further, within the dependently arisen nature it should be known that there are two kinds of clinging to the pervasively imputed nature: one that accompanies awareness, and one that is the latent tendency of habitual perfuming — the first being the clinging as it is presently active, in one at home in language who takes up a characteristic through its name, and the second being its seeds, by whose force even an infant or an animal, with no words available, takes up objects as real and perfumes those seeds further.

4. Section: Two Kinds of Dependently Arisen Nature, What Each Nature Rests On, and a Third Verse

How many kinds does the dependently arisen nature have? It should be known to be as various as the kinds and classes of characteristic — and those, as an earlier fascicle set out at length, are measureless. Further, there are two kinds of dependently arisen nature: that which the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature raises, and that which is raised where there is no such clinging. The first is the defiled portion: body and receptacle world, the view of self and conceit, greed and hatred and delusion, all produced by the eight discriminations and their clinging — affliction, action, and suffering in all their varieties. The second is the purified portion: when hearing, reflecting, and cultivating bring one to non-discriminating knowledge, discrimination turns into correct knowledge, and the defiled dependently arisen of body and world is no longer to be had; broadly, this covers transcendent correct knowledge, the knowledge at once worldly and transcendent that follows it, and the pure land such knowledge brings about. At the first ground the true path of seeing comes first, fundamental knowledge directly realizing suchness; and afterward, on the path of seeing by resemblance, that knowledge fashions an image of suchness and of the measureless differences of action and result — and it is upon this subsequently attained knowledge that there come to be buddhas to speak of, and teachings, and pure lands, and beings. How many kinds does the perfectly accomplished nature have? Because in every place it is of one taste, the perfectly accomplished nature has no established number of classes or divisions. Then, what should each be said to rest upon? The pervasively imputed nature should be said to rest upon three things: characteristic, name, and discrimination. Mental discrimination is what imputes, characteristic and name are what is imputed, and out of the meeting of these the false construction is made and the spoken nature of everything is held to be real. The dependently arisen nature should be said to rest upon the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature and upon its own outflow — that is, upon the discriminating mind that holds the spoken nature of everything to be real, which in brief divides into the eight discriminations, and upon the seeds which that mind's perfuming has laid down, from which the dependently arisen in turn arises. And the perfectly accomplished nature should be said to abide nowhere and rest upon nothing, being the suchness of phenomena, unconditioned, enduring, and leaning on nothing at all. Further, a third summary verse says: whether they are absent or present, and their work; the subtle and the rest, and being without substance; arising, clinging and the rest, knowing them rightly; defilement and suffering, similes, and discriminating. That is ten topics: whether each of the three natures is absent or present; what work each does; how they differ as coarse and subtle; how having or not having substance differs in what it does; how arising and not arising differ in what they do; how they differ as involving clinging or not; how the three natures are rightly known; how they differ as to defilement and suffering; the three natures set out through similes; and how the names and senses of the three natures arise differently.

Master Cháng Bǎi uses the two dependently arisen natures to state how the three natures stand to one another. Subjectively there are two, the imputed that a construing consciousness raises and the accomplished that knowledge reveals — the first is the ordinary person's condition, the second the buddhas'.

5. Section: What Would Go Wrong If Any of the Three Were Missing, and the Five Works Each Performs

If there were no pervasively imputed nature, what would go wrong? Within the dependently arisen nature there would be no naming and no clinging to naming — naming here meaning the activity and clinging to naming meaning its latent seeds. And if there were none of that, defilement and purity could not be known. For it is with this imputed nature as cause that the dependently arisen of a future life arises and is stained by the defilements of affliction, action, and rebirth; and it is by not clinging to the imputed nature upon the dependently arisen and the perfectly accomplished — by the knowledge in which speech does not perfume, in which speech does not accompany awareness, in which speech has no latency left — that the dependently arisen characteristic ceases and the purified perfectly accomplished is realized. Not knowing the difference between defilement and purity, one could neither sever the one nor realize the other; and so it is out of compassion that the pervasively imputed nature is set up at all, to show beings the shape of their own construing, and thereby to free them from the bondage of it. If there were no dependently arisen nature, what would go wrong? Then without any effort at all, every defilement would simply not exist — nobody would need to cultivate moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom; and if there were no defilement, there would be no purity that could be known either. What conditions produce is the support of both defiled and pure phenomena, and without it neither could be spoken of. If there were no perfectly accomplished nature, what would go wrong? Then nothing of the purified class could be known at all. How many works can the pervasively imputed nature perform? Five. First, it can produce the dependently arisen nature. Second, upon that nature it can raise speech. Third, it can produce the clinging to a person. Fourth, it can produce the clinging to phenomena. Fifth, it can take up the perfuming and the heaviness of those two clingings — laying down in the storehouse consciousness the seeds of clinging to self and to phenomena, and with them the heaviness of the two obstructions, by which a being shows himself unwieldy, stiff, obstructed, feeble, and unable to turn freely, going along with defilement and against purity. How many works can the dependently arisen nature perform? Also five. First, it can produce all defiled phenomena, since it is the consciousnesses arisen from conditions that present body and world and the whole of affliction, action, and suffering. Second, it can serve as the support of both the pervasively imputed nature and the perfectly accomplished nature — held to be real upon it, that is the imputed; with the imputed gone from it, the emptiness that shows is the accomplished. Third, it can serve as the support of the clinging to a person. Fourth, it can serve as the support of the clinging to phenomena, since the moment a being's mind stirs it spontaneously takes faculties, objects, consciousness, and world to be real. Fifth, it can serve as the support of the perfuming and heaviness of those two clingings. How many works can the perfectly accomplished nature perform? Also five — because it is the objective sphere by taking which as object there arises what counters those two sets of five works. Taking the perfectly accomplished as object, the noble one's transcendent correct knowledge arises, and that knowledge is precisely what answers the five works of the imputed and the five of the dependently arisen.

6. Section: Coarse and Subtle, Which Has Substance and Which Turns Into What, and Which Arises

Should the pervasively imputed nature be called subtle or coarse? It should be called subtle — because it has followed beings from beginningless time and never left them, so that the moment an ordinary person's mind stirs the imputing is there of itself, while its substance is in fact empty and no knowledge ranges over it, and a worldly mind cannot notice that emptiness. And as with subtle, so should it be known of hard to see and hard to understand: its characteristic, the own natures and differences that naming sets up, is empty, and ordinary knowledge does not see it; and though it has neither substance nor characteristic, it does have its work, and ordinary knowledge does not make that out. Should the dependently arisen nature be called subtle or coarse? It should be called coarse. Its substance is the forms and mental events produced from seeds and having consciousness for their nature; these are what the six consciousnesses take as object, the very things on which seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing rest, and so they are things with substance, ranged over by ordinary knowledge and by worldly knowledge subsequently attained. And yet it is hard to see and hard to understand. Its characteristic is hard to see, for one cannot with a mind still clinging make out that the dependently arisen is momentary and impermanent, like a conjured thing, an image, an echo, a shadow, the moon in water, a mirage, a dream — appearing as something it is not; and its work is hard to understand, since ordinary knowledge does not make out how it produces all defiled things and supports both the imputed and the accomplished. Should the perfectly accomplished nature be called subtle or coarse? It should be called extremely subtle. No ordinary person penetrates it at all, and even the noble ones of the two vehicles do not fully make it out. And as with extremely subtle, so should it be known of extremely hard to see and extremely hard to understand — its characteristic being no characteristic at all, and its work, which counters the ten works of the other two, being invisible to anyone but knowledge. Of these three natures, how many are without substance and yet turn into what has substance? One: the pervasively imputed, which has neither substance nor characteristic, and yet, being what a mind takes as object, raises the clinging of a dependently arisen mind, and through that the whole future of affliction, action, and suffering. How many have substance and turn into what has substance and what does not? One: the dependently arisen, which by the outflow of its own seeds produces further dependently arisen things, and which by serving as the support of the imputed produces what has no substance at all. How many have substance and yet turn into nothing? One: the perfectly accomplished, which is the nature phenomena have, unchanging, what correct knowledge takes as object, unconditioned and apart from all elaboration, and so cannot serve as the condition from which anything else arises. Of these three natures, how many do not arise and yet produce what arises? One — the imputed, unarisen in itself and yet the object upon which clinging is raised and the future arisings that follow. How many arise and produce both what arises and what does not? One — the dependently arisen, which produces the defiled things that arise and also supports the imputed, which does not. And how many neither arise nor produce what arises or does not? One — the perfectly accomplished, which is not something produced from conditions, produces nothing produced from conditions, and produces no imputing either.

7. Section: How to Tell Whether One Is Clinging — Clinging That Is Awake and Clinging That Is Only Latent

How should the clinging and the freedom from clinging with regard to the pervasively imputed nature be known? There are two kinds: clinging or freedom from clinging that is awake, and clinging or freedom from clinging that is latent. If, by provisionally setting up names through speech, one imputes to things a settled own nature, this should be known as the clinging that is awake. If one understands well that there is only a name — and, knowing it to be only a name, does not take those things to have a settled nature — this should be known as freedom from clinging with regard to it. Say the words "sour plum" and there is no plum in the mouth; the words are a token for a fruit that conditions have assembled, and do not deliver the fruit's own dependently arisen shape at all. Knowing that every name is only a name, with no thing standing behind it as the name announces, is what freedom from that clinging means. If one has not pulled out the latent tendency of its perfuming, it should be known that there is latent clinging with regard to it — that is, for as long as the perfuming and the heaviness have not been let go, so that even when no naming is active there is still a spontaneous clinging to the appearances consciousness presents, and the mind carries an unwieldiness it cannot shake. And if that has been cut off forever, it should be known that there is freedom from clinging. How should the clinging and the freedom from clinging with regard to the dependently arisen nature be known? If, because of the awake clinging to the pervasively imputed nature, one further imputes the nature that clinging has produced, this is called the first clinging. That is: the imputing mind, active in naming, takes as its object a dependently arisen thing under its name, seizes within it the appearance that answers to the name — the semblance of a meaning — and then raises a settled conviction that the self and the phenomena which speech points to are really so; and that conviction is the clinging, which in turn perfumes the seeds of the perceiving and perceived aspects and produces the dependently arisen of the future. If one understands well that there are only the various appearances, and does not impute the nature that clinging has produced, this is called freedom from clinging. Knowing that what naming presents is only what the perfumed seeds of the perceiving and perceived aspects raise, only illusory shapes, only a semblance of meaning, one no longer imputes a real substance to what conditions produce, and the discriminated imputing does not become active. And if the bondage of characteristics has not been pulled out for good, then whenever anything is attained within the characteristics, that is called the second clinging — for as long as the seeds remain, the mind's binding to characteristics arises of itself, and something is always being had within them.

8. Section: Overweening Pride About What Has Not Been Realized, and How the Imputed Nature Is Rightly Known

If the bondage of characteristics has been pulled out for good, then, rightly knowing the realm without characteristics, one attains nothing within characteristics; or, at a later time, attaining what there is just as it is, one should be known to be free of clinging. Two things are meant. When fundamental knowledge realizes suchness, the bondage both to what naming presents and to what naming rested on is gone, and no characteristic appears at all. And when the knowledge subsequently attained comes forward, body and world do appear again just as they are and are indeed attained — but they are known as illusory appearance, and no real self or real phenomenon is clung to in them; one is amid characteristics and apart from them, apart from names, apart from speech, apart from what the mind takes hold of. How should the clinging and the freedom from clinging with regard to the perfectly accomplished nature be known? There is no clinging here, since this realm is not a place where clinging can find its footing — it is the emptiness that the selflessness of phenomena reveals, unconditioned, without shape or mark, and nothing there can be taken hold of. But if, with regard to this realm not attained, not touched, not realized, someone raises the overweening pride of having attained, touched, and realized it, that should be known to be the pervasively imputed, and a clinging upon the dependently arisen nature. One who has not yet severed the seeds of the two obstructions has not attained it; one who has not yet personally met and received it has not touched it; one who has not yet reached the nirvāṇa that is its fruit has not realized it. To reach an absorption and take oneself to have thereby attained, touched, and realized the perfectly accomplished is exactly the overweening pride the scriptures name: the characteristic of suchness is still standing there and one feels that emptiness has been realized, while neither the bondage of characteristics nor the heaviness of the seeds has been severed at all. As the elder said in the scripture, having heard the various accounts other elders gave of what they had understood: all these venerable ones are carrying overweening pride, and being held by it cannot understand the ultimate truth whose one taste pervades everything. And how should the pervasively imputed nature be known? One should rightly know that there is only its name and only the imputing; that it is without characteristic, without nature, without arising, without ceasing, without defilement, without purity, quiescent from the beginning and of its own nature nirvāṇa; that it is not past, not future, not present; not bound to the realms and not freed from them; not a bondage and not a liberation; not painful, not pleasant, and not neither painful nor pleasant; that it is of one taste alone and, pervading every place, is like space. In the rope taken for a snake there is no snake, so one cannot ask whether the snake is past, future, or present, or whether it is painful or pleasant, or bound or free. By these and measureless other aspects the pervasively imputed nature should be rightly known.

10. Section: How the Perfectly Accomplished Is Known, Which Nature Defiles, and Three Similes — Space, an Enemy, and a Buried Treasure

How should the perfectly accomplished nature be known? One should rightly know it by the differing aspects already set out — suchness, the real limit, the sphere of phenomena, and measureless differences of that kind. Those names were given before: suchness, since it is true and does not change; real nature, the true nature that ordinary person and noble one alike possess; the nature of truth, which only a noble one realizes; not-inverted and un-inverted; the realm without elaboration; the realm without characteristics; the sphere of phenomena, sphere here meaning cause, since it is the cause from which the qualities of noble ones arise; and the real limit, real because free of inversion and limit because nothing lies beyond it, so that having reached it one has nowhere further to seek. And one should further know the remaining differences: it is without shape or color; it cannot be looked at; it rests on nothing and abides nowhere; it reaches out to nothing as its object; it cannot be made to appear; it cannot be discerned by a discriminating mind; it cannot be brought about by doing anything; it cannot be declared in speech; it is apart from all elaboration; there is nothing in it to take up and nothing to put down — and differences of this kind are measureless. Of these three natures, how many are not themselves defiled and yet make other things defiled? One: the pervasively imputed, which having neither substance nor characteristic can be called neither defiled nor pure, and yet, becoming the object a mind takes up, makes the dependently arisen defiled. How many are defiled in themselves alone? 11. Section: Why Each Nature Is Named as It Is, and Five Things Non-Discriminating Wisdom Is Not

Why is the pervasively imputed nature said to impute? Because of the dependently arisen nature — for it is upon the appearances that consciousness presents and the names that speech expresses, joined to one another, that the clinging to a self and to phenomena is raised. Why is the dependently arisen nature said to depend on other things? Because of causes and conditions: this being so, that is; this arising, that arises. Why is the perfectly accomplished nature said to be perfectly accomplished? Because it is not stained by any affliction or any mass of suffering, and further because it is enduring — pure in its own nature, with the afflictions and suffering alike stilled, and unconditioned and unchanging besides. Now a further question. It has been said that the wisdom which takes up the real is non-discriminating. How should its non-discriminating character be known? Is it by not directing attention? Is it by having passed beyond applied and sustained thought? Is it by there being nothing there at all? Is it by its being of that sort by nature? Or is it by making an effort upon the object it takes? If it were by not directing attention, then its being joined to attention in accordance with reason would not stand to reason — and deep sleep and raving drunkenness would qualify, which is plainly a fault. If it were by having passed beyond applied and sustained thought, how would that not contradict the holy teaching, which says that all the minds and mental factors of the three realms are discrimination? From the second absorption upward the wish for applied and sustained thought is gone, but discrimination is not, and a grasper and a grasped remain. If it were by there being nothing there at all, how would this wisdom not turn out to be no mental factor at all — and what then would make it wisdom? Those who enter the absorption without perception have no wisdom to show for it and may come out of it holding that things happen without causes; and the absorption of cessation, though it stills the evolving consciousnesses, is provisionally established upon that stilling and is not itself the entering into suchness. If it were by its being of that sort by nature, how would this wisdom not turn out to have the nature of form, insensible as mountains and tables are, and not to be a penetrating realization at all? And if it were by making an effort upon the object it takes, how would that not slander non-discriminating wisdom as being apart from effort? Before it, at the causal stage, one certainly cultivates the effort of contemplating the emptiness of person and of phenomena; but when suchness is actually realized, grasper and grasped are gone and no effort is being made. If all of these fail to stand to reason, how then is non-discriminating wisdom to be known? Because it is apart from effort with regard to the object it takes.

12. Section: What Non-Discriminating Wisdom Actually Is, the Close of the Chapter on the Real, and Why a Bodhisattva's Power Is Inconceivable

How is non-discriminating wisdom to be known? Because it is apart from effort with regard to the object it takes. At that moment neither grasper nor grasped appears, so one cannot say that any searching, thinking, or directing of attention is being done upon the object. This object it takes is the suchness of phenomena, apart from any characteristic of presence or absence; and just this is also apart from all discrimination — not only is the pervasively imputed gone, but faculties, objects, consciousness, the receptacle world, grasper and grasped, and speech, the whole defiled dependently arisen, is swept away with it; the perceived aspect is gone and the perceiving mind is nowhere to be found. And because it is drawn forth by the force of what came before, then although it is apart from effort, when the wondrous wisdom joined to concentration upon suchness arises, it can illuminate and take up the characteristic it has as object then and there. The force that came before is the preparatory training: at warmth one contemplates that the referent is nowhere to be had and there is only consciousness, sealing the emptiness of the object; at summit the contemplation deepens; at forbearance one enters the absence of the referent and penetrates that the imputed characteristic has no own nature; and at the highest worldly state one penetrates that the dependently arisen mind which discriminates is empty as well — from which, in a single uninterrupted moment, the path of seeing is entered. And there the knowing wisdom is without discrimination and the suchness it knows is without discrimination, and emptiness is realized directly and taken up — not blank like a log, for the mind is perfectly clear, seeing and yet without a characteristic, taking the emptiness straight on with no false hold of grasper and grasped. Therefore this wisdom is called non-discriminating. So the determination of the section on the real has been given. Further, by five causes it should be known that all the majestic power a bodhisattva has is inconceivable. What are the five? First, because a bodhisattva's majestic power passes beyond every field that searching thought can reach. Second, because no simile in the world can be found for it. Third, because it belongs only to a mind well polished — a mind that has attained mastery and is free of the stains of the two obstructions, held by concentration, clear and unclouded and able to do great good for beings, as a mirror surface polished to the utmost shows every image in it. Fourth, because it shares nothing at all with a mind not established in concentration. And fifth, because it belongs entirely to a mind established in concentration.

13. Section: Ten Ways of Practicing the Teaching, and the Opening of Great Awakening

Further, within the Great Vehicle there are ten ways of practicing the teaching, by which a bodhisattva can bring beings to ripeness. What are the ten? With regard to the discourses and the rest that belong to the Great Vehicle and are included in the bodhisattva collection: writing them out and preserving them; making offerings; giving them away to others; listening respectfully when another expounds them rightly; reading them over for oneself with delight; taking them in and holding them; having taken them in, reciting them aloud in a full voice; expounding and opening them out at length for others; sitting alone in a quiet place weighing and examining them; and entering into the practice that accords with them. That is: copying or having copied and circulating the scriptures with a reverent mind; honoring with incense and flowers the place or the shrine where they are kept; gladly passing volumes on to others rather than keeping them; attending closely when someone is teaching, so that having gained understanding oneself one can also ripen others; reading over what one trusts and loves; going back over what one has read until it is understood, received, and not forgotten; reciting it aloud so that others are glad to hear it; opening it out for others according to what each can take in; withdrawing to a quiet place to weigh the sense again and again until one's view is straight; and finally taking the sense one has grasped as the object of calming and insight, and so accomplishing concentration and the noble path. The first eight belong to the wisdom that comes of hearing, the ninth to the wisdom that comes of reflecting, and the tenth to the wisdom that comes of cultivating. Of these ten ways of practice, how many produce the path of vast merit? All of them. How many are the path of preparatory effort? One: the ninth — for the effort of calming and insight requires that one first have a firm understanding of the teaching, and that understanding comes of weighing and sorting what one has heard, without which one does not know how to practice calming or insight at all. How many are the path that purifies the obstructions? One: the tenth — for it is in actually practicing calming and insight that the obstruction of the afflictions and the obstruction to what is knowable are cleansed, and one passes from the cultivating wisdom that has outflow to the cultivating wisdom that does not. Determinations of this sort that remain will not be set out again. Further, as was said in the section on the wisdom that comes of hearing where the Great Vehicle is taken up, great awakening should be known by five aspects: by its own nature, by its capacity, by its means, by the turning outward, and by the turning back. These were not there analyzed, and are now to be explained. What is the own nature of great awakening? It is a turning of the basis that surpasses that of the śrāvakas and solitary realizers. And it should be known that this turning of the basis has four aspects: being the basis on which arising turns, being the basis on which non-arising turns, being the fruit of having well examined what is to be known, and being the purity of the sphere of phenomena.

14. Section: The Four Aspects of the Turning of the Basis

Being the basis on which arising turns means that it is the basis on which the transcendent path arises and continues in the continuum of a buddha. Arising means the transcendent path coming up; turning means its continuing on. The buddha's continuum is the body of the teaching, and it is upon this that the reflections of the knowledges of the śrāvaka vehicle, of the solitary-realizer vehicle, and of the unsurpassed Great Vehicle all arise, serving as a powerful condition for each, so that those of each disposition find their way out along their own vehicle. If it were not so — if this turning of the basis were not attained — that path ought neither to arise nor to continue. And if that path could occur apart from it, then before the turning of the basis this path ought to have been there already, which does not stand to reason. Being the basis on which non-arising turns means that it is the basis on which all afflictions and all their residual traces do not arise and do not continue. If it were not so — if this turning of the basis were not attained — then all the afflictions and their residues would still find their conditions assembling, and their not arising and not continuing could not be had at all. Being the fruit of having well examined what is to be known means that this turning of the basis is the fruit of having thoroughly penetrated the reality of what is to be known, the suchness of what is to be known. In the causal stage the buddha examined the dependently arisen reality of everything knowable, the world of beings and the world that contains them, and realized the suchness that is the emptiness of person and of phenomena; and the fruit of that is the three bodies, the four knowledges, the purified sphere of phenomena, and the purified buddha land. If it were not so, then the own nature of the buddhas would have to be examined further, would have something further to be severed, and something further to be extinguished — which is not the case, since what has been accomplished there is complete. And being the purity of the sphere of phenomena means that this turning of the basis, because it has been able to sweep away every characteristic, is what a thoroughly purified sphere of phenomena reveals.
