Fascicle 74 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十四 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

The Three Natures Finished, and What Follows

This is the third part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. It carries on from the first verse on the three natures given in the previous fascicle with a second and a third, and with them brings the long Chapter on the Real to a close; the fascicle then determines the Chapter on Awakening — the turning of the basis, why great awakening is inconceivable, ten masteries, and why the Tathāgata alone is a true refuge — the Chapter on the Lineage of Powers, and finally the Chapter on Giving, whose seven purities of giving, each worked out through ten aspects of their own, fill most of the fascicle's second half and bring it to a close exactly where the source lecture-transcript ends.

1. Section: A Second Verse on the Three Natures, and Which of the Five Things Includes Each

This is the third part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. Continuing from the first verse on the three natures given in the previous fascicle, a second summary verse says: what includes them, their being without nature, what is to be known and the rest; the hidden intent and the rest, what they are ranged over by; penetration, and entering upon; their differences, and what they rest upon, last of all. That is nine topics: which of the five things each of the three natures is included in; how three kinds of absence of own nature are set up upon the three natures; what among the three natures is to be fully known, what abandoned forever, what realized; the hidden sense of the three natures with regard to the forbearance of the non-arising of phenomena and the three doors of liberation; which kind of knowledge ranges over each; how penetrating the three natures differs as having or not having characteristics; how, on entering the three natures, one enters upon the perfectly accomplished and thereby sweeps away the dependently arisen; the differences among the three natures; and what the three natures rest upon in arising. Of the three natures and the five things beginning with characteristic — how many of the five things is the first nature included in? None of them at all, since the pervasively imputed nature has neither substance nor characteristic. How many is the second nature included in? Four — characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge — of which characteristic, name, and discrimination are the defiled portion of the dependently arisen and correct knowledge is its purified portion; characteristic and name are what is taken as object, discrimination and correct knowledge are what takes, and all four are conditioned things arisen from conditions. How many is the third nature included in? One: suchness. Then an objection. If the dependently arisen nature is also included in correct knowledge, why was it said earlier that the dependently arisen nature is to be known through the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature? The answer: that passage meant only the defiled portion of the dependently arisen nature, not the purified portion. As for the purified portion, it should be known that it is known through there being no clinging to that. The ordinary person's dependently arisen mind arises and ceases together with the imputing, which is why it is known through that clinging; whereas the noble one's correct knowledge — fundamental knowledge, which is apart from the spoken nature, and the knowledge subsequently attained, which takes up body and world knowing them to be like an illusion — no longer clings to what words set up, and is known through precisely that absence of clinging.

2. Section: How Each Nature Is Without Own Nature, What Is to Be Known, Abandoned, and Realized, and Three Forbearances of Non-Arising

Further, upon the three natures there are three kinds of absence of own nature: the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic, in respect of arising, and in respect of the ultimate. By the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic, the pervasively imputed nature is said to be without own nature — for what naming sets up is established as a characteristic by provisional naming and not by any characteristic of its own. By the absence of own nature in respect of arising, and by the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate, the dependently arisen nature is said to be without own nature: because it does not exist by any nature of its own, and because it is not what purity takes as its object. It arises only by the force of conditions, mind and mental factors from four and form from two, so that what would be arising of itself is simply not there; and when fundamental knowledge realizes suchness, the defiled portion of the dependently arisen does not appear at all, faculties, objects, consciousness, the receptacle world, grasper and grasped, and speech all being absent, since that portion is not what purified knowledge takes. And only by the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate is the perfectly accomplished nature said to be without own nature. Why so? Because this nature is itself the ultimate, and is also what the absence of own nature in all phenomena reveals. Of the three natures, how many are to be fully known? All of them, since anyone who would train must penetrate what is present and what absent in all three. How many are to be abandoned forever? One — the defiled portion of the dependently arisen, the whole chain from ignorance conditioning formations to birth conditioning aging and death, which must be severed for good if one is to leave birth and death and turn the eight consciousnesses into the four knowledges. How many are to be realized? One — the perfectly accomplished, without which no one turns from ordinary person into noble one. Further, by these three natures all the hidden senses of every scripture whose meaning is not drawn out should be settled. That is, the secret speech of the tathāgatas, and the essential meaning of the secret speech that bodhisattvas use throughout the measureless teachings, should all be settled accordingly by these three natures. And by way of example: as the scriptures speak of the forbearance of the non-arising of phenomena, how is it established? It is established by the three natures. By the pervasively imputed nature is established the forbearance that it is unarisen in its very nature; by the dependently arisen nature is established the forbearance that it does not arise of itself; and by the perfectly accomplished nature is established the forbearance that afflictions and the stain of suffering are unarisen. It should be known that this forbearance does not fall back. Take the rope in the dark: the darkness is ignorance, the rope is what conditions produced, and the snake is what imputation adds — there never was a snake, and that is the first forbearance; the rope itself was not there of its own, being made of hemp, and that is the second; and when the darkness lifts, not only is there no snake but the rope too is no longer found, and afflictions and suffering are seen to have never arisen in the suchness that stands revealed, which is the third. Further, the three doors of liberation are likewise established by the three natures: by the pervasively imputed nature is established the door of emptiness, since one takes as object the emptiness of everything words set up; by the dependently arisen nature is established the door of wishlessness, since knowing what conditions produce, one no longer wishes for the dependently arisen of a future life; and by the perfectly accomplished nature is established the door of signlessness, since one takes as object the selflessness of phenomena itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi answers the obvious objection that the previous fascicle said suchness is not called without own nature by any absence of own nature at all, while here the perfectly accomplished is said to be so called by the ultimate one. The two statements are about different things and do not conflict: there the reference was to the perfectly accomplished as the nature things already have, present whether realized or not, which no absence of own nature accounts for; here the reference is to it as what a noble one's knowledge realizes, which shows itself precisely where the imputed has fallen away from the dependently arisen, and so is indeed what the absence of own nature reveals. He also notes that of everything the world offers, only this is finally secure — every fortune turns and every undertaking ends, whereas one who has reached these forbearances does not fall back to being an ordinary person again.

3. Section: Which Knowledge Ranges Over Each Nature, What Penetration Enters, and What It Sweeps Away

The pervasively imputed nature — what kind of knowledge ranges over it? An ordinary person's knowledge, or a noble one's? No knowledge at all ranges over it, since it has no characteristic. Having neither substance nor characteristic, and being just what the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic covers, the nature that words set up is not the objective range of anyone's knowing. The dependently arisen nature — what kind of knowledge ranges over it? It is ranged over by two knowledges, but not by transcendent noble knowledge. It is the objective range of the ordinary person's knowledge, who by naming takes up the aggregates and the receptacle world and every characteristic, name, and discrimination within them; and it is equally the objective range of the knowledge subsequently attained, which takes up the same aggregates and world but, the seeds of affliction having been severed, knows all these conditioned things to be like an illusion and no longer holds name or characteristic to be real. Only when transcendent correct knowledge realizes suchness do the characteristics not appear at all, and so it is not ranged over by that. The perfectly accomplished nature — what kind of knowledge ranges over it? Only a noble one's knowledge ranges over it. When practitioners of contemplation penetrate the pervasively imputed nature, should it be said that their knowledge operates upon characteristics, or without characteristics? If they penetrate it by worldly knowledge, it should be said to operate upon characteristics; if by transcendent knowledge, it should be said to operate without characteristics. An ordinary person contemplating the emptiness of what naming sets up still has the characteristic of emptiness standing before him; and the subsequently attained knowledge, contemplating that same emptiness, still approaches it carrying a characteristic, though it no longer clings to the characteristic of emptiness. Only correct knowledge directly realizing suchness operates without characteristics. As with the pervasively imputed nature, so should it be known of the dependently arisen and the perfectly accomplished. If a practitioner of contemplation truly enters the pervasively imputed nature, what nature should be said to be entered upon? The perfectly accomplished. To enter it truly is to know that the spoken nature of everything is finally empty, and in that very moment correct knowledge is directly realizing suchness. And if the practitioner enters upon the perfectly accomplished nature, what nature should be said to be swept away? The dependently arisen — for when correct knowledge directly realizes suchness, the defiled dependently arisen, characteristic and name and discrimination, no longer appears, like a sky with no cloud in it. How many kinds does the pervasively imputed nature have? Whatever the measure of the own natures and differences set up and established within the dependently arisen nature, just that is the measure of the pervasively imputed nature as well. Therefore it should be known that the pervasively imputed nature has measureless differences — for what conditions produce runs through the sentient and the insentient and fills the ten directions without limit, and naming keeps pace with it exactly. And further, within the dependently arisen nature it should be known that there are two kinds of clinging to the pervasively imputed nature: one that accompanies awareness, and one that is the latent tendency of habitual perfuming — the first being the clinging as it is presently active, in one at home in language who takes up a characteristic through its name, and the second being its seeds, by whose force even an infant or an animal, with no words available, takes up objects as real and perfumes those seeds further.

4. Section: Two Kinds of Dependently Arisen Nature, What Each Nature Rests On, and a Third Verse

How many kinds does the dependently arisen nature have? It should be known to be as various as the kinds and classes of characteristic — and those, as an earlier fascicle set out at length, are measureless. Further, there are two kinds of dependently arisen nature: that which the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature raises, and that which is raised where there is no such clinging. The first is the defiled portion: body and receptacle world, the view of self and conceit, greed and hatred and delusion, all produced by the eight discriminations and their clinging — affliction, action, and suffering in all their varieties. The second is the purified portion: when hearing, reflecting, and cultivating bring one to non-discriminating knowledge, discrimination turns into correct knowledge, and the defiled dependently arisen of body and world is no longer to be had; broadly, this covers transcendent correct knowledge, the knowledge at once worldly and transcendent that follows it, and the pure land such knowledge brings about. At the first ground the true path of seeing comes first, fundamental knowledge directly realizing suchness; and afterward, on the path of seeing by resemblance, that knowledge fashions an image of suchness and of the measureless differences of action and result — and it is upon this subsequently attained knowledge that there come to be buddhas to speak of, and teachings, and pure lands, and beings. How many kinds does the perfectly accomplished nature have? Because in every place it is of one taste, the perfectly accomplished nature has no established number of classes or divisions. Then, what should each be said to rest upon? The pervasively imputed nature should be said to rest upon three things: characteristic, name, and discrimination. Mental discrimination is what imputes, characteristic and name are what is imputed, and out of the meeting of these the false construction is made and the spoken nature of everything is held to be real. The dependently arisen nature should be said to rest upon the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature and upon its own outflow — that is, upon the discriminating mind that holds the spoken nature of everything to be real, which in brief divides into the eight discriminations, and upon the seeds which that mind's perfuming has laid down, from which the dependently arisen in turn arises. And the perfectly accomplished nature should be said to abide nowhere and rest upon nothing, being the suchness of phenomena, unconditioned, enduring, and leaning on nothing at all. Further, a third summary verse says: whether they are absent or present, and their work; the subtle and the rest, and being without substance; arising, clinging and the rest, knowing them rightly; defilement and suffering, similes, and discriminating. That is ten topics: whether each of the three natures is absent or present; what work each does; how they differ as coarse and subtle; how having or not having substance differs in what it does; how arising and not arising differ in what they do; how they differ as involving clinging or not; how the three natures are rightly known; how they differ as to defilement and suffering; the three natures set out through similes; and how the names and senses of the three natures arise differently.

Master Cháng Bǎi uses the two dependently arisen natures to state how the three natures stand to one another. Subjectively there are two, the imputed that a construing consciousness raises and the accomplished that knowledge reveals — the first is the ordinary person's condition, the second the buddhas'.

5. Section: What Would Go Wrong If Any of the Three Were Missing, and the Five Works Each Performs

If there were no pervasively imputed nature, what would go wrong? Within the dependently arisen nature there would be no naming and no clinging to naming — naming here meaning the activity and clinging to naming meaning its latent seeds. And if there were none of that, defilement and purity could not be known. For it is with this imputed nature as cause that the dependently arisen of a future life arises and is stained by the defilements of affliction, action, and rebirth; and it is by not clinging to the imputed nature upon the dependently arisen and the perfectly accomplished — by the knowledge in which speech does not perfume, in which speech does not accompany awareness, in which speech has no latency left — that the dependently arisen characteristic ceases and the purified perfectly accomplished is realized. Not knowing the difference between defilement and purity, one could neither sever the one nor realize the other; and so it is out of compassion that the pervasively imputed nature is set up at all, to show beings the shape of their own construing, and thereby to free them from the bondage of it. If there were no dependently arisen nature, what would go wrong? Then without any effort at all, every defilement would simply not exist — nobody would need to cultivate moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom; and if there were no defilement, there would be no purity that could be known either. What conditions produce is the support of both defiled and pure phenomena, and without it neither could be spoken of. If there were no perfectly accomplished nature, what would go wrong? Then nothing of the purified class could be known at all. How many works can the pervasively imputed nature perform? Five. First, it can produce the dependently arisen nature. Second, upon that nature it can raise speech. Third, it can produce the clinging to a person. Fourth, it can produce the clinging to phenomena. Fifth, it can take up the perfuming and the heaviness of those two clingings — laying down in the storehouse consciousness the seeds of clinging to self and to phenomena, and with them the heaviness of the two obstructions, by which a being shows himself unwieldy, stiff, obstructed, feeble, and unable to turn freely, going along with defilement and against purity. How many works can the dependently arisen nature perform? Also five. First, it can produce all defiled phenomena, since it is the consciousnesses arisen from conditions that present body and world and the whole of affliction, action, and suffering. Second, it can serve as the support of both the pervasively imputed nature and the perfectly accomplished nature — held to be real upon it, that is the imputed; with the imputed gone from it, the emptiness that shows is the accomplished. Third, it can serve as the support of the clinging to a person. Fourth, it can serve as the support of the clinging to phenomena, since the moment a being's mind stirs it spontaneously takes faculties, objects, consciousness, and world to be real. Fifth, it can serve as the support of the perfuming and heaviness of those two clingings. How many works can the perfectly accomplished nature perform? Also five — because it is the objective sphere by taking which as object there arises what counters those two sets of five works. Taking the perfectly accomplished as object, the noble one's transcendent correct knowledge arises, and that knowledge is precisely what answers the five works of the imputed and the five of the dependently arisen.

6. Section: Coarse and Subtle, Which Has Substance and Which Turns Into What, and Which Arises

Should the pervasively imputed nature be called subtle or coarse? It should be called subtle — because it has followed beings from beginningless time and never left them, so that the moment an ordinary person's mind stirs the imputing is there of itself, while its substance is in fact empty and no knowledge ranges over it, and a worldly mind cannot notice that emptiness. And as with subtle, so should it be known of hard to see and hard to understand: its characteristic, the own natures and differences that naming sets up, is empty, and ordinary knowledge does not see it; and though it has neither substance nor characteristic, it does have its work, and ordinary knowledge does not make that out. Should the dependently arisen nature be called subtle or coarse? It should be called coarse. Its substance is the forms and mental events produced from seeds and having consciousness for their nature; these are what the six consciousnesses take as object, the very things on which seeing, hearing, sensing, and knowing rest, and so they are things with substance, ranged over by ordinary knowledge and by worldly knowledge subsequently attained. And yet it is hard to see and hard to understand. Its characteristic is hard to see, for one cannot with a mind still clinging make out that the dependently arisen is momentary and impermanent, like a conjured thing, an image, an echo, a shadow, the moon in water, a mirage, a dream — appearing as something it is not; and its work is hard to understand, since ordinary knowledge does not make out how it produces all defiled things and supports both the imputed and the accomplished. Should the perfectly accomplished nature be called subtle or coarse? It should be called extremely subtle. No ordinary person penetrates it at all, and even the noble ones of the two vehicles do not fully make it out. And as with extremely subtle, so should it be known of extremely hard to see and extremely hard to understand — its characteristic being no characteristic at all, and its work, which counters the ten works of the other two, being invisible to anyone but knowledge. Of these three natures, how many are without substance and yet turn into what has substance? One: the pervasively imputed, which has neither substance nor characteristic, and yet, being what a mind takes as object, raises the clinging of a dependently arisen mind, and through that the whole future of affliction, action, and suffering. How many have substance and turn into what has substance and what does not? One: the dependently arisen, which by the outflow of its own seeds produces further dependently arisen things, and which by serving as the support of the imputed produces what has no substance at all. How many have substance and yet turn into nothing? One: the perfectly accomplished, which is the nature phenomena have, unchanging, what correct knowledge takes as object, unconditioned and apart from all elaboration, and so cannot serve as the condition from which anything else arises. Of these three natures, how many do not arise and yet produce what arises? One — the imputed, unarisen in itself and yet the object upon which clinging is raised and the future arisings that follow. How many arise and produce both what arises and what does not? One — the dependently arisen, which produces the defiled things that arise and also supports the imputed, which does not. And how many neither arise nor produce what arises or does not? One — the perfectly accomplished, which is not something produced from conditions, produces nothing produced from conditions, and produces no imputing either.

7. Section: How to Tell Whether One Is Clinging — Clinging That Is Awake and Clinging That Is Only Latent

How should the clinging and the freedom from clinging with regard to the pervasively imputed nature be known? There are two kinds: clinging or freedom from clinging that is awake, and clinging or freedom from clinging that is latent. If, by provisionally setting up names through speech, one imputes to things a settled own nature, this should be known as the clinging that is awake. If one understands well that there is only a name — and, knowing it to be only a name, does not take those things to have a settled nature — this should be known as freedom from clinging with regard to it. Say the words "sour plum" and there is no plum in the mouth; the words are a token for a fruit that conditions have assembled, and do not deliver the fruit's own dependently arisen shape at all. Knowing that every name is only a name, with no thing standing behind it as the name announces, is what freedom from that clinging means. If one has not pulled out the latent tendency of its perfuming, it should be known that there is latent clinging with regard to it — that is, for as long as the perfuming and the heaviness have not been let go, so that even when no naming is active there is still a spontaneous clinging to the appearances consciousness presents, and the mind carries an unwieldiness it cannot shake. And if that has been cut off forever, it should be known that there is freedom from clinging. How should the clinging and the freedom from clinging with regard to the dependently arisen nature be known? If, because of the awake clinging to the pervasively imputed nature, one further imputes the nature that clinging has produced, this is called the first clinging. That is: the imputing mind, active in naming, takes as its object a dependently arisen thing under its name, seizes within it the appearance that answers to the name — the semblance of a meaning — and then raises a settled conviction that the self and the phenomena which speech points to are really so; and that conviction is the clinging, which in turn perfumes the seeds of the perceiving and perceived aspects and produces the dependently arisen of the future. If one understands well that there are only the various appearances, and does not impute the nature that clinging has produced, this is called freedom from clinging. Knowing that what naming presents is only what the perfumed seeds of the perceiving and perceived aspects raise, only illusory shapes, only a semblance of meaning, one no longer imputes a real substance to what conditions produce, and the discriminated imputing does not become active. And if the bondage of characteristics has not been pulled out for good, then whenever anything is attained within the characteristics, that is called the second clinging — for as long as the seeds remain, the mind's binding to characteristics arises of itself, and something is always being had within them.

8. Section: Overweening Pride About What Has Not Been Realized, and How the Imputed Nature Is Rightly Known

If the bondage of characteristics has been pulled out for good, then, rightly knowing the realm without characteristics, one attains nothing within characteristics; or, at a later time, attaining what there is just as it is, one should be known to be free of clinging. Two things are meant. When fundamental knowledge realizes suchness, the bondage both to what naming presents and to what naming rested on is gone, and no characteristic appears at all. And when the knowledge subsequently attained comes forward, body and world do appear again just as they are and are indeed attained — but they are known as illusory appearance, and no real self or real phenomenon is clung to in them; one is amid characteristics and apart from them, apart from names, apart from speech, apart from what the mind takes hold of. How should the clinging and the freedom from clinging with regard to the perfectly accomplished nature be known? There is no clinging here, since this realm is not a place where clinging can find its footing — it is the emptiness that the selflessness of phenomena reveals, unconditioned, without shape or mark, and nothing there can be taken hold of. But if, with regard to this realm not attained, not touched, not realized, someone raises the overweening pride of having attained, touched, and realized it, that should be known to be the pervasively imputed, and a clinging upon the dependently arisen nature. One who has not yet severed the seeds of the two obstructions has not attained it; one who has not yet personally met and received it has not touched it; one who has not yet reached the nirvāṇa that is its fruit has not realized it. To reach an absorption and take oneself to have thereby attained, touched, and realized the perfectly accomplished is exactly the overweening pride the scriptures name: the characteristic of suchness is still standing there and one feels that emptiness has been realized, while neither the bondage of characteristics nor the heaviness of the seeds has been severed at all. As the elder said in the scripture, having heard the various accounts other elders gave of what they had understood: all these venerable ones are carrying overweening pride, and being held by it cannot understand the ultimate truth whose one taste pervades everything. And how should the pervasively imputed nature be known? One should rightly know that there is only its name and only the imputing; that it is without characteristic, without nature, without arising, without ceasing, without defilement, without purity, quiescent from the beginning and of its own nature nirvāṇa; that it is not past, not future, not present; not bound to the realms and not freed from them; not a bondage and not a liberation; not painful, not pleasant, and not neither painful nor pleasant; that it is of one taste alone and, pervading every place, is like space. In the rope taken for a snake there is no snake, so one cannot ask whether the snake is past, future, or present, or whether it is painful or pleasant, or bound or free. By these and measureless other aspects the pervasively imputed nature should be rightly known.

10. Section: How the Perfectly Accomplished Is Known, Which Nature Defiles, and Three Similes — Space, an Enemy, and a Buried Treasure

How should the perfectly accomplished nature be known? One should rightly know it by the differing aspects already set out — suchness, the real limit, the sphere of phenomena, and measureless differences of that kind. Those names were given before: suchness, since it is true and does not change; real nature, the true nature that ordinary person and noble one alike possess; the nature of truth, which only a noble one realizes; not-inverted and un-inverted; the realm without elaboration; the realm without characteristics; the sphere of phenomena, sphere here meaning cause, since it is the cause from which the qualities of noble ones arise; and the real limit, real because free of inversion and limit because nothing lies beyond it, so that having reached it one has nowhere further to seek. And one should further know the remaining differences: it is without shape or color; it cannot be looked at; it rests on nothing and abides nowhere; it reaches out to nothing as its object; it cannot be made to appear; it cannot be discerned by a discriminating mind; it cannot be brought about by doing anything; it cannot be declared in speech; it is apart from all elaboration; there is nothing in it to take up and nothing to put down — and differences of this kind are measureless. Of these three natures, how many are not themselves defiled and yet make other things defiled? One: the pervasively imputed, which having neither substance nor characteristic can be called neither defiled nor pure, and yet, becoming the object a mind takes up, makes the dependently arisen defiled. How many are defiled in themselves alone? 11. Section: Why Each Nature Is Named as It Is, and Five Things Non-Discriminating Wisdom Is Not

Why is the pervasively imputed nature said to impute? Because of the dependently arisen nature — for it is upon the appearances that consciousness presents and the names that speech expresses, joined to one another, that the clinging to a self and to phenomena is raised. Why is the dependently arisen nature said to depend on other things? Because of causes and conditions: this being so, that is; this arising, that arises. Why is the perfectly accomplished nature said to be perfectly accomplished? Because it is not stained by any affliction or any mass of suffering, and further because it is enduring — pure in its own nature, with the afflictions and suffering alike stilled, and unconditioned and unchanging besides. Now a further question. It has been said that the wisdom which takes up the real is non-discriminating. How should its non-discriminating character be known? Is it by not directing attention? Is it by having passed beyond applied and sustained thought? Is it by there being nothing there at all? Is it by its being of that sort by nature? Or is it by making an effort upon the object it takes? If it were by not directing attention, then its being joined to attention in accordance with reason would not stand to reason — and deep sleep and raving drunkenness would qualify, which is plainly a fault. If it were by having passed beyond applied and sustained thought, how would that not contradict the holy teaching, which says that all the minds and mental factors of the three realms are discrimination? From the second absorption upward the wish for applied and sustained thought is gone, but discrimination is not, and a grasper and a grasped remain. If it were by there being nothing there at all, how would this wisdom not turn out to be no mental factor at all — and what then would make it wisdom? Those who enter the absorption without perception have no wisdom to show for it and may come out of it holding that things happen without causes; and the absorption of cessation, though it stills the evolving consciousnesses, is provisionally established upon that stilling and is not itself the entering into suchness. If it were by its being of that sort by nature, how would this wisdom not turn out to have the nature of form, insensible as mountains and tables are, and not to be a penetrating realization at all? And if it were by making an effort upon the object it takes, how would that not slander non-discriminating wisdom as being apart from effort? Before it, at the causal stage, one certainly cultivates the effort of contemplating the emptiness of person and of phenomena; but when suchness is actually realized, grasper and grasped are gone and no effort is being made. If all of these fail to stand to reason, how then is non-discriminating wisdom to be known? Because it is apart from effort with regard to the object it takes.

12. Section: What Non-Discriminating Wisdom Actually Is, the Close of the Chapter on the Real, and Why a Bodhisattva's Power Is Inconceivable

How is non-discriminating wisdom to be known? Because it is apart from effort with regard to the object it takes. At that moment neither grasper nor grasped appears, so one cannot say that any searching, thinking, or directing of attention is being done upon the object. This object it takes is the suchness of phenomena, apart from any characteristic of presence or absence; and just this is also apart from all discrimination — not only is the pervasively imputed gone, but faculties, objects, consciousness, the receptacle world, grasper and grasped, and speech, the whole defiled dependently arisen, is swept away with it; the perceived aspect is gone and the perceiving mind is nowhere to be found. And because it is drawn forth by the force of what came before, then although it is apart from effort, when the wondrous wisdom joined to concentration upon suchness arises, it can illuminate and take up the characteristic it has as object then and there. The force that came before is the preparatory training: at warmth one contemplates that the referent is nowhere to be had and there is only consciousness, sealing the emptiness of the object; at summit the contemplation deepens; at forbearance one enters the absence of the referent and penetrates that the imputed characteristic has no own nature; and at the highest worldly state one penetrates that the dependently arisen mind which discriminates is empty as well — from which, in a single uninterrupted moment, the path of seeing is entered. And there the knowing wisdom is without discrimination and the suchness it knows is without discrimination, and emptiness is realized directly and taken up — not blank like a log, for the mind is perfectly clear, seeing and yet without a characteristic, taking the emptiness straight on with no false hold of grasper and grasped. Therefore this wisdom is called non-discriminating. So the determination of the section on the real has been given. Further, by five causes it should be known that all the majestic power a bodhisattva has is inconceivable. What are the five? First, because a bodhisattva's majestic power passes beyond every field that searching thought can reach. Second, because no simile in the world can be found for it. Third, because it belongs only to a mind well polished — a mind that has attained mastery and is free of the stains of the two obstructions, held by concentration, clear and unclouded and able to do great good for beings, as a mirror surface polished to the utmost shows every image in it. Fourth, because it shares nothing at all with a mind not established in concentration. And fifth, because it belongs entirely to a mind established in concentration.

13. Section: Ten Ways of Practicing the Teaching, and the Opening of Great Awakening

Further, within the Great Vehicle there are ten ways of practicing the teaching, by which a bodhisattva can bring beings to ripeness. What are the ten? That is: copying or having copied and circulating the scriptures with a reverent mind; honoring with incense and flowers the place or the shrine where they are kept; gladly passing volumes on to others rather than keeping them; attending closely when someone is teaching, so that having gained understanding oneself one can also ripen others; reading over what one trusts and loves; going back over what one has read until it is understood, received, and not forgotten; reciting it aloud so that others are glad to hear it; opening it out for others according to what each can take in; withdrawing to a quiet place to weigh the sense again and again until one's view is straight; and finally taking the sense one has grasped as the object of calming and insight, and so accomplishing concentration and the noble path. 14. Section: The Four Aspects of the Turning of the Basis

Being the basis on which arising turns means that it is the basis on which the transcendent path arises and continues in the continuum of a buddha. Arising means the transcendent path coming up; turning means its continuing on. The buddha's continuum is the body of the teaching, and it is upon this that the reflections of the knowledges of the śrāvaka vehicle, of the solitary-realizer vehicle, and of the unsurpassed Great Vehicle all arise, serving as a powerful condition for each, so that those of each disposition find their way out along their own vehicle. If it were not so — if this turning of the basis were not attained — that path ought neither to arise nor to continue. And if that path could occur apart from it, then before the turning of the basis this path ought to have been there already, which does not stand to reason. Being the basis on which non-arising turns means that it is the basis on which all afflictions and all their residual traces do not arise and do not continue. If it were not so — if this turning of the basis were not attained — then all the afflictions and their residues would still find their conditions assembling, and their not arising and not continuing could not be had at all. Being the fruit of having well examined what is to be known means that this turning of the basis is the fruit of having thoroughly penetrated the reality of what is to be known, the suchness of what is to be known. In the causal stage the buddha examined the dependently arisen reality of everything knowable, the world of beings and the world that contains them, and realized the suchness that is the emptiness of person and of phenomena; and the fruit of that is the three bodies, the four knowledges, the purified sphere of phenomena, and the purified buddha land. If it were not so, then the own nature of the buddhas would have to be examined further, would have something further to be severed, and something further to be extinguished — which is not the case, since what has been accomplished there is complete. And being the purity of the sphere of phenomena means that this turning of the basis, because it has been able to sweep away every characteristic, is what a thoroughly purified sphere of phenomena reveals.

15. Section: Why Great Awakening Is Inconceivable — Neither Identical With Nor Apart From Anything, and Nowhere in Particular

If it were not so, this would have to be impermanent and would have to be conceivable. But this turning of the basis has the character of enduring, and is inconceivable. Further, this inconceivability is called being without two, and should be known by five aspects: by own nature, by place, by abiding, by being one or being several, and by what it accomplishes. Being without two does not mean being merely one thing; it means being apart from false discrimination, with the standing oppositions dissolved, the elaborations of speech cut off, and the equal and undivided entered into — as it was said that awakening has no second characteristic, and so is without two and apart from two. How is it inconceivable by own nature? It is inconceivable whether it is identical with form or apart from form; and inconceivable whether it is identical with feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness or apart from them. It is inconceivable whether it is identical with the earth element or apart from the earth element; and inconceivable whether it is identical with the water, fire, and wind elements or apart from them. It is inconceivable whether it is identical with the sphere of the eye or apart from it; and inconceivable whether it is identical with the spheres of ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind or apart from them. And it is inconceivable whether it exists or does not exist. How is it inconceivable by place? It is inconceivable whether it is in the desire realm or apart from the desire realm; and inconceivable whether it is in the form and formless realms or apart from them — great awakening passes beyond the three realms and is not separate from them, neither the same as them nor other than them. It is inconceivable whether it is among human beings or apart from human beings, and whether it is among the gods or apart from the gods; so that where it is accomplished cannot be settled. 16. Section: How a Buddha Abides, Whether Buddhas Are One or Many, and Two Reasons Nothing Here Can Be Thought

How is it inconceivable by abiding? Abiding in such and such a kind of pleasant abiding — inconceivable. Abiding in such and such a kind of calm abiding — inconceivable. Abiding in an abiding with mind — inconceivable; abiding in an abiding without mind — inconceivable. Abiding in such a kind of noble abiding — inconceivable; abiding in such a kind of divine abiding or brahmā abiding — inconceivable. Kind here means the whole apparatus by which anyone else enters an absorption: the name of the practice, the aspects attended to, the signs that come before entering, by which a practitioner knows that he will shortly enter a particular absorption and by which his teacher knows it of him. Others enter their absorptions that way, taking up a sign well and finding many such marks along the road. A tathāgata does not; no such marks are to be found at all, because his mastery is complete, so that what practice he is in and how he entered it simply cannot be told. How is being one or being several inconceivable? All buddhas abide together in one uncontaminated realm; whether that is being one or being several is inconceivable. In the uncontaminated realm everything is equal and undifferentiated, which speaks for one; and yet buddhas differ in the disposition they set out from, in the preparatory practice that follows from it, and in when they attain — and if there were only one buddha, the provisions another gathered would have been laboured for nothing, the teaching that leads to buddhahood would be missing from what buddhas teach, and there could be no first buddha at all, since each becomes one by hearing the teaching from another before him, without beginning behind and without end ahead. So they are neither one nor several, and that is inconceivable. How is it inconceivable by what it accomplishes? Such and such tathāgatas, of the same realm, the same knowledge, the same strength, the same valour, abiding in the uncontaminated realm and relying on this turning of the basis, are able to do what benefits all beings — and that is inconceivable. Their nature being equal is what the same realm means, and their wisdom, strength, and majesty being equal is what the same knowledge, strength, and valour mean. Further, by two causes it should be known to be inconceivable. Because its sense is apart from speech, and because it passes beyond the road of language, it is inconceivable — for wanting to speak, one must first search out a thought, and it is from that thought that speech arises, and this is beyond such searching altogether. And further, because it is transcendent, there is nothing in the world that can serve as its simile; therefore it is inconceivable.

17. Section: Ten Masteries, and the Four Manifestations by Which a Buddha Works

What is the capacity of great awakening? In brief, there are ten masteries, and these are called its capacity — mastery over lifespan and the rest. As the Basic Section has already set them out, they are: mastery over lifespan, so that one keeps or relinquishes one's life at will and abides in the world as long as one pleases; mastery over mind, so that one enters, remains in, and emerges from any absorption or liberation at will, and the mind is not stained by the world or turned by its objects; mastery over the requisites, so that a moment's attention brings food, clothing, bedding, and medicine into being, and every craft the world knows is at one's command; mastery over action, so that bodily, verbal, and mental deeds proceed unobstructed and the six faculties do one another's work without any of them ceasing to be itself; mastery over birth, so that one takes birth in whatever destiny and place one chooses, unruled by the force of contaminated action; mastery over aspiration, so that every excellent wish is fulfilled; mastery over conviction, so that whatever one resolves upon comes about exactly so, earth becoming gold and water becoming fire at the mind's resolve; mastery over spiritual power, so that the five superknowledges — passing through space, knowing others' minds, and the rest — are entirely free; mastery over the teaching, so that every name, phrase, and syllable is at one's disposal and the setting out of the teaching is skillful in every case; and mastery over knowledge, so that whatever one wishes to know within the field of the knowable is known as it is. These ten come of the six perfections brought to fullness: the first three from giving, in its three kinds of teaching, fearlessness, and goods; action and birth from moral discipline; conviction from patience, since one who bore with beings and went along with what they wished attains a state in which things go along with his own mind; aspiration from energy, since one who never left off what he had begun attains a state in which what he intends is accomplished; spiritual power from concentration, which is what draws the superknowledges forth; and knowledge and the teaching from wisdom. And they answer to ten fears: the fear of death, of the stain of afflictions, of poverty, of evil deeds, of the lower destinies, of not getting what one seeks, of the offence of slandering the teaching, of not making a living while bound to one's pursuits, of the doubt that keeps asking why it should be so, and of an assembly's imposing presence. What is the effortful working of great awakening? In brief, there are four manifestations, and these are called its working: first, in order to ripen beings not yet ripened, manifesting the practice of a bodhisattva; second, in order to liberate beings already ripened, manifesting a tathāgata who is taken up skillfully at one and the same moment throughout a hundred myriads of Jambudvīpas in the three-thousandfold great thousandfold world; third, manifesting a śrāvaka for those same beings to be trained; and fourth, manifesting a solitary realizer for those same beings to be trained.

18. Section: The Manifestations Reach Everywhere, and Two Kinds of Turning Outward

It should be known that all the buddhas, the blessed ones, have unobstructed capacity in these four kinds of manifestation throughout the worlds of the ten directions. The commentators sort the working of that capacity into manifestations of body, of speech, and of mind: of body, displaying supernormal power, displaying taking birth, and displaying the fruits of action; of speech, words that console, words that skillfully lead, and words that expound; of mind, deciding, composing, initiating, and receiving. These three are what the knowledge that accomplishes what is to be done sets in motion, and answer to the three wonders of supernormal power, of admonition, and of prediction; and because all three are undertaken with the ripening of beings in view, they are called the first skillful means, on account of which the scriptures call a buddha the great medicine of wisdom that can cure every disease of affliction. What is the turning outward? It should be known that this turning is in brief of two kinds: a turning for the time being, and a final turning. The turning for the time being means that so long as beings have not yet been ripened and not yet been liberated, the buddhas, the blessed ones, have a turning that manifests — showing to bodhisattvas before the grounds a coarser body of manifestation and to those on the grounds a subtler body of enjoyment for others' sake, changing and arising and ceasing as each occasion requires, in the four kinds of manifestation already described. It is provisional in that it is not there at all times. The final turning means that as the inexhaustible and inconceivable great light of the buddhas' own nature turns, so it turns as the doing of everything that is to be done for all beings. This is the body a buddha enjoys for himself, complete and filling the sphere of phenomena, each of its faculties pervading that whole sphere and its light pervading it too. It is called a turning because it is a conditioned excellence and arises and ceases moment by moment; and it is called final because to the very end of birth and death it continues without break or interval, pure, luminous, and always so.

19. Section: The Turning Back, Closing the Five Aspects of Great Awakening, and Why Praising the Buddha Benefits the One Who Praises

What is the turning back? It should be known that this too is of two kinds: a turning back for the time being, and a final turning back. The turning back for the time being means that when the beings a buddha has been training are already ripened and already liberated, then, without interval, the buddhas, the blessed ones, manifest final nirvāṇa — and this is not final cessation. The final turning back means that the afflictions and every residual trace of them are exhausted once for all, and with them the suffering that had depended on them is exhausted as well — a buddha abiding at every moment in the nirvāṇa that rests nowhere. These, then, are the five aspects — own nature, capacity, working, turning outward, and turning back — by which great awakening should be known. Why is it that one who praises the Tathāgata can thereby do the Tathāgata some good? Whatever praise is offered simply carries out the work of one's own benefit; it does not, by the praising, make the Tathāgata into anything other than what he is, the way a potter's work makes a pot. Why so? Because the tathāgatas keep their own good qualities hidden and want for almost nothing, and are not made glad by being praised nor made unhappy by being disparaged, remaining entirely at their own ease. Further, there are two wonderful things that come of praising and extolling the Tathāgata: it lets the one praising practice pure self-benefit and generate immeasurable merit; and it accomplishes a good even for the Tathāgata, who himself seeks nothing at all. Praise clears away the obstruction that comes from wrong speech — lying, idle chatter, harsh words, and slander — and by that alone produces immeasurable merit for the one who offers it; and when a being praises the Buddha, that being goes on to gain unobstructed eloquence, and to teach others and benefit them is exactly what the Buddha has always wished for beings, so that offering praise itself becomes a way of doing the Buddha good and of making an offering to him, from which vast and inexhaustible merit is produced. Further, the tathāgatas have two kinds of thing that beings do: things they do not approve of, and things they approve of. When beings do not carry out their own benefit, that is what the Tathāgata does not approve of; when they praise him and thereby take up their own benefit, that is what the Tathāgata approves of.

20. Section: Six Aspects of the Tathāgata's Qualities — Perfection, and Freedom from Seven Stains

Further, by six aspects the qualities of the Tathāgata should be known in brief: perfection, freedom from stain, unshakability, having no equal, the working of benefit for beings, and capacity. What is perfection? It means that the tathāgatas have accomplished every quality there is, throughout the three realms and transcending them; and their transcendent qualities pass beyond every path that language can travel, so that no hymn of praise can ever reach them — and it is for this very reason that they deserve to be praised all the more. A companion treatise distinguishes five words for this: first, meaning perfected in the good conduct that belongs to the desire realm; venerable, meaning perfected in the good conduct of the form realm; supreme, meaning perfected in the good conduct of the formless realm; highest, meaning surpassing the good of the entire worldly three realms; and unsurpassed, meaning the transcendent good perfected beyond what any noble one of the two vehicles or any bodhisattva can exceed. What is freedom from stain? All qualities are subject to seven kinds of stain: desire, views, doubt, conceit, arrogance, latent tendencies, and stinginess — and the Tathāgata is entirely free of every one of them forever. Desire-stain means wanting others to know of one's qualities; the Tathāgata's qualities are never sought to be publicized, for he does not desire that others come to know what he has accomplished. View-stain means clinging to one's qualities with a fixed opinion; the Tathāgata has no such clinging toward any quality at all. Doubt-stain means wavering over whether something is truly a quality or truly a fault; the Tathāgata, possessing the knowledge of all aspects, is never in doubt over any of his own accomplishments. Conceit-stain means measuring one's own qualities against another's and looking down on him; the Tathāgata never sets his qualities beside anyone else's. Arrogance-stain means growing intoxicated and elated over one's own excellent state; the Tathāgata never contemplates his qualities in a way that produces such agitation or self-satisfaction. Latent-tendency-stain means that the affliction and its habitual residue still cling unnoticed to one's qualities; the Tathāgata has destroyed the afflictions and their residual traces forever, and knows all things as neither truly existent nor wholly non-existent, illusory appearances, so that nothing clings. And stinginess-stain means being unwilling that others attain what one has attained oneself; the Tathāgata has no stinginess at all toward his qualities, wishing every being to share in and realize what he has realized, never wishing to remain alone at the summit while others stay always beneath him.

21. Section: Unshakability, No Equal, Working for Others, and Capacity — Closing the Six Aspects

What is unshakability? It means that no non-Buddhist teacher can shake the Tathāgata's qualities, that no army of demons can shake them, that no thief can seize them, that no kinsman can ruin them, that no king can ruin them, that fire, water, and wind cannot alter them, and that even when this life's span is exhausted there is no falling away — because the qualities of the tathāgatas are inexhaustible, they are unshakable. The teaching and the realization a buddha holds cannot be turned back by any non-Buddhist argument, and no army of afflictions, of death, of the aggregates, or of the celestial tempter can disturb a mind so thoroughly settled. What a buddha has accomplished is the noble wealth of the transcendent, which produces a joy without dread, without enmity, without calamity, without burning, able to sever the great suffering of lives to come — and for that reason no thief, no kinsman, no king, no fire or water or wind can spoil it. And though this life ends, the qualities carried over from it continue without limit into the next, since a buddha's excellence is as inexhaustible as space itself; and by the great vehicle's turning of the basis, mastery over every phenomenon is attained from within, so that these qualities never fall away. What is having no equal? It means that the qualities of the tathāgatas are vast beyond measure, exalted beyond measure, many beyond measure, and of great power, so that no being, pure or impure, has any equal to them — hence, without equal. Their conditioned and unconditioned excellences, the four knowledges together with everything they comprehend and everything the purified sphere of phenomena includes, are vast beyond measure; their standing as the one worthy of offerings, rightly and fully awakened, perfect in knowledge and conduct, well gone, knower of the world, unsurpassed, tamer of persons, teacher of gods and humans, the awakened, the blessed one, is exalted beyond measure; their three bodies, four knowledges, purified sphere of phenomena, ten powers, three foundations of mindfulness, four fearlessnesses, and the rest of the hundred and forty qualities not shared even with the noble ones of the two vehicles or with bodhisattvas are many beyond measure; and the powers of their six superknowledges, the powers of the ten perfections, and the innate power that comes of deeds accumulated over countless lifetimes give them great power. Not even a noble one of the two vehicles or a bodhisattva on the grounds can equal them, still less an ordinary being turning through the impure destinies. What is the working of benefit for beings? It means that a buddha sets aside the vast, faultless ease he has attained and skillfully manifests the effort of benefiting others — knowing well how each of the three vehicles is to be trained and matched to it, showing a subtler responsive body through the knowledge of equality for bodhisattvas on the grounds, and a coarser manifested body through the knowledge of wondrous discernment and the knowledge that accomplishes what is to be done for bodhisattvas before the grounds, for the two vehicles, and for ordinary beings — abiding always in great compassion, watching the world through all six periods of the day and night, working for beings' benefit without effort and without a single break. And what is capacity? It means that in doing what benefits beings, a buddha accomplishes it perfectly without even having to form the intention first, this skillful knowledge being the very kinship of complete realization, ever single-mindedly devoted to it — for the knowledge of equality, the knowledge of wondrous discernment, and the knowledge that accomplishes what is to be done all rest upon the great mirror-like knowledge as their support, and carry the work of benefiting beings on to the very end of the future, constant and never divided in purpose, so that every being may come to share the path.

22. Section: Why the Gods Are Not a Refuge and the Tathāgata Is — By Outward Form, and by Five Further Marks

Why are the gods, by their outward form, not a refuge? Because they are not seen to hold any discourse with beings toward awakening; because their forms are sometimes fierce and fearsome; because they are habituated to heedlessness and given over to craving; because they abandon the benefit of others, having no compassion; and because they do not understand what is to be done and what is not, not having penetrated the real. Why is the Tathāgata, by his outward form, the true refuge? Because he is seen to hold discourse with beings; because his form is calm and composed, inspiring no fear; because he is free of any indulgence, apart from craving; because he never abandons the benefit of beings, having compassion always; and because he understands well what is to be done and what is not, having thoroughly penetrated the real. Further, by five marks the Tathāgata alone is the true refuge. What are the five? First, because he took awakening for the benefit of all beings; second, because he turns well the eye of the true teaching; third, because his benefiting mind is equal toward those who have done him good and those who have wronged him; fourth, because he has given up every clinging attachment to house and home and kin, his faculties utterly stilled; and fifth, because he is able to resolve every doubt skillfully.

23. Section: By Their Deeds, By the Nature of Things, and By Cause and Effect — Why the Gods Cannot Be a Refuge

Why are the gods, by their deeds, not a refuge? Because their work is simply to abide enjoying the pleasures of desire, and deeds harmful to other beings are to be found among them — wars with the demigods, the casting out of the defeated, and the rest. Why is the Tathāgata, by his deeds, the true refuge? Because his work is to abide in vast, stainless absorption, and his work is to bring about the benefit of beings. Why are the gods, by the nature of things, not a refuge? Because every excellent thing, worldly or transcendent, depends on one's own effort alone — even if one paid the gods every honor, without one's own effort nothing would be gained; and even without honoring them at all, effort alone is enough to attain it. Why are the gods, by cause and effect, not a refuge? Is a god's body obtained by the action that brings it about, or by making offerings to the gods, or without any cause at all? If it is obtained by the action that brings it about, then one should take refuge in one's own action, not in the gods. If it is obtained without cause, one should take refuge in causelessness, not in the gods. And if it is obtained by making offerings to the gods, is that body's cause the offering alone, or the god's will alone, or both together? If by offering alone, the gods would be superfluous, since any offering at all would then bring about a god's body. If by the god's will alone, then offerings would be pointless, since a god could grant that body without any offering being made. And if it is said to be both together, meaning that the offering wins the god over so that whatever one wishes for is thereby granted — then this does not stand to reason either, for there are seven things whose fulfillment is never certain: whether one is taken up by the object of one's offering; whether those who place their confidence in it are taken up by that confidence; whether that confidence is itself well founded; whether one thereby attains the most excellent divine body; whether one thereby attains the most excellent wealth and pleasure; whether one thereby overcomes the demigods and every other enemy; and whether one is exempt from eventual death. None of these seven can be counted on, and so it does not stand to reason to hold that a god's body comes from offerings or from a god's favor, whether singly or together.

25. Section: The Provisional and the Real in Form and the Rest, and the Two Natures Read Through Them

If the consciousness built up by the perfuming of naming takes the pervasively imputed nature as its object, then it must be said that this nature is found neither within, nor without, nor between the two; that it is not already arisen, not yet to arise, not now arising; not already ceased, not yet to cease, not now ceasing; that it is quiescent from the beginning and of its own nature nirvāṇa. He adds a line from an earlier fascicle that keeps the whole point in view: broad learning is what lets one know the teaching, keeps one far from wrongdoing, lets go of what is meaningless, and reaches nirvāṇa — so that learning here is never an end in itself but always the first working part of the path.

27. Section: Seven Qualities That Right Learning Ripens Into — Conviction, Compassion, and the Two Wisdoms

What is a bodhisattva's sphere of conviction? It is revealed by confidence and understanding that the Buddhist teachings are real. What is a bodhisattva's sphere of enhancing conviction? It is revealed by confidence and understanding that the Buddhist teachings have excellent qualities. What is a bodhisattva's sphere of resolve? It is revealed by confidence and understanding that the Buddhist teachings can be attained. What is a bodhisattva's sympathy? It is what arises, following upon it, as sorrow felt for beings before whom suffering has already come. What is a bodhisattva's compassion? It is what arises, following upon it, as grief felt for beings before whom the cause of suffering has come. What is a bodhisattva's discernment? It is penetrating, with regard to what is to be known, the nature things actually have. And what is a bodhisattva's knowledge? It is penetrating, with regard to what is to be known, the full extent of everything there is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the first three as one confidence deepening in three stages: believing the teaching is true, then believing it carries real excellence, then believing that one can actually attain it oneself — a companion treatise calls these believing in what is real, believing in what has qualities, and believing in one's own capacity, and notes that the first is confidence in the cause while the latter two are confidence in the fruit. He distinguishes sympathy from compassion by their object: sympathy answers to a being already suffering, compassion to a being still only accumulating the cause of future suffering, so that compassion looks further ahead. And he pairs discernment with knowledge as fundamental and subsequently attained wisdom: discernment, through fundamental knowledge, sees the single undifferentiated nature that all things share — the seven kinds of suchness running through what is defiled and what is pure alike — while knowledge, through the knowledge attained afterward, sees the full particular extent of each thing's own boundary, as the five aggregates or the twelve sense fields are each counted out one by one. Together, he notes, these seven are what learning was for all along: conviction supplies the ground, compassion supplies the direction, and the two wisdoms supply the eyes to walk it by.

Knowing well that giving can ripen other beings, and giving what one has together with the whole assembly so that it too generates immeasurable merit. And toward one who is poor yet delights in giving, sharing one's own wealth so that he too may give; toward one who is not poor but inwardly stingy, likewise giving him wealth so that he may give; and toward one who wishes to make an offering to the three treasures, at once handing over wealth so that he may carry it out. By this cause, through these two approaches, immeasurable merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the deliberate inclusion of "giving even to one of bad conduct" alongside "giving to one of good conduct": purity of knowledge here does not sort recipients into those who deserve help and those who do not, but matches what is given to what genuine need calls for, whatever the recipient's own conduct has been. On bringing beings to ripeness, he draws out why handing wealth to a would-be donor who cannot bring himself to give it, or to one eager to make an offering to the three treasures, counts as much as gathering wealth oneself: since the mind that wants to give is fragile and can change in the next moment, seizing the chance to enable another's generosity the instant it appears is itself an act of timely giving, generating merit through both the enabling and the enabled act together.

37. Section: Five Skillful Means by Which a Bodhisattva Leads Others to Give

What is purity of knowledge through skillful means? It means leading beings to give through instruction; or leading them to give by exerting a compelling force upon them; or leading them to give by first placing them in one's debt; or leading them to give through the very circumstance of one's own birth; or giving through supernormal power.

Chinese Source

六、由方便善巧智清淨者，謂或由教導令行惠施，或彊力逼令行惠施，或領彼恩令行惠施，或由生故令行惠施，或由神力而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purity of knowledge through skillful means? Leading beings to give through instruction; leading them to give by exerting a compelling force upon them; leading them to give by first placing them in one's debt; leading them to give through the very circumstance of one's own birth; or giving through supernormal power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the two named skillful means an earlier fascicle sets behind the second and third of these: the skillful means born of compelling force, where a bodhisattva who holds real authority over a household or a kingdom warns his own kin or subjects that failing to repay their parents' kindness or breaking the precepts will cost them their allowance, food, or standing, so that beings, moved by fear of the penalty, are driven to abandon wrong and take up good even without any wish to; and the skillful means of repaying kindness by kindness first given, where a bodhisattva quietly does a being some real good — supplying a need, rescuing him from danger, curing an illness — and then, when that being wishes to repay the debt he feels, redirects the repayment itself into taking up the precepts and practicing goodness, telling him plainly that no worldly wealth could count as true repayment. On the fourth means he cites a companion fascicle's account of a bodhisattva descending from the Tuṣita heaven to be born into a noble household precisely so that beings, moved to reverence by the birth itself, come to make offerings gladly — showing that even the circumstance of one's birth can be turned to this same skillful end.

38. Section: Knowing the Faults of Desire — the Present Life and the Life to Come

What is purity of knowledge through the faults of desire? It means giving only after truly knowing the many faults and calamities that belong to the desires themselves. And with regard to the suffering-laden aggregates, one may at times know two kinds of fault in them: one belonging to the present life, and one belonging to lives to come.

Chinese Source

七、由諸欲過患智清淨者，謂於諸欲所有過患如實知已而行惠施。謂於苦蘊中，或時了知二種過患。一者、現法，二者、後法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purity of knowledge through the faults of desire? Giving only after truly knowing the many faults and calamities that belong to the desires themselves. And with regard to the suffering-laden aggregates, one may at times know two kinds of fault in them: one belonging to the present life, and one belonging to lives to come.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi quotes the verse a companion fascicle uses to picture what desire actually is: it never leaves anyone satisfied, however much is consumed; it is shared by a crowd, like a piece of meat many hands are reaching for at once; it is the very condition that breeds wrongdoing and swells craving; the noble ones keep away from it; it rushes toward its own ruin; it stands only by borrowing from many conditions, the way a borrowed ornament must be returned; and it is the ground on which danger and heedlessness both rest. He notes that seeing this clearly, for both the present aggregates and the aggregates of a life yet to come, is itself said to be the means by which craving for desire is actually severed — not abstract doctrine, but the very insight that makes a bodhisattva's giving clean of the acquisitive hunger it might otherwise still carry.

39. Section: Five, Six, Seven, and Eight Kinds of Fault in Desire

At other times one may know five kinds of fault, as the scripture on the five faults describes them. At other times one may know six kinds of fault, knowing that these desires are, in the very word for them, a source of dread — and the rest as fully set out in the scriptures. At other times one may know seven kinds of fault, knowing that desires are impermanent, false, unreal, and deceptive as a thing, like a conjured display that bewilders the fool. And at other times one may know eight kinds of fault, knowing desires to be like a rotted skeleton, and so on as the scripture fully explains, down to being like ripe fruit hanging at the tip of a branch.

Chinese Source

或時了知五種過患，謂如五種過患經說。或時了知六種過患，謂此諸欲是怖增語，如是等類廣說如經。或時了知七種過患，謂知諸欲無常、虛偽、不實、妄事法，譬如幻事惑亂愚夫。或時了知八種過患，謂知諸欲如朽骸骨，如經廣說，乃至猶如樹端熟果。

Ru-Yi Translation

At other times one may know five kinds of fault, as the scripture on the five faults describes them. At other times one may know six kinds of fault, knowing that these desires are, in the very word for them, a source of dread — and the rest as fully set out in the scriptures. At other times one may know seven kinds of fault, knowing that desires are impermanent, false, unreal, and deceptive as a thing, like a conjured display that bewilders the fool. And at other times one may know eight kinds of fault, knowing desires to be like a rotted skeleton, and so on as the scripture fully explains, down to being like ripe fruit hanging at the tip of a branch.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi fills in what the treatise leaves compressed. The five faults, from the Śrāvaka Ground, are that desires give scarcely any real satisfaction while bringing much suffering; that indulging them never brings contentment, never enough, never full; that buddhas and their noble disciples condemn them by every argument there is; that indulging them only builds up and strengthens the fetters; and that there is no evil deed a being will not commit in pursuit of them. The eight, drawn from the discourses, run through a sequence of images: a gnawed bone with no meat on it, a piece of meat many are fighting over, a grass torch carried against the wind that will burn the bearer's own hand, a pit of fire, a venomous snake, something seen only in a dream, borrowed jewelry that must eventually be returned to its owner, and fruit ripe at the very tip of a branch, tempting and about to fall. He notes that the whole point of running through five, six, seven, and eight kinds in turn is not to settle on one canonical count but to let a bodhisattva recognize the same danger from as many angles as it takes, so that giving is finally free of the grasping desires produce.

40. Section: Removing Fourteen Tainted Deeds, and Knowing Which Friends to Keep and Which to Leave

What is purity of knowledge through removing stain? It means giving only after truly knowing that fourteen tainted deeds are to be removed, as the Śikhālaka Discourse explains. What is purity of knowledge through keeping clear of bad company and taking up good company? It means being skilled in knowing to keep clear of four kinds of bad friend and to take up four kinds of good friend, and giving accordingly — this too as the Śikhālaka Discourse explains.

Chinese Source

八、由除垢智清淨者，謂於除遣十四垢業如實知已而行惠施，此如尸佉落迦經說。九、由於友遠離攝受智清淨者，謂能善知遠離四種惡友、攝受四種善友而行惠施，此亦如尸佉落迦經說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purity of knowledge through removing stain? Giving only after truly knowing that fourteen tainted deeds are to be removed, as the Śikhālaka Discourse explains. What is purity of knowledge through keeping clear of bad company and taking up good company? Being skilled in knowing to keep clear of four kinds of bad friend and to take up four kinds of good friend, and giving accordingly — this too as the Śikhālaka Discourse explains.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the Śikhālaka Discourse as the teaching to the young householder Sigālaka preserved in several versions of the āgamas, and unpacks the fourteen tainted deeds it names: the four rooted in affliction — killing, stealing, illicit sex, and false speech; four occasions on which one should not act badly — out of desire, out of hatred, out of delusion, and out of fear; and six habits that drain a household's wealth — drinking, gambling, dissipation, infatuation with entertainment, bad company, and laziness — each of which the discourse itself lists six further faults for. The point of listing these before turning to giving, he notes, is that purity here is not only about the gift itself but about the whole conduct surrounding it: a bodhisattva who still indulges these fourteen habits cannot give with a genuinely clean mind, however generous the gift looks. On the ninth aspect he leaves the four good and four bad friends for the discourse itself to specify, since the text is about to take them up directly.

41. Section: Covering the Six Directions — Closing the Ten Aspects of Purity of Knowledge

What is purity of knowledge through the six directions kept covered? It means giving after having covered the six directions — this too as the Śikhālaka Discourse explains.

Chinese Source

十、由隱覆六方智清淨者，謂隱覆六方而行惠施，此亦如尸佉落迦經說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purity of knowledge through the six directions kept covered? Giving after having covered the six directions — this too as the Śikhālaka Discourse explains.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells the setting behind this teaching: a young householder named Sigālaka used to bathe and bow each morning to the six directions, as his father had taught him, to win the protection of the spirits who dwell there. When the Buddha met him at this practice, he did not dismiss it but redefined the six directions in terms of the six relationships that actually sustain a life — parents as the east, teachers as the south, spouse as the west, kin as the north, servants as the lower direction, and renunciants and the truly accomplished as the upper direction — each covered not by ritual bowing but by five specific duties owed and returned in both directions: children caring for parents and parents guiding children; students honoring teachers and teachers instructing students fully; husband and wife treating each other with respect and consideration; kin supporting one another honestly; a master treating servants fairly and servants serving faithfully in turn. He notes what the treatise is doing by placing this here: a bodhisattva's giving is not clean if it floats free of these ordinary human obligations, so covering the six directions well is itself named as one of the ten aspects that purify the wisdom behind an act of giving. On the four bad and four good friends drawn from the same discourse, he sums the distinction plainly: a bad friend flatters, withdraws in hardship, blocks better company, or shares only in vice, while a good friend restrains you from wrong, shares your fortune and grief alike, protects you from ruin, and stands by you in real need — and adds that even here a bodhisattva's compassion for the bad friend as a being is never withdrawn, only the shared company that would let his own wholesome roots be worn down.

42. Section: The Seventh Purity Opens — Freedom from the Stains of Laziness, Greed, Hatred, and Delusion

What are the ten aspects of purity from stain? First, giving free of the stain of laziness, meaning that one gives constantly, whether the wealth is inward or outward, whether the distance is near or far, whether the body is weary or not weary, whether the body is frail or not frail. Second, giving free of the stain of greed, with regard to one's wealth. Third, giving free of the stain of hatred, with regard to the one who asks. And fourth, giving free of the stain of delusion, with regard to cause and effect.

Chinese Source

云何垢清淨十相？一、遠離懈怠垢而行惠施，謂或內、或外、或近、或遠、或身疲倦、或不疲倦、或身羸劣、或不羸劣而常惠施。二、遠離貪垢而行惠施，謂於財物。三、遠離瞋垢而行惠施，謂於求者。四、遠離癡垢而行惠施，謂於因果。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the ten aspects of purity from stain? First, giving free of the stain of laziness, giving constantly whether the wealth is inward or outward, near or far, whether the body is weary or not, frail or not. Second, giving free of the stain of greed, with regard to one's wealth. Third, giving free of the stain of hatred, with regard to the one who asks. And fourth, giving free of the stain of delusion, with regard to cause and effect.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the seventh purity closes the whole sevenfold decomposition by circling back to the same four root afflictions already named earlier in the chapter, now applied specifically to the moment of giving itself: laziness undermines the constancy of giving regardless of distance or bodily condition; greed clings to the wealth about to be given away; hatred flares toward a petitioner who has become tiresome, forgetting how rare a genuine field of merit actually is; and delusion is simply not understanding what giving's cause and effect actually are. He treats this fourth stain as the root of the other three in practice — one who truly understands the workings of giving's fruit finds laziness, greed, and irritation at the asker all considerably harder to sustain.

43. Section: Four Hindrances Overcome, and Giving Wisely Distributed

Fifth, giving free of the stain of hindrance. This stain of hindrance means four kinds of hindrance: not having made it a habit, being short of means, being too attached to enjoy giving it up, and looking to the fruit. And sixth, giving well distributed, which is exactly freedom from the stain of wrong measure: one who is poor gives only to his own attendants; one of middling wealth gives to his attendants and to the destitute as well; and one of great wealth gives to his attendants, to the destitute, and to everyone else who comes asking besides.

Chinese Source

五、遠離障垢而行惠施。言障垢者，謂四種障。一、不串習，二、匱乏，三、耽湎，四、觀果。六、善分布而行惠施，此即遠離非道理垢。謂貧乏者，於自僕從；若中財者，即於彼所及貧苦所；若大財者，即於彼所，亦於其餘來求者所。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, giving free of the stain of hindrance. This stain means four kinds of hindrance: not having made it a habit, being short of means, being too attached to give it up, and looking to the fruit. And sixth, giving well distributed, which is exactly freedom from the stain of wrong measure: one who is poor gives only to his own attendants; one of middling wealth gives to his attendants and to the destitute as well; and one of great wealth gives to his attendants, to the destitute, and to everyone else who comes asking.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the four hindrances as four distinct moments where giving can stall. Not having made it a habit is countered simply by recognizing the lapse for what it is — an old failure never practiced into a settled disposition — and acting anyway, right now, so the pattern is not carried into a future life. Being short of means is countered by bearing the hardship of giving despite scarcity, so that scarcity does not recur as an obstacle later. Being too attached to let go of something especially fine is countered by seeing that clinging to an illusory possession as though it were real happiness is itself an inversion that only breeds future suffering. And looking to the fruit — giving in the hope of a magnificent return rather than for awakening itself — is countered by seeing that every conditioned thing, wealth included, is impermanent and unreliable, so that the giving is redirected toward unsurpassed awakening rather than a better rebirth. He treats "giving well distributed" as simple prudence rather than partiality: a giver who neglects those nearest and most dependent on him in order to look generous to strangers has not achieved purity, only a different kind of disorder.

44. Section: Giving Made Complete — Seven Things Given, and a Mind Unattached

Seventh, giving through completeness, which is exactly freedom from the stain of diminishment. This has completeness in the matter given and completeness in intention. Completeness in the matter given has, further, seven aspects: giving one's provisions, giving one's land, giving beings such as children and servants, giving ornaments, giving one's house, giving one's dwelling place, and giving one's own body. And completeness in intention means attaining, toward one's own body and toward outward wealth alike, an intention of spontaneous non-attachment.

Chinese Source

七、由圓滿而行惠施，此即遠離諸減少垢。謂事圓滿、意樂圓滿。事圓滿者，復有七相。一、施資產事，二、施國土事，三、施有情事，四、施莊嚴事，五、施舍宅事，六、施居處事，七、施內身事。意樂圓滿者，謂於內身及外財寶，獲得自性無著意樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, giving through completeness, which is exactly freedom from the stain of diminishment. This has completeness in the matter given and completeness in intention. Completeness in the matter given has seven aspects: giving one's provisions, giving one's land, giving beings such as children and servants, giving ornaments, giving one's house, giving one's dwelling place, and giving one's own body. And completeness in intention means attaining, toward one's own body and toward outward wealth alike, an intention of spontaneous non-attachment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the earlier fascicle's own words for what "freedom from diminishment" rules out: a bodhisattva never gives one who asks less than was first promised, only ever the same or more; and never promises something excellent and later gives something inferior instead — only ever what was first promised, or better still if something excellent has since come to hand. The seven things given rise in a clear order of difficulty, from ordinary provisions and land through dependents and possessions to one's house and dwelling place, ending with the body itself; and completeness in intention is what makes the whole list bearable rather than merely aspirational — a spontaneous non-attachment toward both inward body and outward wealth, so that none of the seven, including the last, requires the giver to force himself against a still-clinging mind.

45. Section: Giving Made Clean — Ten Aspects Free of Every Residue of Self-Interest

Eighth, giving through cleanness, which is exactly freedom from the stain of uncleanness. This means the ten kinds of clean giving — giving without holding back, and the rest — as the Basic Section fully explains.

Chinese Source

八、由清淨而行惠施，此即遠離不清淨垢。謂由十種清淨，即無滯無取等，如本地分廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eighth, giving through cleanness, which is exactly freedom from the stain of uncleanness. This means the ten kinds of clean giving — giving without holding back, and the rest — as the Basic Section fully explains.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the ten from the Basic Section in full, since the treatise here only gestures at them: giving without holding back, so that the asker is never made to wait; giving without any of four false graspings, whether at the gift itself, at a supposed absence of any fruit, at killing as a fitting rite for giving, or at giving alone as the complete path; giving without hoarding first and releasing all at once, since the moment a need is actually felt is the only moment giving truly fits; giving without arrogance, neither lording it over the recipient nor growing proud afterward; giving without resting on reputation, knowing that praise is only empty sound, no more substantial than chaff; giving without flinching, so that hearing of a bodhisattva's utmost gifts of flesh and eye never provokes fear or a sense that such generosity is beyond one's own reach; giving of the finest one has rather than the leftover; giving equally toward friend and enemy and stranger alike; giving without expecting gratitude in return, moved instead by compassion for beings burned by their own craving; and giving without hoping for a fine ripened reward, since every conditioned thing is unreliable and great awakening alone is what such giving actually seeks.

46. Section: Observing the Thing Given, the Intention, and the Field — Overcoming the Stain of Wrong Understanding

Ninth, giving through careful observation, which is exactly freedom from the stain of wrong understanding. This means observing the thing given, observing one's own intention, and observing the field one gives to. In observing the thing given, one observes that using it oneself is better than hoarding it, and that giving it away is better than using it oneself. Why so? If one only hoards it, one benefits neither oneself nor another, gaining neither the present life's advantage nor the advantage of a life to come. If bodhisattvas only use it themselves, this benefits themselves alone and not others, and gains the present life's advantage but not the advantage of a life to come. But if bodhisattvas are able to give it away, this at once produces vast delight in them, benefiting both themselves and others, and gaining the advantage of both the present life and lives to come. In observing one's own intention, one should know that the intention has, in brief, four kinds: an intention free of inversion regarding the cause, an intention free of attachment regarding the fruit, an intention of compassion toward beings, and an intention aimed at the perfection of the knowledge of all aspects. It is with intentions of this sort that one gives. In observing the field one gives to, one should know that this is, in brief, by five marks: a place where what is asked can actually be obtained; a place already once asked and now fallen into still greater want; a place already in want and now further without anyone to depend on; a place without anyone to depend on and further given to wrongdoing; and a place having none of these, yet where the practice of good conduct is to be found. These are called the field. And by seven marks a place should be known not to be a field at all: one where the petitioner is extremely violent and has already borne one enmity, coming now in reliance on that same enmity to ask; one where he cannot in the end be persuaded toward anything good; one whose heart is tainted, asking for the sake of a tainted purpose; one who asks only to cause harm and distress; one who is himself a demonic being, or possessed by one, asking for what should not be asked at all; one who asks for one's very parents, or for some other thing that ought never to be given; and one who, once satisfied, will use what was given to no good purpose whatever. By marks such as these, a place should be known as not a field, and observed accordingly.

If one only hoards it, one benefits neither oneself nor another. If bodhisattvas only use it themselves, this benefits themselves alone. But if bodhisattvas are able to give it away, this produces vast delight and benefits both themselves and others. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the five kinds of genuine field plainly: one whose receipt of a gift will pass it on to others still further in need; one who has already been asked once and grown poorer since; one already poor and now with no one at all to depend on; one with no one to depend on who is being driven toward wrongdoing by that very want; and one who, lacking none of these hardships, is nonetheless earnestly practicing good conduct and deserves support for that alone. Against these he sets the seven marks of what is not a field at all — an enemy asking in bad faith, one who cannot be turned toward anything good, one asking for a tainted purpose or to cause harm, one who is himself possessed or asking for something improper, one asking for a person's very parents, and one who will only misuse what is given. He notes the classical fourfold scheme of fields of merit that stands behind all this — fields of compassion, of debt, of virtue, and of suffering — and that discernment here is not coldness toward the undeserving but simply keeping the giving aimed at where it will actually do good.

47. Section: The Tenth and Final Stain Overcome — Giving Endowed with Skillful Means

Tenth, giving endowed with the excellence of skillful means, which is exactly freedom from the fault of being without skillful means. The skillful means meant here are these: making giving into a habit; giving without regard for body or life; feeling compassion for beings; the wisdom that knows the real meaning of things; conviction resolved upon unsurpassed awakening; instructing others toward giving; exerting a compelling force upon them; placing a being where he can bear to receive a kindness and repay it; giving through the very circumstance of one's own birth; and giving through supernormal power.

十、具方便德而行惠施，此即遠離無方便過失垢。此中方便者，謂串習施，不顧身命，悲愍有情，真實義智、無上菩提勝解，教導、彊力逼迫、處任恩報、生及神力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tenth, giving endowed with the excellence of skillful means, which is exactly freedom from the fault of being without skillful means. The skillful means meant here are these: making giving into a habit; giving without regard for body or life; feeling compassion for beings; the wisdom that knows the real meaning of things; conviction resolved upon unsurpassed awakening; instructing others toward giving; exerting a compelling force upon them; placing a being where he can bear to receive a kindness and repay it; giving through the very circumstance of one's own birth; and giving through supernormal power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this closing list circles directly back to the sixth purity's skillful means — instruction, compelling force, first placing a being in one's debt, the circumstance of birth, and supernormal power all reappear here by name, now folded into the tenth and final stain to be overcome. What is added at this last stage is the deeper foundation beneath all five: an unshakeable habit of giving, complete disregard for body and life when giving calls for it, genuine compassion, the wisdom that sees all things as they really are, and conviction fixed on nothing less than unsurpassed awakening. With this the seventh purity — purity from stain — is complete, and with it the full sevenfold, seventy-aspect determination of the bodhisattva's giving that this chapter set out to explain. Here the source transcript comes to its own end, closing this chapter and, with it, fascicle seventy-four.
