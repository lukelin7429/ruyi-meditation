The Embrace of the Truths, Complete

Fascicle 95 covers the embrace of the truths (諦攝) in its entirety, the sixth of sūtra matters' seven gates, decisively explaining the Saṃyukta Āgama's teaching on the four noble truths. All twelve topics of its own summary verse are now complete: wisdom in accordance with truth (distinguishing right and wrong mental attention), embracing (every wholesome dharma, contaminated or not, falls within the four truths), the truth of origin (the hundred and eight kinds of craving and their eleven synonyms), attainment (the Buddha's own turning of the dharma wheel), marks (the ten faults and ten virtues), place (the threefold benefit of going forth), karma (the six functions and marks of the truths' wisdom), obstruction (disbelief, overestimation, procrastination, negligence, and their antidotes), faults (the four faults of failing to realize the truths), dark ripening and the rest (the three kinds of karma), the great meaning (the vast suffering already severed), and what is hard to attain (the rarity of a body, an eye, a wisdom, and provisions capable of realization) — closing with the famous fingernail-dirt-versus-the-great-earth similes on how rare true realization is among all beings.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the embrace of the truths (諦攝), the sixth of sūtra matters' seven gates — after the aggregates section, the sense-bases section, and dependent origination's and food's embraces are all now complete, this decisively explains the Saṃyukta Āgama's teaching on the four noble truths — marking and listing (標列) twelve topics to be explained in the long-form section that follows: wisdom in accordance with truth, embracing, the truth of origin, attainment, marks, place, karma, obstruction, faults, dark ripening and the rest, the great meaning, and what is hard to attain.

2. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — Wisdom in Accordance With Truth: Marking the Distinction of Wisdoms

If, regarding the superior wisdom of the truths, one fails to truly know what is in accordance with truth and what is not in accordance with truth, one is unable to exhaust the contaminants. The reverse of this — truly knowing — one is able to exhaust every contaminant.

Chinese Source

若於諦智增上如理及不如理不如實知，不能盡漏。與此相違，如實知故，能盡諸漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, regarding the superior wisdom of the truths, one fails to truly know what is in accordance with truth and what is not in accordance with truth, one is unable to exhaust the contaminants. The reverse of this — truly knowing — one is able to exhaust every contaminant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the long-form explanation (長行釋), whose twelve sections match the summary verse's twelve topics, beginning with the first, wisdom in accordance with truth (如理), marking the distinction of wisdoms (標智別) — the four noble truths describe dependent origination's flow and its cessation; only genuinely according with them, not merely appearing to, actually severs affliction.

3. Section: Explaining the Names of the Wisdoms Begins — Named Not in Accordance With Truth

One should know that here, hearing improper teachings, wisdom that arises not for the sake of stillness, not for the sake of taming, and not for the sake of nirvāṇa, is called not in accordance with truth.

Chinese Source

當知此中，聞不正法，不為寂靜、不為調伏、不為涅槃所起諸智，名不如理。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that here, hearing improper teachings, wisdom that arises not for the sake of stillness, not for the sake of taming, and not for the sake of nirvāṇa, is called not in accordance with truth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining the names of the wisdoms (釋智名), beginning with named not in accordance with truth (名不如理) — wisdom gained from bad company and wrong teaching cannot tell right from wrong, and so misconstrues the entire path from goal to method to result.

4. Section: Reversing Into the Name "In Accordance With Truth," Closing "Explaining the Names of the Wisdoms" Entirely

Hearing the true Dharma, the reverse of the above, one should know is called in accordance with truth.

Chinese Source

聽聞正法，與上相違，當知如理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hearing the true Dharma, the reverse of the above, one should know is called in accordance with truth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, reversing into the name "in accordance with truth" (翻名如理) closes entirely, closing explaining the names of the wisdoms (釋智名2: 名不如理/翻名如理) entirely — drawing near to the wise, hearing the true Dharma, applying proper attention, and practicing dharma-in-accord-with-dharma is what makes wisdom genuinely "in accordance with truth."

5. Section: Distinguishing These Kinds Begins — Unable to Exhaust the Contaminants: Distinguishing: Bringing Forth Wrong Conduct: By Dwelling in Unwholesome Dharmas

Further, here, a person dwelling in badly-taught doctrine, toward this true Dharma's Buddha and the Buddha's disciples, truly good and excellent persons, takes no delight in looking up to them. Toward the discipline of individual liberation, secretly guarding the sense-doors, and abiding with clear comprehension, and such other dharmas belonging to the noble ones, they do not tame themselves by these, nor undertake to train in them. Toward the noble truths, they lack the illumination and penetration that come from hearing, thinking, and cultivation.

Chinese Source

又於此中，住惡說法補特伽羅，於此正法佛、佛弟子真善丈夫，不樂瞻仰。於別解脫尸羅律儀、密護根門、正知而住，如是等類賢聖法中，不自調伏，不受學轉。於諸聖諦，無聞思修照了通達。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, here, a person dwelling in badly-taught doctrine, toward this true Dharma's Buddha and the Buddha's disciples, truly good and excellent persons, takes no delight in looking up to them. Toward the discipline of individual liberation, secretly guarding the sense-doors, and abiding with clear comprehension, and such other dharmas belonging to the noble ones, they do not tame themselves by these, nor undertake to train in them. Toward the noble truths, they lack the illumination and penetration that come from hearing, thinking, and cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens distinguishing these kinds (辨彼類), beginning with unable to exhaust the contaminants (不能盡漏), distinguishing (辨), bringing forth wrong conduct (出邪行), the first cause, by dwelling in unwholesome dharmas (由住惡法) — without reverence for the Three Jewels or willingness to train in discipline and sense-restraint, no genuine study of the four truths can ever take root. Per the 披尋記, "illumination" and "penetration" both name direct realization of the truths, corresponding respectively to their comprehensive and their inherent nature.

6. Section: By Giving Rise to Wrong Understanding

Further, precisely within these badly-taught dharmas and discipline, hearing improper teachings, one gives rise to wrong, resolute understanding. Toward what is not in accordance with truth, one gives rise to the deluded misconception that it is in accordance with truth; toward what is not in accordance with truth, one fails to truly know that it is not in accordance with truth; and, further, toward the true Dharma heard, which is in accordance with truth, one fails to truly know that it is in accordance with truth.

Chinese Source

又即於彼諸惡說法毗奈耶中，聞不正法，起邪勝解。於不如理，生起如理顛倒妄想；於不如理，不如實知是不如理；又於聽聞正法如理，不如實知是其如理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, precisely within these badly-taught dharmas and discipline, hearing improper teachings, one gives rise to wrong, resolute understanding. Toward what is not in accordance with truth, one gives rise to the deluded misconception that it is in accordance with truth; toward what is not in accordance with truth, one fails to truly know that it is not in accordance with truth; and, further, toward the true Dharma heard, which is in accordance with truth, one fails to truly know that it is in accordance with truth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This is the second cause, by giving rise to wrong understanding (由起邪解) — some outside teachers claim the Buddha and Buddhists are demonic, unaware the real demon lies within their own mind, or claim that faith alone saves regardless of karma, encouraging others not to take responsibility for their own actions; every such distorted view of permanence, bliss, self, and purity counts as this second cause.

7. Section: By Improper Mental Attention Begins — Briefly Stated: Distinguishing the Difference

By not knowing, toward every kind of badly-taught, wrongly-understood dharma bound by affliction and free of liberation, dharmas of distortion not to be reflected upon, one fails to comprehend, yet deliberately reflects upon them. Toward every kind of well-taught, rightly-understood dharma free of affliction's binding and its liberation, dharmas free of distortion that should be reflected upon — namely the discourses and verses of response, and so on, at length — one fails to comprehend, and does not reflect upon them. This too is called improper mental attention.

Chinese Source

由不知故，於諸所有惡說、惡解、有縛無脫、不應思惟顛倒法中，不能解了，而故思惟。於諸所有善說、善解、有脫無縛、應可思惟無顛倒法，所謂契經及應頌等，乃至廣說，不能解了，而不思惟。如是亦名非理作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

By not knowing, toward every kind of badly-taught, wrongly-understood dharma bound by affliction and free of liberation, dharmas of distortion not to be reflected upon, one fails to comprehend, yet deliberately reflects upon them. Toward every kind of well-taught, rightly-understood dharma free of affliction's binding and its liberation, dharmas free of distortion that should be reflected upon — namely the discourses and verses of response, and so on, at length — one fails to comprehend, and does not reflect upon them. This too is called improper mental attention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the third cause, by improper mental attention (由邪作意), beginning with briefly stated (略), distinguishing the difference (辨差別) — the mind is drawn precisely backward, dwelling on distorted dharmas that deserve no reflection, and ignoring undistorted dharmas that do; a kind of intelligence, but one entirely misapplied.

8. Section: Explaining Why This Is Improper, Closing "Briefly Stated" Entirely

Because such mental attention is not for the sake of stillness, not for the sake of taming, and not for the sake of nirvāṇa, it is therefore called improper.

Chinese Source

由此作意不為寂靜、不為調伏、不為涅槃，故名非理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because such mental attention is not for the sake of stillness, not for the sake of taming, and not for the sake of nirvāṇa, it is therefore called improper.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, explaining why this is improper (釋非理) closes entirely, closing briefly stated (略2: 辨差別/釋非理) entirely.

9. Section: Explaining Broadly Begins — Bringing Forth the Conception of a Real Self

Further, because of hearing improper teachings, relying on the predominant condition of three kinds of verbal matter, one displays the various types of conceiving a self across the past, future, and present.

Chinese Source

又復聽聞不正法故，依三言事增上緣力，顯示過去、未來、現在計我品類。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, because of hearing improper teachings, relying on the predominant condition of three kinds of verbal matter, one displays the various types of conceiving a self across the past, future, and present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens explaining broadly (廣), the second of two matters under improper mental attention, beginning with bringing forth the conception of a real self (出計實我) — an outside teacher's discourse constantly narrates what "I" once was, will be, or now am, weaving a false continuity across time out of what is really only ever-changing formations. Per the 披尋記, "three kinds of verbal matter" names discourse about the past, future, and present marks of resultant and not-yet-resultant formations — taking this discourse itself as a condition, discrimination arises, which is what "predominant condition" means.

10. Section: Explaining Its View-Bases Begins — Distinguishing the Mark: Generally Marking

Having thus improperly applied mental attention, one either conditions the object grasped, or conditions the grasping subject; through this improper mental attention, one either conceives a self within the formations, or conceives a self apart from the formations.

Chinese Source

彼既如是不如正理作意思惟，或緣所取事，或緣能取事；此不如理作意思惟，或即諸行分別有我，或離諸行分別有我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus improperly applied mental attention, one either conditions the object grasped, or conditions the grasping subject; through this improper mental attention, one either conceives a self within the formations, or conceives a self apart from the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining its view-bases (釋其見處), the second matter under bringing forth the conception of a real self, beginning with distinguishing the mark (辨相), generally marking (總標) the two great categories — self within the aggregates, and self apart from them.

11. Section: Distinguishing Each Individually Begins — Conceiving a Self Within the Formations: Distinguishing: Conditioning the Object Grasped: Forming Eternalism

Once such a conception has become fixed, if one conditions the object grasped and conceives it as self, this may form eternalism; by this view, one reflects thus: I possess a self, and, within the present dharma, it is real, it is eternal.

Chinese Source

彼於所計得決定時，若緣所取事，分別為我，或成常見，由此見故，作是思惟：我有其我，於現法中是實是常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once such a conception has become fixed, if one conditions the object grasped and conceives it as self, this may form eternalism; by this view, one reflects thus: I possess a self, and, within the present dharma, it is real, it is eternal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens distinguishing each individually (別辨), beginning with conceiving a self within the formations (即行分別有我), distinguishing (辨), conditioning the object grasped (緣所取事), forming eternalism (成常見) — mistaking the five aggregates themselves for a real, permanent, sovereign self.

12. Section: Forming Annihilationism, Closing "Conditioning the Object Grasped" Entirely

Or this may form annihilationism; by this view, one reflects thus: I have no self, though within the present dharma it is real, it is constant.

Chinese Source

或成斷見，由此見故，作是思惟：我無其我，於現法中是實是常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or this may form annihilationism; by this view, one reflects thus: I have no self, though within the present dharma it is real, it is constant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

With this, forming annihilationism (成斷見) closes entirely, closing conditioning the object grasped (緣所取事2: 成常見/成斷見) entirely. Per the 披尋記: this view holds the self real and constant only within the present life, believing it utterly extinguished at death — self-view still underlies this position, since annihilationism is simply eternalism's mirror image, not its absence; one who holds it may reason there is no karmic retribution to fear, and act accordingly.

13. Section: Conditioning the Grasping Subject Begins — Conceiving the View of a Self

If one conditions the grasping subject and conceives a view of self, taking it as self, one reflects thus: I now, by means of self, observe self. Or, holding that self formerly existed but now does not, one reflects thus: I now, by means of self, observe the absence of self.

Chinese Source

若緣能取事，計有我見，分別為我，作是思惟：我今以我觀察於我。或謂我我先有今無，作是思惟：我今以我觀察無我。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one conditions the grasping subject and conceives a view of self, taking it as self, one reflects thus: I now, by means of self, observe self. Or, holding that self formerly existed but now does not, one reflects thus: I now, by means of self, observe the absence of self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens conditioning the grasping subject (緣能取事), the second of two, beginning with conceiving the view of a self (計有我見) — one moment of mind observing another, mistaking the observing itself for a real self, whether judged continuous or already extinguished.

14. Section: Conceiving the View of No Self, Closing "Conditioning the Grasping Subject" and "Distinguishing" Entirely

Or, precisely conditioning the grasping subject, one conceives a view of no self, and, within the present dharma, taking this very absence of self as self, one reflects thus: I now, by means of this absence of self, observe in retrospect that there once was a self.

Chinese Source

或復即緣能取之事，計無我見，於現法中，以其無我分別為我，作是思惟：我今以其無我隨觀昔曾有我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, precisely conditioning the grasping subject, one conceives a view of no self, and, within the present dharma, taking this very absence of self as self, one reflects thus: I now, by means of this absence of self, observe in retrospect that there once was a self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, conceiving the view of no self (計無我見) closes entirely, closing conditioning the grasping subject (緣能取事2: 計有我見/計無我見) entirely, closing distinguishing (辨2: 緣所取事/緣能取事) entirely — some today similarly imagine a "pure," self-less childhood mind as one's "true self," as against the supposedly inauthentic, socially conditioned mind of adulthood, not recognizing that even an infant's mind already carries innate afflictive seeds.

15. Section: The Concluding Statement, Closing "Conceiving a Self Within the Formations" Entirely

Thus, provisionally, the five distinct marks of the object grasped and the grasping subject have been described — five view-bases arising from improperly applied mental attention. Namely, conceiving a self within the formations belonging to all three time periods.

Chinese Source

如是且說所取、能取差別五相，不如正理作意思惟五種見處。謂即三世所有諸行分別有我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, provisionally, the five distinct marks of the object grasped and the grasping subject have been described — five view-bases arising from improperly applied mental attention. Namely, conceiving a self within the formations belonging to all three time periods.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, the concluding statement (結) closes entirely, closing conceiving a self within the formations (即行分別有我2: 辨/結) entirely — five view-bases in all: eternalism and annihilationism toward the object grasped, and three further variants (self observing self, self observing self's absence, and no-self observing a past self) toward the grasping subject.

16. Section: Conceiving a Self Apart From the Formations Begins — Marking It as Improper

Further, by improper inferential mental attention, one conceives a self apart from the formations.

Chinese Source

又復由於不如正理比度作意，離於諸行分別有我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by improper inferential mental attention, one conceives a self apart from the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Sixty-Two Views Sūtra

This opens conceiving a self apart from the formations (離行分別有我), the second of two matters, beginning with marking it as improper (標非理). Various outside paths — Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, Jainism, and others named in the Sixty-Two Views Sūtra — posit exactly such a self, whether dwelling within the aggregates or within some unconditioned reality apart from them, both positions contradicting the Buddhist teaching of genuine non-self.

17. Section: Clarifying What Is Conceived Begins — Bringing Forth Its Various Names: The Agent and the Equivalent Agent

Such a really-existing self as they conceive, either itself directly creates the karma that draws forth future existence, called the agent; or another causes it to be created, called the equivalent agent.

Chinese Source

彼謂如是所計實我，或自能作感後有業，名能作者；或他令作，名等作者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such a really-existing self as they conceive, either itself directly creates the karma that draws forth future existence, called the agent; or another causes it to be created, called the equivalent agent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens clarifying what is conceived (明所計), the second of two matters under conceiving a self apart from the formations, beginning with bringing forth its various names (出異名), the first, the agent and the equivalent agent (能作者等作者) — either the self acts on its own, or some outside power (a creator god, primal matter, time, or space) is said to act on its behalf, the latter an "unequal-cause" theory.

18. Section: The Initiator and the Equivalent Initiator

Or it itself is able to initiate present functioning activity, called the initiator; or another causes it to be initiated, called the equivalent initiator.

Chinese Source

或自能起現法士用，名能起者；或他令起，名等起者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or it itself is able to initiate present functioning activity, called the initiator; or another causes it to be initiated, called the equivalent initiator.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second named form — the self as the immediate source of present action, or else set in motion by some outside creative power.

19. Section: The Generator and the Equivalent Generator

Whether because it itself creates the karma for future existence, or because another causes it to create the karma for future existence, it experiences the resultant ripening — called the generator. Whether because it itself initiates present functioning activity, or because another equivalently initiates present functioning activity, it obtains the fruit of that activity — called the equivalent generator.

Chinese Source

或自己作後有業故，或他令作後有業故，感果異熟，名能生者。或自能起現士用故，或他等起現士用故，得士用果，名等生者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether because it itself creates the karma for future existence, or because another causes it to create the karma for future existence, it experiences the resultant ripening — called the generator. Whether because it itself initiates present functioning activity, or because another equivalently initiates present functioning activity, it obtains the fruit of that activity — called the equivalent generator.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Discourse on the Establishment of Consciousness-Only

The third named form, distinguishing the resultant fruit of future karma (the generator) from the immediate fruit of present functioning activity (the equivalent generator) — as the Discourse on the Establishment of Consciousness-Only explains, "functioning" can mean either a person's own activity (a farmer's labor and its harvest) or a dharma's own causal efficacy (a knife's cutting and what it cuts).

20. Section: The Speaker Begins — the First Meaning

Whether through one's own view or another's view, speech arises accordingly; likewise, whether through what one has personally heard and understood or what another has heard and understood, speech arises accordingly — this is called the speaker.

Chinese Source

或由自見、或由他見隨起言說，如是或由自聞覺知、或由他聞覺知隨起言說，名能說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether through one's own view or another's view, speech arises accordingly; likewise, whether through what one has personally heard and understood or what another has heard and understood, speech arises accordingly — this is called the speaker.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the fourth named form, the speaker (能說者), beginning with the first meaning (第一義) — a self conceived as the source of one's own speech, whether reporting personal views or things learned from others.

21. Section: The Second Meaning, Closing "The Speaker" Entirely

Or, toward one's wife, children, servants, and every other household dependent, establishing and issuing instructions as fitting, causing them to abide accordingly — this too is called the speaker.

Chinese Source

或於妻子及奴婢等所有家屬，隨其所應施設教敕，令住其處，如是亦復名能說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, toward one's wife, children, servants, and every other household dependent, establishing and issuing instructions as fitting, causing them to abide accordingly — this too is called the speaker.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, the second meaning (第二義) closes entirely, closing the speaker (能說者2: 第一義/第二義) entirely — the self conceived also as the one who governs a household, issuing rules others are made to follow.

22. Section: The Recipient and the Equivalent Recipient

Or, when the fruit of future karma has already arisen, this is called the recipient. Or, when the fruit of a person's present functioning activity has already manifestly arisen, this is called the equivalent recipient.

Chinese Source

或復當來業果已生，名能受者。或於現法諸士夫果已現等生，名等受者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, when the fruit of future karma has already arisen, this is called the recipient. Or, when the fruit of a person's present functioning activity has already manifestly arisen, this is called the equivalent recipient.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth named form — the self conceived as the one who receives karmic fruit, whether the delayed fruit of a future life or the immediate fruit already manifest in this one.

23. Section: The Experiencer, and the One Who Relinquishes and Continues Into a Further Aggregate-Set, Closing "Bringing Forth Its Various Names" Entirely

Or, having created various wholesome and unwholesome karma across former lives, one now, within the present dharma, experiences their various resultant ripenings — this is called the experiencer. Or, right up until the lifespan is exhausted and death comes, one is able to relinquish this aggregate-set and continue on into a further one.

Chinese Source

或於過去彼彼生中，造作種種善不善業，今於現法領受種種彼果異熟，名領受者。或有乃至壽量減盡而便夭喪，能捨此蘊，能續餘蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, having created various wholesome and unwholesome karma across former lives, one now, within the present dharma, experiences their various resultant ripenings — this is called the experiencer. Or, right up until the lifespan is exhausted and death comes, one is able to relinquish this aggregate-set and continue on into a further one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, the sixth named form closes, closing bringing forth its various names (出異名6: 能作者等作者/能起者等起者/能生者等生者/能說者/能受者等受者/領受者及能捨續餘蘊) entirely — the same imagined self simply discards one worn-out aggregate-set for a fresh one at death, exactly as one might discard a worn-out garment.

With this, well cultivating (善修習) closes entirely, closing explaining each distinction in turn (隨釋差別4: 善護身/善守根/善住念/善修習) entirely, closing regarding severance through cultivation (於修斷2: 標列因緣/隨釋差別) entirely, closing clarifying the severance of contaminants (明斷漏2: 於見斷/於修斷) entirely, closing able to exhaust every contaminant (能盡諸漏2: 出因緣/明斷漏) entirely, closing distinguishing these kinds (辨彼類2: 不能盡漏/能盡諸漏) entirely, closing wisdom in accordance with truth (如理3: 標智別/釋智名/辨彼類), the first of the embrace of the truths' twelve topics, entirely — arhatship itself, the most utter ultimacy, is reached simply by carrying these same four supports all the way through.

An extensive companion sūtra has the Buddha teach that right and wrong thinking make all the difference in whether contaminants of desire, existence, and ignorance arise or subside — wrong thinking causes what has not yet arisen to arise and what has already arisen to grow; right thinking prevents the one and swiftly extinguishes the other. He lists seven methods for severing affliction: through view, through guarding the sense-faculties, through bodily and mental seclusion, through proper reflection when using life's requisites, through patient endurance, through severing discursive thought, and through cultivating the seven factors of awakening. Elaborating the first method, severance through view, the sūtra has the unlearned ordinary being, lacking a true teacher, wonder whether they had a past existence, what it was like, whether they will have a future one, and what it will be like — precisely the "three kinds of verbal matter" the root text names as the source of self-conception — and from this arise six specific wrong views matching this section's own eleven-view analysis point for point: that a self truly exists (matching eternalism toward the object grasped); that a self truly does not exist (matching annihilationism); that self sees self (matching the first grasping-subject view); that self sees non-self (matching the second); that non-self sees self (matching the third); and, the sūtra adds, that this very self is what speaks, feels, and experiences the fruit of good and bad karma here and now — a view matching the treatise's own account of the self as agent, speaker, and experiencer, apart from the aggregates. Recognizing and severing this fetter of view, the sūtra continues, is the first and foundational step toward the contaminants' complete exhaustion.

Master Cháng Bǎi cites at length the companion sūtra behind this teaching: Venerable Śāriputra, dwelling at Sāvatthī's Jeta Grove, tells the monks that just as an elephant's footprint is the largest among all animal tracks, so the four noble truths embrace every immeasurable wholesome dharma — for the truth of suffering alone comprises birth, aging, sickness, death, meeting the disliked, parting from the loved, not obtaining what is sought, and, in brief, the five clung-to aggregates. He then unfolds each of the four great elements (earth, water, fire, wind) in turn, internal and external: internal earth is hair, nails, teeth, flesh, bone, and the organs; external earth, however vast and however seemingly permanent, is itself subject to arising, ceasing, and eventual destruction by flood, fire, or storm — and if even the vast external elements are impermanent, how much more so this small, craving-born body. The unlearned ordinary being clings to the body as self or as belonging to self; the learned noble disciple does not. When struck, insulted, or praised, the disciple examines contact, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness as impermanent and dependently arisen, remaining unmoved by pain or pleasure alike. The Buddha's saw-simile teaching is invoked for each element in turn: even should bandits saw the body apart limb by limb, the mind must not waver nor the mouth utter an unkind word, but should instead radiate loving-kindness toward the assailant and outward to all directions without limit. Should the practitioner still find the mind clinging to the relish of concentration, unable to rest in equanimity, Śāriputra likens the shame that should follow to a new bride's shame before her parents-in-law — self-rebuke that in turn awakens the equanimity leading to nirvāṇa's stillness, dispassion, and the extinguishing of self-grasping. The sūtra closes with the simile of a house as a composite of wood, mud, and empty space, just as the body is a composite of the four elements enclosing a space filled by consciousness; and with the teaching, repeated by the Buddha, that "to see dependent origination is to see the Dharma; to see the Dharma is to see dependent origination" — seeing all five aggregates as dependently arisen, one grows weary, then dispassionate, then liberated, and knows liberation as it truly is, this being called a monk's great work in the holy teaching, fully accomplished. The assembled monks, hearing Venerable Śāriputra's teaching, rejoiced and put it into practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive series of forty companion sūtras

With this, extending to the remaining dharmas (例餘法) closes entirely, closing right view and the rest (正見等法2: 舉正見/例餘法) entirely, closing explaining each in turn (隨釋其相3: 善資糧攝/有暇圓滿/正見等法) entirely, closing good provisions and the rest are difficult to attain (丑四善資糧等難得2: 標列種種/隨釋其相) entirely, closing **topic twelve, difficult to attain (子十二難得4: 入現觀身難得/見聖諦眼難得/三所成慧難得/善資糧等難得), entirely** — closing **the entire embrace of the truths (諦攝), all twelve topics (如理/攝/集諦/得/相/處/業/障/過/黑異熟等/大義/難得), entirely** — from right view through right thought, effort, speech, action, livelihood, mindfulness, concentration, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, this same rarity applies to every limb of the noble path, whether cultivated once, repeatedly, or extensively.

Master Cháng Bǎi closes this topic — and the whole of the truths' embrace — by citing an extensive series of some forty companion sūtras, nearly all built on the same fingernail-dirt-versus-the-great-earth simile: beings visible in bodily form are as rare as the dirt beneath the Buddha's fingernail against the numberless formless beings, like the great earth; likewise land creatures against water creatures, humans against non-humans, those born where the Buddhadharma can be heard against those born where it cannot, those who attain direct realization against those who do not, those endowed with the threefold wisdom of hearing, thinking, and cultivation against those without it, those who understand the Dharma and Vinaya, who reach it through to the end, who form correct ideas about it, who awaken to it, who rightly resolve upon it, and who correspond to it through cultivation's wisdom — all vastly outnumbered by those who do not. The series continues through beings who recognize their parents' kindness, who revere renunciants and elders, who fear wrongdoing in this life and the next, who practice generosity and precepts, whose bodily, verbal, and mental karma is pure, who keep the five precepts or the eight-precept fast, or who cultivate the ten wholesome courses of action — each again vastly outnumbered by those who do not. A final cluster of sūtras tracks rebirth statistics directly: of beings who die in the hells, the animal realm, or as hungry ghosts, vanishingly few are reborn human or as gods, while the vast majority cycle straight back into the same lower destiny; of beings born human or as gods, only a few remain there in their next life, while the great majority fall to the three evil destinies. The Buddha closes this cycle by recalling that even before ever hearing the four truths taught, his own bodhisattva reflection had already rightly discerned suffering, its origination, its cessation, and the path — arising, as with all these dharmas, first as something already arisen, then as something now arising, then as something about to arise, then practiced, cultivated, extensively cultivated, touched, and finally realized as an adept beyond training. With this, the entire cycle of companion sūtras illustrating the truths' embrace, from wisdom in accordance with truth through what is most difficult to attain, comes to its own close.
