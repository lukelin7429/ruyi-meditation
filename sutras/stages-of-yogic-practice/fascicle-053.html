Fascicle 53 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十三 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Continues

Karma Made Visible, and What Is Never Substantially Real

This fascicle completes the treatise's account of provisionally established phenomena — expressed and unexpressed karma, the two mindless meditative attainments, and the two unconditioned phenomena of space and non-analytical cessation — before opening the chapter's fifth and final topic: the six kinds of skillful understanding, beginning with the aggregates themselves.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 53 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Three Kinds of Expressed Karma — What Body, Speech, and Mind Each Make Visible

Fascicle 53 opens with a brief overview of its own three parts: first, deciding expressed and unexpressed karma; second, deciding the two mindless meditative attainments together with the two unconditioned phenomena of space and non-analytical cessation; and third, opening the six kinds of skillful understanding with the aggregates themselves — their essential nature, meaning, distinctions, sequence, subsumption, and support. The treatise begins by asking what expressed karma is, then answers that it is, in brief, of three kinds: defiled, wholesome, and neutral. It explains each in turn: whenever bodily and verbal expressed karma manifests without departing from the force of the ten unwholesome courses of action — killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle chatter, covetousness, malice, and wrong view — this is called defiled expressed karma; whenever bodily and verbal expressed karma manifests through a vow to abandon these same ten unwholesome courses, this is called wholesome expressed karma; and whenever bodily and verbal expressed karma belongs to the neutral portion of ordinary deportment or skilled craft — an unreflective gesture, an habitual motion performed without any wholesome or unwholesome intention behind it — this is called neutral expressed karma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens this fascicle by noting its own tidy three-part structure: expressed and unexpressed karma first, the two mindless attainments and two unconditioned phenomena second, and the aggregates' own sixfold analysis — essential nature, meaning, distinctions, sequence, subsumption, and support — opening the treatise's fifth and final topic within this chapter third. He clarifies the deliberately fine-grained question raised about neutral expressed karma: an idle, unreflective gesture such as absent-mindedly touching one's own head counts as neutral, but the very same gesture performed deliberately to signal reluctance is no longer neutral at all, since it carries genuine intention and thus becomes a wholesome or unwholesome expression depending on that intention — a reminder that "neutral" here names only truly unreflective, habitual motion, not merely any action lacking obvious moral weight. Having distinguished expressed karma by its three moral qualities, he notes, the treatise turns next to mental expressed karma — the subtlest of the three, since it manifests entirely within the mind, without any outward bodily or verbal sign at all.

2. Milestone: Mental Expressed Karma — The Volition That Moves the Mind Toward, Away From, or Simply Alongside Its Object

The treatise turns to mental expressed karma: whenever a person has no wish to express something to another, and simply gives rise, within their own mind, to reflective deliberation without speaking any word at all — manifesting only wholesome, defiled, or neutral mental phenomena internally — this is called mental expressed karma. The lecture explains this through the function of volition itself: whenever a sense-faculty, its object, and the corresponding consciousness converge, contact arises, and along with it the five universally-operating mental factors of contact, attention, feeling, perception, and volition arise together; when the object is neither pleasant nor unpleasant, volition moves the mind simply to receive it as it is; when the object is pleasant, volition moves the mind to unite with it; and when the object is unpleasant, volition moves the mind to separate from it — and it is precisely this driving activity of volition, coloring the mind with greed, hatred, delusion, or their wholesome and neutral counterparts, entirely without any outward bodily or verbal expression, that constitutes mental expressed karma, visible only to oneself.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the explanation of volition's own threefold movement — toward union with a pleasant object, toward separation from an unpleasant one, or toward simple reception of a neutral one — as a genuinely useful lens for observing one's own mind moment to moment: every reaction to any object whatsoever can be traced back to exactly one of these three basic movements of volition, driving the mind into whatever affliction or wholesome quality follows. He notes mental expressed karma remains entirely private, visible to no one but the person experiencing it, in contrast to bodily and verbal expressed karma which are, by definition, outwardly manifest — setting up the treatise's next task: explaining precisely how bodily, verbal, and mental expression are each defined in their own distinct terms.

3. Milestone: No Body Ever Truly Moves From One Place to Another — Expressed Karma Is Entirely Provisional

The treatise explains why it establishes these three kinds of expressed karma as it does: because every conditioned phenomenon is momentary, it is simply unreasonable to speak of a single body moving from one place to another as though something substantial actually traveled between them — bodily expression is merely a provisional designation applied to this moment-to-moment arising, established precisely to counter the mistaken view that bodily movement is somehow substantially real. The treatise further explains that apart from this same momentary arising of conditioned phenomena, no further, genuinely real function can ever be found, since neither the eye, the ear, nor the mind can ever actually locate one. The lecture elaborates that several rival schools each posited some substantially real entity to explain expressed karma — the Sammitīya positing a genuinely real motion, the Sarvāstivādin positing an infinitesimal particle of "action-matter" distinct from ordinary matter, and the Vaiśeṣika positing a real category of "action" itself — and that this entire passage systematically refutes all three at once: apart from the momentary arising and ceasing of conditioned phenomena themselves, visible through the eye as bodily expression, through the ear as verbal expression, and through the mind as mental expression, no further substantially real function of any kind can be found. The treatise therefore concludes that all expressed karma whatsoever is merely provisionally existent — bodily and verbal expression are, strictly speaking, simply provisionally designated form, while volition itself, the mental factor that actually drives bodily and verbal expression, is the genuine karmic substance capable of yielding a ripened result; expression itself, whether bodily, verbal, or mental, is never substantially real in its own right.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as a direct, three-pronged refutation of rival Abhidharma and Vaiśeṣika realism about action: the Sammitīya's genuinely real motion, the Sarvāstivādin's infinitesimal particle of "action-matter," and the Vaiśeṣika's real category of action itself all fall to the same simple observation — nothing beyond the momentary arising and ceasing of conditioned phenomena can ever actually be found, however carefully the eye, ear, or mind search for it. He highlights the treatise's own careful distinction that volition alone, not bodily or verbal expression itself, is the genuine karmic substance capable of ripening into future results — a point with real practical weight, since it locates moral responsibility squarely in the intention behind an act rather than in the external, physical motion itself. Having completed its account of expressed karma, he notes, the treatise turns to its second major item within this topic: unexpressed karma, the hidden disposition of the mind that persists even when nothing is outwardly done at all — beginning with its most severe category, the unwholesome vow.

4. Milestone: Born Into a Family of Butchers and Executioners — How an Unwholesome Vow Actually Takes Hold

The treatise turns to unexpressed karma, distinguishing three categories: that subsumed under an unwholesome vow, that subsumed under a wholesome vow, and that subsumed under neither. It opens with the first and most severe: someone born into a family whose livelihood is itself unwholesome — butchering sheep, raising chickens or pigs for slaughter, trapping birds, fishing, hunting deer, snaring rabbits, banditry, executing prisoners, restraining oxen or binding elephants for slaughter, sorcery, guarding prisons, slander and fraud, or any occupation habitually oriented toward harming others — who comes to recognize this circumstance and forms the thought, "I shall sustain my life through this very livelihood." At this initial stage, merely going along with family circumstance, this does not yet constitute an unwholesome vow — it remains only a lower-grade inclination, not yet fixed, still capable of change. Only when this same person further resolves, taking genuine delight in and consenting to this livelihood, affirming "this is right, I shall certainly do this," does the treatise call them one bound by an unwholesome vow — a middling grade, since although no action has yet actually been taken, the mind has already firmly consented to the deed as correct. The lecture illustrates unwholesome livelihoods at length — prison guards easily corrupted by the venality surrounding them, coffin-sellers subtly hoping for death to sustain their trade, doctors who might exaggerate a patient's illness to profit from unnecessary treatment — noting that any occupation, however respectable in itself, becomes an unwholesome vow the moment its practitioner's mind orients toward wishing harm upon others for personal gain.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the careful two-stage account of how an unwholesome vow actually forms — first merely going along with circumstance, only later actively affirming it as correct — as a genuinely important moral distinction: a person born into a difficult family trade is not yet morally bound by it simply through circumstance alone, only once they have genuinely, inwardly endorsed that livelihood as right does the unwholesome vow actually take hold. He notes the lecture's own practical counsel for anyone recognizing themselves in such a livelihood: changing occupation may bring real short-term hardship, yet continuing to reinforce this same karmic pattern lifetime after lifetime only entrenches suffering far more deeply in the long run. Having explained how an unwholesome vow first takes hold through mere family circumstance, he notes, the treatise turns to explaining its further consequences — how holding such a vow already constitutes possessing broad unwholesome roots, even before any actual unwholesome deed has been carried out.

5. Milestone: Merely Resolving Is Already Unwholesome — And the Two Nuns Swindled Out of Eighty Million

The treatise explains a further consequence of holding an unwholesome vow: because such a vow is bound up with an exceptionally forceful, improper attention oriented toward harm, a person holding it already possesses broad unwholesome roots — yet has not, at this stage, actually yet accomplished the unwholesome karma of killing or any of the other unwholesome courses of action, since the intended deed has not yet actually been carried out. Only afterward, once this resolve is actually put into action, whether to a lesser or greater degree, does this same person come to accomplish actual unwholesome karma as well, appropriate to what has been done — the treatise here distinguishing three grades: circumstantial inclination alone as the lowest, firm mental resolve without action as the middle grade constituting broad unwholesome roots, and actual completed action as the most severe, fully accomplished unwholesome karma. The treatise then extends this entire principle beyond those merely born into an unwholesome family: whatever person, through whatever circumstance, gives rise to this same fierce, resolute intention toward an unwholesome deed should be understood in exactly the same way — the unwholesome vow is never limited only to hereditary circumstance, but arises equally in anyone whose mind firmly, gladly consents to wrongdoing for whatever reason, even sheer cruelty unconnected to any need for survival. The lecture illustrates this with a striking contemporary account: a fraud syndicate's elaborate lottery scam, in which two Buddhist nuns were persuaded to send installment after installment of money in pursuit of a promised prize, ultimately losing more than eighty million before being expelled from their monastic community — illustrating how the same three grades of unwholesome vow, from initial susceptibility through firm resolve to actually carrying out a scheme, apply just as much to modern fraud as to the traditional unwholesome livelihoods named earlier.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the extension beyond hereditary circumstance as an important safeguard against a too-narrow reading of "unwholesome vow": anyone at all, regardless of upbringing, who firmly and gladly resolves upon wrongdoing — whether for survival, profit, or simple cruelty — falls into exactly the same category, and the middling grade of mere firm resolve, before any action is taken, already constitutes accomplishing broad unwholesome roots. Having completed this account of how unwholesome roots and unwholesome karma are each accomplished in turn, he notes, the treatise turns to what happens to a person who continues holding an unwholesome vow without ever renouncing it: how their misfortune compounds, day after day, for as long as the vow itself remains unabandoned.

6. Milestone: Misfortune Compounds Daily Until Renounced — The Chan Master Who Freed an Empress With a Single Line

The treatise explains that a person continues to be called one of unwholesome vow for as long as this unwholesome intention remains unrenounced: day after day, their unwholesome thoughts accumulate ever more broadly, and their unwholesome deeds manifest ever more often, so that their own lack of merit correspondingly grows, moment by moment, without pause. The lecture illustrates this compounding momentum — whether toward good or ill — with the story of the Dragon-Trousers Chan Master, an unkempt hermit monk living in quiet retreat behind a monastery, overlooked by everyone until an emperor's deceased mother, dissatisfied with the funeral rites performed by every other monk in the region, appeared to her son in a dream insisting that this one remaining, unnoticed hermit be summoned as well; brought reluctantly before the throne, the hermit offered the empress only a single line of instruction — "if not a single thought arises, one is born beyond the heavens" — after which she appeared again in a dream, now at peace, prompting the grateful emperor to reward the monk with fine silk trousers, which the monk immediately gave away entirely to his fellow monastics, keeping only the trousers for himself, thereafter known by that very name. The treatise then explains that unwholesome vow persists only up until it is genuinely renounced, whether through formally taking up ethical precepts, through death, through attaining meditative concentration, or, according to the Mahāyāna's own more generous account, simply through a sincere, resolute vow to abandon the deed — five recognized ways, the lecture notes, by which this bondage may finally be released.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Dragon-Trousers Chan Master's single line — "if not a single thought arises, one is born beyond the heavens" — as this entire passage's own most concentrated teaching: the very same compounding momentum that drives an unwholesome vow deeper day by day works equally in the wholesome direction, each moment of restraint or genuine wisdom reinforcing the next, so that a single, genuinely liberating insight can instantly unwind even a lifetime's accumulated suffering, exactly as it freed the grieving empress in her son's dream. He notes the treatise's own generosity in recognizing five distinct ways an unwholesome vow may be renounced — precepts, death, meditative attainment, an unusual physical condition, or, in the Mahāyāna's own broader account, simply a sincere resolve — as itself an encouraging teaching: renunciation need not wait for formal ordination, since genuine inner resolve alone is already sufficient. Having completed this account of how unwholesome vow first forms, compounds, and may finally be released, he notes, the treatise turns to the five mental factors that invariably accompany such an evil resolve — faithlessness, laziness, forgetfulness, distraction, and corrupted wisdom — beginning with what faithlessness itself actually means.

7. Milestone: Five Companions of an Evil Resolve, and a Chinese Ledger of Merit That Extended a Dying Man's Life by Fifty Years

The treatise completes its account of the five mental factors that invariably accompany an unwholesome vow. Faithlessness names not believing in, not understanding, and not accepting that unwholesome karma yields an undesirable result. Laziness names indulging one's own desire toward an unwholesome deed while failing to make any diligent effort to extinguish it. Forgetfulness names being joined with fault yet failing to genuinely remember that fault as a fault at all. Distraction names a defiled mind continuing restlessly, never settling upon any wholesome object. And corrupted wisdom names a mind persisting in inverted views, actually perceiving superior merit within one's own faults — repeatedly committing the same wrong while remaining convinced one is acting rightly. The treatise then explains the third cause of an unwholesome vow, bad conduct itself: whenever unwholesome intention manifests together with faithlessness and these other four factors, sustained by the force of habitually bad conduct, this is called one of bad conduct. The lecture illustrates the weight of such conduct, and its opposite, with a striking traditional Chinese account of moral ledgers: a wealthy but unfilial man, destined to die at nineteen yet still granted extra years for his charitable deeds, fell gravely ill at thirty-four and, in a vision while apparently dying, was told by a spirit that a single sincere intention to restore a temple could still extend his life by fifty more years — he agreed, revived from apparent death, and used his entire fortune to rebuild the temple, living to at least fifty as later recorded by the very author who visited him — alongside a second story of an illiterate farmer who, out of sheer respect for his children's tutor, uncomplainingly hosted the tutor's many guests for over a decade, his descendants afterward producing an unbroken line of successful scholars and officials, illustrating how even the humblest, quietest virtue compounds into extraordinary fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five accompanying factors — faithlessness, laziness, forgetfulness, distraction, and corrupted wisdom — as together forming the psychological substrate that keeps an unwholesome vow firmly in place, each reinforcing the others: without faith in cause and effect, there is no motivation to change; without diligence, no change is attempted; without memory of one's own faults, no correction is possible; without settled attention, no genuine reflection can occur; and without clear wisdom, one may even come to admire one's own wrongdoing. He treats the temple-restoration story as this section's own vivid proof that the very same principle of compounding momentum applies just as forcefully to virtue as it does to vice — a single sincere intention capable of extending a life by decades, precisely mirroring the earlier teaching that misfortune, too, compounds day by day for as long as it remains unaddressed. Having completed its account of the unwholesome vow in all its causes and accompaniments, he notes, the treatise turns now to its direct opposite: the wholesome vow, the precepts a Buddhist practitioner takes up in pursuit of liberation.

8. Milestone: The Wholesome Vow Begins — Why Only a Buddha or a Pratyekabuddha May Ordain Themselves

The treatise turns to the second category of unexpressed karma: that subsumed under a wholesome vow. It opens by noting the direct contrast with everything just explained: whoever is opposed to the unwholesome vow — accompanied instead by faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, appropriately corresponding to what was described before — should be understood as one whose conduct is sustained by an active wholesome vow, the very precepts a Buddhist practitioner holds, which quietly restrain wrongdoing from within even though this restraint can never be shown outwardly to others. The treatise then explains the necessary condition for actually receiving such precepts: only someone genuinely capable of undertaking them — possessing real revulsion toward worldly existence and real aspiration toward what lies beyond it — can actually receive them. It then distinguishes how precepts are received: some are received through another, some through oneself alone, and one particular case — apart from the monastic precepts specifically — may be received spontaneously, without any preceptor at all, this last possibility belonging solely to buddhas and to those who awaken independently, without a teacher, precisely because their minds are already entirely free of affliction, so precepts naturally accompany their own awakening; every other seeker of ordination, receiving any of the seven categories of precepts, requires a preceptor to administer them properly. The treatise explains why monastic ordination specifically can never be self-administered: not everyone is actually qualified to undertake the monastic life, and if ordination were simply granted to anyone who merely wished it, the entire teaching would lose all order and discipline, with no properly transmitted teaching or discipline left for anyone to actually learn from — it is precisely to preserve this order that a preceptor must always examine and administer monastic ordination directly.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the exception granted only to buddhas and self-awakened pratyekabuddhas as a logical necessity rather than a special privilege: since their minds are already entirely free of the afflictions any precept would otherwise guard against, the precepts simply accompany their own awakening rather than needing to be separately administered — an option unavailable to anyone still in training. 32. Milestone: Serious Illness as a Bar to Ordination — Why Even Sincere Devotion Cannot Overcome an Unsustainable Burden

The treatise turns to the second disqualifying condition: damaged physical basis, meaning someone afflicted with serious conditions such as festering sores or growths, matching the kinds of illness described among the traditional obstacles to ordination. It explains the underlying reason such a person should not be ordained: because their physical basis is genuinely damaged, even sincere ordination would leave them unable to actually serve their own teachers and fellow practitioners as expected, and lacking this capacity, they would instead need to be served and cared for by others — meanwhile still receiving devoted donors' offerings of clothing, food, and other necessities, contributions given in genuine faith. Since such a person can neither repay this service through their own practice nor properly make use of what devoted donors have offered, both kinds of support become genuinely difficult for them to receive without fault, and accepting them regardless would actually diminish their own wholesome qualities rather than support genuine practice — it is precisely to prevent this outcome, harmful to the person themselves as much as to the community, that serious illness bars ordination.

Second, damaged physical basis; first, presenting this category. Whoever carries serious afflictions such as festering sores or growths, matching the conditions described among the traditional obstacles, is said to have damaged physical basis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this reasoning as notably practical rather than merely disciplinary: the concern is not that a seriously ill person is somehow morally unfit, but that ordination under such circumstances would place an unsustainable burden on the community, and would leave the person themselves unable to fulfill the mutual relationship of teaching and service around which monastic life is structured — accepting donations one cannot productively use, the treatise notes, actually erodes rather than builds the recipient's own wholesome qualities. Having explained why serious illness disqualifies ordination, he notes, the treatise turns to the third of the six conditions: damaged male form, addressing a different category of physical circumstance from the "damaged form" already discussed regarding those excluded entirely from either monastic community.
