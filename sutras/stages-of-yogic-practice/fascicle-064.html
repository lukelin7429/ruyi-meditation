Fascicle 64 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十四 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing Opens

Six Reasons the Three Jewels Are Genuinely Precious

With the Ground without Mind now closed, Maitreya opens the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, the vast field of learning spanning inner knowledge of the Dharma, medicine, logic, linguistics, and the practical arts, beginning with what genuine hearing of the Dharma must itself rely upon: the Three Jewels, and the five qualities that make the Buddha, and no other teacher, truly worthy of taking refuge in.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 64 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Ground Opens — Six Reasons the Three Jewels Deserve Their Name

Maitreya marks the transition directly: the decisive discernment of the Ground without Mind now complete, the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing shall now be explained, the vast field of learning a practitioner draws upon in hearing the Dharma, traditionally divided into five sciences, inner knowledge of the Buddhadharma itself, the healing science of medicine, the science of logical proof, the science of language and grammar, and the practical science of craft and trade, a bodhisattva expected to master all five while one seeking only to end affliction as a śrāvaka need master inner knowledge alone. The discussion opens with what genuine hearing of the Dharma must first rely upon, the Three Jewels, and asks why the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha alone deserve to be called jewels at all, answering through six distinct qualities shared by any genuine treasure: their sheer rarity, since one impoverished in merit may pass through countless lifetimes without ever once encountering them; their freedom from stain, since just as a true jewel carries no blemish, the Three Jewels alone can actually remove the defiling afflictions from within; their power, since just as worldly treasure can relieve poverty and neutralize poison, the Three Jewels alone hold the power to dispel the poisons of greed, hatred, and delusion; their capacity to adorn, since just as a jewel adorns the body, the Three Jewels adorn the mind itself, the only ornament that actually matters; their supremacy, the Buddha supreme among the three realms' own noble beings, the Dharma supreme among every teaching, the Sangha supreme among every community; and their changelessness, since just as true gold cannot be altered by fire or hammer, the Three Jewels remain forever unmoved by the eight winds of gain and loss, praise and blame, honor and ridicule, pleasure and pain.

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中聞所成慧地。如是已說無心地決擇。聞所成慧地決擇，我今當說。謂由五處觀察所歸，乃可歸依。一、由身業清淨故；二、由語業清淨故；三、由意業清淨故；四、由於諸有情起大悲故；五、由成就無上法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, within the chapter of decisive discernment. Thus I have already explained the decisive discernment of the Ground without Mind. I shall now explain the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing. One should observe five aspects in what is taken as refuge before genuine refuge can be taken: purity of bodily conduct; purity of verbal conduct; purity of mental conduct; giving rise to great compassion toward all beings; and having accomplished the unsurpassed Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Ratnagotravibhāga's own six meanings of jewel directly, rarity, purity, power, adornment, supremacy, and changelessness, treating this list as the natural and necessary preface to everything the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing will go on to teach, since no amount of learning matters unless what is actually being learned deserves the trust placed in it. He notes that the five qualities of genuine refuge now opening, three kinds of purified conduct joined to great compassion and unsurpassed accomplishment, exist specifically to let a sincere seeker distinguish the Buddha from the ninety-six rival teachers of ancient India, a comparison this section takes up directly rather than leaving refuge as a matter of mere preference.

2. Section: A Teacher Who Watches Six Times Each Day and Night

Each of the five qualities making the Buddha worthy of refuge is explained in turn: purity of bodily conduct, entirely free of killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct; purity of verbal conduct, entirely free of falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, or idle chatter; purity of mental conduct, entirely free of greed, hatred, and delusion; great compassion arising toward all beings without exception, regarding every being, virtuous or otherwise, exactly as a parent regards an only child, ready to guide anyone toward liberation the instant conditions allow; and the accomplishment of the unsurpassed Dharma, perfect and complete awakening itself, wisdom and merit both brought to their utmost fullness. The Lotus Sūtra's own verse on the Buddha's lifespan captures this same great compassion directly: I constantly know which beings practice the path and which do not, and, according to what each is capable of receiving, teach them the Dharma accordingly, always thinking to myself how I might lead every being into the unsurpassed path and swiftly accomplish the Buddha's own body, the Buddha's apparent passing into final nirvana never actually withdrawing from beings at all, but continuing across six periods of every day and night alike, three by day and three by night, to watch with the Buddha eye over the entire world, asking in whom wholesome roots have not yet been planted, and in whom the very fruit of arhatship might yet be established.

Chinese Source

第一處身業清淨，身業不殺盜淫。第二處語業清淨，語業不妄言、不惡口、不兩舌、不綺語。第三處意業清淨，意業沒有貪瞋癡。第四處於諸有情起大悲故，對於一切有情都是等視眾生如自己的獨生子一樣。第五處是由成就無上法故，佛成就無上正等正覺。「我常知眾生、行道不行道，隨所應可度、為說種種法。」「每自作是意、以何令眾生、得入無上道、速成就佛身。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The first aspect is purity of bodily conduct, free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct. The second is purity of verbal conduct, free of falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, and idle chatter. The third is purity of mental conduct, free of greed, hatred, and delusion. The fourth is giving rise to great compassion toward all beings, regarding every being as an only child. The fifth is accomplishing the unsurpassed Dharma, perfect and complete awakening. I constantly know which beings practice the path and which do not, and teach the Dharma according to what each can receive. I always think to myself how I might lead beings into the unsurpassed path and swiftly accomplish the Buddha's own body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Lotus Sūtra's own image of the Buddha watching over the world six times each day and night, three by day and three by night, as this entire section's own warmest and most reassuring teaching, the Buddha's apparent departure into final nirvana never actually meaning abandonment, but a continuing, active vigilance that notices exactly where a given being's own practice stands and waits only for the right conditions to help it forward. He connects this directly to Nāgārjuna's own image of the Buddha and bodhisattvas as those with open eyes looking upon beings still blinded by their own ignorance, unaware that help is even present, precisely why taking refuge in the Three Jewels matters, not as a formality, but as opening oneself to a guidance that Master Cháng Bǎi describes as never actually withheld, only waiting to be recognized.

3. Section: Four Reasons There Are Exactly Three Refuges

Five questions now open in turn: how many kinds of refuge are there, why exactly that many, what marks one as genuinely having taken refuge, how is the practice of refuge actually carried out, and what merit does taking refuge actually bring? The first answer is simple: there are three refuges, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Four causes explain why exactly these three suffice. First, the Buddha's own nature is perfectly tamed and good, having so thoroughly exhausted every attachment and habitual trace that body, speech, and mind require no guarding at all, since none of the three could ever err in the first place. Second, the Buddha is skillful beyond measure in taming beings of absolutely every kind, whether bound by every affliction or already partly freed, of dull, middling, or sharp faculty, of any caste or temperament, householder or renunciant, easy to tame or resistant, needing gentle words or needing sharp rebuke, near to liberation or still very far from it, matching whatever remedy each being's own condition actually calls for across every one of the many realms of existence. Third, the Buddha possesses great compassion in the fullest sense, a compassion taking as its object suffering so deep, so subtle, and so difficult to even recognize that most beings remain entirely unaware how much they actually suffer, cultivated continuously across countless ages without ever tiring, arising through the most intense and deliberate attention toward every being's own good, and utterly pure, since the Buddha helps and then simply lets go, expecting nothing whatsoever in return. Fourth, the Buddha never delights in offerings of mere wealth, but only in genuine practice, exactly as the Sūtra of the Flower Hand declares, that incense, robes, food, and medicine offered to a buddha do not constitute true offering at all, but that anyone who takes up and actually practices the wondrous Dharma realized beneath the Buddha's own tree of awakening offers the one true worship the Buddha genuinely receives.

Chinese Source

答：歸依有三種，謂佛法僧。四緣故有爾所歸依。一、由如來性極調善故；二、於一切種所調、能調善方便故；三、具大悲故；四、以一切財而興供養未將為喜，要以正行而興供養乃生歡喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: there are three kinds of refuge, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Four causes account for exactly this many refuges: the Buddha's own nature is perfectly tamed and good; the Buddha is skillful in every respect toward those to be tamed and the very means of taming them; the Buddha possesses great compassion; and the Buddha does not delight in offerings of mere wealth, but only in offerings made through genuine practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth cause, the Buddha's own indifference to material offering and genuine delight only in a disciple's actual practice, as this entire passage's own most practically important teaching, a direct challenge to any devotional practice that mistakes elaborate ritual offering for what the Buddha himself actually values. He quotes the Sūtra of the Flower Hand's own blunt verse directly, that incense, robes, food, and medicine offered to any buddha do not constitute true offering at all, only genuine practice of the Dharma realized beneath the Buddha's own tree of awakening, treating this as this entire treatise's own recurring insistence that practice, not ceremony, is what devotion actually means.

4. Section: Why the Buddha, and Not the Gods, Deserves to Be Called Teacher

Four further conditions mark what it actually means to take refuge: knowing the Buddha's own genuine virtues; knowing the crucial difference between the Buddha and the various gods, demons, and non-Buddhist teachers, who fail as genuine refuges for two decisive reasons, an actual incapacity to help, since even the gods lack the power, the willingness, the tirelessness, the genuine compassion, and the moral stature the Buddha alone possesses, and simply never being seen to have accomplished anything comparable in the world at all, since the Buddha's own historical existence, serene countenance free of fear, freedom from heedless indulgence, unwavering commitment to benefiting others, and penetrating wisdom stand as facts anyone can actually examine, unlike a god's own claims; one's own sincere vow, arising from a genuine spiritual capacity already present, to seek awakening and accept refuge freely; and simply that no other teacher exists who could possibly be preferred instead. Four distinct practices are then said to constitute the very practice of refuge itself: drawing near to a genuine spiritual friend; actually hearing the true Dharma from that friend, both its words and its meaning; bringing correct attention to what has been heard, reflecting on it exactly as it was actually taught; and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma itself, whether the noble eightfold path and the other factors of awakening for one seeking personal liberation, or the six perfections and four means of gathering others for a bodhisattva pursuing buddhahood on behalf of all beings, one who genuinely accomplishes all four of these practices, and only such a person, being rightly called one who has actually taken refuge at all.

Chinese Source

齊四緣故說能歸依。一、知功德故；二、知差別故；三、自誓願故；四、更不說有餘大師故。當知歸依有四正行。一、親近善士，二、聽聞正法，三、如理作意，四、法隨法行。若有成就此四正行，乃名歸依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Genuine refuge requires four conditions together: knowing the Buddha's virtues; knowing the difference between the Buddha and other teachers; one's own sincere vow; and there being no other teacher to prefer instead. One should know that refuge involves four correct practices: drawing near to a good friend, hearing the true Dharma, bringing correct attention to it, and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. Only one who accomplishes these four practices is genuinely called one who has taken refuge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Display of the Noble Teaching's own extensive case against treating gods and demons as genuine refuges, an incapacity to help rooted in limited power, a need to be flattered and petitioned before helping at all, an attachment to their own heavenly pleasure that leaves them unwilling to endure real hardship for others, and a fundamental lack of true compassion, none of which the Buddha's own conduct exhibits in the slightest. He treats the fourfold practice of refuge, good friend, hearing, reflection, and practice in accordance with the Dharma, as this section's own final, practical answer to what taking refuge actually requires, since without all four genuinely present, reciting the refuge formula itself accomplishes very little on its own.

5. Section: Eight Marks of a Good Friend, and a Second Set of Four Practices

A genuine spiritual friend, capable of guiding a bodhisattva properly, is shown to possess eight distinct qualities: dwelling securely in the moral discipline appropriate to a bodhisattva, without lapse or flaw; broad learning sufficient to actually understand the Dharma being taught; some genuine realization of one's own, not mere book-knowledge alone; compassion that truly grieves for other beings; fearlessness in teaching, never losing mindfulness or eloquence out of fright before an audience; patience under scorn, mockery, or outright hostility; tirelessness in teaching across however many audiences require it; and skillful, unimpeded expression that conveys the Dharma clearly without ever distorting its own true nature, a friend possessing even one or two of these eight already being rare and worth learning from. A proper disciple, in turn, is shown to owe such a friend four things: attending upon them faithfully whether they are well or ill, not merely when convenient; showing them constant courtesy, greeting, bowing, and respectful service; supplying their actual material needs, clothing, food, bedding, and medicine, whenever these fall short; and following their instruction faithfully rather than wandering from teacher to teacher and never going deep enough with any one of them to actually benefit. A second set of four practices then completes the practice of refuge: keeping the six sense faculties from restless wandering toward their objects; studying every training point a bodhisattva should undertake; cultivating genuine compassion for suffering beings, both relieving their present suffering and addressing its very root causes; and constantly making offering to the Three Jewels, whether through material gifts, through bowing and praise, or simply through inwardly recollecting their own genuine virtues.

Chinese Source

當知復有四種正行。一、諸根不掉，二、受學學處，三、悲愍有情，四、應時時間於三寶所勤修供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know there are also four further correct practices: the sense faculties not restlessly wandering; studying the training points; compassion for beings; and, from time to time, diligently cultivating offering toward the Three Jewels.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the eight qualities of a genuine spiritual friend set such a demanding standard that even possessing one or two of them already marks someone worth learning from, a realistic acknowledgment that the fully complete teacher described here remains rare, while the four practices owed in return, faithful attendance, courtesy, material support, and genuine follow-through, describe an ordinary, sustainable relationship rather than a single dramatic gesture of devotion. He treats compassion for beings, addressing not only their present suffering but its actual causes, as this section's own reminder that genuine compassion in practice looks like both direct relief work and patient teaching aimed at preventing suffering before it even arises.

6. Section: Eight Merits That Follow from Genuine Refuge

Those who take refuge sincerely are said to gain eight distinct merits, unfolding across two sets of four. The first set: vast blessing; great joy, arising from three sources at once, delighting in the Three Jewels themselves, delighting in one's own now-purified precepts, and delighting in confident, unshaken trust in one's own actual spiritual progress; genuine concentration, since the very precepts refuge establishes become concentration's own necessary support; and great purity, the transcendent purification that follows once affliction is actually uprooted through sustained practice. The second set: complete protection, whether understood as the guardianship of benevolent spirits drawn near by a mind made pure through refuge, or as the ten inner and ten outer conditions of a genuinely well-favored birth, a human body, a place where the Dharma is actually taught, unimpaired faculties, freedom from any grave past misdeed, freedom from confused or divided faith, a perfected teacher, a perfectly preserved teaching, living exemplars who have actually realized it, its own continued survival in the world, and the material support of generous patrons; a marked diminishment, or even complete disappearance, of every obstacle rooted in wrong belief; entry into the very company of the wise, keeping fellowship with the Teacher and with genuine fellow practitioners of pure conduct; and the delighted approval of the gods themselves, who rejoice at seeing genuine faith arise in the very teaching that, by their own account, once carried them out of a former life and into their present heavenly one, hoping that these same practitioners will one day join their own heavenly company in turn.

Chinese Source

受歸依者獲四功德。一、獲廣大福，二、獲大歡喜，三、獲三摩地，四、獲大清淨。復獲四德。一、大護圓滿；二、於一切種邪信解障皆得輕微，或永滅盡；三、得入聰叡、正行正至、善士眾中，所謂大師、同梵行者；四、為於聖教淨信諸天歡喜愛念。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who take refuge gain four merits: vast blessing, great joy, concentration, and great purity. They also gain four further merits: complete protection; every obstacle rooted in wrong belief becoming markedly diminished or entirely gone; entry into the company of the wise, of correct practice, and of those who have arrived, namely the Teacher and fellow practitioners of pure conduct; and the delighted, loving approval of the gods toward genuine faith in the noble teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the charming detail behind the eighth merit, the gods' own delighted approval, since the very deities looking on are themselves said to have arrived in their heavenly state by virtue of past good conduct traceable to the Dharma, and so watch a new practitioner's own sincere refuge with the same hope one might feel watching a promising student follow a path one has walked before. He treats great joy's own threefold source, the Three Jewels, one's own purified precepts, and confident trust in one's own progress, as a genuinely useful checklist for recognizing whether refuge has actually taken root, since a practitioner lacking any real joy in these three might reasonably ask whether the refuge itself remains more formal than lived.

7. Section: Born from the Buddha's Own Mouth — What Makes the Sangha One Body

A new topic distinguishes the Three Jewels through six further aspects, opening with their distinct function: turning the wheel of the true teaching is the Buddha's own function, passing the Dharma forward for the benefit of all beings; abandoning affliction and the very ground suffering depends upon is the Dharma's own function; and vigorous, ever-increasing advancement in wholesome practice is the Sangha's own function. Considered through faith and understanding, one should cultivate devoted closeness and service toward the Buddha; a heartfelt aspiration to actually realize what is taught toward the Dharma; and toward the Sangha, a confident faith in genuine harmony, dwelling together in one and the same nature, since every disciple is, in five distinct senses, born from the same source: born from the Buddha's own body, no longer merely an ordinary, lowly child of saṃsāra; born from the Buddha's own mouth, arising from the very sound and words of the Dharma once heard; born from the Dharma itself, through correct attention and practice in accordance with what was heard; born through the Dharma's own transformation, following the unchanging path of the Buddha's own dharma-body toward a genuinely similar realization; and equally sharing the Dharma, every disciple alike enjoying only the undefiled wealth of the teaching itself rather than any worldly gain, this same shared nature grounding the six forms of harmonious concord, harmony in body, dwelling together; harmony in speech, free of dispute; harmony in mind, sharing genuine joy; harmony in precepts, practiced together; harmony in view, understood together; and harmony in benefit, shared equally among all.

Chinese Source

云何業故三寶差別？謂轉正教業是佛寶，捨煩惱苦所緣境業是法寶，勇猛增長業是僧寶。云何信解故三寶差別？謂於佛寶，應樹親近承事信解；於法寶所，應樹希求證得信解；於僧寶所，應樹和合同一法性共住信解。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the distinction of the Three Jewels through function? Turning the true teaching is the Buddha's function; abandoning affliction and the object of suffering is the Dharma's function; vigorous increase in practice is the Sangha's function. What is the distinction through faith and understanding? Toward the Buddha, one should cultivate faith through devoted closeness and service; toward the Dharma, faith through aspiring to realize it; toward the Sangha, faith through harmonious dwelling together in one and the same nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold explanation of what makes every disciple share one nature, born of the Buddha's body, mouth, Dharma, and transformation, and equal in sharing the Dharma's own wealth, as an unusually intimate image of spiritual kinship, reframing the entire monastic community not as a mere institution but as something closer to an actual extended family united by a single shared birth. He notes that the six forms of harmonious concord, still recited in monastic communities today, follow directly from this same shared nature, harmony in body, speech, mind, precepts, view, and benefit together describing what genuine community among fellow practitioners should actually look like in daily practice.

8. Section: Better to Suffer Every Pain Than Never Hear the Buddha's Name

Considered through actual practice, one should offer service and support toward the Buddha; cultivate the very skillful means of contemplative practice toward the Dharma; and toward the Sangha, share both wealth and Dharma equally with fellow practitioners of pure conduct, holding precepts and understanding in common as well. Considered through recollection, each of the Three Jewels calls for its own distinct object of remembrance, the Buddha recollected through his own ten powers, four fearlessnesses, and every other excellent quality, or simply through his very name, the Lotus Sūtra itself declaring that even one whose mind is utterly scattered, entering a temple and uttering homage to the Buddha only once, has thereby already planted the very seed of buddhahood, and other scriptures teaching that it is better to endure every possible suffering and still hear the Buddha's voice than to enjoy every possible pleasure and never once hear the Buddha's name, precisely because countless ages of suffering in saṃsāra trace back to having gone this long without ever hearing it. The Dharma, likened to both medicine and a ship carrying beings to nirvana's own far shore, is recollected by distinguishing three kinds of listener, the sightless, who never hear the Dharma at all, the one-eyed, who hears yet never truly takes it to heart, and the truly two-eyed, who listens with full attention and practices exactly as taught, a genuine reverence toward the Dharma illustrated by the story of a nun who prepared an entirely new room, floor scented with fragrant earth, brand-new paper, brush, and ink, and even a mask directing the calligrapher's own breath away from the pages, to have the Lotus Sūtra copied out in the most reverent conditions imaginable, and who, when a visiting monk once borrowed that same copy without first bowing in genuine respect, found the very characters on the page had become entirely invisible to him until he knelt and paid homage properly first. The Sangha is recollected by remembering that even a monk who has broken the precepts still deserves offering and respect rather than contempt, since his very robe continues to plant merit in those who encounter him, exactly as a piece of sandalwood, even once burned, still leaves its own fragrance behind, and as one teaching declares, a broken-precept monk still surpasses every non-Buddhist teacher, so that offering to any monk, worthy or fallen alike, is offering made to the Buddha himself.

Chinese Source

云何修行故三寶差別？謂於佛寶，應修供養承事正行；於法寶所，應修瑜伽方便正行；於僧寶所，應修共受財法正行。云何隨念故三寶差別？應以餘相隨念佛寶，應以餘相隨念法寶，應以餘相隨念僧寶。謂是世尊，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the distinction through practice? Toward the Buddha, cultivate the practice of offering and service; toward the Dharma, cultivate the practice of yogic skillful means; toward the Sangha, cultivate the practice of sharing wealth and Dharma in common. What is the distinction through recollection? Recollect the Buddha jewel, the Dharma jewel, and the Sangha jewel each through their own distinct aspect, namely that this is the World-Honored One, and so extensively explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the nun and the calligrapher as this entire section's own most vivid teaching on reverence, the very characters of the Lotus Sūtra becoming invisible to a monk who had not first shown proper respect, a story he reads less as literal magic than as a pointed illustration of how much a text's own meaning actually depends on the reader's own inner posture toward it. He highlights the sandalwood simile for the Sangha, fragrance remaining even after the wood itself has burned, as this passage's own compassionate resolution of an otherwise uncomfortable teaching, that even a fallen monastic's own robe continues to carry real spiritual value for those who encounter it, however far short of the ideal that particular individual may have fallen.

9. Section: Five Ways the Buddhist Path Outshines the Brahmin's

The sixth and final aspect distinguishes the Three Jewels through the merit their own presence generates: relying on a single being, the Buddha, the most supreme merit is generated; relying on the Dharma itself, that same most supreme merit is generated; and relying on many beings together, the Sangha, the most supreme merit is likewise generated, completing this sixfold analysis of characteristic, function, faith, practice, recollection, and merit. A further topic then opens, distinguishing the genuinely superior from the inferior between Buddhist renunciants and brahmin ascetics through five further aspects. In the teaching each actually hears, the brahmin's own teaching remains empty and inferior in meaning, never openly shown to others, its very wording obscure, while the renunciant's own teaching is complete, openly taught, and clearly expressed, and so superior. In the precepts each actually holds, the brahmin's own precepts permit various forms of harm depending on circumstance or status, while the renunciant's own precepts, forbidding killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and falsehood alike, permit no harm whatsoever, and so are superior. In what each actually embraces, the brahmin embraces fields, houses, wealth, and even wives, servants, and slaves, obstacles to the path all, while the renunciant embraces nothing beyond what actually helps toward freedom from suffering, and so is superior.

Chinese Source

云何生福故三寶差別？謂於佛寶，依一有情生最勝福；於法寶所，即依此法生最勝福；於僧寶所，依多有情生最勝福。復次，由五法故，沙門、婆羅門勝劣差別。何等五法？一者、聞法，二者、戒法，三、攝受法，四、受用法，五、證得法。謂婆羅門所有聞法，義虛劣故，不示他故，文句隱故，是其下劣。沙門聞法與此相違，故是勝妙。又婆羅門所有戒法，隨何、隨分、隨其差別，開許害等，故是下劣。沙門戒法與此相違，故是勝妙。又婆羅門所攝受法，攝受障道田事、宅事、財貨事等，又復攝受妻子、奴婢、僮僕等類，故是下劣。沙門所有攝受之法，除離苦法，更無所有，故是勝妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the distinction through generating merit? Relying on the Buddha jewel, one being, the most supreme merit is generated; relying on the Dharma jewel, this same dharma generates it; relying on the Sangha jewel, many beings generate it. There are also five factors distinguishing the superior from the inferior between renunciants and brahmins: the teaching heard, the precepts held, what is embraced, what is enjoyed, and what is realized. The brahmins' own teaching is empty, inferior, unshown to others, and obscure in wording, and so inferior; the renunciants' teaching is the reverse, and so superior. The brahmins' precepts permit various kinds of harm, and so are inferior; the renunciants' precepts are the reverse, and so superior. The brahmins embrace fields, houses, wealth, wives, servants, and slaves, and so are inferior; the renunciants embrace nothing beyond what frees one from suffering, and so are superior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this direct comparison between Buddhist renunciants and brahmin ascetics as this treatise's own unusually candid apologetic moment, naming specific, checkable differences, openness of teaching, breadth of precepts, and freedom from worldly entanglement, rather than resting the case for the Dharma's superiority on faith claims alone. He notes that the pattern across all three aspects so far, the brahmin's own path permitting exactly what the renunciant's forbids, land, wealth, family entanglement, sets up what the fourth and fifth aspects, still to come, will extend into what each actually enjoys and what each actually realizes at the end of the path.

10. Section: What Is Enjoyed, What Is Finally Realized, and Three Kinds of Seeking

The fourth and fifth aspects complete the comparison between renunciant and brahmin: in what each actually enjoys, the brahmin enjoys ornamentation, song and dance, and outright sexual pleasure, all obstacles to the path, while the renunciant enjoys nothing beyond the faultless wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, and so is superior; and in what each finally realizes, the brahmin's own realization culminates, at best, in rebirth among the Brahma heavens, itself still subject to falling back, still mixed with affliction, and still touched by suffering, while the renunciant's own realization culminates in nirvana itself, never falling back, entirely free of every stain, and entirely and constantly at peace, and so is altogether superior. A further topic then opens, examining three broad kinds of seeking in detail. Seeking after desire itself unfolds across five aspects already named, seeking to acquire, seeking to enjoy, seeking mere amusement, seeking without any real understanding of desire's own danger, and seeking fame through even the smallest wholesome accomplishment. Seeking after continued existence in the higher realms likewise unfolds across five aspects: arising quite naturally, as when a single being spontaneously attains the second absorption as an entire age draws toward its own dissolution, with others simply following that same example; arising through a deliberate vow to be reborn above; arising through the sheer delusion of believing such rebirth to be final and complete, not yet seeing that even the highest heaven remains impermanent; arising through simple aversion to the coarseness of the realm below, without any genuine cutting of affliction; and arising through genuine wisdom, correctly discerning the real danger in every desire and so seeking freedom from it as a means to something greater still.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the final comparison, the Brahma heavens against nirvana itself, as this entire five-part analysis's own decisive conclusion, since even the most exalted rebirth a non-Buddhist path can promise remains touched by impermanence and affliction in a way nirvana simply is not. He notes the striking image behind natural seeking after existence, an entire generation of beings spontaneously rising toward the second absorption as their world approaches its own dissolution, as this treatise's own reminder that even seemingly miraculous spiritual attainment can arise through simple cosmic circumstance rather than any genuine insight, which is exactly why the fifth kind, seeking through actual discernment, is what a serious practitioner should aim for instead.

11. Section: Genuine Seeking After Liberation, Closing the Renunciant's Own Portrait

Seeking after pure conduct, the third and highest kind of seeking, likewise unfolds across five aspects in a first reading: seeking purely and only the undefiled realm itself, nothing less; heading toward its actual attainment by gathering the very requisites nirvana depends upon; presently attaining nirvana with residue; later attaining nirvana without residue; and discerning, through genuine skillful wisdom, how to swiftly realize nirvana in a future life to come. A second reading distinguishes this same seeking through a different fivefold lens: a merely nominal seeking, mistaking the four formless attainments, however exalted, for genuine liberation itself, precisely the error of a non-Buddhist path; the ultimate seeking proper, aiming only at the undefiled realm directly; an observational seeking, cultivating the very skillful wisdom this requires; seeking without genuine skillful means, mistaking the fourth absorption or the formless attainments themselves for liberation and so never actually realizing the truth; and seeking with genuine skillful means, following the correct path of liberation to its own true realization. With these three kinds of seeking, after desire, after continued existence, and after pure conduct, now examined in full, whether pursued rightly, as a genuine renunciant does, or wrongly, as a brahmin or other non-Buddhist teacher does, this entire comparison between the superior and the inferior path reaches its own close.

He notes that closing this entire discussion on exactly this distinction, seeking with genuine skillful means against seeking without it, ties the whole comparison between renunciant and brahmin back to its own real point, that it is ultimately correct understanding, not outward austerity or discipline alone, that separates a genuinely liberating path from one that merely resembles it.

12. Section: Why Inner Knowledge Outshines Every Other Science

A major new topic now opens, turning to the five sciences themselves, already introduced in the Grounds of Wisdom Attained through Hearing. Inner knowledge, the science of the Buddhadharma itself, is declared supreme among every doctrine and school found across all five sciences, and the reason given is fourfold purity. It alone encompasses every meaning belonging to both defilement and purity at once, capable of turning any defiled state into a genuinely pure one. It alone cannot be refuted or overturned by any rival doctrine, its own reasoning simply too sound to be shaken. It alone is genuinely easy to enter, since following its own teaching on the four truths or dependent origination leads naturally toward realizing reality directly. And once entered, its own correct practice never degrades or is lost, sustaining a practitioner in continued purity for as long as that practice continues. The Display of the Noble Teaching's own verse captures this same fourfold purity precisely: encompassing every meaning, unbroken by outside attack, easy to enter, and, once entered, its practice never lost or destroyed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold purity as a genuinely testable claim rather than mere sectarian pride, each of the four qualities, comprehensiveness, unshakeable soundness, accessibility, and durability, something a sincere student can actually verify for themselves through direct study and practice rather than simply accepting on faith. He notes that this section opens what will become an extended and highly systematic treatment of inner knowledge itself, beginning with the ten topics that together comprise the entire scope of what the Buddha actually taught, a framework meant to let any student locate exactly where a given teaching fits within the whole.

13. Section: Ten Topics That Comprise the Buddha's Entire Teaching

A detailed exposition now opens, showing that every teaching the Buddha ever gave falls within just ten topics. The dharma to be cultivated, the practitioner's own sustained, focused, and skillfully applied effort itself. The dharma actually cultivated, every wholesome quality, mundane and transcendent alike. The dharma bound up with fault, everything belonging to the truth of suffering. The dharma bound up with defilement, everything belonging to the truth of origination, ignorance and craving chief among them. The dharma that obstructs, every affliction, self-view, greed, hatred, delusion, pride, and wrong view among them, that blocks a being from ever reaching direct realization. The dharma in accordance with liberation, the thirty-seven factors of awakening belonging to the truth of the path. The dharma of suchness itself, the truth of cessation, reality's own true nature as the very object of non-discriminating wisdom. The dharma of supreme virtues, the ten powers, four fearlessnesses, four kinds of unhindered eloquence, the three samādhis, and the four immeasurable states among the many excellent qualities a practitioner cultivates along the way. The dharma according with the world, cultivating worldly wholesome conduct, abandoning worldly defilement, and continuing, even as an arhat, whatever wholesome conduct remains genuinely fitting. And the dharma of ultimate attainment, the ten qualities of one beyond further training for a śrāvaka, or unsurpassed awakening itself for one following the Great Vehicle.

Among these, the first three gateways are discerned through the latter three: the gateway of true meaning is discerned through the gateway of avoiding two extremes; the gateway of realization is discerned through the gateway of the inconceivable; and the gateway of teaching is discerned through the gateway of intention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these ten topics as an unusually elegant table of contents for the entire Buddhist path, moving in careful order from the practitioner's own effort and its wholesome fruit, through suffering and its cause correctly diagnosed, through what obstructs realization and what actually leads to it, to reality itself, its excellent qualities, its worldly application, and finally its ultimate fruit, nothing genuinely Buddhist falling outside this tenfold scope. He highlights the newly introduced six gateways of reasoning, and their own elegant internal structure, true meaning understood through avoiding extremes, realization through the inconceivable, and teaching through authorial intention, as this section's own promise of a rigorous method for interpreting scripture correctly, a method the following pages will now unpack one gateway at a time.

14. Section: Six Kinds of Truth, from What Everyone Agrees On to What Cannot Be Said at All

The first gateway, true meaning, unfolds into six distinct kinds, already treated at length in the Bodhisattva Grounds' own chapter on true meaning: truth established by simple worldly agreement, whatever ordinary people universally accept as real; truth established by reasoning, the causal and analytical truths a thoughtful mind arrives at, wholesome cause yielding pleasant fruit and unwholesome cause yielding painful fruit among them; truth as the very object of wisdom purified of the affliction obstruction, chiefly the four noble truths themselves; truth as the very object of wisdom purified of the cognitive obstruction, chiefly the emptiness of phenomena realized specifically within a bodhisattva's own domain; established truth, truth given shape through language and designation; and non-established truth, truth realized directly within the mind itself, beyond every path of words and every operation of ordinary thought. Established truth is then explained through three kinds of worldly convention: convention of the world itself, the ordinary designations of house, vase, army, forest, number, self, or being that ordinary experience simply takes for granted; convention of reasoning, the Buddhist teaching's own designations of the aggregates, sense bases, and elements; and convention of realization, the four fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship established according to exactly which afflictions each successive fruit has cut away, from the three fetters severed at stream-entry, through greed, hatred, and delusion thinned at once-returning, the five lower fetters entirely severed at non-returning, to every affliction whatsoever brought to its complete and final end at arhatship.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, briefly, three kinds of worldly convention: convention of the world, convention of reasoning, and convention of realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this six-tiered ladder of truth, from what any ordinary person already agrees is real, up through careful reasoning, the four truths themselves, the deeper emptiness only a bodhisattva's own wisdom discloses, and finally to a truth beyond language altogether, as a genuinely careful acknowledgment that not every claim called true operates at the same depth, a distinction easy to blur when different traditions and different levels of teaching are read side by side without this framework in mind. He highlights the fourfold classification of the four noble fruits according to exactly what affliction each has cut away as this section's own precise, checkable standard for spiritual progress, leaving no room for vague self-assessment about where a practitioner actually stands.

15. Section: A Fourth Convention for the Truth Beyond All Words

A fourth kind of convention is then added to the three already given: convention of ultimate truth itself, the very object supreme wisdom takes as its own domain. Since this ultimate truth cannot, in its own genuine nature, actually be captured in language at all, being realized only directly within the mind, it is nevertheless given a provisional, conventional expression in words solely in order to help give rise to the very wisdom capable of realizing it directly, exactly as a finger pointing at the moon is not itself the moon, yet still serves to guide the eye toward it. Non-established truth is then explained simply as the true suchness of all phenomena, reality's own unchangeable emptiness, known only through direct inner realization rather than through any conventional designation whatsoever, whether the three or four kinds of worldly convention just described. With this, established and non-established truth alike are shown to complete the very structure of true meaning, and the first gateway of reasoning reaches its own close. A second gateway then opens, turning directly to the question of what realization itself actually is.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth convention's own careful reasoning, that ultimate truth is given words only provisionally, purely to help produce the very wisdom that will eventually realize it directly, as this entire treatise's own consistent stance on language and liberation, scripture itself never mistaken for the reality it merely points toward, useful and even necessary, yet ultimately something to be moved through rather than clung to. He notes that the newly opened second gateway, realization, promises to examine this same relationship from another angle entirely, no longer what truth actually is, but what it actually means for a mind to come to know it directly.

16. Section: Four Kinds of Realization, from Ordinary Karma to the Śrāvaka's Own Five

Realization, the second gateway, unfolds into four kinds, briefly stated: the realization belonging to the ripening fruit of ordinary beings' own karma, the realization belonging to the śrāvaka vehicle, the realization belonging to the pratyekabuddha vehicle, and the realization belonging to the Great Vehicle. The first is explained simply: relying on whatever pure or impure karma a being has actually committed as its own cause, that being experiences the ripened fruit of that same karma across the ocean of birth and death, among the five destinies of gods, humans, hell-beings, hungry ghosts, and animals alike. The second, realization belonging to the śrāvaka vehicle, depends first on taking refuge, then on ordaining and cultivating the very adornments proper to a renunciant, faith without pretense, few illnesses, diligence and wisdom, few desires and easy contentment, ease of support, ascetic virtue and dignity, moderation and goodness, discernment and patience, gentleness and excellence, seventeen qualities in all, and upon this foundation unfolds into five further realizations: realization of the ground itself, realization of wisdom, realization of purity, realization of the fruit, and realization of merit.

He notes that placing ordinary karmic realization first, before the śrāvaka's own fivefold path, situates the entire Buddhist path within the widest possible frame, every being's ordinary experience of karmic fruit forming the very backdrop against which a deliberate spiritual path first becomes necessary at all.

17. Section: Three Grounds, Nine Wisdoms, and the Fastest Path to Liberation

The five realizations belonging to the śrāvaka path are now explained one by one. Realization of the ground names three grounds, the ground of seeing, corresponding to the path of seeing and the attainment of stream-entry; the ground of cultivation, corresponding to the path of cultivation spanning from once-returning through the approach to arhatship; and the ultimate ground, corresponding to arhatship itself. Realization of wisdom names nine distinct wisdoms: wisdom of dharma, directly realized within one's own present life; wisdom of its kind, reached instead by inference, extending what is directly known in the desire realm to the form and formless realms beyond direct experience; wisdom of suffering, of origination, of cessation, and of the path, the four truths each directly realized in turn; wisdom subsequently attained, the ordinary, conceptual wisdom that follows once non-discriminating wisdom has already arisen; wisdom of exhaustion, realized when the very last, subtlest affliction is finally cut at the vajra-like absorption bordering arhatship; and wisdom of non-arising, the certainty that no further suffering will ever arise again. Realization of purity names the four kinds of pure faith toward the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, and the precepts. Realization of the fruit names the four fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship. And realization of merit names the four immeasurable states, the eight liberations, the eight mastery-bases, the ten kasinas, and further excellent qualities such as the absorption free of contention, the absorption of aspirational wisdom, unhindered understanding, and the higher forms of direct knowledge. The very cause of this entire fivefold realization is then named: the worldly path of freedom from desire together with the wholesome roots belonging to the branches conducive to liberation and the branches conducive to genuine penetration, the very fastest of śrāvaka practitioners said to reach complete liberation within only three lifetimes, the first spent cultivating the branches conducive to liberation, the second cultivating the branches conducive to penetration, and the third finally entering the path of seeing and reaching liberation's own completion.

18. Section: Three Kinds of Pratyekabuddha, from the Convert to the True Hundred-Eon Seeker

A further topic opens, distinguishing realization belonging to the pratyekabuddha vehicle into three kinds. The first belongs to one who, in a previous life when a buddha was present in the world, already drew near a genuine spiritual friend, heard the true Dharma, and brought correct attention to it, thereby already attaining the wholesome roots of the branches conducive to penetration, yet who does not complete direct realization or reach any śramaṇa fruit in that same lifetime, instead cultivating the six skillful discernments further and reaching the four fruits only in a future life to come. The second belongs to one who, under the same earlier conditions, actually reached direct realization and even attained a śramaṇa fruit, at least stream-entry, yet without yet completing the path, and who must therefore continue cultivating the four truths and the six skillful discernments across a further lifetime, this time one lacking any buddha present in the world, before finally reaching arhatship as what is then called a pratyekabuddha. The third and final kind belongs to the genuine pratyekabuddha proper, one who abides securely in the pratyekabuddha's own spiritual lineage from the very start, spending an entire hundred eons steadily gathering the very requisites and drawing near the Dharma again and again, before finally reaching pratyekabuddha awakening at the end of that immense span, remaining, throughout this entire long preparation, still an ordinary being not yet arrived at the path.

He treats the third, genuine pratyekabuddha's own hundred-eon preparation as this section's own reminder that even a path traditionally associated with solitary, self-taught awakening still depends on an immense span of accumulated practice beforehand, awakening alone in one's final life only after countless earlier lives spent quietly gathering exactly the requisites that make it finally possible.

19. Section: The Bodhisattva's Own Realization, and a Treatise Describes Its Own Architecture

Realization belonging to the Great Vehicle, the fourth and final kind, unfolds across eight aspects: realization through first arousing the very aspiration for unsurpassed awakening; realization through great compassion itself; realization through the perfections, whether six or ten in number; realization through the four means of gathering others, giving, kind speech, beneficial action, and cooperation; realization through the ten grounds themselves, each conferring its own distinct excellent quality; realization through the five infinite spheres and the seven kinds of suchness reached through them, the suchness of ordinary flux, of true reality, of consciousness-only, of establishment, of wrong practice, of purification, and of correct practice, corresponding respectively to the sufferings of birth and death, the twofold emptiness of self and phenomena, mind's own construction of experience, the truth of suffering, the truth of origination, the truth of cessation, and the truth of the path; realization through an utterly sincere faith and understanding toward the Buddhas' and bodhisattvas' own inconceivable majesty, a domain beyond ordinary conjecture yet accepted with genuine confidence once it is properly understood; and realization through the very qualities unique to buddhas alone, the eighteen distinctive dharmas shared with no śrāvaka whatsoever. With this, the entire gateway of realization, and everything already explained at length in the Bodhisattva Grounds, reaches its close, and a third gateway opens directly, asking what teaching itself actually consists of, answered first through three categories: teaching contained within the canon itself, whether the śrāvaka canon or the bodhisattva canon; teaching contained within the treatise-compendium, referring specifically to this very treatise, its seventeen grounds together with its four further compilations, the Compendium of Decisive Discernment, the Compendium of Explanations, the Compendium of Synonyms, and the Compendium of Topics; and teaching contained within both together.

Realization belonging to the Great Vehicle is realization through arousing the aspiration for awakening, through great compassion, through the perfections, through the means of gathering, through the grounds, through the five infinite spheres and the suchness reached through them, through inconceivable majestic faith and understanding, and through the dharmas unique to buddhas. All of this should be known as already explained at length in the Bodhisattva Grounds. What is teaching? Teaching contained within three categories: within the canon, within the treatise-compendium, and within both. What is contained within the canon is the śrāvaka canon and the Great Vehicle canon. What is contained within the treatise-compendium is the seventeen grounds and the four further compilations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this passage's own self-description of the Yogâcārabhūmi's architecture, seventeen grounds together with four compilations, a genuinely rare and useful moment, the treatise pausing to name its own structure directly, exactly the kind of map that helps a serious student see how any single passage, including this very discussion, fits within the whole massive work. He treats the bodhisattva's own eightfold realization, spanning motivation, compassion, practice, the ten grounds, suchness itself, faith before the inconceivable, and the buddhas' own unique qualities, as this fascicle's own most complete summary yet of what the entire Mahāyāna path actually asks of a practitioner, from the very first arising of bodhicitta all the way to qualities belonging to buddhahood alone.

20. Section: Ten Teachings That Together Cover Everything the Treatise Contains

Teaching contained within both the canon and the treatise-compendium together is then shown to unfold across ten distinct kinds. The teaching of the truths' own marks, the sixteen aspects belonging to the four truths themselves. The teaching of full understanding, corresponding to the truth of suffering, which is to be fully known. The teaching of permanent severing, corresponding to the truth of origination, which is to be cut off entirely. The teaching of realization, corresponding to the truth of cessation, which is to be directly realized. The teaching of cultivation, corresponding to the truth of the path, which is to be cultivated. The teaching of classification, the many further subdivisions belonging to each of the four truths in turn. The teaching of the relation between what is depended upon and what depends, the practitioner as what is depended upon, and the four truths themselves as what depends upon that practitioner's own cultivation. The teaching of the obstructing dharma, whatever runs contrary to reaching direct realization at all. The teaching of the accordant dharma, whatever genuinely supports reaching that same realization. And the teaching of fault and merit, the harm that follows from failing to fully know the four truths against the excellent benefit that follows from fully knowing them. Together, these ten teachings are declared to encompass everything contained within both the canon at large and this treatise's own compendium, forming what is called, in brief, the comprehensive treatise-compendium itself.

Chinese Source

二所攝者，略有十種。謂諦相教、遍知教、永斷教、證得教、修習教、即彼品類差別教、即彼所攝所依能依相屬教、遍知等障法教、遍知等順法教、不遍知等遍知等過失功德教。如是能攝一切藏攝及本母攝，是名總略摩怛理迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contained within both is, briefly, of ten kinds: the teaching of the truths' marks, the teaching of full understanding, the teaching of permanent severing, the teaching of realization, the teaching of cultivation, the teaching of classification of these same truths, the teaching of the relation between what is depended upon and what depends within these same truths, the teaching of the dharma that obstructs full understanding, the teaching of the dharma that accords with it, and the teaching of the fault of not fully knowing against the merit of fully knowing. All of this together comprises everything contained within the canon and the treatise-compendium alike, called, in brief, the comprehensive treatise-compendium.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this tenfold classification as a direct extension of the four truths' own already-familiar structure, suffering to be known, origination to be cut off, cessation to be realized, and the path to be cultivated, now paired with classification, dependency, obstruction, accordance, and the contrast of fault against merit, so that the entire teaching gateway ultimately reduces to increasingly refined ways of understanding these same four truths from every possible angle. He notes that naming this entire tenfold structure the comprehensive treatise-compendium closes the third gateway on a note of genuine completeness, everything the Buddha ever taught, whether found in the canon or systematized within this treatise itself, now shown to fit within one single, traceable framework.
