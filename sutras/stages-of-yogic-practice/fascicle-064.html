Fascicle 64 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十四 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing Opens

Six Reasons the Three Jewels Are Genuinely Precious

With the Ground without Mind now closed, Maitreya opens the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, the vast field of learning spanning inner knowledge of the Dharma, medicine, logic, linguistics, and the practical arts, beginning with what genuine hearing of the Dharma must itself rely upon: the Three Jewels, and the five qualities that make the Buddha, and no other teacher, truly worthy of taking refuge in.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 64 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Ground Opens — Six Reasons the Three Jewels Deserve Their Name

Maitreya marks the transition directly: the decisive discernment of the Ground without Mind now complete, the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing shall now be explained, the vast field of learning a practitioner draws upon in hearing the Dharma, traditionally divided into five sciences, inner knowledge of the Buddhadharma itself, the healing science of medicine, the science of logical proof, the science of language and grammar, and the practical science of craft and trade, a bodhisattva expected to master all five while one seeking only to end affliction as a śrāvaka need master inner knowledge alone. The discussion opens with what genuine hearing of the Dharma must first rely upon, the Three Jewels, and asks why the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha alone deserve to be called jewels at all, answering through six distinct qualities shared by any genuine treasure: their sheer rarity, since one impoverished in merit may pass through countless lifetimes without ever once encountering them; their freedom from stain, since just as a true jewel carries no blemish, the Three Jewels alone can actually remove the defiling afflictions from within; their power, since just as worldly treasure can relieve poverty and neutralize poison, the Three Jewels alone hold the power to dispel the poisons of greed, hatred, and delusion; their capacity to adorn, since just as a jewel adorns the body, the Three Jewels adorn the mind itself, the only ornament that actually matters; their supremacy, the Buddha supreme among the three realms' own noble beings, the Dharma supreme among every teaching, the Sangha supreme among every community; and their changelessness, since just as true gold cannot be altered by fire or hammer, the Three Jewels remain forever unmoved by the eight winds of gain and loss, praise and blame, honor and ridicule, pleasure and pain.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, within the chapter of decisive discernment. Thus I have already explained the decisive discernment of the Ground without Mind. I shall now explain the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing. One should observe five aspects in what is taken as refuge before genuine refuge can be taken: purity of bodily conduct; purity of verbal conduct; purity of mental conduct; giving rise to great compassion toward all beings; and having accomplished the unsurpassed Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Ratnagotravibhāga's own six meanings of jewel directly, rarity, purity, power, adornment, supremacy, and changelessness, treating this list as the natural and necessary preface to everything the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing will go on to teach, since no amount of learning matters unless what is actually being learned deserves the trust placed in it. He notes that the five qualities of genuine refuge now opening, three kinds of purified conduct joined to great compassion and unsurpassed accomplishment, exist specifically to let a sincere seeker distinguish the Buddha from the ninety-six rival teachers of ancient India, a comparison this section takes up directly rather than leaving refuge as a matter of mere preference.

2. Section: A Teacher Who Watches Six Times Each Day and Night

Each of the five qualities making the Buddha worthy of refuge is explained in turn: purity of bodily conduct, entirely free of killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct; purity of verbal conduct, entirely free of falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, or idle chatter; purity of mental conduct, entirely free of greed, hatred, and delusion; great compassion arising toward all beings without exception, regarding every being, virtuous or otherwise, exactly as a parent regards an only child, ready to guide anyone toward liberation the instant conditions allow; and the accomplishment of the unsurpassed Dharma, perfect and complete awakening itself, wisdom and merit both brought to their utmost fullness. The Lotus Sūtra's own verse on the Buddha's lifespan captures this same great compassion directly: I constantly know which beings practice the path and which do not, and, according to what each is capable of receiving, teach them the Dharma accordingly, always thinking to myself how I might lead every being into the unsurpassed path and swiftly accomplish the Buddha's own body, the Buddha's apparent passing into final nirvana never actually withdrawing from beings at all, but continuing across six periods of every day and night alike, three by day and three by night, to watch with the Buddha eye over the entire world, asking in whom wholesome roots have not yet been planted, and in whom the very fruit of arhatship might yet be established.

Ru-Yi Translation

The first aspect is purity of bodily conduct, free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct. The second is purity of verbal conduct, free of falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, and idle chatter. The third is purity of mental conduct, free of greed, hatred, and delusion. The fourth is giving rise to great compassion toward all beings, regarding every being as an only child. The fifth is accomplishing the unsurpassed Dharma, perfect and complete awakening. I constantly know which beings practice the path and which do not, and teach the Dharma according to what each can receive. I always think to myself how I might lead beings into the unsurpassed path and swiftly accomplish the Buddha's own body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Lotus Sūtra's own image of the Buddha watching over the world six times each day and night, three by day and three by night, as this entire section's own warmest and most reassuring teaching, the Buddha's apparent departure into final nirvana never actually meaning abandonment, but a continuing, active vigilance that notices exactly where a given being's own practice stands and waits only for the right conditions to help it forward. He connects this directly to Nāgārjuna's own image of the Buddha and bodhisattvas as those with open eyes looking upon beings still blinded by their own ignorance, unaware that help is even present, precisely why taking refuge in the Three Jewels matters, not as a formality, but as opening oneself to a guidance that Master Cháng Bǎi describes as never actually withheld, only waiting to be recognized.

3. Section: Four Reasons There Are Exactly Three Refuges

Five questions now open in turn: how many kinds of refuge are there, why exactly that many, what marks one as genuinely having taken refuge, how is the practice of refuge actually carried out, and what merit does taking refuge actually bring? The first answer is simple: there are three refuges, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Four causes explain why exactly these three suffice. First, the Buddha's own nature is perfectly tamed and good, having so thoroughly exhausted every attachment and habitual trace that body, speech, and mind require no guarding at all, since none of the three could ever err in the first place. Second, the Buddha is skillful beyond measure in taming beings of absolutely every kind, whether bound by every affliction or already partly freed, of dull, middling, or sharp faculty, of any caste or temperament, householder or renunciant, easy to tame or resistant, needing gentle words or needing sharp rebuke, near to liberation or still very far from it, matching whatever remedy each being's own condition actually calls for across every one of the many realms of existence. Third, the Buddha possesses great compassion in the fullest sense, a compassion taking as its object suffering so deep, so subtle, and so difficult to even recognize that most beings remain entirely unaware how much they actually suffer, cultivated continuously across countless ages without ever tiring, arising through the most intense and deliberate attention toward every being's own good, and utterly pure, since the Buddha helps and then simply lets go, expecting nothing whatsoever in return. Fourth, the Buddha never delights in offerings of mere wealth, but only in genuine practice, exactly as the Sūtra of the Flower Hand declares, that incense, robes, food, and medicine offered to a buddha do not constitute true offering at all, but that anyone who takes up and actually practices the wondrous Dharma realized beneath the Buddha's own tree of awakening offers the one true worship the Buddha genuinely receives.

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: there are three kinds of refuge, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Four causes account for exactly this many refuges: the Buddha's own nature is perfectly tamed and good; the Buddha is skillful in every respect toward those to be tamed and the very means of taming them; the Buddha possesses great compassion; and the Buddha does not delight in offerings of mere wealth, but only in offerings made through genuine practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth cause, the Buddha's own indifference to material offering and genuine delight only in a disciple's actual practice, as this entire passage's own most practically important teaching, a direct challenge to any devotional practice that mistakes elaborate ritual offering for what the Buddha himself actually values. He quotes the Sūtra of the Flower Hand's own blunt verse directly, that incense, robes, food, and medicine offered to any buddha do not constitute true offering at all, only genuine practice of the Dharma realized beneath the Buddha's own tree of awakening, treating this as this entire treatise's own recurring insistence that practice, not ceremony, is what devotion actually means.

4. Section: Why the Buddha, and Not the Gods, Deserves to Be Called Teacher

Four further conditions mark what it actually means to take refuge: knowing the Buddha's own genuine virtues; knowing the crucial difference between the Buddha and the various gods, demons, and non-Buddhist teachers, who fail as genuine refuges for two decisive reasons, an actual incapacity to help, since even the gods lack the power, the willingness, the tirelessness, the genuine compassion, and the moral stature the Buddha alone possesses, and simply never being seen to have accomplished anything comparable in the world at all, since the Buddha's own historical existence, serene countenance free of fear, freedom from heedless indulgence, unwavering commitment to benefiting others, and penetrating wisdom stand as facts anyone can actually examine, unlike a god's own claims; one's own sincere vow, arising from a genuine spiritual capacity already present, to seek awakening and accept refuge freely; and simply that no other teacher exists who could possibly be preferred instead. Four distinct practices are then said to constitute the very practice of refuge itself: drawing near to a genuine spiritual friend; actually hearing the true Dharma from that friend, both its words and its meaning; bringing correct attention to what has been heard, reflecting on it exactly as it was actually taught; and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma itself, whether the noble eightfold path and the other factors of awakening for one seeking personal liberation, or the six perfections and four means of gathering others for a bodhisattva pursuing buddhahood on behalf of all beings, one who genuinely accomplishes all four of these practices, and only such a person, being rightly called one who has actually taken refuge at all.

齊四緣故說能歸依。一、知功德故；二、知差別故；三、自誓願故；四、更不說有餘大師故。當知歸依有四正行。一、親近善士，二、聽聞正法，三、如理作意，四、法隨法行。若有成就此四正行，乃名歸依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Genuine refuge requires four conditions together: knowing the Buddha's virtues; knowing the difference between the Buddha and other teachers; one's own sincere vow; and there being no other teacher to prefer instead. One should know that refuge involves four correct practices: drawing near to a good friend, hearing the true Dharma, bringing correct attention to it, and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. Only one who accomplishes these four practices is genuinely called one who has taken refuge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Display of the Noble Teaching's own extensive case against treating gods and demons as genuine refuges, an incapacity to help rooted in limited power, a need to be flattered and petitioned before helping at all, an attachment to their own heavenly pleasure that leaves them unwilling to endure real hardship for others, and a fundamental lack of true compassion, none of which the Buddha's own conduct exhibits in the slightest. He treats the fourfold practice of refuge, good friend, hearing, reflection, and practice in accordance with the Dharma, as this section's own final, practical answer to what taking refuge actually requires, since without all four genuinely present, reciting the refuge formula itself accomplishes very little on its own.

5. Section: Eight Marks of a Good Friend, and a Second Set of Four Practices

A genuine spiritual friend, capable of guiding a bodhisattva properly, is shown to possess eight distinct qualities: dwelling securely in the moral discipline appropriate to a bodhisattva, without lapse or flaw; broad learning sufficient to actually understand the Dharma being taught; some genuine realization of one's own, not mere book-knowledge alone; compassion that truly grieves for other beings; fearlessness in teaching, never losing mindfulness or eloquence out of fright before an audience; patience under scorn, mockery, or outright hostility; tirelessness in teaching across however many audiences require it; and skillful, unimpeded expression that conveys the Dharma clearly without ever distorting its own true nature, a friend possessing even one or two of these eight already being rare and worth learning from. A proper disciple, in turn, is shown to owe such a friend four things: attending upon them faithfully whether they are well or ill, not merely when convenient; showing them constant courtesy, greeting, bowing, and respectful service; supplying their actual material needs, clothing, food, bedding, and medicine, whenever these fall short; and following their instruction faithfully rather than wandering from teacher to teacher and never going deep enough with any one of them to actually benefit. A second set of four practices then completes the practice of refuge: keeping the six sense faculties from restless wandering toward their objects; studying every training point a bodhisattva should undertake; cultivating genuine compassion for suffering beings, both relieving their present suffering and addressing its very root causes; and constantly making offering to the Three Jewels, whether through material gifts, through bowing and praise, or simply through inwardly recollecting their own genuine virtues.

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know there are also four further correct practices: the sense faculties not restlessly wandering; studying the training points; compassion for beings; and, from time to time, diligently cultivating offering toward the Three Jewels.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the eight qualities of a genuine spiritual friend set such a demanding standard that even possessing one or two of them already marks someone worth learning from, a realistic acknowledgment that the fully complete teacher described here remains rare, while the four practices owed in return, faithful attendance, courtesy, material support, and genuine follow-through, describe an ordinary, sustainable relationship rather than a single dramatic gesture of devotion. He treats compassion for beings, addressing not only their present suffering but its actual causes, as this section's own reminder that genuine compassion in practice looks like both direct relief work and patient teaching aimed at preventing suffering before it even arises.

6. Section: Eight Merits That Follow from Genuine Refuge

Those who take refuge sincerely are said to gain eight distinct merits, unfolding across two sets of four. The first set: vast blessing; great joy, arising from three sources at once, delighting in the Three Jewels themselves, delighting in one's own now-purified precepts, and delighting in confident, unshaken trust in one's own actual spiritual progress; genuine concentration, since the very precepts refuge establishes become concentration's own necessary support; and great purity, the transcendent purification that follows once affliction is actually uprooted through sustained practice. The second set: complete protection, whether understood as the guardianship of benevolent spirits drawn near by a mind made pure through refuge, or as the ten inner and ten outer conditions of a genuinely well-favored birth, a human body, a place where the Dharma is actually taught, unimpaired faculties, freedom from any grave past misdeed, freedom from confused or divided faith, a perfected teacher, a perfectly preserved teaching, living exemplars who have actually realized it, its own continued survival in the world, and the material support of generous patrons; a marked diminishment, or even complete disappearance, of every obstacle rooted in wrong belief; entry into the very company of the wise, keeping fellowship with the Teacher and with genuine fellow practitioners of pure conduct; and the delighted approval of the gods themselves, who rejoice at seeing genuine faith arise in the very teaching that, by their own account, once carried them out of a former life and into their present heavenly one, hoping that these same practitioners will one day join their own heavenly company in turn.

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who take refuge gain four merits: vast blessing, great joy, concentration, and great purity. They also gain four further merits: complete protection; every obstacle rooted in wrong belief becoming markedly diminished or entirely gone; entry into the company of the wise, of correct practice, and of those who have arrived, namely the Teacher and fellow practitioners of pure conduct; and the delighted, loving approval of the gods toward genuine faith in the noble teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the charming detail behind the eighth merit, the gods' own delighted approval, since the very deities looking on are themselves said to have arrived in their heavenly state by virtue of past good conduct traceable to the Dharma, and so watch a new practitioner's own sincere refuge with the same hope one might feel watching a promising student follow a path one has walked before. He treats great joy's own threefold source, the Three Jewels, one's own purified precepts, and confident trust in one's own progress, as a genuinely useful checklist for recognizing whether refuge has actually taken root, since a practitioner lacking any real joy in these three might reasonably ask whether the refuge itself remains more formal than lived.

7. Section: Born from the Buddha's Own Mouth — What Makes the Sangha One Body

A new topic distinguishes the Three Jewels through six further aspects, opening with their distinct function: turning the wheel of the true teaching is the Buddha's own function, passing the Dharma forward for the benefit of all beings; abandoning affliction and the very ground suffering depends upon is the Dharma's own function; and vigorous, ever-increasing advancement in wholesome practice is the Sangha's own function. Considered through faith and understanding, one should cultivate devoted closeness and service toward the Buddha; a heartfelt aspiration to actually realize what is taught toward the Dharma; and toward the Sangha, a confident faith in genuine harmony, dwelling together in one and the same nature, since every disciple is, in five distinct senses, born from the same source: born from the Buddha's own body, no longer merely an ordinary, lowly child of saṃsāra; born from the Buddha's own mouth, arising from the very sound and words of the Dharma once heard; born from the Dharma itself, through correct attention and practice in accordance with what was heard; born through the Dharma's own transformation, following the unchanging path of the Buddha's own dharma-body toward a genuinely similar realization; and equally sharing the Dharma, every disciple alike enjoying only the undefiled wealth of the teaching itself rather than any worldly gain, this same shared nature grounding the six forms of harmonious concord, harmony in body, dwelling together; harmony in speech, free of dispute; harmony in mind, sharing genuine joy; harmony in precepts, practiced together; harmony in view, understood together; and harmony in benefit, shared equally among all.

What is the distinction of the Three Jewels through function? Turning the true teaching is the Buddha's function; abandoning affliction and the object of suffering is the Dharma's function; vigorous increase in practice is the Sangha's function. What is the distinction through faith and understanding? Toward the Buddha, one should cultivate faith through devoted closeness and service; toward the Dharma, faith through aspiring to realize it; toward the Sangha, faith through harmonious dwelling together in one and the same nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold explanation of what makes every disciple share one nature, born of the Buddha's body, mouth, Dharma, and transformation, and equal in sharing the Dharma's own wealth, as an unusually intimate image of spiritual kinship, reframing the entire monastic community not as a mere institution but as something closer to an actual extended family united by a single shared birth. He notes that the six forms of harmonious concord, still recited in monastic communities today, follow directly from this same shared nature, harmony in body, speech, mind, precepts, view, and benefit together describing what genuine community among fellow practitioners should actually look like in daily practice.

8. Section: Better to Suffer Every Pain Than Never Hear the Buddha's Name

Considered through actual practice, one should offer service and support toward the Buddha; cultivate the very skillful means of contemplative practice toward the Dharma; and toward the Sangha, share both wealth and Dharma equally with fellow practitioners of pure conduct, holding precepts and understanding in common as well. Considered through recollection, each of the Three Jewels calls for its own distinct object of remembrance, the Buddha recollected through his own ten powers, four fearlessnesses, and every other excellent quality, or simply through his very name, the Lotus Sūtra itself declaring that even one whose mind is utterly scattered, entering a temple and uttering homage to the Buddha only once, has thereby already planted the very seed of buddhahood, and other scriptures teaching that it is better to endure every possible suffering and still hear the Buddha's voice than to enjoy every possible pleasure and never once hear the Buddha's name, precisely because countless ages of suffering in saṃsāra trace back to having gone this long without ever hearing it. The Dharma, likened to both medicine and a ship carrying beings to nirvana's own far shore, is recollected by distinguishing three kinds of listener, the sightless, who never hear the Dharma at all, the one-eyed, who hears yet never truly takes it to heart, and the truly two-eyed, who listens with full attention and practices exactly as taught, a genuine reverence toward the Dharma illustrated by the story of a nun who prepared an entirely new room, floor scented with fragrant earth, brand-new paper, brush, and ink, and even a mask directing the calligrapher's own breath away from the pages, to have the Lotus Sūtra copied out in the most reverent conditions imaginable, and who, when a visiting monk once borrowed that same copy without first bowing in genuine respect, found the very characters on the page had become entirely invisible to him until he knelt and paid homage properly first. The Sangha is recollected by remembering that even a monk who has broken the precepts still deserves offering and respect rather than contempt, since his very robe continues to plant merit in those who encounter him, exactly as a piece of sandalwood, even once burned, still leaves its own fragrance behind, and as one teaching declares, a broken-precept monk still surpasses every non-Buddhist teacher, so that offering to any monk, worthy or fallen alike, is offering made to the Buddha himself.

What is the distinction through recollection? Recollect the Buddha jewel, the Dharma jewel, and the Sangha jewel each through their own distinct aspect, namely that this is the World-Honored One, and so extensively explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the nun and the calligrapher as this entire section's own most vivid teaching on reverence, the very characters of the Lotus Sūtra becoming invisible to a monk who had not first shown proper respect, a story he reads less as literal magic than as a pointed illustration of how much a text's own meaning actually depends on the reader's own inner posture toward it. He highlights the sandalwood simile for the Sangha, fragrance remaining even after the wood itself has burned, as this passage's own compassionate resolution of an otherwise uncomfortable teaching, that even a fallen monastic's own robe continues to carry real spiritual value for those who encounter it, however far short of the ideal that particular individual may have fallen.

9. Section: Five Ways the Buddhist Path Outshines the Brahmin's

The sixth and final aspect distinguishes the Three Jewels through the merit their own presence generates: relying on a single being, the Buddha, the most supreme merit is generated; relying on the Dharma itself, that same most supreme merit is generated; and relying on many beings together, the Sangha, the most supreme merit is likewise generated, completing this sixfold analysis of characteristic, function, faith, practice, recollection, and merit. A further topic then opens, distinguishing the genuinely superior from the inferior between Buddhist renunciants and brahmin ascetics through five further aspects. In the teaching each actually hears, the brahmin's own teaching remains empty and inferior in meaning, never openly shown to others, its very wording obscure, while the renunciant's own teaching is complete, openly taught, and clearly expressed, and so superior. In the precepts each actually holds, the brahmin's own precepts permit various forms of harm depending on circumstance or status, while the renunciant's own precepts, forbidding killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and falsehood alike, permit no harm whatsoever, and so are superior. In what each actually embraces, the brahmin embraces fields, houses, wealth, and even wives, servants, and slaves, obstacles to the path all, while the renunciant embraces nothing beyond what actually helps toward freedom from suffering, and so is superior.

Chinese Source

云何生福故三寶差別？謂於佛寶，依一有情生最勝福；於法寶所，即依此法生最勝福；於僧寶所，依多有情生最勝福。復次，由五法故，沙門、婆羅門勝劣差別。何等五法？一者、聞法，二者、戒法，三、攝受法，四、受用法，五、證得法。謂婆羅門所有聞法，義虛劣故，不示他故，文句隱故，是其下劣。沙門聞法與此相違，故是勝妙。又婆羅門所有戒法，隨何、隨分、隨其差別，開許害等，故是下劣。沙門戒法與此相違，故是勝妙。又婆羅門所攝受法，攝受障道田事、宅事、財貨事等，又復攝受妻子、奴婢、僮僕等類，故是下劣。沙門所有攝受之法，除離苦法，更無所有，故是勝妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the distinction through generating merit? Relying on the Buddha jewel, one being, the most supreme merit is generated; relying on the Dharma jewel, this same dharma generates it; relying on the Sangha jewel, many beings generate it. There are also five factors distinguishing the superior from the inferior between renunciants and brahmins: the teaching heard, the precepts held, what is embraced, what is enjoyed, and what is realized. The brahmins' own teaching is empty, inferior, unshown to others, and obscure in wording, and so inferior; the renunciants' teaching is the reverse, and so superior. The brahmins' precepts permit various kinds of harm, and so are inferior; the renunciants' precepts are the reverse, and so superior. The brahmins embrace fields, houses, wealth, wives, servants, and slaves, and so are inferior; the renunciants embrace nothing beyond what frees one from suffering, and so are superior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this direct comparison between Buddhist renunciants and brahmin ascetics as this treatise's own unusually candid apologetic moment, naming specific, checkable differences, openness of teaching, breadth of precepts, and freedom from worldly entanglement, rather than resting the case for the Dharma's superiority on faith claims alone. He notes that the pattern across all three aspects so far, the brahmin's own path permitting exactly what the renunciant's forbids, land, wealth, family entanglement, sets up what the fourth and fifth aspects, still to come, will extend into what each actually enjoys and what each actually realizes at the end of the path.

10. Section: What Is Enjoyed, What Is Finally Realized, and Three Kinds of Seeking

A further topic then opens, examining three broad kinds of seeking in detail. Seeking after desire itself unfolds across five aspects already named, seeking to acquire, seeking to enjoy, seeking mere amusement, seeking without any real understanding of desire's own danger, and seeking fame through even the smallest wholesome accomplishment. Seeking after continued existence in the higher realms likewise unfolds across five aspects: arising quite naturally, as when a single being spontaneously attains the second absorption as an entire age draws toward its own dissolution, with others simply following that same example; arising through a deliberate vow to be reborn above; arising through the sheer delusion of believing such rebirth to be final and complete, not yet seeing that even the highest heaven remains impermanent; arising through simple aversion to the coarseness of the realm below, without any genuine cutting of affliction; and arising through genuine wisdom, correctly discerning the real danger in every desire and so seeking freedom from it as a means to something greater still.

11. Section: Genuine Seeking After Liberation, Closing the Renunciant's Own Portrait

Seeking after pure conduct, the third and highest kind of seeking, likewise unfolds across five aspects in a first reading: seeking purely and only the undefiled realm itself, nothing less; heading toward its actual attainment by gathering the very requisites nirvana depends upon; presently attaining nirvana with residue; later attaining nirvana without residue; and discerning, through genuine skillful wisdom, how to swiftly realize nirvana in a future life to come. A second reading distinguishes this same seeking through a different fivefold lens: a merely nominal seeking, mistaking the four formless attainments, however exalted, for genuine liberation itself, precisely the error of a non-Buddhist path; the ultimate seeking proper, aiming only at the undefiled realm directly; an observational seeking, cultivating the very skillful wisdom this requires; seeking without genuine skillful means, mistaking the fourth absorption or the formless attainments themselves for liberation and so never actually realizing the truth; and seeking with genuine skillful means, following the correct path of liberation to its own true realization. With these three kinds of seeking, after desire, after continued existence, and after pure conduct, now examined in full, whether pursued rightly, as a genuine renunciant does, or wrongly, as a brahmin or other non-Buddhist teacher does, this entire comparison between the superior and the inferior path reaches its own close.

12. Section: Why Inner Knowledge Outshines Every Other Science

A major new topic now opens, turning to the five sciences themselves, already introduced in the Grounds of Wisdom Attained through Hearing. It alone cannot be refuted or overturned by any rival doctrine, its own reasoning simply too sound to be shaken. It alone is genuinely easy to enter, since following its own teaching on the four truths or dependent origination leads naturally toward realizing reality directly. And once entered, its own correct practice never degrades or is lost, sustaining a practitioner in continued purity for as long as that practice continues. The Display of the Noble Teaching's own verse captures this same fourfold purity precisely: encompassing every meaning, unbroken by outside attack, easy to enter, and, once entered, its practice never lost or destroyed.

13. Section: Ten Topics That Comprise the Buddha's Entire Teaching

A detailed exposition now opens, showing that every teaching the Buddha ever gave falls within just ten topics. The dharma to be cultivated, the practitioner's own sustained, focused, and skillfully applied effort itself. The dharma actually cultivated, every wholesome quality, mundane and transcendent alike. The dharma bound up with fault, everything belonging to the truth of suffering. The dharma bound up with defilement, everything belonging to the truth of origination, ignorance and craving chief among them. The dharma that obstructs, every affliction, self-view, greed, hatred, delusion, pride, and wrong view among them, that blocks a being from ever reaching direct realization. The dharma in accordance with liberation, the thirty-seven factors of awakening belonging to the truth of the path. The dharma of suchness itself, the truth of cessation, reality's own true nature as the very object of non-discriminating wisdom. The dharma of supreme virtues, the ten powers, four fearlessnesses, four kinds of unhindered eloquence, the three samādhis, and the four immeasurable states among the many excellent qualities a practitioner cultivates along the way. The dharma according with the world, cultivating worldly wholesome conduct, abandoning worldly defilement, and continuing, even as an arhat, whatever wholesome conduct remains genuinely fitting. And the dharma of ultimate attainment, the ten qualities of one beyond further training for a śrāvaka, or unsurpassed awakening itself for one following the Great Vehicle.

14. Section: Six Kinds of Truth, from What Everyone Agrees On to What Cannot Be Said at All

The first gateway, true meaning, unfolds into six distinct kinds, already treated at length in the Bodhisattva Grounds' own chapter on true meaning: truth established by simple worldly agreement, whatever ordinary people universally accept as real; truth established by reasoning, the causal and analytical truths a thoughtful mind arrives at, wholesome cause yielding pleasant fruit and unwholesome cause yielding painful fruit among them; truth as the very object of wisdom purified of the affliction obstruction, chiefly the four noble truths themselves; truth as the very object of wisdom purified of the cognitive obstruction, chiefly the emptiness of phenomena realized specifically within a bodhisattva's own domain; established truth, truth given shape through language and designation; and non-established truth, truth realized directly within the mind itself, beyond every path of words and every operation of ordinary thought. Established truth is then explained through three kinds of worldly convention: convention of the world itself, the ordinary designations of house, vase, army, forest, number, self, or being that ordinary experience simply takes for granted; convention of reasoning, the Buddhist teaching's own designations of the aggregates, sense bases, and elements; and convention of realization, the four fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship established according to exactly which afflictions each successive fruit has cut away, from the three fetters severed at stream-entry, through greed, hatred, and delusion thinned at once-returning, the five lower fetters entirely severed at non-returning, to every affliction whatsoever brought to its complete and final end at arhatship.

15. Section: A Fourth Convention for the Truth Beyond All Words

A fourth kind of convention is then added to the three already given: convention of ultimate truth itself, the very object supreme wisdom takes as its own domain. Since this ultimate truth cannot, in its own genuine nature, actually be captured in language at all, being realized only directly within the mind, it is nevertheless given a provisional, conventional expression in words solely in order to help give rise to the very wisdom capable of realizing it directly, exactly as a finger pointing at the moon is not itself the moon, yet still serves to guide the eye toward it. Non-established truth is then explained simply as the true suchness of all phenomena, reality's own unchangeable emptiness, known only through direct inner realization rather than through any conventional designation whatsoever, whether the three or four kinds of worldly convention just described. With this, established and non-established truth alike are shown to complete the very structure of true meaning, and the first gateway of reasoning reaches its own close. 18. Section: Three Kinds of Pratyekabuddha, from the Convert to the True Hundred-Eon Seeker

A further topic opens, distinguishing realization belonging to the pratyekabuddha vehicle into three kinds. What is contained within the treatise-compendium is the seventeen grounds and the four further compilations.

20. Section: Ten Teachings That Together Cover Everything the Treatise Contains

Teaching contained within both the canon and the treatise-compendium together is then shown to unfold across ten distinct kinds. The teaching of the truths' own marks, the sixteen aspects belonging to the four truths themselves. The teaching of full understanding, corresponding to the truth of suffering, which is to be fully known. The teaching of permanent severing, corresponding to the truth of origination, which is to be cut off entirely. The teaching of realization, corresponding to the truth of cessation, which is to be directly realized. The teaching of cultivation, corresponding to the truth of the path, which is to be cultivated. The teaching of classification, the many further subdivisions belonging to each of the four truths in turn. The teaching of the relation between what is depended upon and what depends, the practitioner as what is depended upon, and the four truths themselves as what depends upon that practitioner's own cultivation. The teaching of the obstructing dharma, whatever runs contrary to reaching direct realization at all. The teaching of the accordant dharma, whatever genuinely supports reaching that same realization. And the teaching of fault and merit, the harm that follows from failing to fully know the four truths against the excellent benefit that follows from fully knowing them. Together, these ten teachings are declared to encompass everything contained within both the canon at large and this treatise's own compendium, forming what is called, in brief, the comprehensive treatise-compendium itself.

21. Section: Sixteen Aspects of the Four Truths, and Twelve Further Kinds of Teaching

The truths' own marks are explained in full through sixteen aspects, four belonging to each truth in turn: suffering understood as impermanent, as suffering itself, as empty, and as without self; origination understood as cause, as origin, as arising, and as condition; cessation understood as cessation itself, as peace, as excellence, and as genuine freedom; and the path understood as path, as suchness, as practice, and as deliverance, a fourfold contemplation of each truth taught in careful detail within the Grounds of Śrāvakas. Teaching of the matter itself is explained first, simply naming the actual substance of each phenomenon individually, form and the rest, eye and the rest, the very causal realities every other teaching ultimately refers back to. Teaching of conceptual distinctions is explained next, unfolding across the skillful classifications of the aggregates, elements, sense bases, dependent origination, the possible and impossible, the faculties, and the truths; the thirty-seven factors of awakening; and further conceptual distinctions such as possessing form or lacking it, visible or invisible, resistant or unresistant, countless buddhas across countless ages continuing to teach these same distinctions in ever-greater and inexhaustible detail.

22. Section: Ten Perfections of a Dharma Teacher, and Speech Sweet Enough to Persuade

23. Section: Seven Aspects of Logic for Defeating a Rival's Argument

Teaching that examines other traditions concerns the seven aspects of the science of logic, relied upon to overturn rival doctrines and establish one's own teaching soundly, already explained at length in the Grounds of Wisdom Attained through Hearing: the very nature of debate itself, unfolding into six kinds, ordinary discussion, scholarly discourse, contention, slander, correct advocacy, and instruction; the venue of debate, whether before a king, a magistrate, a large assembly, the wise, sympathetic renunciants and brahmins, or genuine seekers of the truth; the basis of debate, ten in number, two things to be established, essential nature and differentiation, and eight means of establishing them, proposition, reason, example, similarity, dissimilarity, direct perception, inference, and authoritative testimony; the very ornaments that make an argument compelling, five in number, mastery of both one's own and a rival's position, complete and fitting expression, fearlessness, dignified composure, and worthiness of respect; the three ways an argument can actually be defeated, conceding the point outright, faltering in one's own words, or committing an actual error in speech; liberation from debate, weighing beforehand whether to actually engage at all through careful observation of what stands to be gained or lost, of the assembly present, and of one's own genuine skill or lack of it; and the very effective functions debate can serve, mastering both traditions, courageous fearlessness, and inexhaustible eloquence.

24. Section: Five Basic Realities, and Teaching That Hides Versus Teaching That Reveals

Teaching of conventional truth is explained through the five basic realities that structure all experience, characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct wisdom, since concept always precedes designation and designation always precedes speech, so that whatever arises through the power of name, concept, and language, every characteristic, name, and discriminating thought that experience actually presents, belongs entirely to conventional truth, whether bound to the desire realm, the form and formless realms, or entirely unbound altogether. Teaching of ultimate truth names the four noble truths directly, together with suchness, reality's own limit, the dharma realm, and every other name given to reality exactly as it is. Hidden teaching refers chiefly to the śrāvaka canon, since the Buddha, teaching according to the śrāvaka disciple's own capacity, taught only the path to personal liberation and never openly disclosed the deeper teaching of the ālaya-consciousness, this restraint itself counting, from the Great Vehicle's own perspective, as a kind of hidden teaching. Explicit teaching refers chiefly to the Great Vehicle canon, openly encouraging its own disciples toward the six perfections and the complete bodhisattva path all the way to unsurpassed awakening, though even here a smaller portion remains more indirectly expressed.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five basic realities, characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct wisdom, as this treatise's own compact summary of how ordinary experience is actually constructed, concept preceding language and language in turn shaping how experience itself gets carved up into distinct things at all, everything on this side of the ledger belonging to convention rather than to ultimate reality directly. He notes that calling the śrāvaka teaching hidden is not a criticism but a recognition of skillful pedagogy, the Buddha withholding the ālaya-consciousness's own deeper teaching from disciples for whom the simpler path to personal liberation was itself already sufficient and appropriate.

25. Section: Fourteen Questions the Buddha Refused to Answer

Teaching of what can be answered names the four seals of the Dharma directly: all formations are impermanent, all feelings are suffering, all phenomena are without self, and nirvana is peace, teachings the Buddha answered and taught openly without reservation. Teaching of what cannot be answered concerns instead those questions the Buddha deliberately declined to resolve at all, such as whether the world is eternal, met only with the reply that this question cannot be answered, or whether the Tathāgata exists or does not exist after death, met with exactly the same refusal, fourteen such unanswerable questions in total. Four reasons explain why the Buddha declined to answer these questions at all. First, because the very premise fails to exist, as when someone asks whether the self is identical to or different from the five aggregates, a question with nothing real underlying it at all, since no genuine self exists in the first place for the question to even be about. Second, because answering would bring no genuine benefit, exactly as the Buddha himself declared, holding up a handful of siṃśapā leaves and comparing them to the vast forest of leaves representing everything he had actually realized, explaining that he taught only what genuinely helps a listener progress toward liberation, leaving everything else, however true, deliberately unspoken. Third, because the matter is simply too profound, as when someone asks whether the self exists or does not exist, either answer only encouraging a listener to seize upon some mistaken view of self all the more firmly, or asks about the Tathāgata's own state after death, a question resting on assumptions no ordinary, undeveloped mind can actually follow through to any correct conclusion. And fourth, because some things are simply established just as they naturally are, as when someone asks whether suchness is the same as or different from the phenomena it underlies, a question already resolved by suchness's own unchangeable nature, neither same nor different by definition, so that pursuing the question further accomplishes nothing a sincere practitioner's own direct realization would not already make clear.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the siṃśapā leaves parable as one of the most quietly humbling images in the entire canon, a fully awakened Buddha holding up a mere handful of leaves against an entire forest to represent the sheer scale of what he actually understood yet chose not to teach, precisely because his own criterion for speech was never completeness but genuine benefit to the listener in front of him. He notes that the fourteen unanswerable questions, spanning the world's own eternity, the self's own existence, and the Tathāgata's own fate after death, share a single deep structure across all four reasons, each one either resting on a false premise, offering no practical benefit, exceeding what an untrained mind could actually verify, or already settled by reality's own nature, a pattern worth recognizing whenever a question, however fascinating, turns out to lead nowhere useful.

26. Section: Four More Reasons for Silence, Closing the Teaching Gateway

Four further reasons are then given for these same fourteen unanswerable questions: that non-Buddhist teachers themselves proclaim false answers to them, each already exhibiting a mistaken clinging to increase or diminishment, or to sameness or difference, regarding the self and other phenomena; that such questions simply do not accord with reality as it actually is; that they bring no genuine benefit at all, since two further causes explain this lack of benefit precisely, one, that pursuing them draws attention away from correctly reflecting on cause and effect, and two, that it draws attention away from correctly reflecting on defilement and purity; and that they serve only as fertile ground for further contention, since whatever position one takes on the world's own inhabitants or the surrounding world itself, whether things are exactly so, otherwise than so, or not so at all, invites nothing but further dispute. With every reason for these deliberate silences now fully explained, the entire teaching gateway, and its full twelvefold classification, reaches its own close. A fourth gateway then opens directly, asking what it actually means to avoid both extremes and find the middle way instead.

He treats the newly opened fourth gateway, avoiding the two extremes, as a natural next step after so much attention already given to what should and should not be answered, since finding the middle way itself requires exactly this same skill, recognizing which of two opposing positions to actually decline rather than settling into either one.

27. Section: Six Extremes to Avoid, and Six Things Never Worth Speculating About

Avoiding the two extremes, the fourth gateway, is explained through six kinds, already treated at length in earlier passages of this treatise: avoiding the extreme of exaggerating what is not genuinely real into real existence, treating phenomena that are actually illusory as though they possessed some fixed, independent reality; avoiding the opposite extreme of denying what is genuinely real into complete non-existence, denying the very emptiness of self and phenomena that constitutes reality's own true nature, as though nothing at all remained once illusion were seen through; avoiding the mistaken clinging to eternalism, believing one's own body and mind to be permanent and unchanging; avoiding the mistaken clinging to nihilism, believing that absolutely nothing remains after death; avoiding indulgence in sensory pleasure, which only nourishes greed, hatred, and delusion and obstructs genuine practice; and avoiding self-mortification, which only nourishes delusion in turn and brings no genuine benefit to practice either, these six falling naturally into three paired extremes, exaggeration against diminishment, eternalism against nihilism, and pleasure against harsh asceticism, every one of them to be avoided by a practitioner walking the middle way instead. A fifth gateway then opens, asking what should never be speculated about at all, answered through six distinct kinds: speculation about the self, about sentient beings in general, about the world itself, about the karmic fruit of beings' own actions, about the meditative states meditators themselves actually experience, and about the Buddhas' own domain.

28. Section: Sixteen Ways the Mind Spins Stories About a Self That Isn't There

Speculation about the self is explained first: relying on the view of a real self, a person may wonder whether they themselves existed in some past life, under what name, eating what food, related to whom, born into what lineage, having accomplished what great deeds, extending this same idle wondering across all three times, a pursuit entirely without point, since no genuine self can be found in the past, the present, or the future to be the subject of any of it, this same tendency sometimes driving a practitioner to chase after meditative attainment purely to glimpse a past life rather than to actually cut affliction at its root. Others speculate instead about the self's own future form, whether it will possess form or not, whether it will retain perception, lose it, or neither, a set of possibilities the Brahmajāla Sūtra catalogs at far greater length, and the same forbidden speculation extends equally to the eternalist view holding self and world to be permanent, the nihilist view holding nothing to remain after death, and the view holding the present enjoyment of the five desires, or the various form-realm absorptions, to already constitute nirvana itself, every one of these views resting on exactly the same underlying confusion whether applied to the self's own past or its own future. A further pattern of speculation asks whether the very life-principle is identical to the body itself, effectively claiming the self to be physical form, or entirely different from the body, effectively claiming the self to be purely mental, or somehow both physical and non-physical, pervading everywhere without division or lack, sixteen distinct non-Buddhist theories in total, every one of them rooted in the same basic ignorance of not yet seeing that no fixed self exists to be either the body, apart from the body, or anything else at all.

He treats this section's own brevity, compared to the sixteen-fold analysis just given for the self, as reflecting how directly the same underlying diagnosis now simply transfers over, a shorter treatment because the real work of dismantling the error was already done in the section just before it.

30. Section: Āryadeva's Own Calm Before an Assassin, and Why a Buddha's Domain Cannot Be Guessed

Speculation about the world completes this same triad, one who relies on the view of a real self likewise wondering whether the surrounding world of mountains, rivers, and earth is permanent or subject to annihilation, resting on exactly the same mistaken foundation already diagnosed in speculation about the self and about sentient beings, whether such speculation proceeds from an explicit view or without one. The master Āryadeva is said to have embodied this teaching directly when a hostile opponent came to assassinate him beneath a tree, meeting the attack by declaring simply that there is no self, no being, no one killed, and no one who kills, and no act of killing at all, since self, being, and world alike are, by their own nature, without any fixed reality to begin with. Speculation about beings' own karmic fruit is shown to be inconceivable through four further marks, the sheer number of possible destinations, thirty-six in the desire realm, eighteen in the form realm, and four in the formless realm, fifty-eight in all; the endless variety of what a being's own body and nature can actually be; the endless variety of karmic causes, pure and impure alike; and the endless variety of resulting fruit, desirable or undesirable, this sheer complexity alone making the whole matter impossible to fully resolve through ordinary reasoning. Speculation about the meditative states meditators themselves experience is inconceivable for three reasons already given, suchness's own profundity, the mind's own freedom once genuine mastery is reached, and the very attainment of the undefiled realm through non-discriminating wisdom. And speculation about the Buddhas' own domain adds two further reasons still, that domain being entirely unobstructed, and that domain being precisely what accomplishes whatever task each being actually needs, closing the entire gateway of the inconceivable in full.

31. Section: Sixteen Intentions Behind Everything the Buddha Ever Said

The intention to show reveals the four kinds of genuine truth in order to help a student learn wholesome conduct. The intention to inspire aversion condemns six classes of dark, unwholesome conduct rooted in craving, conduct that breeds contention, suffering, harm, calamity, consuming inner torment, and further craving still, precisely in order to help a being turn away from desire itself. The intention to exhort settles those who already trust the teaching into its actual training. The intention to praise and encourage steadies anyone whose heart grows discouraged partway through study, practice, or attainment. The intention to rejoice openly celebrates anyone practicing with genuine vigor toward nirvana and the thirty-seven factors of awakening. And the intention to bring in draws a being who remains neutral toward the teaching, neither hostile nor yet convinced, into genuine, joyful confidence in it at last.

32. Section: The Buddha Confesses He Cannot Cut Anyone's Doubt but Their Own

The intention to cut off doubt resolves genuine uncertainty through direct and reasoned teaching, illustrated by an exchange in which a non-Buddhist questioner suggested to the Buddha that, since he was all-powerful, beings need only rely on him entirely rather than undertake the hard work of practice themselves, and the Buddha replied plainly that he holds no power whatsoever to simply cut through another's doubt on their behalf, that each being must see the noble truth directly for themselves in order to actually cross the raging torrent of birth and death. The intention to bring to maturity ripens the faculties of one already inside the teaching, carrying them from the first fruit all the way to arhatship itself. The intention concerning concentration teaches the very methods and virtues of meditative absorption a practitioner needs in order to cultivate insight upon a genuinely settled foundation. The intention concerning liberation carries one whose faculties have already ripened onward to arhatship or to unsurpassed awakening, the complete and final release from saṃsāra's own turning. And the intention of special meaning conceals a deeper significance beneath its own ordinary words, exactly as the Compendium of the Great Vehicle declares, that only one who has already served buddhas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges river can actually understand the meaning of the Great Vehicle's own teaching, a statement Vasubandhu himself explains as pointing not to any merely verbal comprehension available to any ordinary person at all, but to genuine realization of the three natures themselves, achieved only through countless past lives spent in the actual presence of buddhas.

33. Section: All Dharmas Without Self-Nature — The Intentions Come to Their Close

The remaining intentions complete this sixteenfold catalogue. The intention to produce faultless joy names the genuine, blameless delight a practitioner feels upon actually realizing the teaching directly. The intention to produce respect names the natural reverence a listener comes to feel toward the very teacher whose words led them to understand the Dharma's own excellent meaning. The intention that the Dharma eye continually turns names the Buddha's own teaching as the very eye guiding beings across the three kinds of listener already discussed, the sightless, the one-eyed, and the genuinely two-eyed, toward nirvana's own far shore without interruption. The intention to cultivate abundant wholesome conduct names the Buddha's constant encouragement to do good and refrain from evil, urged throughout the entire body of his teaching. And the intention to subdue every clung-to characteristic, the sixteenth and final kind, is illustrated directly by the Buddha's own declaration that all phenomena are without any fixed nature of their own, neither arising nor ceasing, originally and always still, their own true nature already nirvana itself, a teaching belonging especially to the signless Great Vehicle tradition associated with the Middle Way schools, aimed at breaking every characteristic a being still clings to and thereby opening the way to realizing reality exactly as it is. With all sixteen intentions now explained, the entire sixfold framework of true meaning, realization, teaching, avoiding the two extremes, the inconceivable, and intention, first opened many pages earlier, reaches its own complete close.

He calls the completion of this sixfold gateway framework, spanning true meaning down through intention, one of this fascicle's own genuine landmarks, an entire systematic method for reading Buddhist scripture correctly now fully in place before the treatise turns to explore how these same six gateways interlock with one another in even finer detail.

34. Section: Four Foundations of Mindfulness Answer Four Ancient Errors

A further discussion now opens, explaining precisely why true meaning must be discerned through avoiding the two extremes, beginning with the extreme of exaggeration as it applies to the four noble truths themselves, unfolding into four mistaken beliefs: the extreme of exaggerating a self, the extreme of exaggerating permanence, the extreme of exaggerating purity, and the extreme of exaggerating pleasure, the very four inverted views ordinary beings habitually fall into. Against these four the Buddha established the four foundations of mindfulness directly: contemplating the body as impure counters the inverted belief that finds purity where none exists; contemplating feeling as suffering counters the inverted belief that finds pleasure where only suffering exists; contemplating mind as impermanent counters the inverted belief that finds permanence in what constantly arises and perishes; and contemplating phenomena as without self counters the inverted belief that finds a fixed self where none can be found, cultivating these four foundations together yielding the four kinds of settled wisdom that directly know each of the four truths in turn. The exaggeration of self is then examined at length: no genuine self can be found apart from the five aggregates, since arising, characteristic, and function are found only within the aggregates themselves, extensively refuted through several classical arguments, that if a self caused the aggregates to arise the way a seed causes a sprout, that self would have to be impermanent just as a seed is; that if a self shaped experience the way a potter shapes clay, that self would likewise have to be impermanent; and that if a self merely contained the aggregates the way space contains every object within it, a self of this kind could still perform none of the actual functions falsely attributed to it, exactly as a sickle alone cannot cut without a hand to wield it, and fire alone can just as easily arise from mere friction and dry conditions without needing any separate agent lighting it at all.

Because of this, every view of a self is simply the false exaggeration of self.

He highlights the sickle and fire similes refuting a self that merely serves as the instrument of action as this section's own most vivid argument, ordinary function requiring only the actual causes and conditions already present, with no separate, controlling self needed to explain anything that actually happens.

35. Section: Denying the Truths Denies Direct Sight Itself, and What Craving Actually Is

The opposite extreme, diminishment, is explained just as directly: denying the four noble truths outright, treating what has actually been established as though it possessed no reality at all, amounts to slandering all three genuine sources of knowledge at once, direct perception, since suffering and its origin can actually be observed, inference, since suffering's own cause can be reasoned to and its cessation reasoned toward through diligent cultivation of the path, and scriptural authority, and slanders the entire causal structure of defilement and purity alike, since the truths of suffering and origination form the defiled cause and effect while cessation and the path form the pure cause and effect. Only by avoiding both this diminishment and the opposite exaggeration already described can a practitioner actually gain genuine confidence in the four truths, come to a decisive understanding of them, and gradually realize complete and final purity, confirming exactly why avoiding the two extremes is necessary to properly discern true meaning at all. A far more detailed exposition of the four truths themselves then opens. The truth of suffering is explained first: birth and all its accompanying miseries already described at length, or, stated briefly, every single event belonging to the very defilement of rebirth itself. The truth of origination is explained next: both affliction's own defilement and karma's own defilement together, craving singled out by the Buddha himself as the truly supreme cause among every affliction, for two decisive reasons, that craving alone underlies both the wish for future existence and the wish to be free of it, and that craving alone has the universal power to give rise to still further craving once it has already arisen.

He highlights craving's own two-sided power, underlying both the wish for continued existence and the wish to escape it, as this passage's own precise explanation of why craving alone, among every affliction, earns the specific designation of origination's own supreme cause, wanting and not-wanting turning out to be two expressions of the very same underlying grasping.

36. Section: Eight Kinds of Stillness, and the Eight Supports That Keep Ordinary Life Turning

The truth of cessation is explained next as the permanent severing of every affliction, unfolding across eight aspects. Nirvana with residue possesses four kinds of stillness: the stillness of suffering, since no further rebirth into the three realms will ever occur again; the stillness of affliction, greed, hatred, and delusion entirely and permanently ended; the stillness of no longer harming any being, since without affliction only wholesome conduct remains possible; and the stillness of equanimity, the mind resting always in the most supreme, unattached equanimity, also called the six constant feelings. Nirvana without residue possesses four further kinds of stillness, reached once even the body itself is finally relinquished: the stillness beyond all number and expression, a state simply too complete to be measured or spoken; the stillness of every support, since the eight supports ordinary life actually depends upon, the support of mere designation, providing names like self and being for what is truly just the five aggregates; the support of embracing, family, servants, friends, property, and worldly enterprise; the support of sustenance, the four foods that keep the body itself alive; the support of continued flux, the twelve links of dependent origination that keep the wheel of rebirth turning; the support of obstruction, the very demons of the aggregates, affliction, the heavens, and death itself; the support of suffering, every kind of grief and misery found across the three realms; the support of pleasure, attachment to the very bliss of meditative absorption; and the support of what comes at the very end, the wish for continued existence still further ahead, are all finally left behind entirely; the stillness of suffering tied to any remaining support, since no bodily or mental suffering whatsoever remains once no support at all is left to suffer through; and the stillness of doubt arising from suffering tied to any remaining support, every uncertain question about whether even a fully liberated being might still somehow suffer now entirely resolved, this specific state, craving exhausted and freedom from desire complete, marking nirvana with residue, and utter, final extinguishing marking nirvana without residue.

The Buddha himself, addressing what is supreme among these, singled out the noble eightfold path in particular, precisely because it is this eightfold path above all else that actually embraces and brings about the realization of every śramaṇa fruit a practitioner could hope to attain.

38. Section: A Śrāvaka's Own Threefold Path, from Gathering Requisites to Final Purity

The path of the śrāvaka vehicle unfolds across its own three stages in turn. The path of requisites gathers the fourteen conditions already treated at length in the Grounds of Śrāvakas, one's own personal perfection and the perfection of one's surroundings, genuine aspiration toward the Dharma, precepts and restraint of the senses, moderation in eating, a disciplined practice of wakefulness maintained through the earlier, middle, and later parts of the night alike, clear knowing in every posture, genuine spiritual friendship, hearing and reflecting on the true Dharma, freedom from every obstacle, generosity free of any expectation of return, and the seventeen further qualities that together constitute a genuine renunciant's own adornment. The preparatory path, addressing what is supreme among its own stages, names the very factors of awakening cultivated across the four stages of heat, summit, patience, and supreme worldly dharma, the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest. And the path of purification names every one of the thirty-seven factors of awakening as actually realized across the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and the ultimate path, together with every further excellent quality this purification draws forth in turn, miraculous powers, the absorption free of contention, the absorption of aspirational wisdom, and unhindered understanding among them, this entire threefold structure completing the śrāvaka vehicle's own account of the path.

39. Section: The Bodhisattva's Own Path, and Why Suffering Is Always Named First

The bodhisattva vehicle's own path unfolds more simply: the preparatory path is embraced entirely within the six perfections, giving, precepts, patience, diligence, meditative absorption, and wisdom, while the path of purification is embraced entirely within the perfection of wisdom itself, the very insight into the twofold emptiness of self and phenomena. This is stated in terms of what is supreme for the bodhisattva path specifically, since a bodhisattva in fact cultivates every one of the thirty-seven factors of awakening a śrāvaka cultivates as well, and more besides, the two vehicles differing not in what the more restricted path omits but in how much further the wider path actually extends. The truths of suffering and origination are further shown to divide into three kinds each, according to whether they are bound to the desire realm, the form realm, or the formless realm, extending in truth to a genuinely boundless number once the innumerable worlds throughout the ten directions are taken into account, and the truths of cessation and the path divide in exactly the same threefold and boundless way, since they exist specifically to counter suffering and origination in each of these same three realms. The very sequence in which the four truths are taught is explained last: suffering is named first and origination second, together comprising defilement's own complete account, precisely because ordinary beings first wish to understand their own suffering before wishing to understand its cause; cessation is named third and the path fourth, together comprising purity's own complete account, precisely because beings who then wish for genuine happiness must next learn the path that actually leads to it, this consistent movement from result to cause in both pairs reflecting the Buddha's own skillful attention to how an ordinary listener's mind actually works.

He highlights the explanation for why suffering and cessation are each named before their own respective causes, matching the Buddha's teaching to how an ordinary mind naturally moves from noticing a result to asking after its cause, as a small but telling instance of this treatise's own recurring insistence that even the order in which truths are taught reflects real pedagogical care rather than arbitrary sequence.

40. Section: Thirteen Kinds of Person the Four Truths Establish

The four truths are shown to establish exactly thirteen kinds of person: ordinary beings bound to the desire, form, and formless realms respectively; trainees still on the path within each of these same three realms; adepts who have completed training within each of these same three realms; pratyekabuddhas, found specifically within the desire realm; bodhisattvas, found within both the desire realm and the form realm; and the Tathāgata, whose own domain is simply inconceivable. Mapped against the four truths themselves, every ordinary being belongs to suffering and origination alike, still caught within saṃsāra's own defiled cause and effect; every trainee, together with every bodhisattva still cultivating the path, belongs to the truth of the path; and every adept, together with the pratyekabuddha and the Tathāgata himself, belongs to the truth of cessation, having already reached its own genuine fruit. No pratyekabuddha or bodhisattva is found within the formless realm at all, since beings reborn there remain so deeply absorbed in concentration, with wisdom correspondingly shallow, that no being there can actually be taught or guided toward awakening, which is also why bodhisattvas, while willing to be reborn in the form realm where concentration and wisdom remain properly balanced, overwhelmingly choose the desire realm instead, precisely because its own beings, caught in a turbulent mixture of pleasure and pain, are the ones who most genuinely need guiding.

Chinese Source

謂欲界異生、色界異生、無色界異生、欲界有學、色界有學、無色界有學、欲界無學、色界無學、無色界無學、欲界獨覺、欲界菩薩、色界菩薩、不可思議如來。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ordinary beings of the desire, form, and formless realms; trainees of the desire, form, and formless realms; adepts of the desire, form, and formless realms; pratyekabuddhas of the desire realm; bodhisattvas of the desire realm; bodhisattvas of the form realm; and the inconceivable Tathāgata.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the explanation for why no pratyekabuddha or bodhisattva arises within the formless realm, beings there simply too deeply absorbed for any teaching to reach them at all, as a striking reminder that the most refined meditative state is not automatically the most spiritually useful one, depth of absorption without corresponding wisdom actually closing off the very possibility of further guidance. He highlights the bodhisattva's own preference for the desire realm specifically, despite its turbulence, as this section's own quiet restatement of the entire bodhisattva ideal, choosing to be reborn precisely where beings suffer most and need help most, rather than in whatever realm would be most comfortable for the bodhisattva alone.

41. Section: Twelve Kinds of Conduct Behind Every Kind of Person

A further topic examines these thirteen kinds of person through thirteen further aspects, conduct, obstruction, mind, affliction, karma, faculty, realm, faith and understanding, aspiration, latent tendency, rebirth, habituation, and grouping, beginning with conduct itself, unfolding into twelve kinds: wholesome conduct, unwholesome conduct, and neutral conduct; the conduct of actually renouncing the household life; the conduct of the very stream leading toward nirvana, drawing near a genuine friend, hearing the true Dharma, bringing correct attention to it, and practicing in accordance with it; the conduct of guarding oneself, upholding precepts and the restraint of the senses to prevent affliction and unwholesome conduct from ever arising; the conduct of seeking a favorable rebirth, cultivating the five precepts and ten wholesome deeds; the conduct of freedom from desire, cultivating the four worldly absorptions and four formless attainments; the conduct of liberation, cultivating the four truths and dependent origination on the transcendent path itself; the conduct of refining one's own faculties, combining undefiled cultivation with meditative absorption until faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom all grow fully mature; the conduct of drawing forth miraculous powers; and the conduct of benefiting others, teaching the Dharma to help other beings after one's own liberation has already been reached.

Chinese Source

復次，造作者，有十二種。謂善造作、不善造作、無記造作、出家造作、彼勝流造作、彼防護造作、生造作、離欲造作、解脫造作、練根造作、引發神通造作、發起他義造作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, conduct is of twelve kinds: wholesome conduct, unwholesome conduct, neutral conduct, the conduct of renunciation, the conduct of that supreme stream, the conduct of guarding oneself, the conduct of seeking rebirth, the conduct of freedom from desire, the conduct of liberation, the conduct of refining the faculties, the conduct of drawing forth miraculous powers, and the conduct of benefiting others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twelvefold classification of conduct as effectively tracing an entire spiritual biography from ordinary moral action through renunciation, guarded discipline, favorable rebirth, meditative freedom, transcendent liberation, matured faculties, miraculous ability, and finally teaching others, each kind of conduct corresponding naturally to wherever a given practitioner, among the thirteen kinds of person just listed, actually stands on their own path. He notes that the very last kind, benefiting others, quietly closes the entire sequence on the same note the earlier discussion of great compassion opened on, genuine liberation naturally overflowing into teaching, rather than ending in private attainment alone.

42. Section: Three Obstructions Only a Buddha Fully Escapes

The remaining kinds of obstruction close this second aspect: affliction obstruction, overcome by attaining liberation through wisdom; concentration obstruction, overcome only by attaining liberation in both respects together, wisdom and concentration alike; and the cognitive obstruction, overcome fully only by the Tathāgatas themselves, since even an arhat freed from every affliction may still carry this subtler obstruction to complete omniscience. Mind is then shown to be, briefly, of two kinds, obstructed mind and unobstructed mind, only a Buddha ever possessing the latter entirely. Affliction likewise divides into two kinds, its own active manifestation and its underlying latent tendency. And karma divides into two kinds, the very intention behind an act and the intention already carried into action, corresponding to the three stages of thought recognized elsewhere in this treatise, deliberation weighing what to do, decision settling the matter, and motivation actually setting body and speech into motion, the first two belonging to intention itself and the third belonging to intention already acted upon.

Chinese Source

十、煩惱障。謂由彼故，說慧解脫心得解脫。十一、定障。謂由彼故，說俱分解脫心得解脫。十二、所知障。謂由彼故，說諸如來心得解脫。復次，心者，略有二種。一、有障心，二、無障心。煩惱者，亦略有二種。謂纏及隨眠。業者，亦略有二種。謂思及思已。

Ru-Yi Translation

Affliction obstruction is overcome through liberation by wisdom. Concentration obstruction is overcome through liberation in both respects. The cognitive obstruction is overcome only by the Tathāgatas' own mind. Mind is, briefly, of two kinds: obstructed mind and unobstructed mind. Affliction is likewise of two kinds: manifestation and latent tendency. Karma is likewise of two kinds: intention and intention already acted upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the graduated escape from these three obstructions, wisdom alone overcoming affliction, wisdom joined with concentration overcoming both affliction and its subtler grip, and only buddhahood overcoming the cognitive obstruction entirely, as a precise map of exactly how far each level of attainment actually reaches, useful for correcting any assumption that arhatship alone already represents the very furthest point the path can go. He notes that karma's own twofold division, mere intention against intention already carried into action, reflects a consistent concern throughout this treatise with the mind's own inner activity as morally decisive in its own right, well before any outward deed is actually performed.

43. Section: Eight Circumstances Where the Dharma Simply Cannot Be Heard

Faculty, realm, faith and understanding, and aspiration are each likewise shown to divide into a purity-according kind and an impurity-according kind, faculty and aspiration counting as the very fruit of sustained cultivation, realm and faith and understanding counting instead as its cause, since genuine faith and understanding must first be present as cause before the corresponding aspiration can arise as fruit. Latent tendency divides into two kinds as well, the destructible, belonging to a being who genuinely possesses the spiritual potential for one of the three vehicles, and the indestructible, belonging to one who lacks any such potential at all, that being's own underlying afflictions never actually capable of being cut. And rebirth divides into the inopportune and the opportune, eight specific inopportune circumstances traditionally named in which the Dharma simply cannot be heard at all, birth in hell, where suffering never ceases; birth as a hungry ghost, endlessly seeking food; birth as an animal, unable to understand language; birth in a heaven of excessively long life, mind too subtle or too still to engage in reflection; birth on the northern continent, whose inhabitants enjoy such effortless, thousand-year lives that no urgency to seek the Dharma ever arises; birth blind, deaf, or mute, the sense faculties themselves too impaired to learn; birth with excessive worldly cleverness, trusting only one's own intellect and nothing beyond it; and birth either before a buddha has appeared in the world or after one has already passed away, opportune rebirth simply meaning birth into any circumstance where the Dharma can actually be encountered and practiced instead.

Chinese Source

根者，亦略有二種。謂順淨分及順不淨分。如根，如是界、信解、意樂，當知亦爾。此中差別者，根是果性，界是因性，信解是因性，意樂是果性。隨眠者，亦略有二種。謂可害及非可害。生者，亦略有二種。謂無暇生及有暇生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Faculty is, briefly, of two kinds: according with purity and according with impurity. Realm, faith and understanding, and aspiration should be known in just the same way. The difference among these is that faculty is the fruit, realm is the cause, faith and understanding is the cause, and aspiration is the fruit. Latent tendency is, briefly, of two kinds: destructible and indestructible. Rebirth is, briefly, of two kinds: inopportune rebirth and opportune rebirth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eight inopportune circumstances as a genuinely humbling list once read carefully, since it includes not only obvious misfortune, the lower realms and sensory impairment, but equally the northern continent's own effortless comfort and excessive worldly cleverness itself, quietly suggesting that both extreme suffering and extreme ease or intelligence can equally shut a being off from ever encountering the Dharma at all. He treats simply being born into a time and place where the teaching can actually be heard and practiced, named here only briefly as opportune rebirth, as itself a rare and genuinely fortunate condition this entire list is implicitly asking the reader not to take for granted.

44. Section: Wrongly Fixed, Rightly Fixed, and Undecided — Three Groupings Complete the Thirteen

The thirteenth and final aspect, grouping, completes this entire analysis, unfolding into three kinds each further divided in two. Wrongly fixed grouping names, first, one who inherently lacks the very spiritual potential for any of the three vehicles at all, incapable by nature of ever entering the certainty of realization, and second, one who, whatever potential may be present, has committed one of the five heinous crimes or their equivalent, thereby blocking realization in this present life regardless. Rightly fixed grouping names, first, one who genuinely possesses this potential yet still stands before the path of seeing, still gathering requisites or applying preparatory effort, and second, one who has already entered the certainty of realization directly, the path of seeing itself now actually reached. And indeterminate grouping names, first, one who possesses the potential for one of the three vehicles yet remains, as an ordinary being, genuinely undecided among them, and second, one whose eventual path is settled only by whichever teacher's own circumstances happen to be encountered first, becoming an arhat if a śrāvaka teacher is met, or pursuing unsurpassed awakening if a bodhisattva's own teaching is met instead. With grouping now explained, the entire thirteenfold analysis, spanning conduct, obstruction, mind, affliction, karma, faculty, realm, faith and understanding, aspiration, latent tendency, rebirth, habituation, and grouping alike, reaches its own complete close, each aspect illustrated further still through careful questions distinguishing exactly which combinations of wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral conduct, or of karmic and habitual obstruction, can and cannot actually occur together in a single being.

Chinese Source

邪性定聚復有二種。一、本性邪性定，二、方便邪性定。正性定亦有二種。一、本性正性定，二、方便正性定。不定亦有二種。一、本性不定，二、方便不定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wrongly fixed grouping is also of two kinds: inherently wrongly fixed, and circumstantially wrongly fixed. Rightly fixed grouping is also of two kinds: inherently rightly fixed, and circumstantially rightly fixed. Indeterminate grouping is also of two kinds: inherently indeterminate, and circumstantially indeterminate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between inherent and circumstantial fixing, in each of the three groupings alike, as this closing aspect's own reminder that a being's own spiritual trajectory depends on both an underlying potential and the actual choices and encounters of a given lifetime, neither factor alone ever telling the complete story. He notes that closing this entire thirteenfold analysis with grouping, the very question of which vehicle, if any, a being's own path leads toward, brings the whole discussion of the thirteen kinds of person back around to its own deepest concern, not merely classifying beings but understanding what actually determines where each one is ultimately headed.

45. Section: Three Natures Open the Deepest Truth of All

A further and considerably deeper topic now opens, non-established truth itself, defined as the very perfected nature of all phenomena, reality exactly as it actually is, the sole object of noble wisdom's own direct perception, known only to the wisdom of the noble ones and never accessible through ordinary conceptual thought at all. Discerning this deepest truth likewise requires avoiding two extremes, and the extreme of exaggeration here is explained through the three natures every phenomenon actually possesses: the imagined nature, every characteristic and property established purely through convention and name, a merely assumed reality that ordinary beings, encountering such names again and again, come to treat as though genuinely and inherently real, precisely the trap sprung whenever a mere label, being called foolish, for instance, is mistaken for an actual, fixed property of the person so named; the dependent nature, the very nature of phenomena that arise entirely through other conditions, through causal condition, immediately antecedent condition, objective condition, and dominant condition together, possessing no independent nature of their own at all, and therefore said to lack self-nature in the sense of arising; and the perfected nature, reality's own true and complete nature, the very ground the other two natures both ultimately depend upon and fail to see clearly. The imagined nature is what actually constitutes the extreme of exaggeration, ordinary beings mistaking what is merely named and assumed for something genuinely and independently existent in its own right.

Chinese Source

云何非安立真實？謂諸法真如圓成實自性，聖智所行、聖智境界、聖智所緣。云何增益邊？謂諸法自性略有三種。一、遍計所執自性，二、依他起自性，三、圓成實自性。遍計所執自性者，謂諸所有名言安立諸法自性，依假名言，數數周遍計度諸法而建立故。依他起自性者，謂眾緣生他力所起諸法自性，非自然有，故說無性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is non-established truth? The very suchness and perfected nature of all phenomena, the domain, object, and support of noble wisdom alone. What is the extreme of exaggeration? The self-nature of phenomena is, briefly, of three kinds: the imagined nature, the dependent nature, and the perfected nature. The imagined nature is whatever self-nature of phenomena is established through name and convention, repeatedly and pervasively conceived and constructed on the basis of assumed names. The dependent nature is the self-nature of phenomena arising through many conditions and through the power of other things, not existing of its own accord, and so said to be without self-nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three-natures framework, imagined, dependent, and perfected, as this treatise's own most philosophically foundational teaching within the entire Yogâcāra tradition, and highlights the everyday example of being called foolish and reacting as though a fixed, real property had genuinely been identified, as a perfectly ordinary instance of exactly the imagined nature this passage warns against, the mind treating a mere label as though it disclosed something inherently and independently true. He notes that the dependent nature's own careful analysis into four kinds of condition, causal, immediately antecedent, objective, and dominant, gives this teaching real analytical precision rather than leaving emptiness as a vague slogan, every phenomenon's own lack of independent existence traceable to specific, nameable conditions it actually depends upon.

46. Section: A Dream, a Dreamer, and Waking Up to Find Nothing There

The perfected nature completes this threefold analysis, reality's own true nature exactly as described earlier, the domain, object, and support of noble wisdom alone, the very ground that liberates a practitioner from the bondage of characteristics and the deeper bondage of afflictive residue alike, and draws forth every excellent quality the path can offer. The Compendium of the Great Vehicle illustrates all three natures together through the image of a dream: the very mind that dreams, arising entirely through its own causes and conditions, corresponds to the dependent nature; the vivid people, vehicles, and houses that appear within the dream, mistaken by the dreamer as genuinely real for as long as the dream continues, correspond to the imagined nature; and the moment of waking, discovering that none of it was ever actually there at all, corresponds to the perfected nature, reality's own fundamental emptiness now directly seen. The imagined nature is shown to constitute the very extreme of exaggeration through three further arguments. First, if a name itself conferred genuine, independent self-nature on its object, then a single thing bearing several different names would absurdly have to possess several distinct bodies at once, one for each name it happened to carry. Second, if name and meaning were genuinely identical, cognition of an object would have to arise the instant that object was encountered regardless of whether its name was yet known, yet in fact no such recognition occurs until the name has actually been learned, proving name and object are not the same thing at all. And third, if name gave rise to meaning rather than meaning existing first and receiving a name afterward, a newborn infant, who sees color and form clearly enough yet has not yet learned any word for them, should be entirely unable to perceive them at all, when in fact the infant plainly does, proving that the object itself always precedes whatever name later comes to label it.

Chinese Source

圓成實自性者，謂如前說。謂諸法真如，聖智所行、聖智境界、聖智所緣，乃至能令證得清淨，能令解脫一切相縛及麤重縛，亦令引發一切功德。此自性中，彼自性有不應理故。又若略說，由三因緣不應道理。謂種種非一品類名言所安立故；若離名言，彼覺不生故；又彼名言依義轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The perfected nature is as already explained: the suchness of all phenomena, the domain, object, and support of noble wisdom, able to bring about the realization of purity, liberation from every bond of characteristics and afflictive residue, and to draw forth every excellent quality. Within this nature, the assumption of that other, imagined nature does not accord with reason. Briefly stated, this is so for three reasons: because such things are established through varied and non-uniform names; because, apart from a name, no cognition of the thing would arise; and because names themselves follow upon meaning already present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the dream simile as this entire three-natures teaching's own most accessible illustration, since everyone has experienced exactly this progression directly, a vivid dream world taken for genuinely real while it lasts, and then simply gone, without residue, the instant one wakes, waking itself requiring no argument at all to recognize what the dream never actually was. He highlights the infant example as this passage's own most charmingly concrete proof among the three arguments, a newborn child's own plain ability to see color and form well before ever learning a single word for either, offered as simple, observable evidence that reality itself always comes first, language arriving only afterward to describe what was already there.

47. Section: Beyond Mere Words — What the Special Meaning Actually Points To

The extreme of diminishment for this deepest truth is explained in turn: denying the very reality of the dependent and perfected natures alike, slandering them as though they too were entirely without existence, exactly as denying the four truths themselves was already shown to be a genuine error, since without any real dependent nature giving rise to experience, and without any real perfected nature to be finally realized, the entire path of practice would become simply pointless. Avoiding both this diminishment and the opposite exaggeration together allows the true-meaning gateway to be properly discerned through the gateway of avoiding the two extremes exactly as intended. The special meaning behind the sixteenth intention discussed much earlier is then explained in full: understanding what a scripture truly means requires far more than merely parsing its own words, sentences, and phrases on the surface, since genuine understanding must set aside attachment to language itself and penetrate through to the very ultimate truth those words can only ever gesture toward, the dependent and perfected natures lying beyond speech altogether, this alone constituting the special meaning a sincere student must actually seek.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: the intention of special meaning is not the meaning corresponding merely to the words, phrases, and sentences of the teaching, but is understood only by setting aside such words and penetrating to the further ultimate meaning they point toward.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the parallel between denying the four truths and denying the dependent and perfected natures as this treatise's own consistent warning against a nihilism that mistakes genuine emptiness teaching for outright denial of everything whatsoever, when in fact the entire point of these teachings is to correct a mistaken way of grasping reality, never to deny that a real path and a real fruit actually exist to be walked and realized. He treats the special meaning's own final definition, penetrating past mere words to the ultimate truth they only gesture toward, as this entire fascicle's own reminder that scriptural study, however necessary as a starting point, was never meant to end in scholarship alone.

48. Section: How the Six Gateways Unlock One Another in Sequence

Just as true meaning is discerned through avoiding the two extremes, so too, correspondingly, are the gateways of realization and teaching discerned through the two gateways that follow them, realization understood through the inconceivable, and teaching understood through intention, this same pattern of the first three gateways depending on the latter three now shown to hold consistently across every pairing. A natural sequence runs through this entire framework: once true meaning has genuinely been discerned through avoiding the two extremes, a practitioner becomes able to actually realize the very meaning that discernment discloses; and once this realization is achieved, the remaining gateway of realization becomes discerned through the inconceivable in turn, while every teaching the Buddhas have ever given becomes discerned through their own underlying intention as well, each gateway thus unlocking the next in careful order rather than standing as six merely parallel, unrelated categories. Genuinely discerning the gateway of true meaning is said to open the way into five distinct kinds of entering the certainty of realization: entering while not yet free of desire, corresponding to the very first fruit of stream-entry itself; entering while doubly free of desire, for one who had already suppressed six of the nine grades of the desire realm's own afflictions through worldly cultivation beforehand, and so leaps directly to the second fruit of once-returning upon reaching the path of seeing; entering while already free of desire, for one who had already suppressed all nine such grades beforehand, and so leaps directly to the third fruit of non-returning; entering as a pratyekabuddha; and entering as a bodhisattva, realizing the very suchness of both self and phenomena's own emptiness upon first reaching the ground of great joy. With this, the entire discussion embracing the noble teaching's own meaning, unfolding across the ten topics and the six gateways of reasoning alike, reaches its own complete and final close.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five kinds of entering realization as a precise map of how much worldly preparation a practitioner has already completed before ever reaching the path of seeing itself, a being who has already suppressed most or all of the desire realm's own affliction through ordinary meditative discipline beforehand able to leap straight past the earlier fruits the moment genuine insight finally arrives, while one without such preparation must still pass through stream-entry first. He marks this section's own closing declaration, the entire discussion of the noble teaching's meaning now complete across ten topics and six gateways alike, as one of this fascicle's own genuine landmarks, an enormous systematic undertaking, opened many pages earlier, now fully resolved before the treatise turns to yet another topic.

49. Section: Why Ultimate Truth Must Be Taught Alongside Conventional Truth

A new topic opens, distinguishing the very characteristics of the two truths, conventional and ultimate, together with the coarse residue of affliction each is meant to counter. A question is posed directly: if established, conventional truth already counts as genuine truth in its own right, for what reason must non-established, ultimate truth be additionally revealed at all? The answer is direct: without also revealing this deeper, non-established truth, two distinct kinds of liberation would remain impossible to achieve, liberation from the very bondage of characteristics, and liberation from the coarse residue of affliction that clings on even after its own outward manifestation has been suppressed, established truth alone never reaching deep enough to actually sever either one.

Chinese Source

問：若安立諦建立為諦，何因緣故，更復顯示非安立諦？答：若離非安立諦，二種解脫不應道理。謂於相縛及麤重縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if established truth is already established as truth, for what reason is non-established truth additionally revealed? Answer: without non-established truth, two kinds of liberation would not accord with reason, namely liberation from the bondage of characteristics and from the bondage of coarse afflictive residue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this question and answer as this treatise's own justification for why conventional truth, however genuinely useful for ordinary understanding and moral reasoning, could never by itself constitute the entire Buddhist path, since real liberation requires reaching past every characteristic the mind still clings to and past even the subtle residue affliction leaves behind once its own active manifestation has already been calmed. He notes that this distinction between merely suppressing an affliction's outward activity and actually uprooting its own coarse residue echoes a concern already raised much earlier in this fascicle's own discussion of manas and its four constant afflictions, the same insistence that surface calm is not the same achievement as genuine, complete severing.

50. Section: Why Concentration Alone, However Excellent, Cannot Free the Mind

The reasoning behind this necessity unfolds across three further exchanges. First, whoever's mind still operates entirely within established, conventional truth inevitably operates only among characteristics, and so remains unable to free itself from the very bondage of characteristics, and, failing that, remains equally unable to free itself from the bondage of coarse afflictive residue either, while whoever's mind actually operates within non-established truth no longer dwells among characteristics at all, and so genuinely achieves both liberations together, confirming why non-established truth must be taught. Second, if only this deepest truth actually liberates, why teach established truth at all, and the answer is that established truth alone allows the requisite path and the preparatory path to actually become pure in the first place, since no practitioner can leap directly into wordless realization without the conventional teaching that first makes any practice possible at all. And third, an objection is raised and answered: could a mind still operating among characteristics, resting in excellent concentration within the fourth absorption and contemplating the four truths through their own sixteen aspects, not also achieve both liberations this way, and the answer is no, for two decisive reasons, first, because such a practitioner, however excellent their concentration, plainly remains impure and unliberated in actual fact, proving that conceptual mind alone cannot accomplish this liberation regardless of how refined it becomes, and second, because if it could, no genuine difference would remain between the mundane path and the transcendent path at all, when a real difference between them plainly still exists, confirming that both established and non-established truth must be taught together, each serving a purpose the other cannot fulfill alone.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever operates within non-established truth does not operate among characteristics, and so achieves liberation from the bondage of characteristics, and consequently from the bondage of coarse residue as well. If one possessed excellent concentration within the fourth absorption, contemplating the truths, such a practitioner ought to achieve complete purity from both bonds, yet remains impure, which does not accord with reason. Moreover, no difference between the mundane and transcendent paths could then be established, though such a difference genuinely exists, which likewise does not accord with reason.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the empirical test embedded in the third exchange, that a practitioner resting in excellent fourth-absorption concentration while contemplating the four truths conceptually still remains, in plain fact, unliberated, as this passage's own refreshingly direct method of settling an otherwise abstract philosophical question, simply pointing to what is actually observed rather than arguing purely from definition. He highlights the closing argument from the real difference between mundane and transcendent paths as an equally practical proof, since if conceptual contemplation alone sufficed for complete liberation, this entire distinction the whole treatise has carefully maintained across dozens of prior sections would collapse into nothing, confirming both kinds of truth remain genuinely necessary.

51. Section: Five Faces of Coarse Residue, and a New Territory Opens

Coarse residue itself is then explained directly: briefly stated, an incapacity and inflexibility of body and mind alike, unfolding across five further aspects. The mark of heaviness, dullness and drowsiness weighing down body and mind together. The mark of rigidity, affliction burning fiercely and intensely. The mark of obstruction, the mind still dwelling within a genuinely defiled state. The mark of timidity, a mind that cannot rouse or elevate itself, convinced in advance that the noble path lies simply beyond its own reach. And the mark of lacking mastery, unable to act as one actually wishes even when a wholesome deed is genuinely intended, afflictive residue itself quietly blocking the very follow-through, the first two of these five according with defilement's own increase and the latter three directly opposing purity's own arising, together completing this entire discussion embracing the noble teaching's own meaning. A wholly new topic then opens, turning to what Buddhism itself should know, unfolding first into three kinds of abiding, the first being daily abiding itself, nourished through five distinct causes: nourishment through food, the four foods sustaining the very faculties and elements of the body; nourishment through sleep, restoring the body and preventing harm such as blindness that deprivation would otherwise bring; nourishment through avoiding imbalance, neither overeating nor undereating, neither sleeping too much nor too little, avoiding intoxicants, and maintaining emotional equanimity rather than lingering in grief or rage; nourishment through pure conduct, the noble eightfold path together with genuine freedom from improper sexual conduct; and nourishment through meditative attainment itself, these five gathered together, in brief, into two: nourishment that sustains, and nourishment that prevents harm.

The above concludes the decisive discernment embracing the noble teaching's own meaning. These nourishments are, briefly, of two kinds: sustaining nourishment, and nourishment that prevents harm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the explicit closing formula, marking the end of everything embracing the noble teaching's own meaning, as confirmation of just how vast this single topic actually was, unfolding across ten topics, six gateways of reasoning, their own intricate interlocking, and finally the three natures and two truths, before finally giving way to an entirely new subject. He treats the five nourishments now opening this new territory, what Buddhism itself should know, as a deliberately grounding return to the concrete and physical after so much abstract philosophy, food, sleep, balance, conduct, and meditation together simply describing what keeps an ordinary body and mind healthy enough to practice at all.

52. Section: Four Ways Food Itself Sustains a Living Being

Sustaining nourishment itself unfolds across four kinds: nourishment through transformation, ordinary edible food, entering the body and breaking down there to actually nourish the faculties; nourishment through joy, the pleasant contact arising from a defiled union of faculty, object, and consciousness, its own agreeable sensation directly enriching mind and its mental factors; nourishment through hope, the sixth consciousness's own wish for a desired object, sustaining a being's faculties through hope alone exactly as a thirsty traveler, merely arriving within sight of water, is sustained a while longer simply by the hope of drinking it; and nourishment through grasping, the ālaya-consciousness's own continuous holding of the body itself, without which the body would simply collapse and die at once, each of these four together completing the very four foods already named earlier in this fascicle. A further topic then opens, lifelong abiding, unfolding across five activities that continue throughout an entire life: bodily activity, the in-breath and out-breath together with the four postures of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down; verbal activity, the investigation and reflection that give rise to speech; mental activity, the ongoing activity of feeling, perception, and the rest; karmic activity, whatever pure or impure deeds of body, speech, and mind are actually committed, ripening into a desirable or undesirable fruit to come; and life activity, the very life faculty itself, sustained for however long a given lifespan lasts, an arhat's own cultivation of a small further store of merit even said to be capable of extending this same lifespan somewhat further still.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Sustaining nourishment is, briefly, of four kinds: nourishment through transformation, through joy, through hope, and through grasping and holding. Furthermore, there are five activities: bodily activity, verbal activity, mental activity, karmic activity, and life activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the image of a thirsty traveler sustained merely by the sight of water and the hope of drinking it as a genuinely vivid illustration of nourishment through hope, a mental food every reader has likely experienced directly, hope itself keeping the body going a while longer even before any actual water reaches the lips. He highlights the arhat's own capacity to extend a lifespan through cultivated merit as a small but striking detail, life activity itself shown to remain, even for a fully liberated being, something that can still be consciously shaped rather than simply endured as a fixed and unchangeable span.

53. Section: Five Kinds of Heedfulness for the Householder, Ten for the Renunciant

A further topic opens, the continuing abiding of wholesome dharma toward an agreeable future rebirth, unfolding across five kinds of heedfulness that keep wholesome conduct steadily increasing. For a householder, this heedfulness itself unfolds into five further kinds already explained elsewhere: heedfulness in seeking wealth, free of greed, hatred, and delusion in the pursuit itself; heedfulness in guarding wealth once obtained, never squandering it through carelessness; heedfulness in guarding one's own health and vigor; heedfulness in guarding one's own reputation; and heedfulness in the actual practice of the Dharma or any other undertaking, these five together drawing forth five further powers, the power of good precepts, of good friendship, of freedom from stinginess, of trustworthiness, and of the Dharma itself, which in turn yield five kinds of genuine good fortune, being loved by all, wealth and true self-mastery, prevailing over hostility, benefiting all who depend on oneself, and finally proceeding toward a genuinely favorable rebirth. For a renunciant, heedfulness instead unfolds into ten critical moments never to be neglected: when facing the temptation to transgress precepts; when cultivating meditative absorption, casting off the five hindrances the instant they threaten to arise; when death itself actually approaches, holding fast to mindfulness of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha so as to die well and proceed toward a favorable rebirth; when going out to beg for alms, watching carefully how the six senses respond to whatever is encountered; when undertaking any ordinary task at all; when receiving honor or material support, never growing attached or arrogant because of it; when seeking further learning, setting aside merely decorative rhetoric in favor of learning genuinely aimed at nirvana itself; when reflecting on the true Dharma, ensuring this reflection remains genuinely pure; when actually practicing solitude, avoiding whatever obstacles arise from improperly mixing with others; and when contemplating the ultimate truth itself, correctly discerning what genuinely exists from what does not, free of any trace of misplaced confidence.

Chinese Source

Heedfulness according with the householder's life is, further, of five kinds, already explained elsewhere. Heedfulness according with the renunciant's life is, further, of ten kinds, to be extensively explained in the decisive discernment corresponding to monastic discipline in the Grounds of Śrāvakas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the householder's own fivefold heedfulness, spanning wealth, health, reputation, and practice together, as a genuinely holistic account of lay virtue, refusing to separate spiritual discipline from the ordinary responsibilities of earning, protecting, and using one's own resources well. He highlights the renunciant's own ten critical moments, especially the vivid instruction to die with mindfulness of the Three Jewels held firmly in mind, as this passage's own reminder that heedfulness is not a single sustained state but something that must be actively renewed again and again at each of life's own genuinely decisive junctures.

54. Section: What Has Form Unfolds into Five Kinds, Closing the Two Worldly Dharmas

What has form itself unfolds into five kinds, completing the account of the two worldly dharmas begun with name: the primary elements themselves, earth, water, fire, and wind; what is derived from the primary elements, such as the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body faculties; what possesses both visible appearance and physical resistance, meaning whatever can actually be seen by the fleshly eye and also occupies its own physical space, namely visible objects themselves; what is invisible yet still possesses physical resistance, including the five faculties along with sound and the other objects that can be sensed yet not seen; and what is both invisible and free of physical resistance, the restrained and unrestrained forms and the like belonging to the mental object's own sphere, cognized by the sixth consciousness alone. A wholly new topic then opens, the two roots of affliction, the first of which is ignorance itself, unfolding into five kinds: ignorance regarding meaning, an all-pervading confusion toward every object of knowledge whatsoever; ignorance regarding view, a confusion, rooted in wrong understanding, toward the absence of self across past, present, and future and toward both defiled and pure causation; ignorance of heedlessness, a confusion regarding the workings of karmic cause and result that leads one to long merely for rebirth in the heavens rather than genuine practice; ignorance regarding the truth itself, a failure to rightly penetrate the Three Jewels and the Four Noble Truths; and ignorance of overweening pride, the mistaken presumption that having attained mere meditative calm is already equivalent to attaining the fruits of a genuine renunciant.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Form has five kinds: the primary elements, and what is derived from the primary elements, distinguished further into what has visible appearance and resistance, what is invisible yet has resistance, and what is invisible and free of resistance. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this fivefold classification of ignorance is itself a condensation of the nineteen kinds of ignorance already given in the Basic Section's Ground of Reflection and Investigation, each of the five gathering several of the nineteen beneath it, with the ignorance regarding meaning alone said to pervade and include all the rest, since any confusion at all toward any knowable object whatsoever already counts as this most basic form of ignorance. He treats the fifth kind, the overweening pride that mistakes mere calm-abiding for actual liberation, as a genuinely important warning for any serious meditator, since deep concentration alone, however blissful, is not yet the fruit of the path.

55. Section: Five Kinds of Craving for Existence, Read Two Different Ways

The second root of affliction, craving, likewise unfolds into five kinds: craving rooted in the nature of things itself, arising naturally as an age draws toward its own close and beings spontaneously seek rebirth in a higher heaven; craving rooted in a vow, arising at other times through a deliberately made aspiration toward a higher realm; craving rooted in delusion, the mistaken view, held by non-Buddhist practitioners, that rebirth in the higher realms of form or formlessness is itself already nirvana; craving rooted in weariness, the ordinary being's own wish to escape the lower realm's coarseness in favor of the higher realm's own subtlety and purity; and craving rooted in careful reflection, belonging to a noble one of the higher-flowing type within the form realm who, through correct reflection, systematically abandons desire for each lower level in succession until nirvana itself is finally reached. This same fivefold craving admits of a second reading as well, favored by Master Kuiji: the first, read instead as a spontaneous grasping of the seventh consciousness toward the ālaya-consciousness as a self, present in every ordinary being who has not yet severed affliction; the third, read instead as the delusion-born craving of a non-Buddhist who mistakes heavenly rebirth itself for liberation, a craving that in fact only perpetuates the cycle of birth and death; and the fourth, read instead as a weariness that abandons the precepts to return to the five desires, casting off one's own status as a renunciant and disciple altogether.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, there are five kinds of craving for existence: craving rooted in the nature of things, craving rooted in a vow, craving rooted in delusion, craving rooted in weariness, and craving rooted in careful reflection.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi presents three separate interpretive traditions here side by side — Han Qingjing's own reading of these five cravings as five kinds of seeking already explained earlier in this same fascicle, and two further readings attributed to Master Kuiji — rather than insisting on a single correct account, treating this openness itself as an invitation for the practitioner to weigh each reading and adopt whichever illuminates their own practice most clearly. He singles out the fifth craving, careful reflection, as the crucial exception among the five, the only craving that itself leads systematically toward liberation rather than away from it, since even a noble one still possessing some residual craving can use correct reflection to progressively abandon it.

56. Section: Five Marks Establish Shamelessness, Their Reversal Establishes a Genuine Conscience

A further topic opens, the two kinds of practice, one contrary to and one according with genuine cultivation, the first being shamelessness and lack of conscience, established through five marks: no sense of shame when defiled states actually arise and manifest; no sense of shame when wholesome states fail to arise and manifest at all; no sense of shame in casting off one's own precepts and returning to lay life; no sense of shame in drawing close to bad friends; and no sense of shame in having accomplished nothing at all despite however long one has practiced. The five marks that reverse each of these in turn establish, by direct contrast, a genuine sense of shame and conscience: feeling ashamed when defilement arises, feeling ashamed at any failure to cultivate the wholesome, actually upholding one's precepts rather than treating their loss with indifference, actively drawing close to good friends instead of bad ones, and feeling a genuine and productive shame at one's own continued lack of accomplishment on the path, a shame that spurs on rather than discourages further diligent effort.

One should know that the five excellent marks reversing these establish a genuine shame and conscience.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi observes that there are, in effect, two kinds of good disciple: one who having received the precepts never transgresses them at all, and a second who, upon transgressing, is able to repent quickly and genuinely out of a real sense of shame, and that this second kind of disciple, however imperfect, remains a genuinely good disciple precisely because shame itself, rather than being a burden, is what keeps the path of practice alive and moving forward.

57. Section: Five Marks of a Bad Teacher, Five Marks of a Bad Friend

A further topic opens, establishing the distinction between good and bad speakers of the Dharma and between good and bad friends, first taking up the bad ones in turn. A bad speaker of the Dharma is established through five marks: an absence of right conduct altogether, lacking even the basics of drawing close to good teachers, hearing the true Dharma, reflecting properly, and practicing accordingly; an actual presence of wrong conduct going beyond mere absence; a lack of genuine conviction, without any firm or powerful understanding of the Dharma at all; an absence of any shame regarding one's own wrongdoing; and a lack of discipline, having taken no precepts to uphold in the first place. A bad friend is likewise established through five marks: an absence of shame, spanning the very same five shameless conditions already given above; the presence of wrong views, including belief in a self, in permanence, in annihilation, or in the absence of cause and effect; laziness, an unwillingness to diligently abandon evil and cultivate good; wrongful conduct, actual perverse and mistaken deeds of body and speech; and a base and cowardly disposition, an unwillingness to practice at all born from the mistaken conviction that one could never succeed regardless of effort. A closing formula then notes that reversing each of these same marks establishes, by direct contrast, both a good speaker of the Dharma and a good friend.

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the fifth mark of a bad friend, a base and cowardly disposition that gives up before even trying out of a mistaken conviction that success is impossible, as a subtler danger than open laziness or wrong view, since it can masquerade as humility while in fact quietly foreclosing any real progress on the path. He treats the treatise's own closing formula, reversing all five marks at once to establish the good counterpart, as a deliberately economical teaching device, entrusting the reader to actively perform this reversal rather than spelling out each positive quality all over again.

58. Section: Five Kinds of Calm-Abiding, Five Kinds of Insight

A brief closing formula establishes the good speaker of the Dharma and the good friend by direct reversal of the five marks already given for their bad counterparts, a good speaker of the Dharma possessing right conduct, freedom from wrong conduct, genuine conviction, a sense of shame, and discipline, and a good friend possessing a sense of shame, right view, diligence, right conduct, and a courageous rather than cowardly disposition. A new topic then opens, the establishment of calm-abiding and insight together. Calm-abiding itself unfolds into five kinds: the mundane calm-abiding belonging to the approach-concentration not yet reaching full absorption; the mundane calm-abiding belonging to the four root form-realm absorptions; the mundane calm-abiding belonging to the four root formless-realm absorptions; the transcendent calm-abiding belonging to the śrāvaka's and pratyekabuddha's own mental engagement, oriented chiefly around the Four Noble Truths for the śrāvaka and the twelve links of dependent origination for the pratyekabuddha; and the transcendent calm-abiding belonging to the bodhisattva's own mental engagement, oriented around the four investigations and four kinds of genuine knowledge already explained in the Bodhisattva Ground. Insight likewise unfolds into five kinds: insight into the exhaustive nature of things, a comprehensive discernment of each dharma's own nature, as when one thoroughly examines the five aggregates one by one and finds nothing further to be found beyond them; insight into the nature of things as they truly are, discerning the shared characteristics of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self common to all dharmas without exception; insight bearing a mental image, engaging its object with an accompanying conceptual discrimination; insight through reflective inquiry, a broad investigative searching directed toward its object; and insight through detailed observation, a still more thorough and searching examination of the true nature of dharmas, these five together distinguished simply by their differing degree of contemplative depth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fivefold classification of calm-abiding as a useful map of the entire Buddhist path's own contemplative range, running from ordinary approach-concentration all the way up through the transcendent calm-abiding proper to a bodhisattva, a single technical term thus shown to name an entire spectrum of attainment rather than one fixed state. He highlights the distinction between insight into the exhaustive nature of things and insight into things as they truly are as especially important for meditators to keep straight, since the former is a comprehensive survey of a dharma's own particulars, such as each of the five aggregates in turn, while the latter cuts directly to the universal marks — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self — shared by every dharma without exception.

59. Section: Three Outflows — Desire, Existence, and Ignorance

A further topic opens, the three outflows, the first being the outflow of desire, established, briefly, through five marks: producing latent tendencies through the desire realm's own indeterminate objects, since craving the five desirable objects of the desire realm itself perfumes afflictive seeds; according with unwholesome conduct, since this same afflictive outflow inclines one to act on afflictive habits; opposing the wholesome, since it causes one's own deeds to run contrary to wholesome dharma; indulging in desires, since it produces deep attachment to form, sound, smell, taste, and touch and to greed, hatred, delusion, and sensual craving generally; and giving rise to the fruit of the suffering of change and the suffering of suffering itself, since it corrupts the mind and thereby produces painful results. The second outflow, the outflow of existence, is likewise established through five marks: producing the afflictions leading to the inferior desire realm, rooted in craving for the desire realm's own existence; producing the afflictions leading to the middling form realm, rooted in craving for meditative bliss and thus for a form-realm existence; producing the afflictions leading to the sublime formless realm, rooted in the mistaken non-Buddhist pursuit of the four formless heavens as liberation itself; producing the afflictions of craving non-existence, the mistaken wish that no future rebirth-body arise at all, a wish which, absent right view, can collapse into either nihilism or overweening pride; and producing the afflictions of craving further existence, the ordinary craving for a future rebirth-body that itself gives rise to every manner of further affliction.

Master Cháng Bǎi points out that the outflow of existence's fourth mark, craving non-existence, is a genuinely subtle trap, since a being weary of endless rebirth may reach for annihilation itself as an escape, and without the Buddha's own right view to distinguish this from true nirvana, that very craving can curdle into either a nihilistic wrong view or a prideful presumption of attainment not actually reached. He notes Master Kuiji's own alternate gloss, reading the inferior and sublime realms here as referring instead to unfortunate and fortunate destinies respectively, as a reminder that even within a single commentarial tradition, more than one legitimate reading of a compact technical phrase can coexist.

60. Section: The Outflow of Ignorance and the Sixty-Two Wrong Views

The third outflow, the outflow of ignorance arising from a mistaken wish for liberation, is established, briefly, through five further marks corresponding to five classes of wrong view, drawn from the sixty-two views of non-Buddhist schools already catalogued in the Compendium of Reflection and Investigation. Proponents of a view holding a self to possess perception after death give rise to ignorance through this very view, itself unfolding into sixteen positions generated by crossing four ways of conceiving the self — as possessing form, as formless, as both, as neither — together with four further qualifiers of boundedness, unboundedness, both, or neither, and by additional distinctions of the self having one perception, various perceptions, limited perception, boundless perception, or being purely blissful, purely painful, both, or neither. Proponents of a view holding the self to lack perception after death give rise to ignorance through eight parallel positions built from the same form/formless/both/neither and bounded/unbounded/both/neither framework, as do proponents of a view holding the self to be neither perceiving nor non-perceiving after death, through another eight parallel positions. Proponents of annihilationism give rise to ignorance through seven positions, holding that whichever kind of rebirth-body one presently possesses, whether in the desire realm, the form realm, or any of the four formless-realm heavens, is simply and utterly annihilated at death without remainder. Proponents of a view of nirvana already present in this very life give rise to ignorance through five positions, mistaking, in ascending order, present enjoyment of the five sense pleasures, or the attainment of the first, second, third, or fourth meditative absorption itself, for nirvana already achieved.

He highlights the final class, mistaking present enjoyment or meditative bliss for nirvana itself, as the most immediately relevant for a practicing meditator, since it names a trap any diligent practitioner could genuinely fall into, prematurely mistaking the calm and joy of deep absorption for liberation actually attained.

61. Section: Closing the Science of Inner Knowledge, Opening the Science of Logic

The five positions mistaking a present state for nirvana are completed by adding, to the four meditative absorptions already given, the enjoyment of the five desirable sense objects itself, all five of these views alike counting as ignorance, since each still rests on a mistaken liberation-view rooted in belief in a self that does not in fact exist. Master Kuiji's own reading gathers these same wrong views instead under forty-four positions concerning a self projected into the future, noting that views concerning a self projected into the past or spanning past and present belong here as well, so that however a self is imagined across any of the three times, such imagining itself remains ignorance at its very root, and every one of the many outflows just catalogued in this section ultimately traces back to this same single mistaken grasping at a self. This closes the entire topic of the two roots of affliction together with the three outflows built upon them, and the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing's own treatment of inner knowledge now gives way to its second great science, logic, itself unfolding, according to the Basic Section's own overview, into seven aspects: a treatise's nature, its proper venue, its foundation, its adornment, its defeat, its means of escape, and its most productive methods, of which this fascicle takes up three in turn, beginning with the five kinds of inference by which the differences among all dharmas are known: inference by characteristic, discerning a dharma through its associated marks as presently observed or previously seen; inference by substance, discerning a dharma through its own essential nature together with its further particular distinctions; inference by function, discerning a dharma through its own operative power and through whether the conduct associated with it is wrong or right; inference by dharma, discerning a dharma through whether it is defiled or pure and through whether it belongs to the conventional or the ultimate truth; and inference by cause and result, discerning a dharma through whether its causes and results are near or distant in time and through whether those results are desirable or undesirable.

復次，略由五相，應知諸法差別道理。一、由相故；二、由體故；三、由業故；四、由法故；五、由因果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, briefly, by five marks, one should know the reasoning behind the distinctions among dharmas: by characteristic, by substance, by function, by dharma, and by cause and result.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own closing summary — that every one of the sixty-two wrong views, however elaborately dressed up, ultimately traces back to a single mistaken grasping at a self — as the entire ignorance-outflow discussion's real payoff, a reminder that doctrinal complexity in these texts is not an end in itself but a means of exposing one root error from every conceivable angle. He notes that logic's own place in the five sciences, arriving only after inner knowledge has been thoroughly laid out, reflects the treatise's own priorities, right reasoning here serving the deeper aim of correctly discerning the Dharma rather than functioning as a self-sufficient discipline of debate.

62. Section: Three Kinds of Ultimate Treatise — by Hearing, by Disputation, by Right Practice

A further topic opens within logic, the nature of a treatise itself, recalling first that discourse as such had already been shown, in the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing's own basic section, to unfold into six kinds — ordinary worldly speech, speech according with sound principle, contentious speech, abusive speech spoken in anger, speech according with the true reasoning of the Buddhadharma, and speech that actually instructs others in accordance with the Buddha's own teaching — these six now regrouped into three pairs of three treatises apiece, all amounting to the same content under different names. The first such grouping of three treatises comprises the ultimate treatise of mere hearing, the ultimate treatise of disputation, and the ultimate treatise of right practice. The ultimate treatise of mere hearing refers to the various harmful incantations of the brāhmaṇa non-Buddhists, whose adherents hold that liberation is reached simply by hearing the sound of a mantra recited, without any need to understand its actual meaning at all. The ultimate treatise of disputation refers to the logical treatises of the non-Buddhist schools generally, logic itself having in fact originated among non-Buddhist thinkers before Master Dignāga later composed a Buddhist logic expressly to subdue them, these non-Buddhist disputations being driven by the wish of each side to prove its own teaching most ultimate and to defeat its rivals, and being consequently flawed and incapable of leading any being out of the three realms, since their true aim is never liberation but rather exposing the opponent's faults and claiming victory. The ultimate treatise of right practice refers simply to the Buddha's own noble teaching, since it alone furnishes the practitioner with a correct practice actually capable of reaching the far shore of nirvana. A second grouping of three treatises then begins to open, though this fascicle's own text has not yet gone on to name its three members.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, there are three kinds of treatise: the ultimate treatise of hearing, the ultimate treatise of disputation, and the ultimate treatise of right practice. The ultimate treatise of hearing refers to the various harmful incantations of the brāhmaṇas. The ultimate treatise of disputation refers to the logical treatises of the non-Buddhist schools. The ultimate treatise of right practice refers to the Buddha's noble teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the historical note that logic itself originated among non-Buddhist schools, only later being taken up and refined by Master Dignāga for Buddhist purposes, as a valuable piece of context for understanding why this fascicle treats non-Buddhist disputation with such open suspicion, the very tool of formal reasoning having first been forged for besting rivals in argument rather than for discerning truth. He highlights the contrast drawn here between disputation aimed at victory and right practice aimed at liberation as the passage's own real point, a warning that mastery of logical form is no guarantee whatsoever of genuine spiritual benefit unless it is placed in service of an aim beyond merely winning the argument.

63. Section: Before Composing Any Treatise, Bow to Two Teachers and Hold Six Motivations

A second grouping of three treatises maps directly onto the first: the treatise of no benefit, corresponding to the ultimate treatise of mere hearing, since it brings the being who relies on it no genuine benefit at all; the treatise of wrong meaning, corresponding to the ultimate treatise of disputation, since the non-Buddhist logic it employs is simply mistaken reasoning; and the treatise of ultimate meaning, corresponding to the ultimate treatise of right practice, namely the Buddha's own noble teaching, supreme among all teachings as the very meaning of the ultimate truth. A third grouping likewise maps onto the same first grouping: the treatise of deceit, again the brāhmaṇa's harmful incantations, deceptive because upholding them in fact yields only an evil result; the treatise of falsehood, again the non-Buddhist logical treatises, false because their reasoning proceeds from a self that was never real to begin with, and is consequently untrue however elaborate its method; and the treatise of liberation from suffering, again the Buddhadharma, since it alone actually enables a being to escape the suffering fruit of the three realms. This closes the decisive discernment of a treatise's own nature. A new topic then opens, the adornment of a treatise, beginning with what one who wishes to compose a treatise must first do: bow in homage to two revered teachers before daring to compose at all. Out of reverence for the Dharma itself, one must first bow to the root teacher of the treatise, the Tathāgata alone truly deserving this title, since only the Buddha establishes the noble teaching in the first place; and out of reverence for its meaning, one must also bow to the teacher who opens up and clarifies that meaning, a noble bodhisattva such as Maitreya, capable of expounding the Dharma's own profound significance — so that one composing the Yogācārabhūmi itself, for instance, would first bow to the root teacher Śākyamuni Buddha and then to the meaning-clarifying teacher Maitreya Bodhisattva, a distinction further elaborated elsewhere as three kinds of teacher altogether: the great teacher who establishes the noble teaching, the successor teacher who transmits it, such as the great disciples Śāriputra and Mahākāśyapa or bodhisattvas like Maitreya and Nāgārjuna who expound its meaning, and the inheriting teacher, an ordinary spiritual guide or preceptor who simply helps others keep the teaching in mind. A composer must further hold six motivations before proceeding: the wish that the teaching's own letter and meaning circulate ever more widely; the wish that beings of every kind of faith and understanding might, through this very composition, come to enter the true Dharma; the wish to restore and reopen meanings that have been lost or fallen into obscurity; the wish to condense what has grown too scattered or to expand what has remained too condensed, so that a learner grasps the teaching both broadly and concisely; the wish to reveal profound meaning not otherwise made plain; and the wish to adorn the Dharma's meaning with beautiful and skillful words so as to give rise to pure faith in those who encounter it.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, if one wishes to compose a treatise, one should first bow in homage to the two revered teachers before one may compose. Out of reverence for the Dharma, one should first bow to the root teacher of the treatise; out of reverence for the meaning, one should further bow to the teacher who opens up and clarifies the meaning. One who wishes to compose a treatise must hold six motivations before one may compose: that the teaching and its meaning may circulate widely; that beings of various faith and understanding may, through this cause, enter the true Dharma; that meanings lost and fallen into obscurity may be reopened; that what is too scattered may be condensed and what is too condensed may be expanded; that profound meaning may be revealed; and that the Dharma's meaning may be adorned with beautiful words so as to give rise to pure faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as Maitreya's own instruction manual for composing any treatise at all, and thus quietly self-referential, since the Yogācārabhūmi itself must have been composed according to exactly these same standards it now lays down. He highlights the requirement to bow first to the root teacher and only then to the meaning-clarifying teacher as a small but telling detail of proper order, reverence for the Buddha as the source of the Dharma always preceding reverence for whichever bodhisattva or teacher later opens up and clarifies its meaning for later generations.

64. Section: Four Virtues of a Composer — Freedom from Arrogance, and Great Compassion

A further topic opens: when about to compose a treatise, one must first settle oneself in four virtues before proceeding. The first is freedom from arrogance toward the masters of former times. What is this freedom from arrogance? When composing, one must never harbor the thought that, since the ancient masters were able to compose treatises, how could one possibly fail to compose one too — comparing oneself against Maitreya, Asaṅga, Nāgārjuna, or Āryadeva, who each composed treatises of their own, and thinking simply that since they were disciples of the Buddha and one is oneself also a disciple of the Buddha, therefore one too must be able to compose; rather, one must set aside any such prideful and defiled attitude, composing instead only out of the six motivations already given, before a treatise may properly be composed. The second virtue is great compassion toward all beings. What is this great compassion? When composing, one must reflect: if this treatise is not composed, countless beings will surely regress from the wholesome dharma the Buddha has taught, and other beings besides, never having had occasion to see this wholesome dharma at all, will continue falling into birth, old age, sickness, and so on at great length through the sufferings of birth and death; one must further reflect that, because of this very composition, any being at all who comes to understand and put into practice even a single well-spoken phrase of its profound meaning will thereby surely obtain vast benefit and ease throughout the long night of saṃsāra to come. Only after actually generating this exalted resolve, born of compassion for beings caught in this cycle and unaware of any way out, may one properly proceed to compose a treatise.

將造論時，要以四德先自安處，乃可造論。一、於昔諸師應離憍慢；二、於有情類當起大悲；三、於同法者深生敬愛；四、不欲彰己有勝技能。云何於昔諸師應離憍慢？謂造論時，無如是心：古昔諸師尚能造論，況我今者當不造耶。要離如是憍慢染心，乃應造論。云何於有情類當起大悲？謂造論時，作如是觀：若不造論，無量有情於諸善法定當退失，又餘情類墮生老病，乃至廣說。是諸有情因我造論，若能解了乃至一句善說妙義，如是如是當奉行者，彼於長夜必獲大義利益安樂。要發如是增上心已，乃應造論。

Ru-Yi Translation

When about to compose a treatise, one must first settle oneself in four virtues before one may compose: freedom from arrogance toward the masters of former times; great compassion toward beings; deep reverence and affection toward fellow practitioners of the same Dharma; and no wish to display one's own superior skill. What is freedom from arrogance toward the masters of former times? It means that when composing, one holds no such thought as: since the masters of old were able to compose, how could I possibly fail to compose now. One must be free of this arrogant and defiled attitude before one may compose. What is great compassion toward beings? It means that when composing, one reflects: if the treatise is not composed, countless beings will surely regress from wholesome dharma, and other beings besides will fall into birth, old age, sickness, and so on at length. Any being who, because of my composing, comes to understand and practice even a single well-spoken phrase of its subtle meaning will surely obtain great benefit and ease through the long night of saṃsāra. One must generate this exalted resolve before one may compose.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi finds this passage remarkably humane for what is otherwise an intensely technical treatise, Maitreya himself pausing amid the driest classificatory material to insist that a treatise's composer must be moved above all by compassion, never by the wish merely to match the achievements of past masters or to prove one's own cleverness. He notes that this same warning against composing out of mere pride remains directly applicable today to any teacher or writer taking up Buddhist material, the correct motivation mattering every bit as much as the correctness of the content itself.
