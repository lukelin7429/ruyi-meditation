Fascicle 64 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十四 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing Opens

Six Reasons the Three Jewels Are Genuinely Precious

With the Ground without Mind now closed, Maitreya opens the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, the vast field of learning spanning inner knowledge of the Dharma, medicine, logic, linguistics, and the practical arts, beginning with what genuine hearing of the Dharma must itself rely upon: the Three Jewels, and the five qualities that make the Buddha, and no other teacher, truly worthy of taking refuge in.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 64 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Ground Opens — Six Reasons the Three Jewels Deserve Their Name

Maitreya marks the transition directly: the decisive discernment of the Ground without Mind now complete, the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing shall now be explained, the vast field of learning a practitioner draws upon in hearing the Dharma, traditionally divided into five sciences, inner knowledge of the Buddhadharma itself, the healing science of medicine, the science of logical proof, the science of language and grammar, and the practical science of craft and trade, a bodhisattva expected to master all five while one seeking only to end affliction as a śrāvaka need master inner knowledge alone. The discussion opens with what genuine hearing of the Dharma must first rely upon, the Three Jewels, and asks why the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha alone deserve to be called jewels at all, answering through six distinct qualities shared by any genuine treasure: their sheer rarity, since one impoverished in merit may pass through countless lifetimes without ever once encountering them; their freedom from stain, since just as a true jewel carries no blemish, the Three Jewels alone can actually remove the defiling afflictions from within; their power, since just as worldly treasure can relieve poverty and neutralize poison, the Three Jewels alone hold the power to dispel the poisons of greed, hatred, and delusion; their capacity to adorn, since just as a jewel adorns the body, the Three Jewels adorn the mind itself, the only ornament that actually matters; their supremacy, the Buddha supreme among the three realms' own noble beings, the Dharma supreme among every teaching, the Sangha supreme among every community; and their changelessness, since just as true gold cannot be altered by fire or hammer, the Three Jewels remain forever unmoved by the eight winds of gain and loss, praise and blame, honor and ridicule, pleasure and pain.

Ru-Yi Translation

The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, within the chapter of decisive discernment. Thus I have already explained the decisive discernment of the Ground without Mind. I shall now explain the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing. One should observe five aspects in what is taken as refuge before genuine refuge can be taken: purity of bodily conduct; purity of verbal conduct; purity of mental conduct; giving rise to great compassion toward all beings; and having accomplished the unsurpassed Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Ratnagotravibhāga's own six meanings of jewel directly, rarity, purity, power, adornment, supremacy, and changelessness, treating this list as the natural and necessary preface to everything the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing will go on to teach, since no amount of learning matters unless what is actually being learned deserves the trust placed in it. He notes that the five qualities of genuine refuge now opening, three kinds of purified conduct joined to great compassion and unsurpassed accomplishment, exist specifically to let a sincere seeker distinguish the Buddha from the ninety-six rival teachers of ancient India, a comparison this section takes up directly rather than leaving refuge as a matter of mere preference.

2. Section: A Teacher Who Watches Six Times Each Day and Night

Each of the five qualities making the Buddha worthy of refuge is explained in turn: purity of bodily conduct, entirely free of killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct; purity of verbal conduct, entirely free of falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, or idle chatter; purity of mental conduct, entirely free of greed, hatred, and delusion; great compassion arising toward all beings without exception, regarding every being, virtuous or otherwise, exactly as a parent regards an only child, ready to guide anyone toward liberation the instant conditions allow; and the accomplishment of the unsurpassed Dharma, perfect and complete awakening itself, wisdom and merit both brought to their utmost fullness. The Lotus Sūtra's own verse on the Buddha's lifespan captures this same great compassion directly: I constantly know which beings practice the path and which do not, and, according to what each is capable of receiving, teach them the Dharma accordingly, always thinking to myself how I might lead every being into the unsurpassed path and swiftly accomplish the Buddha's own body, the Buddha's apparent passing into final nirvana never actually withdrawing from beings at all, but continuing across six periods of every day and night alike, three by day and three by night, to watch with the Buddha eye over the entire world, asking in whom wholesome roots have not yet been planted, and in whom the very fruit of arhatship might yet be established.

Ru-Yi Translation

The first aspect is purity of bodily conduct, free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct. The second is purity of verbal conduct, free of falsehood, harsh speech, divisive speech, and idle chatter. The third is purity of mental conduct, free of greed, hatred, and delusion. The fourth is giving rise to great compassion toward all beings, regarding every being as an only child. The fifth is accomplishing the unsurpassed Dharma, perfect and complete awakening. I constantly know which beings practice the path and which do not, and teach the Dharma according to what each can receive. I always think to myself how I might lead beings into the unsurpassed path and swiftly accomplish the Buddha's own body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Lotus Sūtra's own image of the Buddha watching over the world six times each day and night, three by day and three by night, as this entire section's own warmest and most reassuring teaching, the Buddha's apparent departure into final nirvana never actually meaning abandonment, but a continuing, active vigilance that notices exactly where a given being's own practice stands and waits only for the right conditions to help it forward. He connects this directly to Nāgārjuna's own image of the Buddha and bodhisattvas as those with open eyes looking upon beings still blinded by their own ignorance, unaware that help is even present, precisely why taking refuge in the Three Jewels matters, not as a formality, but as opening oneself to a guidance that Master Cháng Bǎi describes as never actually withheld, only waiting to be recognized.

3. Section: Four Reasons There Are Exactly Three Refuges

Five questions now open in turn: how many kinds of refuge are there, why exactly that many, what marks one as genuinely having taken refuge, how is the practice of refuge actually carried out, and what merit does taking refuge actually bring? The first answer is simple: there are three refuges, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Four causes explain why exactly these three suffice. First, the Buddha's own nature is perfectly tamed and good, having so thoroughly exhausted every attachment and habitual trace that body, speech, and mind require no guarding at all, since none of the three could ever err in the first place. Second, the Buddha is skillful beyond measure in taming beings of absolutely every kind, whether bound by every affliction or already partly freed, of dull, middling, or sharp faculty, of any caste or temperament, householder or renunciant, easy to tame or resistant, needing gentle words or needing sharp rebuke, near to liberation or still very far from it, matching whatever remedy each being's own condition actually calls for across every one of the many realms of existence. Third, the Buddha possesses great compassion in the fullest sense, a compassion taking as its object suffering so deep, so subtle, and so difficult to even recognize that most beings remain entirely unaware how much they actually suffer, cultivated continuously across countless ages without ever tiring, arising through the most intense and deliberate attention toward every being's own good, and utterly pure, since the Buddha helps and then simply lets go, expecting nothing whatsoever in return. Fourth, the Buddha never delights in offerings of mere wealth, but only in genuine practice, exactly as the Sūtra of the Flower Hand declares, that incense, robes, food, and medicine offered to a buddha do not constitute true offering at all, but that anyone who takes up and actually practices the wondrous Dharma realized beneath the Buddha's own tree of awakening offers the one true worship the Buddha genuinely receives.

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: there are three kinds of refuge, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. Four causes account for exactly this many refuges: the Buddha's own nature is perfectly tamed and good; the Buddha is skillful in every respect toward those to be tamed and the very means of taming them; the Buddha possesses great compassion; and the Buddha does not delight in offerings of mere wealth, but only in offerings made through genuine practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth cause, the Buddha's own indifference to material offering and genuine delight only in a disciple's actual practice, as this entire passage's own most practically important teaching, a direct challenge to any devotional practice that mistakes elaborate ritual offering for what the Buddha himself actually values. He quotes the Sūtra of the Flower Hand's own blunt verse directly, that incense, robes, food, and medicine offered to any buddha do not constitute true offering at all, only genuine practice of the Dharma realized beneath the Buddha's own tree of awakening, treating this as this entire treatise's own recurring insistence that practice, not ceremony, is what devotion actually means.

4. Section: Why the Buddha, and Not the Gods, Deserves to Be Called Teacher

Four further conditions mark what it actually means to take refuge: knowing the Buddha's own genuine virtues; knowing the crucial difference between the Buddha and the various gods, demons, and non-Buddhist teachers, who fail as genuine refuges for two decisive reasons, an actual incapacity to help, since even the gods lack the power, the willingness, the tirelessness, the genuine compassion, and the moral stature the Buddha alone possesses, and simply never being seen to have accomplished anything comparable in the world at all, since the Buddha's own historical existence, serene countenance free of fear, freedom from heedless indulgence, unwavering commitment to benefiting others, and penetrating wisdom stand as facts anyone can actually examine, unlike a god's own claims; one's own sincere vow, arising from a genuine spiritual capacity already present, to seek awakening and accept refuge freely; and simply that no other teacher exists who could possibly be preferred instead. Four distinct practices are then said to constitute the very practice of refuge itself: drawing near to a genuine spiritual friend; actually hearing the true Dharma from that friend, both its words and its meaning; bringing correct attention to what has been heard, reflecting on it exactly as it was actually taught; and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma itself, whether the noble eightfold path and the other factors of awakening for one seeking personal liberation, or the six perfections and four means of gathering others for a bodhisattva pursuing buddhahood on behalf of all beings, one who genuinely accomplishes all four of these practices, and only such a person, being rightly called one who has actually taken refuge at all.

齊四緣故說能歸依。一、知功德故；二、知差別故；三、自誓願故；四、更不說有餘大師故。當知歸依有四正行。一、親近善士，二、聽聞正法，三、如理作意，四、法隨法行。若有成就此四正行，乃名歸依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Genuine refuge requires four conditions together: knowing the Buddha's virtues; knowing the difference between the Buddha and other teachers; one's own sincere vow; and there being no other teacher to prefer instead. One should know that refuge involves four correct practices: drawing near to a good friend, hearing the true Dharma, bringing correct attention to it, and practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. Only one who accomplishes these four practices is genuinely called one who has taken refuge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Display of the Noble Teaching's own extensive case against treating gods and demons as genuine refuges, an incapacity to help rooted in limited power, a need to be flattered and petitioned before helping at all, an attachment to their own heavenly pleasure that leaves them unwilling to endure real hardship for others, and a fundamental lack of true compassion, none of which the Buddha's own conduct exhibits in the slightest. He treats the fourfold practice of refuge, good friend, hearing, reflection, and practice in accordance with the Dharma, as this section's own final, practical answer to what taking refuge actually requires, since without all four genuinely present, reciting the refuge formula itself accomplishes very little on its own.

5. Section: Eight Marks of a Good Friend, and a Second Set of Four Practices

A genuine spiritual friend, capable of guiding a bodhisattva properly, is shown to possess eight distinct qualities: dwelling securely in the moral discipline appropriate to a bodhisattva, without lapse or flaw; broad learning sufficient to actually understand the Dharma being taught; some genuine realization of one's own, not mere book-knowledge alone; compassion that truly grieves for other beings; fearlessness in teaching, never losing mindfulness or eloquence out of fright before an audience; patience under scorn, mockery, or outright hostility; tirelessness in teaching across however many audiences require it; and skillful, unimpeded expression that conveys the Dharma clearly without ever distorting its own true nature, a friend possessing even one or two of these eight already being rare and worth learning from. A proper disciple, in turn, is shown to owe such a friend four things: attending upon them faithfully whether they are well or ill, not merely when convenient; showing them constant courtesy, greeting, bowing, and respectful service; supplying their actual material needs, clothing, food, bedding, and medicine, whenever these fall short; and following their instruction faithfully rather than wandering from teacher to teacher and never going deep enough with any one of them to actually benefit. A second set of four practices then completes the practice of refuge: keeping the six sense faculties from restless wandering toward their objects; studying every training point a bodhisattva should undertake; cultivating genuine compassion for suffering beings, both relieving their present suffering and addressing its very root causes; and constantly making offering to the Three Jewels, whether through material gifts, through bowing and praise, or simply through inwardly recollecting their own genuine virtues.

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know there are also four further correct practices: the sense faculties not restlessly wandering; studying the training points; compassion for beings; and, from time to time, diligently cultivating offering toward the Three Jewels.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the eight qualities of a genuine spiritual friend set such a demanding standard that even possessing one or two of them already marks someone worth learning from, a realistic acknowledgment that the fully complete teacher described here remains rare, while the four practices owed in return, faithful attendance, courtesy, material support, and genuine follow-through, describe an ordinary, sustainable relationship rather than a single dramatic gesture of devotion. He treats compassion for beings, addressing not only their present suffering but its actual causes, as this section's own reminder that genuine compassion in practice looks like both direct relief work and patient teaching aimed at preventing suffering before it even arises.

6. Section: Eight Merits That Follow from Genuine Refuge

Those who take refuge sincerely are said to gain eight distinct merits, unfolding across two sets of four. The first set: vast blessing; great joy, arising from three sources at once, delighting in the Three Jewels themselves, delighting in one's own now-purified precepts, and delighting in confident, unshaken trust in one's own actual spiritual progress; genuine concentration, since the very precepts refuge establishes become concentration's own necessary support; and great purity, the transcendent purification that follows once affliction is actually uprooted through sustained practice. The second set: complete protection, whether understood as the guardianship of benevolent spirits drawn near by a mind made pure through refuge, or as the ten inner and ten outer conditions of a genuinely well-favored birth, a human body, a place where the Dharma is actually taught, unimpaired faculties, freedom from any grave past misdeed, freedom from confused or divided faith, a perfected teacher, a perfectly preserved teaching, living exemplars who have actually realized it, its own continued survival in the world, and the material support of generous patrons; a marked diminishment, or even complete disappearance, of every obstacle rooted in wrong belief; entry into the very company of the wise, keeping fellowship with the Teacher and with genuine fellow practitioners of pure conduct; and the delighted approval of the gods themselves, who rejoice at seeing genuine faith arise in the very teaching that, by their own account, once carried them out of a former life and into their present heavenly one, hoping that these same practitioners will one day join their own heavenly company in turn.

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who take refuge gain four merits: vast blessing, great joy, concentration, and great purity. They also gain four further merits: complete protection; every obstacle rooted in wrong belief becoming markedly diminished or entirely gone; entry into the company of the wise, of correct practice, and of those who have arrived, namely the Teacher and fellow practitioners of pure conduct; and the delighted, loving approval of the gods toward genuine faith in the noble teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the charming detail behind the eighth merit, the gods' own delighted approval, since the very deities looking on are themselves said to have arrived in their heavenly state by virtue of past good conduct traceable to the Dharma, and so watch a new practitioner's own sincere refuge with the same hope one might feel watching a promising student follow a path one has walked before. He treats great joy's own threefold source, the Three Jewels, one's own purified precepts, and confident trust in one's own progress, as a genuinely useful checklist for recognizing whether refuge has actually taken root, since a practitioner lacking any real joy in these three might reasonably ask whether the refuge itself remains more formal than lived.

7. Section: Born from the Buddha's Own Mouth — What Makes the Sangha One Body

A new topic distinguishes the Three Jewels through six further aspects, opening with their distinct function: turning the wheel of the true teaching is the Buddha's own function, passing the Dharma forward for the benefit of all beings; abandoning affliction and the very ground suffering depends upon is the Dharma's own function; and vigorous, ever-increasing advancement in wholesome practice is the Sangha's own function. Considered through faith and understanding, one should cultivate devoted closeness and service toward the Buddha; a heartfelt aspiration to actually realize what is taught toward the Dharma; and toward the Sangha, a confident faith in genuine harmony, dwelling together in one and the same nature, since every disciple is, in five distinct senses, born from the same source: born from the Buddha's own body, no longer merely an ordinary, lowly child of saṃsāra; born from the Buddha's own mouth, arising from the very sound and words of the Dharma once heard; born from the Dharma itself, through correct attention and practice in accordance with what was heard; born through the Dharma's own transformation, following the unchanging path of the Buddha's own dharma-body toward a genuinely similar realization; and equally sharing the Dharma, every disciple alike enjoying only the undefiled wealth of the teaching itself rather than any worldly gain, this same shared nature grounding the six forms of harmonious concord, harmony in body, dwelling together; harmony in speech, free of dispute; harmony in mind, sharing genuine joy; harmony in precepts, practiced together; harmony in view, understood together; and harmony in benefit, shared equally among all.

What is the distinction of the Three Jewels through function? Turning the true teaching is the Buddha's function; abandoning affliction and the object of suffering is the Dharma's function; vigorous increase in practice is the Sangha's function. What is the distinction through faith and understanding? Toward the Buddha, one should cultivate faith through devoted closeness and service; toward the Dharma, faith through aspiring to realize it; toward the Sangha, faith through harmonious dwelling together in one and the same nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold explanation of what makes every disciple share one nature, born of the Buddha's body, mouth, Dharma, and transformation, and equal in sharing the Dharma's own wealth, as an unusually intimate image of spiritual kinship, reframing the entire monastic community not as a mere institution but as something closer to an actual extended family united by a single shared birth. He notes that the six forms of harmonious concord, still recited in monastic communities today, follow directly from this same shared nature, harmony in body, speech, mind, precepts, view, and benefit together describing what genuine community among fellow practitioners should actually look like in daily practice.

8. Section: Better to Suffer Every Pain Than Never Hear the Buddha's Name

Considered through actual practice, one should offer service and support toward the Buddha; cultivate the very skillful means of contemplative practice toward the Dharma; and toward the Sangha, share both wealth and Dharma equally with fellow practitioners of pure conduct, holding precepts and understanding in common as well. Considered through recollection, each of the Three Jewels calls for its own distinct object of remembrance, the Buddha recollected through his own ten powers, four fearlessnesses, and every other excellent quality, or simply through his very name, the Lotus Sūtra itself declaring that even one whose mind is utterly scattered, entering a temple and uttering homage to the Buddha only once, has thereby already planted the very seed of buddhahood, and other scriptures teaching that it is better to endure every possible suffering and still hear the Buddha's voice than to enjoy every possible pleasure and never once hear the Buddha's name, precisely because countless ages of suffering in saṃsāra trace back to having gone this long without ever hearing it. The Dharma, likened to both medicine and a ship carrying beings to nirvana's own far shore, is recollected by distinguishing three kinds of listener, the sightless, who never hear the Dharma at all, the one-eyed, who hears yet never truly takes it to heart, and the truly two-eyed, who listens with full attention and practices exactly as taught, a genuine reverence toward the Dharma illustrated by the story of a nun who prepared an entirely new room, floor scented with fragrant earth, brand-new paper, brush, and ink, and even a mask directing the calligrapher's own breath away from the pages, to have the Lotus Sūtra copied out in the most reverent conditions imaginable, and who, when a visiting monk once borrowed that same copy without first bowing in genuine respect, found the very characters on the page had become entirely invisible to him until he knelt and paid homage properly first. The Sangha is recollected by remembering that even a monk who has broken the precepts still deserves offering and respect rather than contempt, since his very robe continues to plant merit in those who encounter him, exactly as a piece of sandalwood, even once burned, still leaves its own fragrance behind, and as one teaching declares, a broken-precept monk still surpasses every non-Buddhist teacher, so that offering to any monk, worthy or fallen alike, is offering made to the Buddha himself.

What is the distinction through recollection? Recollect the Buddha jewel, the Dharma jewel, and the Sangha jewel each through their own distinct aspect, namely that this is the World-Honored One, and so extensively explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the nun and the calligrapher as this entire section's own most vivid teaching on reverence, the very characters of the Lotus Sūtra becoming invisible to a monk who had not first shown proper respect, a story he reads less as literal magic than as a pointed illustration of how much a text's own meaning actually depends on the reader's own inner posture toward it. He highlights the sandalwood simile for the Sangha, fragrance remaining even after the wood itself has burned, as this passage's own compassionate resolution of an otherwise uncomfortable teaching, that even a fallen monastic's own robe continues to carry real spiritual value for those who encounter it, however far short of the ideal that particular individual may have fallen.

9. Section: Five Ways the Buddhist Path Outshines the Brahmin's

The sixth and final aspect distinguishes the Three Jewels through the merit their own presence generates: relying on a single being, the Buddha, the most supreme merit is generated; relying on the Dharma itself, that same most supreme merit is generated; and relying on many beings together, the Sangha, the most supreme merit is likewise generated, completing this sixfold analysis of characteristic, function, faith, practice, recollection, and merit. A further topic then opens, distinguishing the genuinely superior from the inferior between Buddhist renunciants and brahmin ascetics through five further aspects. In the teaching each actually hears, the brahmin's own teaching remains empty and inferior in meaning, never openly shown to others, its very wording obscure, while the renunciant's own teaching is complete, openly taught, and clearly expressed, and so superior. In the precepts each actually holds, the brahmin's own precepts permit various forms of harm depending on circumstance or status, while the renunciant's own precepts, forbidding killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and falsehood alike, permit no harm whatsoever, and so are superior. In what each actually embraces, the brahmin embraces fields, houses, wealth, and even wives, servants, and slaves, obstacles to the path all, while the renunciant embraces nothing beyond what actually helps toward freedom from suffering, and so is superior.

Chinese Source

云何生福故三寶差別？謂於佛寶，依一有情生最勝福；於法寶所，即依此法生最勝福；於僧寶所，依多有情生最勝福。復次，由五法故，沙門、婆羅門勝劣差別。何等五法？一者、聞法，二者、戒法，三、攝受法，四、受用法，五、證得法。謂婆羅門所有聞法，義虛劣故，不示他故，文句隱故，是其下劣。沙門聞法與此相違，故是勝妙。又婆羅門所有戒法，隨何、隨分、隨其差別，開許害等，故是下劣。沙門戒法與此相違，故是勝妙。又婆羅門所攝受法，攝受障道田事、宅事、財貨事等，又復攝受妻子、奴婢、僮僕等類，故是下劣。沙門所有攝受之法，除離苦法，更無所有，故是勝妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the distinction through generating merit? Relying on the Buddha jewel, one being, the most supreme merit is generated; relying on the Dharma jewel, this same dharma generates it; relying on the Sangha jewel, many beings generate it. There are also five factors distinguishing the superior from the inferior between renunciants and brahmins: the teaching heard, the precepts held, what is embraced, what is enjoyed, and what is realized. The brahmins' own teaching is empty, inferior, unshown to others, and obscure in wording, and so inferior; the renunciants' teaching is the reverse, and so superior. The brahmins' precepts permit various kinds of harm, and so are inferior; the renunciants' precepts are the reverse, and so superior. The brahmins embrace fields, houses, wealth, wives, servants, and slaves, and so are inferior; the renunciants embrace nothing beyond what frees one from suffering, and so are superior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this direct comparison between Buddhist renunciants and brahmin ascetics as this treatise's own unusually candid apologetic moment, naming specific, checkable differences, openness of teaching, breadth of precepts, and freedom from worldly entanglement, rather than resting the case for the Dharma's superiority on faith claims alone. He notes that the pattern across all three aspects so far, the brahmin's own path permitting exactly what the renunciant's forbids, land, wealth, family entanglement, sets up what the fourth and fifth aspects, still to come, will extend into what each actually enjoys and what each actually realizes at the end of the path.

10. Section: What Is Enjoyed, What Is Finally Realized, and Three Kinds of Seeking

A further topic then opens, examining three broad kinds of seeking in detail. Seeking after desire itself unfolds across five aspects already named, seeking to acquire, seeking to enjoy, seeking mere amusement, seeking without any real understanding of desire's own danger, and seeking fame through even the smallest wholesome accomplishment. Seeking after continued existence in the higher realms likewise unfolds across five aspects: arising quite naturally, as when a single being spontaneously attains the second absorption as an entire age draws toward its own dissolution, with others simply following that same example; arising through a deliberate vow to be reborn above; arising through the sheer delusion of believing such rebirth to be final and complete, not yet seeing that even the highest heaven remains impermanent; arising through simple aversion to the coarseness of the realm below, without any genuine cutting of affliction; and arising through genuine wisdom, correctly discerning the real danger in every desire and so seeking freedom from it as a means to something greater still.

11. Section: Genuine Seeking After Liberation, Closing the Renunciant's Own Portrait

Seeking after pure conduct, the third and highest kind of seeking, likewise unfolds across five aspects in a first reading: seeking purely and only the undefiled realm itself, nothing less; heading toward its actual attainment by gathering the very requisites nirvana depends upon; presently attaining nirvana with residue; later attaining nirvana without residue; and discerning, through genuine skillful wisdom, how to swiftly realize nirvana in a future life to come. A second reading distinguishes this same seeking through a different fivefold lens: a merely nominal seeking, mistaking the four formless attainments, however exalted, for genuine liberation itself, precisely the error of a non-Buddhist path; the ultimate seeking proper, aiming only at the undefiled realm directly; an observational seeking, cultivating the very skillful wisdom this requires; seeking without genuine skillful means, mistaking the fourth absorption or the formless attainments themselves for liberation and so never actually realizing the truth; and seeking with genuine skillful means, following the correct path of liberation to its own true realization. With these three kinds of seeking, after desire, after continued existence, and after pure conduct, now examined in full, whether pursued rightly, as a genuine renunciant does, or wrongly, as a brahmin or other non-Buddhist teacher does, this entire comparison between the superior and the inferior path reaches its own close.

12. Section: Why Inner Knowledge Outshines Every Other Science

A major new topic now opens, turning to the five sciences themselves, already introduced in the Grounds of Wisdom Attained through Hearing. It alone cannot be refuted or overturned by any rival doctrine, its own reasoning simply too sound to be shaken. It alone is genuinely easy to enter, since following its own teaching on the four truths or dependent origination leads naturally toward realizing reality directly. And once entered, its own correct practice never degrades or is lost, sustaining a practitioner in continued purity for as long as that practice continues. The Display of the Noble Teaching's own verse captures this same fourfold purity precisely: encompassing every meaning, unbroken by outside attack, easy to enter, and, once entered, its practice never lost or destroyed.

13. Section: Ten Topics That Comprise the Buddha's Entire Teaching

A detailed exposition now opens, showing that every teaching the Buddha ever gave falls within just ten topics. The dharma to be cultivated, the practitioner's own sustained, focused, and skillfully applied effort itself. The dharma actually cultivated, every wholesome quality, mundane and transcendent alike. The dharma bound up with fault, everything belonging to the truth of suffering. The dharma bound up with defilement, everything belonging to the truth of origination, ignorance and craving chief among them. The dharma that obstructs, every affliction, self-view, greed, hatred, delusion, pride, and wrong view among them, that blocks a being from ever reaching direct realization. The dharma in accordance with liberation, the thirty-seven factors of awakening belonging to the truth of the path. The dharma of suchness itself, the truth of cessation, reality's own true nature as the very object of non-discriminating wisdom. The dharma of supreme virtues, the ten powers, four fearlessnesses, four kinds of unhindered eloquence, the three samādhis, and the four immeasurable states among the many excellent qualities a practitioner cultivates along the way. The dharma according with the world, cultivating worldly wholesome conduct, abandoning worldly defilement, and continuing, even as an arhat, whatever wholesome conduct remains genuinely fitting. And the dharma of ultimate attainment, the ten qualities of one beyond further training for a śrāvaka, or unsurpassed awakening itself for one following the Great Vehicle.

14. Section: Six Kinds of Truth, from What Everyone Agrees On to What Cannot Be Said at All

The first gateway, true meaning, unfolds into six distinct kinds, already treated at length in the Bodhisattva Grounds' own chapter on true meaning: truth established by simple worldly agreement, whatever ordinary people universally accept as real; truth established by reasoning, the causal and analytical truths a thoughtful mind arrives at, wholesome cause yielding pleasant fruit and unwholesome cause yielding painful fruit among them; truth as the very object of wisdom purified of the affliction obstruction, chiefly the four noble truths themselves; truth as the very object of wisdom purified of the cognitive obstruction, chiefly the emptiness of phenomena realized specifically within a bodhisattva's own domain; established truth, truth given shape through language and designation; and non-established truth, truth realized directly within the mind itself, beyond every path of words and every operation of ordinary thought. Established truth is then explained through three kinds of worldly convention: convention of the world itself, the ordinary designations of house, vase, army, forest, number, self, or being that ordinary experience simply takes for granted; convention of reasoning, the Buddhist teaching's own designations of the aggregates, sense bases, and elements; and convention of realization, the four fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship established according to exactly which afflictions each successive fruit has cut away, from the three fetters severed at stream-entry, through greed, hatred, and delusion thinned at once-returning, the five lower fetters entirely severed at non-returning, to every affliction whatsoever brought to its complete and final end at arhatship.

15. Section: A Fourth Convention for the Truth Beyond All Words

A fourth kind of convention is then added to the three already given: convention of ultimate truth itself, the very object supreme wisdom takes as its own domain. Since this ultimate truth cannot, in its own genuine nature, actually be captured in language at all, being realized only directly within the mind, it is nevertheless given a provisional, conventional expression in words solely in order to help give rise to the very wisdom capable of realizing it directly, exactly as a finger pointing at the moon is not itself the moon, yet still serves to guide the eye toward it. Non-established truth is then explained simply as the true suchness of all phenomena, reality's own unchangeable emptiness, known only through direct inner realization rather than through any conventional designation whatsoever, whether the three or four kinds of worldly convention just described. With this, established and non-established truth alike are shown to complete the very structure of true meaning, and the first gateway of reasoning reaches its own close. 18. Section: Three Kinds of Pratyekabuddha, from the Convert to the True Hundred-Eon Seeker

A further topic opens, distinguishing realization belonging to the pratyekabuddha vehicle into three kinds. What is contained within the treatise-compendium is the seventeen grounds and the four further compilations.

20. Section: Ten Teachings That Together Cover Everything the Treatise Contains

Teaching contained within both the canon and the treatise-compendium together is then shown to unfold across ten distinct kinds. The teaching of the truths' own marks, the sixteen aspects belonging to the four truths themselves. The teaching of full understanding, corresponding to the truth of suffering, which is to be fully known. The teaching of permanent severing, corresponding to the truth of origination, which is to be cut off entirely. The teaching of realization, corresponding to the truth of cessation, which is to be directly realized. The teaching of cultivation, corresponding to the truth of the path, which is to be cultivated. The teaching of classification, the many further subdivisions belonging to each of the four truths in turn. The teaching of the relation between what is depended upon and what depends, the practitioner as what is depended upon, and the four truths themselves as what depends upon that practitioner's own cultivation. The teaching of the obstructing dharma, whatever runs contrary to reaching direct realization at all. The teaching of the accordant dharma, whatever genuinely supports reaching that same realization. And the teaching of fault and merit, the harm that follows from failing to fully know the four truths against the excellent benefit that follows from fully knowing them. Together, these ten teachings are declared to encompass everything contained within both the canon at large and this treatise's own compendium, forming what is called, in brief, the comprehensive treatise-compendium itself.

21. Section: Sixteen Aspects of the Four Truths, and Twelve Further Kinds of Teaching

The truths' own marks are explained in full through sixteen aspects, four belonging to each truth in turn: suffering understood as impermanent, as suffering itself, as empty, and as without self; origination understood as cause, as origin, as arising, and as condition; cessation understood as cessation itself, as peace, as excellence, and as genuine freedom; and the path understood as path, as suchness, as practice, and as deliverance, a fourfold contemplation of each truth taught in careful detail within the Grounds of Śrāvakas. Teaching of the matter itself is explained first, simply naming the actual substance of each phenomenon individually, form and the rest, eye and the rest, the very causal realities every other teaching ultimately refers back to. Teaching of conceptual distinctions is explained next, unfolding across the skillful classifications of the aggregates, elements, sense bases, dependent origination, the possible and impossible, the faculties, and the truths; the thirty-seven factors of awakening; and further conceptual distinctions such as possessing form or lacking it, visible or invisible, resistant or unresistant, countless buddhas across countless ages continuing to teach these same distinctions in ever-greater and inexhaustible detail.

22. Section: Ten Perfections of a Dharma Teacher, and Speech Sweet Enough to Persuade

23. Section: Seven Aspects of Logic for Defeating a Rival's Argument

Teaching that examines other traditions concerns the seven aspects of the science of logic, relied upon to overturn rival doctrines and establish one's own teaching soundly, already explained at length in the Grounds of Wisdom Attained through Hearing: the very nature of debate itself, unfolding into six kinds, ordinary discussion, scholarly discourse, contention, slander, correct advocacy, and instruction; the venue of debate, whether before a king, a magistrate, a large assembly, the wise, sympathetic renunciants and brahmins, or genuine seekers of the truth; the basis of debate, ten in number, two things to be established, essential nature and differentiation, and eight means of establishing them, proposition, reason, example, similarity, dissimilarity, direct perception, inference, and authoritative testimony; the very ornaments that make an argument compelling, five in number, mastery of both one's own and a rival's position, complete and fitting expression, fearlessness, dignified composure, and worthiness of respect; the three ways an argument can actually be defeated, conceding the point outright, faltering in one's own words, or committing an actual error in speech; liberation from debate, weighing beforehand whether to actually engage at all through careful observation of what stands to be gained or lost, of the assembly present, and of one's own genuine skill or lack of it; and the very effective functions debate can serve, mastering both traditions, courageous fearlessness, and inexhaustible eloquence.

24. Section: Five Basic Realities, and Teaching That Hides Versus Teaching That Reveals

Teaching of conventional truth is explained through the five basic realities that structure all experience, characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct wisdom, since concept always precedes designation and designation always precedes speech, so that whatever arises through the power of name, concept, and language, every characteristic, name, and discriminating thought that experience actually presents, belongs entirely to conventional truth, whether bound to the desire realm, the form and formless realms, or entirely unbound altogether. Teaching of ultimate truth names the four noble truths directly, together with suchness, reality's own limit, the dharma realm, and every other name given to reality exactly as it is. Hidden teaching refers chiefly to the śrāvaka canon, since the Buddha, teaching according to the śrāvaka disciple's own capacity, taught only the path to personal liberation and never openly disclosed the deeper teaching of the ālaya-consciousness, this restraint itself counting, from the Great Vehicle's own perspective, as a kind of hidden teaching. Explicit teaching refers chiefly to the Great Vehicle canon, openly encouraging its own disciples toward the six perfections and the complete bodhisattva path all the way to unsurpassed awakening, though even here a smaller portion remains more indirectly expressed.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five basic realities, characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct wisdom, as this treatise's own compact summary of how ordinary experience is actually constructed, concept preceding language and language in turn shaping how experience itself gets carved up into distinct things at all, everything on this side of the ledger belonging to convention rather than to ultimate reality directly. He notes that calling the śrāvaka teaching hidden is not a criticism but a recognition of skillful pedagogy, the Buddha withholding the ālaya-consciousness's own deeper teaching from disciples for whom the simpler path to personal liberation was itself already sufficient and appropriate.

25. Section: Fourteen Questions the Buddha Refused to Answer

Teaching of what can be answered names the four seals of the Dharma directly: all formations are impermanent, all feelings are suffering, all phenomena are without self, and nirvana is peace, teachings the Buddha answered and taught openly without reservation. Teaching of what cannot be answered concerns instead those questions the Buddha deliberately declined to resolve at all, such as whether the world is eternal, met only with the reply that this question cannot be answered, or whether the Tathāgata exists or does not exist after death, met with exactly the same refusal, fourteen such unanswerable questions in total. Four reasons explain why the Buddha declined to answer these questions at all. First, because the very premise fails to exist, as when someone asks whether the self is identical to or different from the five aggregates, a question with nothing real underlying it at all, since no genuine self exists in the first place for the question to even be about. Second, because answering would bring no genuine benefit, exactly as the Buddha himself declared, holding up a handful of siṃśapā leaves and comparing them to the vast forest of leaves representing everything he had actually realized, explaining that he taught only what genuinely helps a listener progress toward liberation, leaving everything else, however true, deliberately unspoken. Third, because the matter is simply too profound, as when someone asks whether the self exists or does not exist, either answer only encouraging a listener to seize upon some mistaken view of self all the more firmly, or asks about the Tathāgata's own state after death, a question resting on assumptions no ordinary, undeveloped mind can actually follow through to any correct conclusion. And fourth, because some things are simply established just as they naturally are, as when someone asks whether suchness is the same as or different from the phenomena it underlies, a question already resolved by suchness's own unchangeable nature, neither same nor different by definition, so that pursuing the question further accomplishes nothing a sincere practitioner's own direct realization would not already make clear.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the siṃśapā leaves parable as one of the most quietly humbling images in the entire canon, a fully awakened Buddha holding up a mere handful of leaves against an entire forest to represent the sheer scale of what he actually understood yet chose not to teach, precisely because his own criterion for speech was never completeness but genuine benefit to the listener in front of him. He notes that the fourteen unanswerable questions, spanning the world's own eternity, the self's own existence, and the Tathāgata's own fate after death, share a single deep structure across all four reasons, each one either resting on a false premise, offering no practical benefit, exceeding what an untrained mind could actually verify, or already settled by reality's own nature, a pattern worth recognizing whenever a question, however fascinating, turns out to lead nowhere useful.

26. Section: Four More Reasons for Silence, Closing the Teaching Gateway

Four further reasons are then given for these same fourteen unanswerable questions: that non-Buddhist teachers themselves proclaim false answers to them, each already exhibiting a mistaken clinging to increase or diminishment, or to sameness or difference, regarding the self and other phenomena; that such questions simply do not accord with reality as it actually is; that they bring no genuine benefit at all, since two further causes explain this lack of benefit precisely, one, that pursuing them draws attention away from correctly reflecting on cause and effect, and two, that it draws attention away from correctly reflecting on defilement and purity; and that they serve only as fertile ground for further contention, since whatever position one takes on the world's own inhabitants or the surrounding world itself, whether things are exactly so, otherwise than so, or not so at all, invites nothing but further dispute. With every reason for these deliberate silences now fully explained, the entire teaching gateway, and its full twelvefold classification, reaches its own close. A fourth gateway then opens directly, asking what it actually means to avoid both extremes and find the middle way instead.

He treats the newly opened fourth gateway, avoiding the two extremes, as a natural next step after so much attention already given to what should and should not be answered, since finding the middle way itself requires exactly this same skill, recognizing which of two opposing positions to actually decline rather than settling into either one.

27. Section: Six Extremes to Avoid, and Six Things Never Worth Speculating About

Avoiding the two extremes, the fourth gateway, is explained through six kinds, already treated at length in earlier passages of this treatise: avoiding the extreme of exaggerating what is not genuinely real into real existence, treating phenomena that are actually illusory as though they possessed some fixed, independent reality; avoiding the opposite extreme of denying what is genuinely real into complete non-existence, denying the very emptiness of self and phenomena that constitutes reality's own true nature, as though nothing at all remained once illusion were seen through; avoiding the mistaken clinging to eternalism, believing one's own body and mind to be permanent and unchanging; avoiding the mistaken clinging to nihilism, believing that absolutely nothing remains after death; avoiding indulgence in sensory pleasure, which only nourishes greed, hatred, and delusion and obstructs genuine practice; and avoiding self-mortification, which only nourishes delusion in turn and brings no genuine benefit to practice either, these six falling naturally into three paired extremes, exaggeration against diminishment, eternalism against nihilism, and pleasure against harsh asceticism, every one of them to be avoided by a practitioner walking the middle way instead. A fifth gateway then opens, asking what should never be speculated about at all, answered through six distinct kinds: speculation about the self, about sentient beings in general, about the world itself, about the karmic fruit of beings' own actions, about the meditative states meditators themselves actually experience, and about the Buddhas' own domain.

28. Section: Sixteen Ways the Mind Spins Stories About a Self That Isn't There

Speculation about the self is explained first: relying on the view of a real self, a person may wonder whether they themselves existed in some past life, under what name, eating what food, related to whom, born into what lineage, having accomplished what great deeds, extending this same idle wondering across all three times, a pursuit entirely without point, since no genuine self can be found in the past, the present, or the future to be the subject of any of it, this same tendency sometimes driving a practitioner to chase after meditative attainment purely to glimpse a past life rather than to actually cut affliction at its root. Others speculate instead about the self's own future form, whether it will possess form or not, whether it will retain perception, lose it, or neither, a set of possibilities the Brahmajāla Sūtra catalogs at far greater length, and the same forbidden speculation extends equally to the eternalist view holding self and world to be permanent, the nihilist view holding nothing to remain after death, and the view holding the present enjoyment of the five desires, or the various form-realm absorptions, to already constitute nirvana itself, every one of these views resting on exactly the same underlying confusion whether applied to the self's own past or its own future. A further pattern of speculation asks whether the very life-principle is identical to the body itself, effectively claiming the self to be physical form, or entirely different from the body, effectively claiming the self to be purely mental, or somehow both physical and non-physical, pervading everywhere without division or lack, sixteen distinct non-Buddhist theories in total, every one of them rooted in the same basic ignorance of not yet seeing that no fixed self exists to be either the body, apart from the body, or anything else at all.

He treats this section's own brevity, compared to the sixteen-fold analysis just given for the self, as reflecting how directly the same underlying diagnosis now simply transfers over, a shorter treatment because the real work of dismantling the error was already done in the section just before it.

30. Section: Āryadeva's Own Calm Before an Assassin, and Why a Buddha's Domain Cannot Be Guessed

Speculation about the world completes this same triad, one who relies on the view of a real self likewise wondering whether the surrounding world of mountains, rivers, and earth is permanent or subject to annihilation, resting on exactly the same mistaken foundation already diagnosed in speculation about the self and about sentient beings, whether such speculation proceeds from an explicit view or without one. The master Āryadeva is said to have embodied this teaching directly when a hostile opponent came to assassinate him beneath a tree, meeting the attack by declaring simply that there is no self, no being, no one killed, and no one who kills, and no act of killing at all, since self, being, and world alike are, by their own nature, without any fixed reality to begin with. Speculation about beings' own karmic fruit is shown to be inconceivable through four further marks, the sheer number of possible destinations, thirty-six in the desire realm, eighteen in the form realm, and four in the formless realm, fifty-eight in all; the endless variety of what a being's own body and nature can actually be; the endless variety of karmic causes, pure and impure alike; and the endless variety of resulting fruit, desirable or undesirable, this sheer complexity alone making the whole matter impossible to fully resolve through ordinary reasoning. Speculation about the meditative states meditators themselves experience is inconceivable for three reasons already given, suchness's own profundity, the mind's own freedom once genuine mastery is reached, and the very attainment of the undefiled realm through non-discriminating wisdom. And speculation about the Buddhas' own domain adds two further reasons still, that domain being entirely unobstructed, and that domain being precisely what accomplishes whatever task each being actually needs, closing the entire gateway of the inconceivable in full.

31. Section: Sixteen Intentions Behind Everything the Buddha Ever Said

A sixth and final gateway opens, intention, unfolding into sixteen distinct kinds: the intention to show, the intention to inspire aversion, the intention to exhort, the intention to praise and encourage, the intention to rejoice, the intention to bring in, the intention to cut off doubt, the intention to bring to maturity, the intention concerning concentration, the intention concerning liberation, the intention of special meaning, the intention to produce faultless joy in those who realize the teaching, the intention to produce respect in listeners toward the teacher, the intention that the Dharma eye continually turns, the intention to cultivate abundant wholesome conduct, and the intention to subdue every clung-to characteristic. The intention to show reveals the four kinds of genuine truth in order to help a student learn wholesome conduct. The intention to inspire aversion condemns six classes of dark, unwholesome conduct rooted in craving, conduct that breeds contention, suffering, harm, calamity, consuming inner torment, and further craving still, precisely in order to help a being turn away from desire itself. The intention to exhort settles those who already trust the teaching into its actual training. The intention to praise and encourage steadies anyone whose heart grows discouraged partway through study, practice, or attainment. The intention to rejoice openly celebrates anyone practicing with genuine vigor toward nirvana and the thirty-seven factors of awakening. And the intention to bring in draws a being who remains neutral toward the teaching, neither hostile nor yet convinced, into genuine, joyful confidence in it at last.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sixteenfold catalogue of intention as this treatise's own final and most human gateway of the six, since it asks not what a teaching means or how it should be classified, but why the Buddha actually chose to say it, addressing a discouraged student differently from a hostile skeptic, and a devoted practitioner differently again from one who has not yet decided what to believe at all. 32. Section: The Buddha Confesses He Cannot Cut Anyone's Doubt but Their Own

The intention to cut off doubt resolves genuine uncertainty through direct and reasoned teaching, illustrated by an exchange in which a non-Buddhist questioner suggested to the Buddha that, since he was all-powerful, beings need only rely on him entirely rather than undertake the hard work of practice themselves, and the Buddha replied plainly that he holds no power whatsoever to simply cut through another's doubt on their behalf, that each being must see the noble truth directly for themselves in order to actually cross the raging torrent of birth and death. The intention to bring to maturity ripens the faculties of one already inside the teaching, carrying them from the first fruit all the way to arhatship itself. The intention concerning concentration teaches the very methods and virtues of meditative absorption a practitioner needs in order to cultivate insight upon a genuinely settled foundation. The intention concerning liberation carries one whose faculties have already ripened onward to arhatship or to unsurpassed awakening, the complete and final release from saṃsāra's own turning. And the intention of special meaning conceals a deeper significance beneath its own ordinary words, exactly as the Compendium of the Great Vehicle declares, that only one who has already served buddhas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges river can actually understand the meaning of the Great Vehicle's own teaching, a statement Vasubandhu himself explains as pointing not to any merely verbal comprehension available to any ordinary person at all, but to genuine realization of the three natures themselves, achieved only through countless past lives spent in the actual presence of buddhas.

He treats Vasubandhu's own gloss on the Ganges-sands statement as this section's own clearest illustration of special meaning itself, a sentence that sounds, on its surface, like an impossibly high bar for even beginning to study the Great Vehicle, yet actually points toward the depth of genuine realization required for true understanding rather than functioning as a literal precondition barring ordinary students from ever starting at all.

33. Section: All Dharmas Without Self-Nature — The Intentions Come to Their Close

The remaining intentions complete this sixteenfold catalogue. The intention to produce faultless joy names the genuine, blameless delight a practitioner feels upon actually realizing the teaching directly. The intention to produce respect names the natural reverence a listener comes to feel toward the very teacher whose words led them to understand the Dharma's own excellent meaning. The intention that the Dharma eye continually turns names the Buddha's own teaching as the very eye guiding beings across the three kinds of listener already discussed, the sightless, the one-eyed, and the genuinely two-eyed, toward nirvana's own far shore without interruption. The intention to cultivate abundant wholesome conduct names the Buddha's constant encouragement to do good and refrain from evil, urged throughout the entire body of his teaching. And the intention to subdue every clung-to characteristic, the sixteenth and final kind, is illustrated directly by the Buddha's own declaration that all phenomena are without any fixed nature of their own, neither arising nor ceasing, originally and always still, their own true nature already nirvana itself, a teaching belonging especially to the signless Great Vehicle tradition associated with the Middle Way schools, aimed at breaking every characteristic a being still clings to and thereby opening the way to realizing reality exactly as it is. With all sixteen intentions now explained, the entire sixfold framework of true meaning, realization, teaching, avoiding the two extremes, the inconceivable, and intention, first opened many pages earlier, reaches its own complete close.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own closing declaration, that all phenomena are without self-nature and originally already nirvana, as this entire sixteen-intention catalogue's own most fitting final word, since subduing every clung-to characteristic is precisely what every other intention, showing, exhorting, encouraging, maturing, and the rest, has been quietly working toward all along. He calls the completion of this sixfold gateway framework, spanning true meaning down through intention, one of this fascicle's own genuine landmarks, an entire systematic method for reading Buddhist scripture correctly now fully in place before the treatise turns to explore how these same six gateways interlock with one another in even finer detail.

34. Section: Four Foundations of Mindfulness Answer Four Ancient Errors

A further discussion now opens, explaining precisely why true meaning must be discerned through avoiding the two extremes, beginning with the extreme of exaggeration as it applies to the four noble truths themselves, unfolding into four mistaken beliefs: the extreme of exaggerating a self, the extreme of exaggerating permanence, the extreme of exaggerating purity, and the extreme of exaggerating pleasure, the very four inverted views ordinary beings habitually fall into. Against these four the Buddha established the four foundations of mindfulness directly: contemplating the body as impure counters the inverted belief that finds purity where none exists; contemplating feeling as suffering counters the inverted belief that finds pleasure where only suffering exists; contemplating mind as impermanent counters the inverted belief that finds permanence in what constantly arises and perishes; and contemplating phenomena as without self counters the inverted belief that finds a fixed self where none can be found, cultivating these four foundations together yielding the four kinds of settled wisdom that directly know each of the four truths in turn. The exaggeration of self is then examined at length: no genuine self can be found apart from the five aggregates, since arising, characteristic, and function are found only within the aggregates themselves, extensively refuted through several classical arguments, that if a self caused the aggregates to arise the way a seed causes a sprout, that self would have to be impermanent just as a seed is; that if a self shaped experience the way a potter shapes clay, that self would likewise have to be impermanent; and that if a self merely contained the aggregates the way space contains every object within it, a self of this kind could still perform none of the actual functions falsely attributed to it, exactly as a sickle alone cannot cut without a hand to wield it, and fire alone can just as easily arise from mere friction and dry conditions without needing any separate agent lighting it at all.

Chinese Source

Because of this, every view of a self is simply the false exaggeration of self.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise one-to-one matching of the four inversions to the four foundations of mindfulness, impurity countering the belief in purity, suffering countering the belief in pleasure, impermanence countering the belief in permanence, and non-self countering the belief in self, as this passage's own elegant demonstration of exactly why the avoiding-extremes gateway is necessary to properly discern true meaning at all, each foundation of mindfulness functioning as a precisely targeted antidote rather than a generic meditative exercise. He highlights the sickle and fire similes refuting a self that merely serves as the instrument of action as this section's own most vivid argument, ordinary function requiring only the actual causes and conditions already present, with no separate, controlling self needed to explain anything that actually happens.

35. Section: Denying the Truths Denies Direct Sight Itself, and What Craving Actually Is

The opposite extreme, diminishment, is explained just as directly: denying the four noble truths outright, treating what has actually been established as though it possessed no reality at all, amounts to slandering all three genuine sources of knowledge at once, direct perception, since suffering and its origin can actually be observed, inference, since suffering's own cause can be reasoned to and its cessation reasoned toward through diligent cultivation of the path, and scriptural authority, and slanders the entire causal structure of defilement and purity alike, since the truths of suffering and origination form the defiled cause and effect while cessation and the path form the pure cause and effect. Only by avoiding both this diminishment and the opposite exaggeration already described can a practitioner actually gain genuine confidence in the four truths, come to a decisive understanding of them, and gradually realize complete and final purity, confirming exactly why avoiding the two extremes is necessary to properly discern true meaning at all. A far more detailed exposition of the four truths themselves then opens. The truth of suffering is explained first: birth and all its accompanying miseries already described at length, or, stated briefly, every single event belonging to the very defilement of rebirth itself. The truth of origination is explained next: both affliction's own defilement and karma's own defilement together, craving singled out by the Buddha himself as the truly supreme cause among every affliction, for two decisive reasons, that craving alone underlies both the wish for future existence and the wish to be free of it, and that craving alone has the universal power to give rise to still further craving once it has already arisen.

He highlights craving's own two-sided power, underlying both the wish for continued existence and the wish to escape it, as this passage's own precise explanation of why craving alone, among every affliction, earns the specific designation of origination's own supreme cause, wanting and not-wanting turning out to be two expressions of the very same underlying grasping.

36. Section: Eight Kinds of Stillness, and the Eight Supports That Keep Ordinary Life Turning

The truth of cessation is explained next as the permanent severing of every affliction, unfolding across eight aspects. Nirvana with residue possesses four kinds of stillness: the stillness of suffering, since no further rebirth into the three realms will ever occur again; the stillness of affliction, greed, hatred, and delusion entirely and permanently ended; the stillness of no longer harming any being, since without affliction only wholesome conduct remains possible; and the stillness of equanimity, the mind resting always in the most supreme, unattached equanimity, also called the six constant feelings. Nirvana without residue possesses four further kinds of stillness, reached once even the body itself is finally relinquished: the stillness beyond all number and expression, a state simply too complete to be measured or spoken; the stillness of every support, since the eight supports ordinary life actually depends upon, the support of mere designation, providing names like self and being for what is truly just the five aggregates; the support of embracing, family, servants, friends, property, and worldly enterprise; the support of sustenance, the four foods that keep the body itself alive; the support of continued flux, the twelve links of dependent origination that keep the wheel of rebirth turning; the support of obstruction, the very demons of the aggregates, affliction, the heavens, and death itself; the support of suffering, every kind of grief and misery found across the three realms; the support of pleasure, attachment to the very bliss of meditative absorption; and the support of what comes at the very end, the wish for continued existence still further ahead, are all finally left behind entirely; the stillness of suffering tied to any remaining support, since no bodily or mental suffering whatsoever remains once no support at all is left to suffer through; and the stillness of doubt arising from suffering tied to any remaining support, every uncertain question about whether even a fully liberated being might still somehow suffer now entirely resolved, this specific state, craving exhausted and freedom from desire complete, marking nirvana with residue, and utter, final extinguishing marking nirvana without residue.

He highlights the eight supports ordinary life depends upon, from mere naming through embracing, sustenance, continued flux, obstruction, suffering, pleasure, and the wish for still further existence, as a genuinely comprehensive inventory of everything a being clings to without ever examining, each one entirely absent, the text insists, once nirvana without residue is actually reached.

The Buddha himself, addressing what is supreme among these, singled out the noble eightfold path in particular, precisely because it is this eightfold path above all else that actually embraces and brings about the realization of every śramaṇa fruit a practitioner could hope to attain.

38. Section: A Śrāvaka's Own Threefold Path, from Gathering Requisites to Final Purity

The path of the śrāvaka vehicle unfolds across its own three stages in turn. The path of requisites gathers the fourteen conditions already treated at length in the Grounds of Śrāvakas, one's own personal perfection and the perfection of one's surroundings, genuine aspiration toward the Dharma, precepts and restraint of the senses, moderation in eating, a disciplined practice of wakefulness maintained through the earlier, middle, and later parts of the night alike, clear knowing in every posture, genuine spiritual friendship, hearing and reflecting on the true Dharma, freedom from every obstacle, generosity free of any expectation of return, and the seventeen further qualities that together constitute a genuine renunciant's own adornment. The preparatory path, addressing what is supreme among its own stages, names the very factors of awakening cultivated across the four stages of heat, summit, patience, and supreme worldly dharma, the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest. And the path of purification names every one of the thirty-seven factors of awakening as actually realized across the path of seeing, the path of cultivation, and the ultimate path, together with every further excellent quality this purification draws forth in turn, miraculous powers, the absorption free of contention, the absorption of aspirational wisdom, and unhindered understanding among them, this entire threefold structure completing the śrāvaka vehicle's own account of the path.

39. Section: The Bodhisattva's Own Path, and Why Suffering Is Always Named First

The bodhisattva vehicle's own path unfolds more simply: the preparatory path is embraced entirely within the six perfections, giving, precepts, patience, diligence, meditative absorption, and wisdom, while the path of purification is embraced entirely within the perfection of wisdom itself, the very insight into the twofold emptiness of self and phenomena. This is stated in terms of what is supreme for the bodhisattva path specifically, since a bodhisattva in fact cultivates every one of the thirty-seven factors of awakening a śrāvaka cultivates as well, and more besides, the two vehicles differing not in what the more restricted path omits but in how much further the wider path actually extends. The truths of suffering and origination are further shown to divide into three kinds each, according to whether they are bound to the desire realm, the form realm, or the formless realm, extending in truth to a genuinely boundless number once the innumerable worlds throughout the ten directions are taken into account, and the truths of cessation and the path divide in exactly the same threefold and boundless way, since they exist specifically to counter suffering and origination in each of these same three realms. The very sequence in which the four truths are taught is explained last: suffering is named first and origination second, together comprising defilement's own complete account, precisely because ordinary beings first wish to understand their own suffering before wishing to understand its cause; cessation is named third and the path fourth, together comprising purity's own complete account, precisely because beings who then wish for genuine happiness must next learn the path that actually leads to it, this consistent movement from result to cause in both pairs reflecting the Buddha's own skillful attention to how an ordinary listener's mind actually works.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the clarification that a bodhisattva also cultivates every factor of awakening a śrāvaka does, simply stated here in terms of what is uniquely supreme to the bodhisattva path, as an important corrective against imagining the two vehicles as entirely separate tracks sharing nothing in common, when in fact the bodhisattva path fully contains the śrāvaka path while extending considerably further still. He highlights the explanation for why suffering and cessation are each named before their own respective causes, matching the Buddha's teaching to how an ordinary mind naturally moves from noticing a result to asking after its cause, as a small but telling instance of this treatise's own recurring insistence that even the order in which truths are taught reflects real pedagogical care rather than arbitrary sequence.
