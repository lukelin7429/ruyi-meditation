Fascicle 19 · 瑜伽師地論卷第十九 · The Essential-Body Verses, Topic Fourteen Continues

What Makes Speech Genuinely Good

Fascicle 19 continues directly from fascicle 18's stopping point, mid-way through the fourteenth and largest of the essential-body-verse topics — "discourse," twenty-seven sub-parts in all. It opens with the sixth sub-part: what separates a noble one's speech from ordinary speech, and why the wise are said to speak only what is pleasing, undefiling, and wholly good.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 19 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Topic Fourteen, Sub-Part 6: What Makes Speech Genuinely Good

Continuing directly from fascicle 18, the fourteenth topic's sixth sub-part names three qualities that distinguish a noble one's speech from ordinary speech — pleasing, undefiling, and wholly good — then examines a subtlety: these three do not always travel together, and speech genuinely worth calling "good" needs the third far more than the other two.

Chinese Source

賢聖嘗說最善語　愛非不愛語第二
諦非不諦語第三　法非非法語第四

Ru-Yi Translation

The noble ones always speak the most excellent speech: pleasing, not displeasing, first; true, not false, second; in accord with the Dharma, not against it, third.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the three qualities as pleasing speech that genuinely delights the hearer, undefiling speech that is simply true, and wholly good speech that actually leads someone out of an unwholesome state and into a wholesome one — and stresses that only the last of the three is indispensable, since the other two can each occur without it. Flattery that praises a nonexistent virtue is pleasing but neither true nor good, since it only reinforces the hearer's vanity. A harsh rebuke driven by real anger can be entirely true — the fault really was committed — yet be neither pleasing nor good, since it lands as an attack rather than genuine correction, even if the underlying facts are accurate; he notes this differs from a parent's stern correction of a child, which despite its harsh tone is still genuinely aimed at the child's good and so counts as both true and good speech at once. And Dharma-teaching that neither praises nor blames anyone in particular, simply presenting the teaching itself, can be wholly good and genuinely lead someone toward liberation without being calculated to please or aimed at correcting any specific fault.

He illustrates the real-world cost of speaking well without judging the occasion with the well-known story of the monk Mahālla, who begged the wiser Śāriputra to teach him a beautiful verse of blessing after watching it earn Śāriputra two fine blankets from a delighted patron celebrating a run of good fortune — only to recite that same verse, word for word, to the same household on a later visit when tragedy had struck them instead, and to be beaten and driven off for it; his blind attempts afterward to patch the mistake by parroting whatever phrase he'd last been scolded with (offered indiscriminately to a funeral procession, then a wedding party, then a hunter, then a laundress) earned him beating after beating until the Buddha finally explained, gently, that genuine blessing-speech requires reading the occasion correctly, not simply reciting fine words learned by rote. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it is that goodness in speech is inseparable from timing and context — the same words can bless or wound depending entirely on whether they actually fit the moment and the listener in front of you.

2. Sub-Part 7: The Fruit of Genuine Practice, for Renunciant and Householder Alike

The seventh sub-part names five qualities that make up genuine practice — faith, a sense of shame, precepts, generosity, and, for the ordained, the Dharma itself — and the three benefits that follow from them, tracing the path slightly differently for monastics and for laypeople.

Chinese Source

信慚戒施法　善人所稱讚　是名趣天道　能往天世間

Ru-Yi Translation

Faith, a sense of shame, precepts, generosity, and the Dharma — this is what the good praise, this is called the path to the heavens, and by this one is able to go to the heavenly worlds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the five qualities as they unfold for a monastic: genuine confidence in the Buddha's own realized teaching; a sense of shame at remaining a householder at all, since household life is saturated with desire in a way that blocks even the first step toward freedom; taking and keeping the precepts, then living simply and contentedly on whatever robes, food, and bedding come one's way, without demanding more; practicing generosity even as a renunciant, by never privately hoarding any properly acquired offering but sharing it with fellow practitioners; and immersing oneself in the Dharma itself — receiving it through hearing, working it through by reflection, and finally penetrating it through cultivation, then passing it on to others out of genuine compassion. He explains the three resulting benefits: being praised by the Buddhas and their genuinely awakened disciples, since only those who have themselves seen the truth are qualified to recognize it in another; being carried, once the accompanying merit and wisdom are sufficiently complete, toward rebirth among the pure abodes reserved for non-returners; and, even where that fullness isn't yet reached, being guaranteed rebirth in a fortunate realm regardless, since a stream-enterer or once-returner can no longer fall to the three lower realms at all.

For laypeople the same five qualities take a lay shape — faith in the teaching, shame at breaking precepts or clinging to wealth once one understands where such conduct leads, taking whatever precepts fit one's circumstances, and simple generosity — with the Dharma-study and teaching-others piece typically less developed, so the promised fruit is correspondingly the praise of the wise and heavenly rebirth, without the specific promise of the pure abodes reserved for advanced monastic practice. He illustrates lay devotion's power with the well-known story of a servant named Guṇamati, executed by the tyrant king Ajātaśatru simply for cleaning and lighting incense at a neglected memorial stūpa built by his imprisoned father in the Buddha's honor — who was reborn instantly among the gods for that single act of devotion, and upon hearing the Buddha teach in her new heavenly life, immediately attained stream-entry. A believing heart, he notes, is available to anyone regardless of ordination status, and its fruit is no less real for that.

3. Sub-Part 8: How Extensive Learning Leads All the Way to Nirvāṇa

The eighth sub-part traces genuine learning across four stages, from simply knowing the teaching through to nirvāṇa itself — the first two belonging to the mundane path shared by anyone seeking a favorable rebirth, the last two belonging to the supramundane path that actually ends saṃsāra.

Chinese Source

多聞能知法　多聞能遠惡　多聞捨無義　多聞得涅槃

Ru-Yi Translation

Through extensive learning one knows the teaching; through extensive learning one keeps far from evil; through extensive learning one relinquishes what is meaningless; through extensive learning one attains nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first two lines as the mundane path: hearing and genuinely understanding teaching on generosity, precepts, and the causes of favorable rebirth is "knowing the teaching," while actually living by that understanding — steering clear of the unwholesome actions that lead to misfortune and cultivating the ones that lead to a good rebirth — is "keeping far from evil." He stresses that real learning always completes itself in changed conduct; hearing without living accordingly is only information, not the multiple learning the verse has in mind.

The remaining two lines then move to the supramundane path. "Relinquishing what is meaningless" is hearing and genuinely grasping the Four Noble Truths and dependent origination well enough to see that saṃsāra's endless round of rebirth, however elaborated, is ultimately without lasting value compared to nirvāṇa's peace — a shift from merely accumulating merit within saṃsāra to actually wanting out of it. "Attaining nirvāṇa" then plays out two different ways depending on how ripe a practitioner's faculties already are: someone whose faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom are already mature, with the necessary merit and wisdom fully accumulated, can move directly from hearing the teaching into direct realization of the truths, or, if already a stream-enterer, straight through to full liberation; someone whose faculties are not yet mature simply keeps steadily avoiding unwholesome action, building concentration on the foundation of precepts, and building wisdom on the foundation of concentration, trusting — the way an object released from a height is bound to fall — that sustained practice on this path leads to nirvāṇa exactly as surely, even without knowing in advance how long the road will be.

He illustrates the transformative power of hearing the Dharma even from someone hostile to it with the well-known story of a bitter, faithless housewife in Kauśāmbī who slammed the door on a begging monk with contemptuous abuse, only to watch him instantly manifest as a bloated, rotting corpse before her eyes — and, fleeing to fetch her husband, found the "corpse" following her, whole and unharmed, the moment she looked back. When her husband arrived with a blade to deal with the monk, the monk conjured a crystal house no weapon could breach, then asked him to set down not the sword in his hand but the sword still in his heart — a request so uncannily accurate that the man's hostility collapsed into genuine remorse on the spot, and both husband and wife, hearing the Dharma the monk then taught them, attained stream-entry within that same encounter. Master Cháng Bǎi's point is that this same power — described afterward by the Buddha simply as tireless, disciplined, unflagging learning joined to unflagging practice — is available to anyone who commits to it the same way, not a special gift reserved for the already-devout.

4. Sub-Part 9: Unstained as Space, Unshaken as Indra's Banner

The ninth sub-part paints the freed mind in a single, memorable image: untouched by anything the way space itself cannot be stained, unshaken the way the banner of the god Indra never falls regardless of which way the wind blows, and finding no pleasure at all in returning to the "muddy heat" of saṃsāra once it has tasted the cool, clear pool of genuine release.

Chinese Source

智者如空無染汙　不動猶如天帝幢
如泛清涼盈滿池　不樂淤泥生死海

Ru-Yi Translation

The wise one is like space, unstained; unshaken, like the banner of the god of the devas; drifting as though on a cool, brimming pool, taking no pleasure in the muddy sea of birth-and-death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the four images across three complementary lenses. Taken as a portrait of full arhatship alone, the first line describes a mind wholly beyond the eight worldly winds — gain and loss, praise and blame, fame and disgrace, pleasure and pain — untouched the way space itself cannot be dirtied by anything thrown into it; the middle two lines describe a non-returner still advancing toward arhatship, whose mind, settled in the signless concentration through sustained mindfulness practice, can no longer be shaken by self-conceit, by any of those same eight worldly winds, or by any residual longing for desire-realm rebirth — as stable as Indra's own banner, which legend held could never be blown down; and the closing lines describe that same non-returner's own inner motivation, still willing to relinquish even the deep, refined pleasure of form- and formless-realm meditative absorption — itself compared here to inviting but ultimately stifling "hot mud" — for the truly cool, open freedom of complete liberation.

Taken instead as one continuous portrait of the arhat alone, he explains, the same four images simply describe three facets of a single life: undefiled in the ordinary business of eating, speaking, and traveling about, since none of it is grasped at or clung to; unshaken because completely without attachment to any mental image at all; and taking no pleasure in continued existence, since even a body sustained by residual past karma remains, in the end, simply one more thing to be tired of. And taken as a threefold typology of arhats themselves, the four images distinguish the arhat liberated by wisdom alone (undefiled, but not yet master of the deepest absorptions), the advanced trainee who has personally verified the deepest absorption in the body though not yet full liberation (unshaken), and the arhat doubly liberated in both wisdom and concentration, master of cessation-attainment itself (taking no pleasure in continued existence at all) — three real, well-attested profiles of accomplished practice, not competing interpretations so much as three angles on what full freedom from desire actually looks like in practice.

5. Sub-Part 10: Seeing the Buddha's Form Is Not the Same as Knowing the Buddha

The tenth sub-part echoes what would later become one of the best-known verses in the entire Buddhist canon, from the Diamond Sūtra: seeing the Buddha's physical form, or hearing his voice, is not the same as truly knowing him. Five verses lay out four ways of failing to know the Buddha and one way of genuinely succeeding.

Chinese Source

若以色量我　以音聲尋我　欲貪所執持　彼不能知我
若於內了知　於外不能見　由內果觀察　彼音聲所引
若於內無知　於外而能見　由外果觀察　亦音聲所引
若於內無知　於外不能見　彼普障愚夫　亦音聲所引
若於內了知　於外亦能見　英雄出離慧　非音聲所引

Ru-Yi Translation

If one measures me by form, or seeks me by sound — held fast by desire and craving, such a one cannot know me. If one knows within but cannot see without, seeing only through the fruit of inner meditation, such a one is still led by mere sound. If one has no knowledge within yet can see without, seeing only through the fruit of outer sense, such a one is still led by mere sound. If one has no knowledge within and cannot see without, wholly obstructed and confused, such a one too is led by mere sound. But if one knows within and also sees without — the hero possessed of the wisdom of renunciation — such a one is not led by mere sound.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi works through four ways ordinary beings mistake seeing the Buddha for knowing him. The first simply reasons from outward signs — the thirty-two marks of a great being, eloquent teaching, evidently virtuous followers — to the conclusion "this must be a buddha," without any inner realization at all; lacking that realization, such a person remains fully vulnerable to bad company and wrong teaching, and history shows this type does go on to slander the Three Jewels when a persuasive enough false teacher comes along. The second has cultivated the four absorptions and gained the divine eye, so can see the Buddha directly across great distances in meditation — a real attainment, but one still unaccompanied by liberating wisdom, and so just as vulnerable to being talked out of right view by a plausible outsider. The third has only the ordinary physical eye, perhaps living during the Buddha's own lifetime and lucky enough to see him directly — vivid, but equally unprotected without wisdom. The fourth has neither: no meditative sight, no physical sight, whether from blindness or simply from being born after the Buddha's final passing — the most thoroughly obstructed case of all, left with nothing but secondhand report to build any confidence on.

Only the fifth type — a genuine noble one, from the preparatory stages through full realization, whose desire has actually been abandoned and uprooted rather than merely suppressed — truly knows the Buddha, and Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to explain exactly what that means: inwardly, through meditation joined with insight, actually realizing the Buddha's dharma-body — that is, awakening to the same dependent-arising, empty-of-self-nature reality the Buddha himself awakened to, since a buddha's deepest identity was never the physical body at all but this realization itself; and outwardly, still perfectly able to look at a Buddha statue, a stūpa, or a painted image and correctly recognize it as a representation rather than the ultimate reality it points to, without either dismissing it or mistaking the pointer for the moon. Such a person's confidence in the Three Jewels can never be talked away by any outside voice, because it was never secondhand to begin with. He closes with the well-known story of the nun Utpalavarṇā, who used her psychic power to transform into a wheel-turning king so she could push to the very front of the crowd welcoming the Buddha back from teaching his mother in the heavens — only to be told, once she reverted to her own form and bowed, that she had not actually been first to see him at all: the elder Subhūti, who had stayed behind in his cave simply contemplating emptiness rather than joining the crowd, had genuinely seen the Buddha first, precisely because seeing dependent origination itself is what seeing the Buddha actually means.

6. Sub-Part 11: Even Attachment to Meditation Itself Is Still Defilement

The eleventh sub-part turns to a subtler trap than ordinary sense-desire: a practitioner who has genuinely mastered the five sense-based cravings but still clings, however refined the object, to the mind's own experience of concentration — the sixth and most powerful of the six sense-bases, still capable of its own defilement.

Chinese Source

第六增上王　染時染自取　於無染不染　染者名愚夫

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth is the sovereign — when defiled, it defiles by grasping for itself; where there is no defilement, nothing is defiled; whoever is defiled is called a fool.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the sixth" as mind itself — the sixth sense-base governing the other five, called "sovereign" because it is what actually directs and makes use of them. His focus falls on a specific and easily missed case: someone who has already crossed the "flood" of the five sense-desires, mastering attraction to sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and textures well enough to attain genuine meditative absorption, can still remain caught by a sixth, subtler flood — craving the very peace and pleasure of that absorption itself, treating the stillness of concentration as "mine" the same way an untrained mind treats a beautiful object as desirable. This, he stresses, is still craving in every meaningful sense, and it still plants the same seed for future suffering, since attachment even to the most refined states of form- and formless-realm concentration still binds a being to further rebirth within those realms — rebirth that, however pleasant by comparison to the lower realms, remains rebirth all the same.

He explains the antidote as the same fourfold mindfulness practice used everywhere else in the treatise, applied here specifically to the mind observing its own experience of concentration: recognizing craving for absorption as a genuine danger the moment it arises, refusing to let the mind simply follow that craving's pull, and repeatedly training the mind to rest in freedom from craving rather than in the craved object itself — at which point, once this defilement is genuinely uprooted rather than merely suppressed, no further defiled result follows either, since a cause that no longer arises produces no further fruit. Someone who instead simply follows wherever this subtle craving leads, without either restraining it or applying any deliberate remedy, remains — whatever their level of meditative accomplishment — still bound to suffering by that same craving, and the treatise does not hesitate to call such a person, however accomplished their concentration, simply a fool. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this is in some ways a harder trap to escape than ordinary sense-desire, since a meditator confident in their own attainment is often the least willing to hear correction from anyone whose concentration is less advanced than their own — precisely the stubbornness that keeps this particular defilement so persistent.

7. Sub-Part 12: The Fortress of Bone That Locks Wholesome Roots Out

The twelfth sub-part pictures the mind as a fortress — walled with bone, bound with sinew, plastered with flesh — held under siege from within by four defilements: craving and aversion, which trap the householder still absorbed in worldly desire, and conceit and concealment, which trap even someone who has gone forth, if that going-forth was into a mistaken teaching.

Chinese Source

有城骨為牆　筋肉而塗飾　其中有貪恚　慢覆所任持

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a city walled with bone, plastered over with sinew and flesh — and within it, craving and aversion, conceit and concealment, hold their ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the "city" as the mind itself — the eighth consciousness that gathers experience, the seventh that constantly grasps at it as "self," and the six that actively perceive — housed within a body built, quite literally, from bone as its stonework, sinew as its binding cord, and flesh as its plaster. He then explains why four particular defilements are named as the city's occupying forces rather than any others: craving and aversion belong to the ordinary householder still absorbed in sense-pleasure, who clings to whatever is pleasing and resents whatever is not, remaining thereby thoroughly unreceptive to teaching that would ask them to give any of it up. Conceit and concealment belong instead to someone who has gone forth — but into a mistaken teaching, so that whatever confidence or apparent attainment they gain becomes simply one more reason for pride, and whatever fault they commit gets hidden away rather than honestly disclosed, precisely the two habits that make correction from any wiser source impossible to receive even when it is freely offered out of compassion.

He extends the warning to Buddhist practitioners themselves, noting this danger is not confined to outsiders: a monastic who gains a little meditative stability, or simply arrives already carrying unexamined convictions from an earlier life or an earlier teacher, can develop exactly the same conceit and concealment, becoming just as closed to correction as any outsider — the city's walls hold just as firmly whether the fortress belongs to a non-Buddhist ascetic or to someone technically ordained within the Buddha's own teaching. He closes with a well-known verse comparing genuine practice to guarding a walled city: watching for the six sense-thieves by day, staying alert through the night as well, so that when the mind's own general — clear awareness — is truly in command, no weapons are even needed to keep the peace, since a thief who finds no opening simply has nothing to steal.

8. Sub-Part 13: Drawing In Like a Tortoise, Rising Through the Absorptions

The thirteenth sub-part offers one of this fascicle's most vivid images: a practitioner advancing through the absorptions by drawing the mind in exactly as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, growing progressively less disturbed and more genuinely peaceable to live alongside — the sign of real, not merely apparent, progress.

Chinese Source

如龜藏支於自殼　苾芻善攝意尋思
無所依止不惱他　證般涅槃無所謗

Ru-Yi Translation

As a tortoise draws its limbs into its own shell, the monk skillfully draws in the mind's own rumination — relying on nothing, troubling no one — and realizes final nirvāṇa, beyond all reproach.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the tortoise image through the successive absorptions. Attaining the first absorption means abandoning the three unwholesome kinds of rumination — over desire, over aversion, over harm — the way a tortoise pulls all six limbs inside its shell where no predator can reach them; the mind, similarly drawn in from the six sense-objects, becomes correspondingly hard to disturb. But he stresses a practitioner does not stop there: even the first absorption's own wholesome forms of applied and sustained thought are, relatively speaking, still an agitation worth relinquishing, so the same withdrawing motion repeats — releasing the first absorption's rumination to reach the second, then its joy to reach the third, then its pleasure to reach the fourth, and on upward through each of the formless spheres to the very peak of conditioned existence. Progress through the entire ladder, in other words, is really one repeated gesture of letting go rather than a series of unrelated achievements.

The verse's third phrase, "relying on nothing, troubling no one," describes what genuine progress looks like from the outside: a practitioner who does not become attached to any level of concentration attained, remains easy to live alongside, gentle enough to draw others toward wanting to practice near them, and free of the anger that would otherwise make daily community life a source of constant friction. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this social ease is itself a diagnostic — someone whose meditative attainment leaves them harder to get along with, more irritable, or more inclined to look down on others has misunderstood what the practice is actually for, since real freedom from craving naturally expresses itself as freedom from ill will as well. Carried all the way through, this same letting-go finally issues in nirvāṇa itself — a conclusion so thoroughly earned, he notes, that it leaves no honest ground for anyone, of any persuasion, to find fault with it.

He closes the sub-part by naming the six qualities (or, bundled differently, two) that make such a practitioner genuinely easy to live alongside — goodwill in body, speech, and mind; sharing material things fairly; and holding the same precepts and the same view as one's companions in practice — and by explaining that this same freedom is what finally makes someone immune to distorting the teaching in either direction: neither slandering what is genuinely wholesome as worthless nor excusing what is genuinely harmful as harmless, since a mind no longer clouded by craving has no motive left to bend the truth either way. He summarizes the whole sub-part as answering four sources of disturbance in turn — unwholesome and even wholesome rumination, both settled through the tortoise-like withdrawal into deepening absorption; attachment to the pleasure of concentration itself, settled through relying on nothing; friction with fellow practitioners, settled through the six qualities of genuine harmony; and distortion of the teaching by wrong view, settled through the clear seeing that makes such distortion simply impossible once the truths have actually been realized.

9. Sub-Part 14: Why the Buddha Chose to Relinquish His Own Remaining Years

The fourteenth sub-part explains one of the most personal moments in the Buddha's own biography: his deliberate choice to enter final nirvāṇa at eighty rather than living out a fuller natural lifespan, comparing the act to a chick finally breaking free of the shell that once sustained it.

Chinese Source

等不等而生　牟尼捨有行　內樂定差別　如俱舍卵生

Ru-Yi Translation

Equal, yet unequal, in what arose — the sage relinquished the formations of existence. Through the distinction of inner joy and concentration, it was like a chick breaking from its shell.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the verse's opening puzzle — "equal, yet unequal" — as comparing the Buddha's physical body before and after his enlightenment (unchanged, still marked by the same thirty-two major and eighty minor auspicious marks, hence "equal") against his inner realization (utterly transformed by the attainment of complete, unobstructed wisdom, hence "unequal"). Only the mind changes through practice, he stresses; the body carries on exactly as before. Grounded in that same inner mastery — the "inner joy" of meditative stillness joined with the "sage's joy" of realized wisdom — the Buddha possessed genuine freedom over the length of his own life, able to extend it or relinquish it as he judged best, rather than being simply carried along by the karmic momentum that determines an ordinary being's lifespan.

He recounts the well-known episode behind this choice: though the Buddha could, by tradition, have lived a full hundred years, he chose to enter final nirvāṇa at eighty, explicitly leaving the remaining share of merit-based longevity as a kind of inheritance sustaining his later disciples' ability to practice without want. When the Buddha asked Ānanda three times whether he wished him to remain in the world longer, Ānanda — still only a stream-enterer at the time, his resolve clouded — failed to answer, and only realized what had been lost once the decision was already made, weeping and asking why the Buddha could not simply stay. Even then, the Buddha's freedom over his own lifespan showed itself once more: informed that the wandering ascetic Subhadra, on the verge of real breakthrough after a lifetime of near-miss meditative attainment, still needed guidance, the Buddha extended his life by three months specifically to teach him the eightfold path — Subhadra reached arhatship almost the instant he heard it, becoming the Buddha's very last direct disciple, and the Buddha entered parinirvāṇa immediately after.

Master Cháng Bǎi closes with the verse's own image: just as a chick, having grown to fullness inside its shell, finally breaks free of what had until then sustained and protected it, the Buddha's relinquishing of his remaining lifespan was not a loss but the natural completion of a process — passing from the "shell" of a physical existence that had done its work into the boundless freedom of nirvāṇa without any remaining trace, a release made possible only by the very mastery ordinary craving for continued existence can never grant.

10. Sub-Part 15: Mud, Demons, Nets, and Rivers — Four Images for Four Afflictions

The fifteenth sub-part reaches for four vivid, ordinary images of helplessness — sinking in mud, possession by a hostile spirit, entanglement in a net, being swept along a fast-flowing river — to describe exactly how craving, aversion, delusion, and attachment each strip a being of freedom in their own distinct way.

Chinese Source

無淤泥等欲　無魑魅等瞋　無羅網等癡　無江河等愛

Ru-Yi Translation

Let there be no craving like sinking mud; no aversion like a hostile spirit; no delusion like an entangling net; no attachment like a rushing river.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each image as a distinct portrait of unfreedom. Someone still absorbed in ordinary desire-realm craving, mired in the genuinely unclean, foul-smelling reality of sensual indulgence once seen honestly rather than through the gloss of attraction, finds it simply impossible to bring forth new wholesome qualities or protect and grow the ones already present — exactly like a person sinking deeper into mud the more they struggle. Someone who has gone forth into the Buddha's own teaching but still carries a hostile, quick-tempered nature remains, despite outward robes, unable to keep the precepts consistently and prone to abusing fellow practitioners with harsh speech — hosting, in effect, the same violent, unpredictable spirit the image of demonic possession suggests. Someone who has instead gone forth into a mistaken teaching entirely, entangled in wrong view as thoroughly as a fish or bird caught in a net, continues circling through saṃsāra with no way out, since the net itself is the very framework through which they interpret their own experience. And someone who has reached the higher meditative realms of form or formlessness, suppressing but never actually uprooting the seeds of desire-realm craving, inevitably falls back to the lower realm once that suppression's temporary power runs out — carried helplessly downstream the way a river carries anything not anchored to solid ground, difficult to escape precisely because the undertow is invisible until it's already too late.

He then names what each affliction specifically blocks: craving blocks the very possibility of genuine renunciation, since a mind still absorbed in desire cannot make the decisive break household life requires; aversion blocks freedom from harming others, since a mind gripped by anger will injure even itself, let alone anyone else; delusion blocks honoring one's parents, since a being confused enough to deny basic cause and effect will just as readily deny any debt of gratitude toward the two people who gave them life; and attachment blocks genuine generosity, since someone swept along by craving cannot bring themselves to use their own resources for their own sake, let alone give any of it away. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in pairing each affliction with its specific casualty is practical rather than merely descriptive: a practitioner examining their own difficulty with any one of these four practices — renunciation, non-harming, filial respect, or generosity — can trace the blockage back to its specific root affliction, rather than treating the difficulty as some vague, undifferentiated failure of willpower.

11. Sub-Part 16: Only the Buddha Is Genuinely Free of Idle Speculation

The sixteenth sub-part surveys three basic kinds of seeking any unawakened being pursues, argues that all three — even the subtlest, mistaken for liberation itself — still amount to nothing more than idle speculation with no lasting foothold, and names the Buddha as the sole exception, having relinquished every form of seeking entirely.

Chinese Source

虛空無鳥跡　外道無沙門　愚夫樂戲論　如來則無有

Ru-Yi Translation

As space bears no trace of a bird's flight, so is there no true renunciant among non-Buddhist paths. Fools delight in idle speculation — but in the Tathāgata there is none.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names three kinds of seeking that between them cover every unawakened being's aspiration: seeking sensual pleasure, aimed at a better rebirth among the desire-realm's various heavens where the five pleasures can be enjoyed more fully; seeking a superior existence, aimed at the more refined embodiment and pleasure of the first through third form-realm absorptions; and seeking release itself, aimed at genuine liberation through the renunciant path — though he flags a crucial trap within this third category: someone who mistakes stillness in the four formless absorptions for actual liberation, without ever uprooting the underlying craving those absorptions only temporarily suppress, is practicing what the treatise calls a "mistaken" seeking of release, indistinguishable in its ultimate result from the first two.

He explains why "no true renunciant" applies so broadly: genuine renunciant status, in any of its four traditional grades from a still-training practitioner through a fully realized noble one, requires the noble eightfold path actually be established in the mind — something no non-Buddhist teaching, however rigorous its own austerities, ever manages, and something even a mistaken pursuit of the formless absorptions within the Buddhist fold fails to establish either, since taking mere stillness for liberation forecloses the path just as surely as any outsider's error does. All three kinds of seeking, he stresses, share the same fundamental shape: reaching for some particular realm of rebirth, and finding, once reached, that it offers no more lasting foothold than a bird finds in open sky — real enough while it lasts, but leaving no trace once the karma that produced it is exhausted, and never actually escaping the cycle of arising and passing that defines saṃsāra itself.

Only the Buddha, having relinquished every one of these three forms of seeking without exception, is genuinely free of what the verse calls "idle speculation" — not because he lacks understanding of any realm a being might seek, but because he holds no aspiration toward any of them at all, the aspiration itself being precisely what keeps ordinary seeking circling without ever landing anywhere solid. Master Cháng Bǎi extends the same freedom, by the same logic, to any of the Buddha's own disciples who pursue genuine liberation rather than any of the three forms of seeking still bound to rebirth — the crucial variable being not effort, which the mistaken meditator on formless stillness may have in abundance, but whether the aspiration itself has actually been relinquished at the root.

12. Sub-Part 17: The Genuine Sage, Whom Even the Gods Cannot Recognize

The seventeenth sub-part portrays the true arhat — free of every foothold consciousness clings to, past every wall and moat that keeps ordinary beings confined — and explains why such a person's inner freedom is invisible not just to confused human onlookers but even to the gods themselves.

Chinese Source

住戲論皆無　踰牆塹離愛　牟尼遊世間　天人不能識

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of every abode, free of all idle speculation, having crossed the wall and the moat, free of craving — the sage wanders the world, unrecognized by gods or men.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies four things ordinary beings remain bound to that the true sage has entirely relinquished: the "four abodes" of consciousness — the habitual clinging of mind to form, feeling, perception, and mental formation as somehow constituting a stable "me," which keeps rebirth cycling from one life to the next; idle speculation, meaning the wrong views (whether adding false permanence and purity to things that have none, or wrongly denying cause and effect altogether) that keep a being circling without ever landing on the truth; the "wall" of desire-realm craving that keeps beings confined to that lowest, most turbulent realm; and the "moat" of the subtler ignorance that keeps beings confined even within the more refined form and formless realms, unable to cross into genuine freedom despite how peaceful those realms may feel from the inside. Ordinary beings, he explains, remain under the control of what the tradition personifies as four kinds of demonic force — the five aggregates themselves, affliction, death, and the temptations of the highest desire-realm heaven — precisely because they never manage to cross any of these four barriers; the arhat alone has crossed all four completely, and moves through the world with a freedom no onlooker, however devout, can actually perceive.

He explains this invisibility through a double inversion: ordinary people feel ashamed on the arhat's behalf for begging alms rather than keeping their own family wealth, and for pursuing extinction in nirvāṇa rather than a pleasant rebirth in heaven — precisely backward, since it is clinging to wealth and craving further existence that genuinely deserve shame, not their opposites. The same inversion runs through fear: ordinary people find nothing frightening about circling endlessly through saṃsāra, while finding the prospect of nirvāṇa's freedom from self genuinely alarming — again exactly backward, since it is bondage to the four demonic forces that should terrify a thinking person, not release from them. He illustrates how thoroughly conditioned perception can mislead with the well-known story of the Korean monk Wŏnhyo, who drank gratefully from what he took, in pitch darkness, to be a cool spring during a night's journey toward Tang China to study the Dharma — only to discover at dawn that he had drunk from a skull filled with rainwater beside a decaying corpse, and was violently sickened the instant he knew it, though the water itself had not changed at all between the two moments. Realizing on the spot that everything is mind-made — the water was neither pure nor foul in itself, only his own perception made it so — he turned back without ever reaching China, having already found, in that single moment of insight, exactly what he had set out so far to find.

13. Sub-Part 18: Four Yokes That Only a Non-Returner Can Truly Cast Off

The eighteenth sub-part explains why identical meditative attainment — even reaching the very peak of conditioned existence — produces two entirely different outcomes depending on whether the meditator is an ordinary being or a genuine non-returner: one remains yoked and eventually falls back to the lower realms, the other is free for good.

Chinese Source

若有熏除諸尋思　於內無餘離分別
超過礙著諸色想　四軛蠲除不往生

Ru-Yi Translation

If one purges every rumination, becomes, within, entirely free of discursive thought, and passes beyond every obstruction of perceptions tied to form — the four yokes cast off, one is reborn there no more.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the verse onto the successive absorptions: purging defiled rumination over desire, aversion, and harm yields the first absorption; then purging even that absorption's own wholesome forms of rumination — since these remain, relatively speaking, an agitation — yields the second; then releasing the second absorption's joy yields the third, and releasing the third absorption's pleasure yields the fourth; and finally passing beyond every perception still tied to physical form altogether yields the four formless absorptions, culminating at the very peak of conditioned existence. Named individually, these constitute the "four yokes": the yoke of defiled rumination, the yoke of undefiled rumination, the yoke of attachment to joy-and-pleasure, and the yoke of attachment to form-based perception — cast off, in sequence, on the way from the first absorption to the very summit of saṃsāra.

The crucial distinction the sub-part exists to draw, he stresses, is that a non-returner who reaches this same summit has genuinely uprooted the seeds of desire-realm craving along the way, through the wisdom of the four foundations of mindfulness practiced alongside the concentration itself — and so, once yoke-free, stays free, never falling back to a lower realm again. An ordinary meditator who reaches the identical summit through concentration alone, without that accompanying wisdom, has only temporarily suppressed the same afflictions rather than uprooted them; the yokes are loosened, not actually broken, and once the eighty-thousand-eon lifespan available at that peak eventually runs its course, such a being falls right back down into the desire realm exactly as before. Two practitioners can reach the very same meditative peak, in other words, and still end up in entirely different places — one genuinely free, the other simply on an extremely long pause before the same old cycle resumes — a distinction that turns entirely on wisdom, not on depth of concentration.

14. Sub-Part 19: Four Practices That Ripen Pure Faith Into Nirvāṇa

The nineteenth sub-part lays out four progressively deeper practices — generosity, guarding against wrongdoing through patience, sustained cultivation, and the final exhaustion of every affliction — tracing a single continuous path from an ordinary layperson's first steps to an arhat's complete liberation.

Chinese Source

惠施令福增　防非滅怨害　修善捨諸惡　惑盡得涅槃

Ru-Yi Translation

Generosity increases merit; guarding against wrongdoing dissolves enmity and harm; cultivating what is wholesome relinquishes every evil; and the exhaustion of affliction attains nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the four lines as four stages on one continuous path. "Generosity increases merit" describes a layperson of genuine faith who cares generously for the seven categories of people they are responsible for — parents, spouse, household help, friends, dependents — free of any stinginess, which steadily builds the wealth and stability that make further practice possible. "Guarding against wrongdoing dissolves enmity" describes someone who has gone forth and, when insulted, struck, or falsely accused, guards their precepts by refusing to answer with anger — meeting the offender instead with the same loving-kindness practiced toward all beings everywhere, since a single moment of hatred can undo a vast accumulation of merit, disturb sleep and appetite alike, and in the worst cases provoke real violence in return; this patience, sustained, brings a peaceful, well-regarded rebirth free of enemies. "Cultivating what is wholesome relinquishes every evil" describes the practitioner who builds concentration on that same foundation of precepts, and wisdom on that concentration, until direct realization of the truths permanently severs the causes and results that lead to the lower realms. And "the exhaustion of affliction attains nirvāṇa" describes the same practitioner's continued cultivation gradually eliminating every remaining fetter, until they enter nirvāṇa with residue while still alive and, at life's end, nirvāṇa without residue at all.

He illustrates the second stage's real difficulty and its reward with a well-known story: a wealthy merchant, returning home unexpectedly late after a long trip, once paid a passing monk ten thousand pieces of gold for a single verse of advice — "step forward three paces and reflect, step back three paces and consider; when anger rises, pause and weigh it well, and quelling that fire is the most auspicious thing of all" — only to find, entering his darkened bedroom that same night, what looked like two pairs of shoes by his wife's bed. Reaching for a knife in a fury, he suddenly recalled the verse, forced himself to pause and ask first rather than act — and learned that his wife had simply set out an extra pair of shoes for good luck at the new year, missing him terribly, with nothing untoward at all. Master Cháng Bǎi's point is direct: the ten thousand gold pieces were, in the end, the cheapest thing the merchant ever bought, since the same impulse unchecked would have cost him everything that actually mattered. He closes by summarizing all four lines as one arc — wealth enabling stability, patience enabling harmony, cultivation exhausting the causes of the lower realms, and the complete exhaustion of affliction exhausting suffering itself — the natural order in which pure faith actually ripens into full liberation.

15. Sub-Part 20: The Teaching Shared by No Other Tradition

The twentieth sub-part gives one of Buddhism's single most famous verses — quoted across nearly every branch of the tradition — as the treatise's own answer to what actually sets the Buddha's teaching apart from every other path: not any particular doctrine, but this precise, three-part structure of practice.

Chinese Source

諸惡者莫作　諸善者奉行　自調伏其心　是諸佛聖教

Ru-Yi Translation

Do not commit any evil; uphold and practice every good; tame your own mind completely — this is the teaching of all buddhas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the three lines as precepts, concentration, and wisdom respectively, each with its own internal structure. "Do not commit any evil" means someone of genuine faith cutting off every unwholesome action across body, speech, and mind, in every circumstance and toward every kind of object, sentient or otherwise, and then formally taking up the precepts as the concrete expression of that resolve. "Uphold and practice every good" then names six marks of genuinely well-kept precepts — abiding securely in the precepts themselves, guarding each individual rule of the code, perfecting one's conduct and deportment, avoiding disreputable places, treating even minor faults as genuinely alarming, and formally undertaking every training rule — which together form the base from which concentration is built, and concentration in turn the base from which wisdom is built, so that this single line actually contains the whole of the threefold training.

"Tame your own mind completely" then names the fruit of that training as it plays out in three stages: first, genuinely seeing the five aggregates as impermanent, painful, empty, and without self produces real revulsion toward clinging to them as "me" or "mine"; that revulsion, sustained, naturally loosens the grip of craving even without deliberately fighting it moment to moment, since the underlying appetite has already lost its appeal; and that loosened grip, cultivated further, eventually uproots the dormant seeds of affliction entirely rather than merely suppressing their outward expression — genuine liberation, not just good behavior. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in closing is that this compressed sequence — cutting off evil, cultivating good, and taming the mind all the way through to liberation — is precisely what no non-Buddhist path, however rigorous its own ethical code or however deep its own meditative attainment, actually offers as a complete and self-sufficient path, which is why the verse can rightly claim to be, in its own words, the teaching common to every buddha and shared by no other tradition.

16. Sub-Part 21: The Restless Mind, and What Actually Tames It

The twenty-first sub-part names the mind's stubborn, restless attraction to distraction and sense-pleasure as the real obstacle practitioners face — honest about how often practice fails to hold even after real effort — before explaining exactly what steady taming of the mind actually looks like.

Chinese Source

難調伏輕躁　淪墜於諸欲　善調伏其心　心調引安樂

Ru-Yi Translation

Hard to tame, restless, forever sinking into desires — but a mind well tamed, once tamed, brings genuine ease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is unusually candid about how honest this verse is regarding the actual experience of practice: even a practitioner sincerely and continuously engaged in wholesome cultivation cannot claim to remain permanently free of craving, aversion, and delusion, or permanently free of dullness and restlessness — the mind, habituated over countless past lives to noise and distraction, keeps sliding back toward craving, aversion, and confusion no matter how strong the initial resolve, and even a mind forcibly settled into stillness for a time keeps drifting back toward the same old attraction to sights, sounds, and sensory pleasure. This is not a sign of failure, he stresses, but simply an accurate description of what an unenlightened mind is actually like — the verse names the problem honestly before offering any solution.

The solution it then gives is the same three-stage sequence seen throughout this fascicle: recognizing this restless, backsliding tendency as itself a source of suffering, refusing to indulge or follow it, and instead repeatedly choosing to cultivate wholesome states and one-pointed concentration — genuine effort sustained specifically through the setbacks, not merely effort applied once and expected to hold. On the foundation of that genuine, settled concentration, seeing the five aggregates as they truly are becomes possible; that seeing produces real revulsion toward clinging to them; that revulsion loosens craving's grip without needing to fight every arising moment by moment; and that loosened grip, sustained further, finally uproots affliction at the root rather than merely suppressing its outward show. Master Cháng Bǎi's closing point is practical: a mind tamed this way, patiently and repeatedly rather than in one decisive stroke, brings genuine ease in the present life and, having exhausted the causes of future suffering along the way, brings that same ease permanently thereafter — the entire path compressed into a single honest observation about how hard the mind actually is to work with, followed by exactly what working with it well requires.

17. Sub-Part 22: Knowing When to Calm, When to Rouse, When to Simply Let Be

The twenty-second sub-part names a specific meditative skill available to a stream-enterer: knowing, moment to moment, whether the mind currently needs calming, rousing, or simply being left in balanced equanimity — a discernment that yields four distinct, describable fruits.

Chinese Source

於心相善知　能餐遠離味　靜慮常委念　受無染喜樂

Ru-Yi Translation

Knowing well the mind's own signs, one savors the taste of seclusion; through meditation, mindfulness constant and thorough, one experiences joy and ease untainted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the specific skill this stream-enterer has developed as knowing which of three signs the mind currently calls for: the sign of calming, when the mind needs settling on a stilling object; the sign of rousing, when the mind has grown dull or lax and needs deliberate energizing through an inspiring object; and the sign of equanimity, when the mind is already well-balanced and needs neither adjustment, simply steady, effortless continuation on its object without either excessive effort or excessive slack — like a car already cruising smoothly on the highway, needing neither the brake nor the accelerator. Getting this discernment right, applied moment to moment rather than mechanically, is what separates genuine progress from either force-fed concentration or lazy drift.

He then explains the four resulting fruits in sequence. The first is genuinely tasting the ease of seclusion — the mind, settled on a single object and freed from the coarseness of untrained agitation, able to actually enjoy the bodily and mental calm concentration provides rather than merely enduring the sitting. The second is a genuine, uncontaminated form of insight — examining the specific and general characteristics of experience (each aggregate's own nature, and the shared truth of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self running through all of them) with real clarity rather than borrowed conviction. The third is sustained, wholehearted persistence in cultivating the factors of awakening — continuous and thorough practice free of both laziness and fear, the confidence that comes from having actually seen some of what is being pursued rather than merely hoping it exists. And the fourth, the culmination, is the full six-fold equanimity of complete awakening itself — meeting every sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, and thought without either elation or distress, craving, aversion, and delusion exhausted without remainder, enjoying both the refined pleasure of deep absorption and the undisturbed peace beyond it, genuinely at ease in the present moment. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in laying out all four so precisely is that meditative skill is not vague or unfalsifiable — a practitioner can check their own progress against these four concrete, recognizable milestones rather than simply trusting that something is happening.

18. Sub-Part 23: What a Genuine Monk Actually Looks Like

The twenty-third sub-part draws the arhat's portrait in unusually concrete, everyday terms — how they earn a living, how little they own, how they keep their precepts, what they have permanently stopped being able to do — culminating in a picture not of abstract sanctity but of a specific, describable way of living.

Chinese Source

無工巧活輕自己　樂勝諸根盡解脫
無家無所無希望　斷欲獨行真苾芻

Ru-Yi Translation

No contrived livelihood, holding oneself lightly, delighting in what is excellent, the roots exhausted, and liberated — homeless, possessing nothing, hoping for nothing, desire cut off, walking alone: such is the genuine monk.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes the verse as five things genuinely accomplished and five things permanently given up. Accomplished: a livelihood free of any contrivance — never currying favor with the powerful, never dropping hints about one's own supposed attainment to attract donors, receiving teaching only for the sake of liberation and never to build a reputation; holding oneself lightly — keeping possessions to the barest minimum, robes enough only to cover the body, food enough only to fill the stomach, genuinely content with whatever comes; delighting in what is excellent — cherishing the training rules enough that not even mortal danger, let alone some small material gain, could induce breaking them; the roots exhausted — greed, hatred, and delusion uprooted so thoroughly that even the rare lapse into an unwholesome thought is caught and dismissed almost instantly, the way a drop of water vanishes the moment it lands on a red-hot pan; and liberation itself — the mind genuinely freed from every fetter, every dormant seed, every active manifestation of affliction, through sustained cultivation built on the realization already attained.

Permanently given up: no home, meaning never again abandoning the precepts to return to lay life; no possessions, meaning never again hoarding or claiming anything as genuinely "mine" to enjoy; no hope, meaning never again lying — not even under threat to life or livelihood, since nothing is left to protect by lying; desire cut off, meaning no return to any sexual activity whatsoever; and walking alone, meaning a mind no longer entangled with anything at all, self-sufficient rather than needing external validation or company to feel complete. He traces the deeper root beneath all five relinquishments to a single correct understanding: the arhat has fully seen through dependent origination and so holds none of the four mistaken theories about where pleasure and pain actually come from — not that a truly existing self produces them, not that another causes them, not that self and other jointly cause them, and not that they arise from no cause at all — each of which, in its own way, still smuggles in some version of a real, substantial "self" behind experience. Only this thoroughgoing insight into non-self, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is what actually makes every one of the five relinquishments effortless and permanent rather than a rule grimly enforced from outside — the whole portrait resolving, in the end, into a single coherent picture of what genuinely seeing through self-clinging looks like in an ordinary daily life of eating, dressing, and walking from town to town.

19. Sub-Part 24: The Mind That Has Traveled Far, Alone, Bodiless, Asleep in a Cave

The twenty-fourth sub-part offers one of the treatise's most striking psychological portraits: the mind across three times, described through four images — traveling immeasurably far, arising utterly alone, having no fixed "body" of its own, and lying dormant like something asleep in a cave — before naming whoever genuinely tames this mind the true "brahmin."

Chinese Source

心遠行獨行　無身寐於窟　能調伏難伏　我說婆羅門

Ru-Yi Translation

The mind travels far, travels alone, has no body, and lies dormant in its cave — whoever tames this mind, so hard to tame, that one I call a brahmin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the verse's four images onto mind across the three times. "Traveling far" describes the mind's past: across countless past lives, in an incalculable variety of bodies and circumstances, this same continuum of experience has kept moving forward, driven by karma and craving, with no findable starting point and no single "self" ever actually doing the traveling — dependent arising without an agent behind it. "Traveling alone" and "having no body" together describe the mind's present: unlike the physical body, which stays recognizably the same kind of thing from birth to death, mental states arise strictly one at a time, never two together, each one conditioned into being and then gone the instant its condition passes — sometimes greed, sometimes its opposite, sometimes anger, sometimes calm, with no fixed underlying "nature" that is inherently defiled, since the mind's own baseline is neutral and only becomes defiled when paired, moment by moment, with whatever affliction happens to be present. "Lying dormant in its cave" describes the mind's future: whatever craving and karma have been sown now lie stored, unseen, as latent seeds carried forward — like someone asleep in a cave, present but not yet active — ready to ripen into the next rebirth whenever the right conditions arrive.

He explains the closing two lines as the whole point of walking through these four images in the first place: someone genuinely wise, having clearly seen the mind's past as agentless drift, its present as neutral and momentary rather than fixed, and its future as entirely a function of present choices, uses exactly this understanding to cultivate revulsion toward clinging, freedom from desire, and finally cessation itself — crossing entirely beyond the view of a permanent self and reaching, at last, solid ground. Only such a person, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, deserves the title "brahmin" in its true sense — not by birth or claimed status, but because they have done the one thing genuinely difficult to do: taming a mind that by its very nature never sits still.
