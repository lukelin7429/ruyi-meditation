What Makes Speech Genuinely Good

Fascicle 19 continues directly from fascicle 18's stopping point, carrying the fourteenth and largest of the essential-body-verse topics — "discourse," twenty-seven sub-parts in all, from good speech through Maitreya's five questions to the five marks of a genuinely virtuous life — all the way to completion. With this fascicle, the entire fourteen-topic essential-body-verse cycle that opened in fascicle 17 is finished in full.

1. Topic Fourteen, Sub-Part 6: What Makes Speech Genuinely Good

Continuing directly from fascicle 18, the fourteenth topic's sixth sub-part names three qualities that distinguish a noble one's speech from ordinary speech — pleasing, undefiling, and wholly good — then examines a subtlety: these three do not always travel together.

Chinese Source

賢聖嘗說最善語　愛非不愛語第二
諦非不諦語第三　法非非法語第四

Ru-Yi Translation

The noble ones always speak the most excellent speech; pleasing, not displeasing, second; true, not false, third; in accord with the Dharma, not against it, fourth.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，言善語者，所謂善說、善言、善論。當知善說有三種相。所謂悅意、無染、唯善。由第一語，令他慶悅。由第二語，令自尸羅終無穿缺。由第三語，能令他人出不善處，安住善處，因此引攝利益安樂。或有愛語，非諦、非法。謂如有一，以美妙言，稱讚他人非真實德。或有諦語，非愛、非法。謂如有一，以染污心，發麁惡言，訶責他人真實過惡。或有法語，亦愛亦諦。謂如有一，善知稱讚及與訶責。知可稱讚、可訶責已，然不稱讚，亦不訶責，唯善方便為說正法，能令彼人出不善處安住善處。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，所有善語，若標、若釋。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, 'excellent speech' means good saying, good words and good discourse. Good saying should be understood to have three marks: it is pleasing, it is untainted, and it is purely wholesome. By the first kind of speech one gladdens others. By the second, one's own precepts are never breached. By the third, one is able to lead others out of unwholesome conditions and establish them in wholesome conditions, and thereby bring about benefit and happiness. There is pleasing speech that is neither true nor in accord with the Dharma: someone, with fine words, praises in others virtues they do not really have. There is true speech that is neither pleasing nor in accord with the Dharma: someone, with a defiled mind, utters harsh words rebuking others for faults they really have. There is speech in accord with the Dharma that is both pleasing and true: someone knows well how to praise and how to rebuke; having recognized what deserves praise and what deserves rebuke, they neither praise nor rebuke, but simply, with skillful means, teach the true Dharma, which is able to lead that person out of unwholesome conditions and establish them in wholesome conditions. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows all excellent speech, both by naming it and by explaining it. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the three qualities as pleasing speech that genuinely delights the hearer, undefiling speech that is simply true, and wholly good speech that actually leads someone out of an unwholesome state and into a wholesome one — and notes, following the treatise, that the three do not always come together. Flattery that praises a nonexistent virtue is pleasing but neither true nor good, since it only reinforces the hearer's vanity. A harsh rebuke driven by real anger can be entirely true — the fault really was committed — yet be neither pleasing nor good, since it lands as an attack rather than genuine correction, even if the underlying facts are accurate; he notes this differs from a parent's stern correction of a child, which despite its harsh tone is still genuinely aimed at the child's good and so counts as both true and loving speech at once. And Dharma-teaching that neither praises nor blames anyone in particular, simply presenting the teaching itself, can be wholly good and genuinely lead someone toward liberation without being calculated to please or aimed at correcting any specific fault.

He illustrates the real-world cost of speaking well without judging the occasion with the well-known story of the monk Mahālla, who begged the wiser Śāriputra to teach him a beautiful verse of blessing after watching it earn Śāriputra two fine blankets from a delighted patron celebrating a run of good fortune — only to recite that same verse, word for word, to the same household on a later visit when tragedy had struck them instead, and to be beaten and driven off for it; his blind attempts afterward to patch the mistake by parroting whatever phrase he'd last been scolded with (offered indiscriminately to a funeral procession, then a wedding party, then a hunter, then a laundress) earned him beating after beating until the Buddha finally explained, gently, that genuine blessing-speech requires reading the occasion correctly, not simply reciting fine words learned by rote. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it is that goodness in speech is inseparable from timing and context — the same words can bless or wound depending entirely on whether they actually fit the moment and the listener in front of you.

2. Sub-Part 7: The Fruit of Genuine Practice, for Renunciant and Householder Alike

The seventh sub-part names five qualities that make up genuine practice — faith, a sense of shame, precepts, generosity, and, for the ordained, the Dharma itself — and the three benefits that follow from them, tracing the path slightly differently for monastics and for laypeople.

Chinese Source

信慚戒施法　善人所稱讚　是名趣天道　能往天世間

Ru-Yi Translation

Faith, a sense of shame, precepts, generosity, and the Dharma — this is what the good praise, this is called the path to the heavens, and by this one is able to go to the heavenly worlds.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，於佛所證法毘奈耶，獲得正信。恥在居家。受持淨戒。趣得衣服、飲食、臥具，便生喜足；減除器物，儉約資緣。凡所獲得如法利養，終無私隱，必與智人同梵行者而共受用。所有正法初中後善，稱揚梵行，所謂契經乃至論議，皆能受持、研尋、究達，傳授他人，廣為開闡。彼既成就是諸善法，當知必獲三種勝利。一者、諸佛諸佛弟子真實善人之所稱讚。二者、若彼尸羅財施之所攝引福德資糧、法施攝引智慧資糧善圓滿者，便得趣入證解脫處，清淨諸天眾同分中。三者、若彼二種資糧猶未圓滿，便能令彼速得圓滿。身壞已後定生善趣，多往天上樂世界中。復有差別。謂如有一，於佛所證法毘奈耶獲得正信；信惡尸羅當墮惡趣，信慳貪者得貧窮報。如是信已，於現法中惡戒、慳貪深生羞恥。以羞恥故，棄惡尸羅，受清淨戒；棄捨慳貪，以無垢心安處居家，廣說乃至善行布施。由此因緣，於現法中聖賢所讚；身壞已後，乃至當生善趣天上樂世界中。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，在家、出家二種正行，及正行果所有勝利。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone gains right faith in the Dharma and Vinaya realized by the Buddha. They feel shame at remaining in household life. They take up and keep the pure precepts. Whatever clothing, food and drink, and bedding they come by, they are content with; they reduce their belongings and are frugal in their requisites. Whatever gains they obtain in accordance with the Dharma, they never keep hidden for themselves, but always share them with wise companions in the holy life. The true Dharma, good in the beginning, middle and end, which extols the holy life, namely the discourses and so on up to the treatises, they are all able to take up and keep, investigate, penetrate thoroughly, pass on to others and expound widely. Having accomplished these wholesome qualities, they should be understood to gain without fail three benefits. First, they are praised by the buddhas, the buddhas' disciples and truly good people. Second, if their store of merit, drawn in by precepts and the giving of material things, and their store of wisdom, drawn in by the giving of the Dharma, are well fulfilled, they are able to enter the place where liberation is realized, among the company of the pure gods. Third, if those two stores are not yet fulfilled, these qualities enable them to fulfill them quickly. After the breaking up of the body they are certain to be reborn in a good destiny, mostly going to the happy worlds of the heavens. There is a further distinction. Someone gains right faith in the Dharma and Vinaya realized by the Buddha; they believe that those of bad conduct will fall into the bad destinies, and that the miserly receive poverty as their result. Having this faith, they feel deep shame here and now at bad conduct and at miserliness. Because of this shame, they abandon bad conduct and take up the pure precepts; they abandon miserliness and, with a mind free of stain, settle in household life, and so on at length, up to practicing generosity well. For this reason they are praised here and now by the noble and the worthy, and after the breaking up of the body they will be reborn, and so on, in the happy worlds of the heavens, a good destiny. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the two kinds of right practice, of householders and of those gone forth, and all the benefits that are the fruit of right practice. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the five qualities as they unfold for a monastic: genuine confidence in the Buddha's own realized teaching; a sense of shame at remaining a householder at all, since household life is saturated with desire in a way that blocks even the first step toward freedom; taking and keeping the precepts, then living simply and contentedly on whatever robes, food, and bedding come one's way, without demanding more; practicing generosity even as a renunciant, by never privately hoarding any properly acquired offering but sharing it with fellow practitioners; and immersing oneself in the Dharma itself — receiving it through hearing, working it through by reflection, and finally penetrating it through cultivation, then passing it on to others out of genuine compassion. He explains the three resulting benefits: being praised by the Buddhas and their genuinely awakened disciples, since only those who have themselves seen the truth are qualified to recognize it in another; being carried, once the accompanying merit and wisdom are sufficiently complete, into the company of the pure gods — in the commentarial gloss (披尋記), those of the form and formless realms who have already seen the truths; and, even where that fullness isn't yet reached, being guaranteed rebirth in a fortunate realm regardless, since a stream-enterer or once-returner can no longer fall to the three lower realms at all.

For laypeople the same five qualities take a lay shape — faith in the teaching, shame at breaking precepts or clinging to wealth once one understands where such conduct leads, taking whatever precepts fit one's circumstances, and simple generosity — with the Dharma-study and teaching-others piece typically less developed, so the promised fruit is correspondingly the praise of the wise and heavenly rebirth, without the specific promise of the company of the pure gods. He illustrates lay devotion's power with the well-known story of a servant named Guṇamati, executed by the tyrant king Ajātaśatru simply for cleaning and lighting incense at a neglected memorial stūpa built by his imprisoned father in the Buddha's honor — who was reborn instantly among the gods for that single act of devotion, and upon hearing the Buddha teach in her new heavenly life, immediately attained stream-entry. A believing heart, he notes, is available to anyone regardless of ordination status, and its fruit is no less real for that.

3. Sub-Part 8: How Extensive Learning Leads All the Way to Nirvāṇa

The eighth sub-part traces genuine learning across four stages, from simply knowing the teaching through to nirvāṇa itself — the first two belonging to the mundane path shared by anyone seeking a favorable rebirth, the last two belonging to the supramundane path that actually ends saṃsāra.

Chinese Source

多聞能知法　多聞能遠惡　多聞捨無義　多聞得涅槃

Ru-Yi Translation

Through extensive learning one knows the teaching; through extensive learning one keeps far from evil; through extensive learning one relinquishes what is meaningless; through extensive learning one attains nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，於依先時正所應作施論、戒論、生天之論，無倒教法恭敬聽聞；聞已遂能了知其義。謂現法中種種惡行，及當惡趣苦無義因諸惡行所，應速遠離；及往善趣，捨生惡趣苦無義因。彼由了知如是法義，法隨法行，能遠苦因、能引樂因；由此因緣，得樂捨苦。若於增上四聖諦等相應教法恭敬聽聞，聞已遂能了知其義。謂一切有生死大苦寂靜涅槃。彼由了知如是法義，若根已熟，資糧已滿，便能獲得如是義識。心清淨故，纔聞法已，於諸聖諦未現觀者，能入現觀；已現觀者，便得漏盡。若根未熟，資糧未滿，即由如是遠離諸惡，依增上戒起增上心，依增上心發增上慧，由此能捨一切苦本煩惱無義，證得涅槃。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，先聞正法，如理思惟；先如理思，法隨法行；法隨法行為先因故，得勝利果。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone respectfully listens to the unerring teaching that should properly come first, namely talk on giving, talk on precepts and talk on rebirth in heaven, and having heard it, comes to understand its meaning: that the various kinds of bad conduct here and now, which are the meaningless causes of suffering in the bad destinies to come, should quickly be abandoned; and that one should go to the good destinies and give up the meaningless causes of the suffering of rebirth in the bad destinies. Having understood the meaning of such teachings, they practice the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma, so that they can keep far from the causes of suffering and bring about the causes of happiness; for this reason they gain happiness and leave suffering behind. Again, they respectfully listen to the teachings connected with higher matters, such as the four noble truths, and having heard them, come to understand their meaning: that all existence is the great suffering of birth-and-death, and that nirvana is peace. Having understood the meaning of such teachings, if their faculties are already ripe and their stores already full, they are able to gain consciousness of this meaning. Because their mind is pure, as soon as they hear the Dharma, if they have not yet directly realized the noble truths they are able to enter direct realization; if they have already directly realized them, they attain the exhaustion of the outflows. If their faculties are not yet ripe and their stores not yet full, then through keeping far from all evils in this way, relying on higher morality they give rise to higher mind, and relying on higher mind they develop higher wisdom; by this they are able to relinquish the afflictions, the root of all suffering and meaningless, and realize nirvana. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows this: first one hears the true Dharma and then reflects on it properly; first one reflects properly and then practices the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma; and with practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma as the prior cause, one obtains the fruit of benefit. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first two lines as the mundane path: hearing and genuinely understanding teaching on generosity, precepts, and the causes of favorable rebirth is "knowing the teaching," while actually living by that understanding — steering clear of the unwholesome actions that lead to misfortune and cultivating the ones that lead to a good rebirth — is "keeping far from evil." He stresses that real learning always completes itself in changed conduct; hearing without living accordingly is only information, not the multiple learning the verse has in mind.

The remaining two lines then move to the supramundane path. "Relinquishing what is meaningless" is hearing and genuinely grasping the Four Noble Truths and dependent origination well enough to see that saṃsāra's endless round of rebirth, however elaborated, is ultimately without lasting value compared to nirvāṇa's peace — a shift from merely accumulating merit within saṃsāra to actually wanting out of it. "Attaining nirvāṇa" then plays out two different ways depending on how ripe a practitioner's faculties already are: someone whose faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom are already mature, with the necessary merit and wisdom fully accumulated, can move directly from hearing the teaching into direct realization of the truths, or, if already a stream-enterer, straight through to full liberation; someone whose faculties are not yet mature simply keeps steadily avoiding unwholesome action, building concentration on the foundation of precepts, and building wisdom on the foundation of concentration, trusting — the way an object released from a height is bound to fall — that sustained practice on this path leads to nirvāṇa exactly as surely, even without knowing in advance how long the road will be.

He illustrates the transformative power of hearing the Dharma even from someone hostile to it with the well-known story of a bitter, faithless housewife in Kauśāmbī who slammed the door on a begging monk with contemptuous abuse, only to watch him instantly manifest as a bloated, rotting corpse before her eyes — and, fleeing to fetch her husband, found the "corpse" following her, whole and unharmed, the moment she looked back. When her husband arrived with a blade to deal with the monk, the monk conjured a crystal house no weapon could breach, then asked him to set down not the sword in his hand but the sword still in his heart — a request so uncannily accurate that the man's hostility collapsed into genuine remorse on the spot, and both husband and wife, hearing the Dharma the monk then taught them, attained stream-entry within that same encounter. Master Cháng Bǎi's point is that this same power — described afterward by the Buddha simply as tireless, disciplined, unflagging learning joined to unflagging practice — is available to anyone who commits to it the same way, not a special gift reserved for the already-devout.

4. Sub-Part 9: Unstained as Space, Unshaken as Indra's Banner

The ninth sub-part paints the freed mind in a single, memorable image: untouched by anything the way space itself cannot be stained, unshaken the way the banner of the god Indra never falls regardless of which way the wind blows, and finding no pleasure at all in returning to the "muddy heat" of saṃsāra once it has tasted the cool, clear pool of genuine release.

Chinese Source

智者如空無染汙　不動猶如天帝幢
如泛清涼盈滿池　不樂淤泥生死海

Ru-Yi Translation

The wise one is like space, unstained; unshaken, like the banner of the god of the devas; drifting as though on a cool, brimming pool, taking no pleasure in the muddy sea of birth-and-death.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，辯阿羅漢苾芻心善解脫，超諸戲論，猶如虛空。何以故？譬如虛空離諸戲論，淨與不淨皆不能染。諸阿羅漢亦復如是，一切世法若順、若違皆不能染。所謂利、衰，乃至苦、樂。又諸有學已離欲貪，向阿羅漢，於四念住善住其心，修無相心三摩地時，如天帝幢；於其一切動發憍舉、戲論營為、生願俱行所有貪愛，不能傾動。又諸有學已離欲貪，得不還果，於上解脫心生欲樂。譬如遊泛清冷泉池，於愛味定上分諸結熱淤泥中，終不欣樂。由於此中不欣樂故，亦不欣樂生死大海。復有差別。謂阿羅漢所有飲食、言說、遊行，處無相住，有餘依苦之所隨逐。如其次第，三處應知。復有差別。謂慧解脫諸阿羅漢、有學身證，及俱解脫諸阿羅漢。如其次第，三處應知。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，離三界欲於佛聖旨猶有餘依，離欲界貪勝進道攝，及不還果。復有差別。謂略顯示解脫勝利、等持勝利、智慧勝利。復有差別。謂略顯示增上心、慧學所得果。及顯增上心、慧二學。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse it is explained that the mind of an arhat monk is well liberated and has passed beyond all idle speculation, like space. Why? Just as space is free of all idle speculation and can be stained neither by the pure nor by the impure, so too arhats cannot be stained by any worldly condition, whether favorable or adverse, namely gain and loss, and so on up to pain and pleasure. Again, those in training who are already free of sensual greed and are heading toward arhatship, their minds well settled in the four foundations of mindfulness, are like the banner of the lord of the gods when they cultivate the signless concentration of mind: they cannot be shaken by any craving that accompanies agitation and elation, idle speculation and busy activity, or the arising of wishes. Again, those in training who are already free of sensual greed and have attained the fruit of non-return give rise to desire and delight for the higher liberation. Like someone drifting in a cool, clear pool, they never take pleasure in the hot mud of the higher fetters, the relishing of concentration. Because they take no pleasure in this, they also take no pleasure in the great sea of birth-and-death. There is a further distinction: an arhat's eating and drinking, speaking and wandering; their dwelling in the signless abiding; and their still being followed by the suffering of the remainder of the basis. The three places should be understood in this order. There is a further distinction: arhats liberated by wisdom; those in training who are body-witnesses; and arhats liberated both ways. The three places should be understood in this order. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows freedom from desire for the three realms while, according to the Buddha's noble intent, a remainder of the basis still exists; the path of further progress after freedom from sensual greed; and the fruit of non-return. There is a further distinction: it briefly shows the benefit of liberation, the benefit of concentration and the benefit of wisdom. There is a further distinction: it briefly shows the fruits obtained from the trainings in higher mind and higher wisdom, and shows the two trainings in higher mind and higher wisdom themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the four images across three complementary lenses. Taken as a portrait of full arhatship alone, the first line describes a mind wholly beyond the eight worldly winds — gain and loss, praise and blame, fame and disgrace, pleasure and pain — untouched the way space itself cannot be dirtied by anything thrown into it; the second line describes a non-returner still advancing toward arhatship, whose mind, settled in the signless concentration through sustained mindfulness practice, can no longer be shaken by self-conceit, by any of those same eight worldly winds, or by any residual longing for desire-realm rebirth — as stable as Indra's own banner, which legend held could never be blown down; and the last two lines describe that same non-returner's own inner motivation, still willing to relinquish even the deep, refined pleasure of form- and formless-realm meditative absorption — itself compared here to inviting but ultimately stifling "hot mud" — for the truly cool, open freedom of complete liberation.

Taken instead as one continuous portrait of the arhat alone, he explains, the same four images simply describe three facets of a single life: undefiled in the ordinary business of eating, speaking, and traveling about, since none of it is grasped at or clung to; unshaken because completely without attachment to any mental image at all; and taking no pleasure in continued existence, since even a body sustained by residual past karma remains, in the end, simply one more thing to be tired of. And taken as a threefold typology of arhats themselves, the four images distinguish the arhat liberated by wisdom alone (undefiled, but not yet master of the deepest absorptions), the advanced trainee who has personally verified the deepest absorption in the body though not yet full liberation (unshaken), and the arhat doubly liberated in both wisdom and concentration, master of cessation-attainment itself (taking no pleasure in continued existence at all) — three real, well-attested profiles of accomplished practice, not competing interpretations so much as three angles on what full freedom from desire actually looks like in practice.

5. Sub-Part 10: Seeing the Buddha's Form Is Not the Same as Knowing the Buddha

The tenth sub-part echoes what would later become one of the best-known verses in the entire Buddhist canon, from the Diamond Sūtra: seeing the Buddha's physical form, or hearing his voice, is not the same as truly knowing him. Five verses lay out four ways of failing to know the Buddha and one way of genuinely succeeding.

Chinese Source

若以色量我　以音聲尋我　欲貪所執持　彼不能知我
若於內了知　於外不能見　由內果觀察　彼音聲所引
若於內無知　於外而能見　由外果觀察　亦音聲所引
若於內無知　於外不能見　彼普障愚夫　亦音聲所引
若於內了知　於外亦能見　英雄出離慧　非音聲所引

Ru-Yi Translation

If one measures me by form, or seeks me by sound — held fast by desire and craving, such a one cannot know me. If one knows within but cannot see without, seeing only through the fruit of inner meditation, such a one is still led by mere sound. If one has no knowledge within yet can see without, seeing only through the fruit of outer sense, such a one is still led by mere sound. If one has no knowledge within and cannot see without, wholly obstructed and confused, such a one too is led by mere sound. But if one knows within and also sees without — the hero possessed of the wisdom of renunciation — such a one is not led by mere sound.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，體是異生，未斷虛妄分別欲貪，觀見世尊具三十二大丈夫相，遂便測量：此薄伽梵，定是如來應正等覺，其所說法決定微妙，其弟子眾所行必善。彼於後時，近不善人，聞不正法，隨逐他論及他音聲，信順於他，他所引攝；他所引故，於佛法僧還生毀謗。如是皆由不如實知如來法身，故致如此。復有異生，由內靜慮果天眼通，遠見世尊，便作是解：此薄伽梵，定是如來應正等覺。餘如前說。復有由外欲界繫業果報肉眼，見已測量；當知彼亦隨逐他論及他音聲，信順於他，他所引攝。復有異生，於爾所見都無所有；彼普被障，長時為他音聲所引。若諸賢聖，除斷、調伏、超越欲貪，得聖慧眼。彼由如是聖慧眼故，於內證解如來法身。雖於外見如來色身，或見制多，或圖畫等，而能了知非第一義應正等覺。彼由如是於內正知，於外正觀，不隨他論及他音聲，不信順他，非他所引，於佛法僧決定信受。如是皆由如實了知如來法身，故致如此。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，若唯世俗見如來者，則不決定；若以勝義見如來者，是則決定。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone who is by nature an ordinary being, and has not yet cut off false discrimination and sensual greed, sees the World-Honored One endowed with the thirty-two marks of a great man and measures him accordingly: 'This Blessed One must certainly be a Tathāgata, an arhat, a perfectly awakened one; the Dharma he teaches must certainly be subtle and wonderful, and what his community of disciples practices must certainly be good.' Later, keeping company with bad people and hearing what is not the true Dharma, they follow others' doctrines and others' voices, put their faith in others and are led by others; and being led by others, they in turn come to slander the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha. All this happens because they do not truly know the Dharma body of the Tathāgata. Again, there are ordinary beings who, with the divine eye that is the fruit of inner meditation, see the World-Honored One from afar and so understand: 'This Blessed One must certainly be a Tathāgata, an arhat, a perfectly awakened one.' The rest is as explained before. Again, there are those who see him with the fleshly eye, the result of outward karma bound to the desire realm, and having seen him, measure him; it should be understood that they too follow others' doctrines and others' voices, put their faith in others and are led by others. Again, there are ordinary beings who have seen nothing at all of what has been described; they are obstructed on every side and for a long time are led by others' voices. But the noble ones have cut off, tamed and passed beyond sensual greed and have gained the noble eye of wisdom. By that noble eye of wisdom they understand inwardly, through realization, the Dharma body of the Tathāgata. Although outwardly they see the Tathāgata's form body, or see a stupa, or paintings and the like, they understand that this is not the perfectly awakened one in the ultimate sense. Because they rightly know inwardly and rightly see outwardly in this way, they do not follow others' doctrines and others' voices, do not put their faith in others and are not led by others; they have settled faith in the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha. All this happens because they truly know the Dharma body of the Tathāgata. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows that if one sees the Tathāgata only conventionally, one's faith is not settled; if one sees the Tathāgata in the ultimate sense, it is settled. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi works through four ways ordinary beings mistake seeing the Buddha for knowing him. The first simply reasons from outward signs — the thirty-two marks of a great being, eloquent teaching, evidently virtuous followers — to the conclusion "this must be a buddha," without any inner realization at all; lacking that realization, such a person remains fully vulnerable to bad company and wrong teaching, and history shows this type does go on to slander the Three Jewels when a persuasive enough false teacher comes along. The second has cultivated the four absorptions and gained the divine eye, so can see the Buddha directly across great distances in meditation — a real attainment, but one still unaccompanied by liberating wisdom, and so just as vulnerable to being talked out of right view by a plausible outsider. The third has only the ordinary physical eye, perhaps living during the Buddha's own lifetime and lucky enough to see him directly — vivid, but equally unprotected without wisdom. The fourth has neither: no meditative sight, no physical sight, whether from blindness or simply from being born after the Buddha's final passing — the most thoroughly obstructed case of all, left with nothing but secondhand report to build any confidence on.

Only the fifth type — a worthy or noble one, from the stage of preparatory practice through full realization, whose desire has actually been abandoned and uprooted rather than merely suppressed — truly knows the Buddha, and Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to explain exactly what that means: inwardly, through meditation joined with insight, actually realizing the Buddha's dharma-body — that is, awakening to the same dependent-arising, empty-of-self-nature reality the Buddha himself awakened to, since a buddha's deepest identity was never the physical body at all but this realization itself; and outwardly, still perfectly able to look at a Buddha statue, a stūpa, or a painted image and correctly recognize it as a representation rather than the ultimate reality it points to, without either dismissing it or mistaking the pointer for the moon. Such a person's confidence in the Three Jewels can never be talked away by any outside voice, because it was never secondhand to begin with. He closes with the well-known story of the nun Utpalavarṇā, who used her psychic power to transform into a wheel-turning king so she could push to the very front of the crowd welcoming the Buddha back from teaching his mother in the heavens — only to be told, once she reverted to her own form and bowed, that she had not actually been first to see him at all: the elder Subhūti, who had stayed behind in his cave simply contemplating emptiness rather than joining the crowd, had genuinely seen the Buddha first, precisely because seeing dependent origination itself is what seeing the Buddha actually means.

6. Sub-Part 11: Even Attachment to Meditation Itself Is Still Defilement

The eleventh sub-part turns to a subtler trap than ordinary sense-desire: a practitioner who has genuinely mastered the five sense-based cravings but still clings, however refined the object, to the mind's own experience of concentration — the sixth and most powerful of the six sense-bases, still capable of its own defilement.

Chinese Source

第六增上王　染時染自取　於無染不染　染者名愚夫

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth is the sovereign — there is a time of defilement, and defilement by grasping for oneself; where there is no defilement, nothing is defiled; whoever is defiled is called a fool.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，第六增上王者，謂心、意、識。若有已渡五暴流，未渡第六意暴流；爾時其心隨逐諸定所有愛味，故名染時。復有補特伽羅，於長夜染取為己有，於可愛法執藏不捨，是故說彼為染自取。貪名為染；因貪所生當來世苦，亦名為染。若染自取，於所染心不隨功用，攝受、遮止、修意對治作意故；如是彼心於現法中無有染污。於無染心，此染自取當來世中因彼諸苦，亦無有染。若有於彼隨作功用，而不攝受、亦不遮止、不修意對治作意故；依此苦因，長夜受苦。於此苦因不能遠離，故名愚夫。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，遠離苦因所有勝利，及顯苦因能感自苦，是愚夫性。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, 'the sixth, the sovereign' means mind, mentation and consciousness. Suppose someone has already crossed the five floods but has not yet crossed the sixth, the flood of the mind; then their mind follows the relishing of the concentrations, and so this is called 'a time of defilement'. Again, there is a person who for a long time has been defiled, grasping things as their own, holding on to agreeable things and not letting them go; therefore they are said to be 'defiled by grasping for oneself'. Greed is called defilement; the future suffering born from greed is also called defilement. If one who is defiled by grasping for oneself does not exert themselves in following the defiled mind, but restrains it, checks it and applies the attention that cultivates the mind's antidote, then that mind is not defiled here and now. For a mind without defilement, this defilement of grasping for oneself brings no defilement either through the sufferings it would cause in the future. But if someone exerts themselves in following it, and does not restrain it, does not check it and does not apply the attention that cultivates the mind's antidote, then, relying on this cause of suffering, they experience suffering for a long time. Because they cannot keep far from this cause of suffering, they are called a fool. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the benefit of keeping far from the cause of suffering, and shows that it is the nature of a fool that the cause of suffering brings about one's own suffering. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the sixth" as mind itself — the sixth sense-base governing the other five, called "sovereign" because it is what actually directs and makes use of them. His focus falls on a specific and easily missed case: someone who has already crossed the "flood" of the five sense-desires, mastering attraction to sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and textures well enough to attain genuine meditative absorption, can still remain caught by a sixth, subtler flood — craving the very peace and pleasure of that absorption itself, treating the stillness of concentration as "mine" the same way an untrained mind treats a beautiful object as desirable. This, he stresses, is still craving in every meaningful sense, and it still plants the same seed for future suffering, since attachment even to the most refined states of form- and formless-realm concentration still binds a being to further rebirth within those realms — rebirth that, however pleasant by comparison to the lower realms, remains rebirth all the same.

He explains the antidote as the same fourfold mindfulness practice used everywhere else in the treatise, applied here specifically to the mind observing its own experience of concentration: recognizing craving for absorption as a genuine danger the moment it arises, refusing to let the mind simply follow that craving's pull, and repeatedly training the mind to rest in freedom from craving rather than in the craved object itself — at which point, once this defilement is genuinely uprooted rather than merely suppressed, no further defiled result follows either, since a cause that no longer arises produces no further fruit. Someone who instead simply follows wherever this subtle craving leads, without either restraining it or applying any deliberate remedy, remains — whatever their level of meditative accomplishment — still bound to suffering by that same craving, and the treatise does not hesitate to call such a person, however accomplished their concentration, simply a fool. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this is in some ways a harder trap to escape than ordinary sense-desire, since a meditator confident in their own attainment is often the least willing to hear correction from anyone whose concentration is less advanced than their own — precisely the stubbornness that keeps this particular defilement so persistent.

7. Sub-Part 12: The Fortress of Bone That Locks Wholesome Roots Out

The twelfth sub-part pictures the mind as a fortress — walled with bone, bound with sinew, plastered with flesh — held under siege from within by four defilements: craving and aversion, which trap the householder still absorbed in worldly desire, and conceit and concealment, which trap even someone who has gone forth, if that going-forth was into a mistaken teaching.

Chinese Source

有城骨為牆　筋肉而塗飾　其中有貪恚　慢覆所任持

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a city walled with bone, plastered over with sinew and flesh — and within it, craving and aversion, conceit and concealment, hold their ground.

Chinese Source

This city has only bones for its bricks and stones, sinews in place of ropes and cords, and flesh for its plaster; the frame of the body is the wall that surrounds it on every side. Because the former are attached to sense pleasures and seek sense pleasures, and do not turn away from base conduct, they do not even accept the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya with faith, let alone cultivate what is good. The latter, relying on a badly proclaimed Dharma, become arrogant and cannot of their own accord go to the Buddha, the World-Honored One, or to his disciples. Even if the Buddha, the World-Honored One, or a disciple of the Buddha, out of compassion, goes to them in person, their mind is defiled by concealment, a secondary affliction and entanglement, so that they do not even truthfully disclose their own faults, let alone have faith in and understanding of the wholesome qualities and cultivate them. So it should be understood that the wholesome qualities connected with the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya cannot enter either of these two kinds of mind-city, still less be taken by them as their own. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows that householders and those gone forth alike, through four kinds of defiling conditions, lose and spoil the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the "city" as the mind itself — the eighth consciousness that gathers experience, the seventh that constantly grasps at it as "self," and the six that actively perceive — housed within a body built, quite literally, from bone as its stonework, sinew as its binding cord, and flesh as its plaster. He then explains why four particular defilements are named as the city's occupying forces rather than any others: craving and aversion belong to the ordinary householder still absorbed in sense-pleasure, who clings to whatever is pleasing and resents whatever is not, remaining thereby thoroughly unreceptive to teaching that would ask them to give any of it up. Conceit and concealment belong instead to someone who has gone forth — but into a mistaken teaching, so that whatever confidence or apparent attainment they gain becomes simply one more reason for pride, and whatever fault they commit gets hidden away rather than honestly disclosed, precisely the two habits that make correction from any wiser source impossible to receive even when it is freely offered out of compassion.

He extends the warning to Buddhist practitioners themselves, noting this danger is not confined to outsiders: a monastic who gains a little meditative stability, or simply arrives already carrying unexamined convictions from an earlier life or an earlier teacher, can develop exactly the same conceit and concealment, becoming just as closed to correction as any outsider — the city's walls hold just as firmly whether the fortress belongs to a non-Buddhist ascetic or to someone technically ordained within the Buddha's own teaching. He closes with a well-known verse comparing genuine practice to guarding a walled city: watching for the six sense-thieves by day, staying alert through the night as well, so that when the mind's own general — clear awareness — is truly in command, no weapons are even needed to keep the peace, since a thief who finds no opening simply has nothing to steal.

8. Sub-Part 13: Drawing In Like a Tortoise, Rising Through the Absorptions

The thirteenth sub-part offers one of this fascicle's most vivid images: a practitioner advancing through the absorptions by drawing the mind in exactly as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, growing progressively less disturbed and more genuinely peaceable to live alongside — the sign of real, not merely apparent, progress.

Chinese Source

如龜藏支於自殼　苾芻善攝意尋思
無所依止不惱他　證般涅槃無所謗

Ru-Yi Translation

As a tortoise draws its limbs into its own shell, the monk skillfully draws in the mind's own rumination — relying on nothing, troubling no one — and realizes final nirvāṇa, slandering nothing.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，依初靜慮捨三惡尋。所謂欲尋、恚尋、害尋。又能棄捨初靜慮地諸善尋思，安住無尋無伺定中。如龜藏支於其自㲉，略攝尋思亦復如是。無尋無伺定者，應知此上乃至有頂。彼於此定正安住時，不生愛味。出已成就可愛樂法，調順柔和，易可共住，不惱有智同梵行者。又為智人同梵行者欣樂共住。又復成就無違諍法。彼由如是正方便故，於諸聖諦能入現觀，及得漏盡。彼於諸法不由他信，獲得善淨勝智見故，如實了知法真是法，毘柰耶真是毘柰耶。由如是知故，終不依止諸見顛倒，於法謗法，及於非法亦謗非法；終不顯示非法為法，法為非法，非毘柰耶為毘柰耶，或毘柰耶為非毘柰耶。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，善說法者四種擾亂斷對治道。何等名為四種擾亂？一、染不染尋思擾亂，二、於勝定愛味擾亂，三、互相違諍訟擾亂，四、於正道誹謗擾亂。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone, relying on the first meditation, abandons the three bad kinds of thought: thoughts of sense desire, thoughts of ill will and thoughts of harm. They are further able to give up even the wholesome thoughts of the stage of the first meditation and settle in concentration without applied and sustained thought. Like a tortoise drawing its limbs into its own shell, they likewise draw in their thinking. Concentration without applied and sustained thought should be understood to extend from here upward to the peak of existence. While they are rightly settled in this concentration, they do not relish it. Having emerged from it, they possess agreeable and delightful qualities: they are tractable and gentle, easy to live with, and do not trouble wise companions in the holy life. Moreover, wise companions in the holy life delight in living with them. They also possess the quality of being free of contention. By such right effort they are able to enter direct realization of the noble truths and attain the exhaustion of the outflows. They do not depend on faith in others concerning the teachings, because they have gained good, pure and superior knowledge and vision; they know as it really is that the Dharma is truly the Dharma and the Vinaya is truly the Vinaya. Knowing thus, they never rely on inverted views to slander the Dharma where there is Dharma, or to slander what is not the Dharma where there is no Dharma; they never present what is not the Dharma as the Dharma, or the Dharma as not the Dharma, what is not the Vinaya as the Vinaya, or the Vinaya as not the Vinaya. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the path that counteracts and cuts off four kinds of disturbance for those of the well-proclaimed Dharma. What are the four kinds of disturbance? First, the disturbance of defiled and undefiled thinking; second, the disturbance of relishing superior concentration; third, the disturbance of mutual contention and quarreling; fourth, the disturbance of slandering the right path. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the tortoise image through the successive absorptions. Attaining the first absorption means abandoning the three unwholesome kinds of rumination — over desire, over aversion, over harm — the way a tortoise pulls all six limbs inside its shell where no predator can reach them; the mind, similarly drawn in from the six sense-objects, becomes correspondingly hard to disturb. But he stresses a practitioner does not stop there: even the first absorption's own wholesome forms of applied and sustained thought are, relatively speaking, still an agitation worth relinquishing, so the same withdrawing motion repeats — releasing the first absorption's rumination to reach the second, then its joy to reach the third, then its pleasure to reach the fourth, and on upward through each of the formless spheres to the very peak of conditioned existence. Progress through the entire ladder, in other words, is really one repeated gesture of letting go rather than a series of unrelated achievements.

The verse's third phrase, "relying on nothing, troubling no one," describes what genuine progress looks like from the outside: a practitioner who does not become attached to any level of concentration attained, remains easy to live alongside, gentle enough to draw others toward wanting to practice near them, and free of the anger that would otherwise make daily community life a source of constant friction. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this social ease is itself a diagnostic — someone whose meditative attainment leaves them harder to get along with, more irritable, or more inclined to look down on others has misunderstood what the practice is actually for, since real freedom from craving naturally expresses itself as freedom from ill will as well. Carried all the way through, this same letting-go finally issues in nirvāṇa itself — and, as the treatise explains the closing phrase, one who reaches it slanders nothing, neither denying the Dharma nor misrepresenting what is not the Dharma.

He closes the sub-part by naming the six qualities (or, bundled differently, two) that make such a practitioner genuinely easy to live alongside — goodwill in body, speech, and mind; sharing material things fairly; and holding the same precepts and the same view as one's companions in practice — and by explaining that this same freedom is what finally makes someone immune to distorting the teaching in either direction: neither slandering what is genuinely wholesome as worthless nor excusing what is genuinely harmful as harmless, since a mind no longer clouded by craving has no motive left to bend the truth either way. He summarizes the whole sub-part as answering four sources of disturbance in turn — unwholesome and even wholesome rumination, both settled through the tortoise-like withdrawal into deepening absorption; attachment to the pleasure of concentration itself, settled through relying on nothing; friction with fellow practitioners, settled through the six qualities of genuine harmony; and distortion of the teaching by wrong view, settled through the clear seeing that makes such distortion simply impossible once the truths have actually been realized.

9. Sub-Part 14: Why the Buddha Chose to Relinquish His Own Remaining Years

The fourteenth sub-part explains one of the most personal moments in the Buddha's own biography: his deliberate choice to enter final nirvāṇa at eighty rather than living out a fuller natural lifespan, comparing the act to a chick finally breaking free of the shell that once sustained it.

Chinese Source

等不等而生　牟尼捨有行　內樂定差別　如俱舍卵生

Ru-Yi Translation

Equal, yet unequal, in what arose — the sage relinquished the formations of existence. Through the distinction of inner joy and concentration, it was like a chick breaking from its shell.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂佛示現住最後有菩薩位時，先所獲得三十有二大丈夫相，八十隨好圓滿莊嚴妙色身生，於後證得阿耨多羅三藐三菩提時，其色身生與前正等。其名身生，由勝無漏不相似故，與前不等。又佛示現內寂靜樂、及沙門樂、為依止故，得定自在；如定心力，捨諸壽行及諸有行。彼捨邊際妙色身生，與前正等。其名身生與前不等，故有差別。如因其㲉卵生雞等，依卵而生，即此生已，漸漸增長，種類相似，破㲉而出。如是如來色身、名身差別道理，當知亦爾。此中差別，謂佛世尊若不棄捨諸壽行者，應滿壽量方般涅槃。定力所持，捨壽行故，不滿壽量而般涅槃。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，捨諸壽行，色身、名身二種差別，及顯棄捨所依因緣。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. When the Buddha manifested dwelling in the stage of a bodhisattva in his last existence, the birth of his body of form, the wonderful form body perfectly adorned with the thirty-two marks of a great man and the eighty minor marks that he had previously attained, was, when he later realized unsurpassed perfect awakening, exactly equal to what it had been before. The birth of his name body, however, was not equal to what it had been before, because the superior untainted [qualities] are dissimilar to what came before. Again, the Buddha manifested that, relying on the inner happiness of peace and the happiness of the ascetic, he attained mastery of concentration, and by the power of the concentrated mind relinquished the formations of life and the formations of existence. The birth of the wonderful form body at that final point of relinquishing was exactly equal to what it had been before; the birth of the name body was not equal to what it had been before; hence there is a difference. Just as a chick and the like are born from an egg, depending on the egg for their birth, and once born gradually grow, being similar in kind, and break out of the shell, so too should the principle of the difference between the Tathāgata's form body and name body be understood. The distinction here is that if the Buddha, the World-Honored One, had not relinquished the formations of life, he would have entered final nirvana only on completing his lifespan; because, sustained by the power of concentration, he relinquished the formations of life, he entered final nirvana without completing his lifespan. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the relinquishing of the formations of life, the two kinds of difference between the form body and the name body, and the cause and condition on which the relinquishing depends. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the verse's opening puzzle — "equal, yet unequal" — as comparing the Buddha's physical body before and after his enlightenment (unchanged, still marked by the same thirty-two major and eighty minor auspicious marks, hence "equal") against his inner realization (utterly transformed by the attainment of complete, unobstructed wisdom, hence "unequal"). Only the mind changes through practice, he stresses; the body carries on exactly as before. Grounded in that same inner mastery — the "inner joy" of meditative stillness joined with the "sage's joy" of realized wisdom — the Buddha possessed genuine freedom over the length of his own life, able to extend it or relinquish it as he judged best, rather than being simply carried along by the karmic momentum that determines an ordinary being's lifespan.

He recounts the well-known episode behind this choice: though the Buddha could, by tradition, have lived a full hundred years (some say a hundred and twenty), he chose to enter final nirvāṇa at eighty, explicitly leaving the remaining share of merit-based longevity as a kind of inheritance sustaining his later disciples' ability to practice without want. When the Buddha asked Ānanda three times whether he wished him to remain in the world longer, Ānanda — still only a stream-enterer at the time, his resolve clouded — failed to answer, and only realized what had been lost once the decision was already made, weeping and asking why the Buddha could not simply stay. Even then, the Buddha's freedom over his own lifespan showed itself once more: informed that the wandering ascetic Subhadra, on the verge of real breakthrough after a lifetime of near-miss meditative attainment, still needed guidance, the Buddha extended his life by three months specifically to teach him the eightfold path — Subhadra reached arhatship almost the instant he heard it, becoming the Buddha's very last direct disciple, and the Buddha entered parinirvāṇa immediately after.

Master Cháng Bǎi closes with the verse's own image: just as a chick, having grown to fullness inside its shell, finally breaks free of what had until then sustained and protected it, the Buddha's relinquishing of his remaining lifespan was not a loss but the natural completion of a process — passing out of the "shell" — which the commentarial gloss (披尋記) reads as ignorance, the covering that had enclosed him — into the boundless freedom of nirvāṇa without any remaining trace, a release made possible only by the very mastery ordinary craving for continued existence can never grant.

10. Sub-Part 15: Mud, Demons, Nets, and Rivers — Four Images for Four Afflictions

The fifteenth sub-part reaches for four vivid, ordinary images of helplessness — sinking in mud, possession by a hostile spirit, entanglement in a net, being swept along a fast-flowing river — to describe exactly how craving, aversion, delusion, and attachment each strip a being of freedom in their own distinct way.

Chinese Source

無淤泥等欲　無魑魅等瞋　無羅網等癡　無江河等愛

Ru-Yi Translation

Let there be no craving like sinking mud; no aversion like a hostile spirit; no delusion like an entangling net; no attachment like a rushing river.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂有四種能為世俗不自在法，世間現見能令有情不自在轉。一者、陷溺淤泥；二者、鬼魅所著；三者、入於羅網；四者、墮駛流河，隨流漂溺。復有四種能為真實不自在法，能令有情不自在轉，當知亦爾。何等為四？謂如有一，生長欲界，陷溺不淨腥臊生臭諸欲淤泥，不能自在引發守護增長善法。又如有一，棄捨諸欲，於善說法毘奈耶中而得出家。心懷忿怒，性多惡言；由忿所持，不得自在；不數學處，動生違越；於諸智者同梵行所，屢以麁言擊刺、訶擯、侵惱、毀辱。又如有一，棄捨諸欲，於惡說法毘奈耶中而得出家，入諸惡魔大癡見網。彼既入已，流轉生死，不得自在。又如有一，生長上分諸離欲地，於諸愛結未能永斷，亦未遍知，不得自在，還生下界，順流而住，難可出離。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，諸界、諸品愚夫纏縛。復有差別。謂如有一，陷欲淤泥，不能自在於善說法毘奈耶中清淨出家。又如有一，為性忿怒，忿怒所蔽，憤恚纏心，尚於自身或害、或損，何況於他。又如有一，成就癡品諸惡邪見。謂無父母，毀謗父母，於父母所反悕敬養，況自能為。又如有一，廣集諸欲，貪愛所漂，不得自在；尚不欲自食，況能惠於他。如是四法，當知能障諸聰慧者四應知法。謂善說法毘奈耶中清淨出家、遠離恚害、敬事父母、樂行慧施。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. There are four conventional things that make one unfree, which are seen in the world to make beings unable to act freely: first, sinking into mud; second, being possessed by a ghost or demon; third, being caught in a net; fourth, falling into a swift river and being swept along and drowned. It should be understood that there are likewise four real things that make one unfree, which make beings unable to act freely. What are the four? Someone born and raised in the desire realm sinks into the mud of sense pleasures, impure, rank and foul-smelling, and cannot freely bring about, guard and increase wholesome qualities. Again, someone gives up sense pleasures and goes forth in the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya, but harbors anger in their heart and is by nature given to harsh speech; held by anger, they are not free. They do not train in the points of training and readily transgress them; toward wise companions in the holy life they repeatedly use harsh words to sting, rebuke, expel, trouble and insult them. Again, someone gives up sense pleasures and goes forth in a badly proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya, and enters the net of great delusion and wrong views of the evil Māra. Having entered it, they wander on in birth-and-death and are not free. Again, someone born and raised in the higher stages free from desire has not yet cut off for ever, nor yet fully understood, the fetters of craving; they are not free, are reborn again in the lower realm, abide going with the stream, and find it hard to escape. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the bondage of fools in all realms and of all classes. There is a further distinction. Someone sinks into the mud of sense desire and cannot freely go forth in purity in the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya. Again, someone is by nature angry; covered over by anger, with resentment entangling their mind, they harm or injure even themselves, let alone others. Again, someone possesses the evil wrong views belonging to the class of delusion, namely that there is no father and mother; slandering their father and mother, they instead expect respect and support from their father and mother, let alone being able to provide these themselves. Again, someone accumulates sense pleasures widely and, swept along by greed and craving, is not free; they do not even wish to eat themselves, let alone give to others. It should be understood that these four things obstruct four things that the intelligent ought to know: going forth in purity in the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya, keeping far from anger and harm, respectfully serving father and mother, and delighting in practicing wise generosity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each image as a distinct portrait of unfreedom. Someone still absorbed in ordinary desire-realm craving, mired in the genuinely unclean, foul-smelling reality of sensual indulgence once seen honestly rather than through the gloss of attraction, finds it simply impossible to bring forth new wholesome qualities or protect and grow the ones already present — exactly like a person sinking deeper into mud the more they struggle. Someone who has gone forth into the Buddha's own teaching but still carries a hostile, quick-tempered nature remains, despite outward robes, unable to keep the precepts consistently and prone to abusing fellow practitioners with harsh speech — hosting, in effect, the same violent, unpredictable spirit the image of demonic possession suggests. Someone who has instead gone forth into a mistaken teaching entirely, entangled in wrong view as thoroughly as a fish or bird caught in a net, continues circling through saṃsāra with no way out, since the net itself is the very framework through which they interpret their own experience. And someone who has reached the higher meditative realms of form or formlessness, suppressing but never actually uprooting the seeds of desire-realm craving, inevitably falls back to the lower realm once that suppression's temporary power runs out — carried helplessly downstream the way a river carries anything not anchored to solid ground, difficult to escape precisely because the undertow is invisible until it's already too late.

He then names what each affliction specifically blocks: craving blocks the very possibility of genuine renunciation, since a mind still absorbed in desire cannot make the decisive break household life requires; aversion blocks freedom from harming others, since a mind gripped by anger will injure even itself, let alone anyone else; delusion blocks honoring one's parents, since a being confused enough to deny basic cause and effect will just as readily deny any debt of gratitude toward the two people who gave them life; and attachment blocks genuine generosity, since someone swept along by craving cannot bring themselves to use their own resources for their own sake, let alone give any of it away. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in pairing each affliction with its specific casualty is practical rather than merely descriptive: a practitioner examining their own difficulty with any one of these four practices — renunciation, non-harming, filial respect, or generosity — can trace the blockage back to its specific root affliction, rather than treating the difficulty as some vague, undifferentiated failure of willpower.

11. Sub-Part 16: Only the Buddha Is Genuinely Free of Idle Speculation

The sixteenth sub-part surveys three basic kinds of seeking any unawakened being pursues, argues that all three — even the subtlest, mistaken for liberation itself — still amount to nothing more than idle speculation with no lasting foothold, and names the Buddha as the sole exception, having relinquished every form of seeking entirely.

Chinese Source

虛空無鳥跡　外道無沙門　愚夫樂戲論　如來則無有

Ru-Yi Translation

As a bird finds no footing in space, so is there no true renunciant among non-Buddhist paths. Fools delight in idle speculation — but in the Tathāgata there is none.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂有眾生悕樂勝欲，欲求所攝；又有眾生悕樂勝身，有求所攝；又有眾生悕樂沙門及婆羅門所有解脫，梵行求攝。此中欲求、有求攝者，謂我因少分布施、少分持戒，當得往生善趣天上樂世界中，以妙五欲而自賞納，歡娛遊戲。彼既修習如是願已，得最勝欲及最勝身。譬如眾鳥翱翔虛空，遍虛空中無安足處，如是眾生，於其所得無常諸欲及身分中都無安住，當知亦爾。若樂沙門及婆羅門所有解脫梵行求攝，復有二種。或依善說法，或依惡說法。依惡說法諸外道輩，並無沙門。依善說法邪梵行求所攝受者，亦無沙門。正梵行求所攝受者，得有沙門。又此一切三門所攝，或欲求門、或有求門、或梵行求門。如是皆名樂著戲論。當知如來棄捨一切所有悕求，故無戲論。即以此義，類知如來諸弟子眾，正梵行求所攝受者，亦無戲論。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，離善說法及毘奈耶勤精進者，皆空無益。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. There are beings who long for superior sense pleasures; this is included in the search for sense pleasures. There are beings who long for a superior body; this is included in the search for existence. There are beings who long for the liberation of ascetics and brahmins; this is included in the search for the holy life. Among these, those included in the search for sense pleasures and the search for existence think: 'Through a little generosity and a little keeping of the precepts, I shall be reborn in the happy worlds of the heavens, a good destiny, and there enjoy the wonderful five sense pleasures, amusing and disporting myself.' Having cultivated this wish, they obtain the most excellent sense pleasures and the most excellent body. Just as birds soaring in space find no footing anywhere in space, so it should be understood that these beings find no stable abiding in the impermanent sense pleasures and bodies they obtain. Those who delight in the liberation of ascetics and brahmins, included in the search for the holy life, are again of two kinds: those relying on a well-proclaimed Dharma and those relying on a badly proclaimed Dharma. Among the non-Buddhists who rely on a badly proclaimed Dharma there are no ascetics at all. Among those relying on the well-proclaimed Dharma who are included in a wrong search for the holy life there are also no ascetics. Among those included in a right search for the holy life there are ascetics. Again, all of these are included in three gateways: the gateway of the search for sense pleasures, the gateway of the search for existence, and the gateway of the search for the holy life. All of these are called delighting in idle speculation. It should be understood that the Tathāgata has given up all longing and searching, and therefore has no idle speculation. By this same meaning one may infer that the Tathāgata's community of disciples who are included in a right search for the holy life likewise have no idle speculation. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows that all who strive diligently apart from the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya are empty and without benefit. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names three kinds of seeking that between them cover every unawakened being's aspiration: seeking sensual pleasure, aimed at a better rebirth among the desire-realm's various heavens where the five pleasures can be enjoyed more fully; seeking a superior existence, aimed at the more refined embodiment and pleasure of the first through third form-realm absorptions; and seeking release itself, aimed at genuine liberation through the renunciant path — though he flags a crucial trap within this third category: someone who mistakes stillness in the four formless absorptions for actual liberation, without ever uprooting the underlying craving those absorptions only temporarily suppress, is practicing what the treatise calls a "mistaken" seeking of release, indistinguishable in its ultimate result from the first two.

He explains why "no true renunciant" applies so broadly: genuine renunciant status, in any of its four traditional kinds — the śramaṇa who conquers by the path, who teaches the path, who lives by the path, and who defiles the path — requires the noble eightfold path actually be established in the mind — something no non-Buddhist teaching, however rigorous its own austerities, ever manages, and something even a mistaken pursuit of the formless absorptions within the Buddhist fold fails to establish either, since taking mere stillness for liberation forecloses the path just as surely as any outsider's error does. All three kinds of seeking, he stresses, share the same fundamental shape: reaching for some particular realm of rebirth, and finding, once reached, that it offers no more lasting foothold than a bird finds in open sky — real enough while it lasts, but leaving no trace once the karma that produced it is exhausted, and never actually escaping the cycle of arising and passing that defines saṃsāra itself.

Only the Buddha, having relinquished every one of these three forms of seeking without exception, is genuinely free of what the verse calls "idle speculation" — not because he lacks understanding of any realm a being might seek, but because he holds no aspiration toward any of them at all, the aspiration itself being precisely what keeps ordinary seeking circling without ever landing anywhere solid. Master Cháng Bǎi extends the same freedom, by the same logic, to any of the Buddha's own disciples who pursue genuine liberation rather than any of the three forms of seeking still bound to rebirth — the crucial variable being not effort, which the mistaken meditator on formless stillness may have in abundance, but whether the aspiration itself has actually been relinquished at the root.

12. Sub-Part 17: The Genuine Sage, Whom Even the Gods Cannot Recognize

The seventeenth sub-part portrays the true arhat — free of every foothold consciousness clings to, past every wall and moat that keeps ordinary beings confined — and explains why such a person's inner freedom is invisible not just to confused human onlookers but even to the gods themselves.

Chinese Source

住戲論皆無　踰牆塹離愛　牟尼遊世間　天人不能識

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of every abode, free of all idle speculation, having crossed the wall and the moat, free of craving — the sage wanders the world, unrecognized by gods or men.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. An arhat monk, for ever free of greed and craving, is, in four respects, liberated and free from the lord to whom all fools are subject, the evil Māra, the enemy, and wanders as they please through quiet places and villages. There are fools who, meeting such a true arhat, do not truly know their most ultimate freedom in wandering, and so falsely give rise to contempt on two counts: 'Why has this son of good family given up the precious wealth he owned to support his life, and instead seeks requisites that belong to others? Why has he given up the means of rebirth in heaven, and strives with hardship and diligence for the annihilation of existence?' These fools, seeing that rebirth in heaven has superior qualities and that household life has much wealth, falsely belittle the sage. The gods they serve cannot even understand the vast virtues of this sage, still less can those who serve them recognize them. How are arhats free of craving, in four respects, liberated and free from the lord to whom all fools are subject, the evil Māra, the enemy? Fools, through the four stations of consciousness, are driven by their lord, Māra the enemy, and made to come and go among the five destinies in birth-and-death; not so the arhat. Again, fools, as if for a grave offense, are driven by their lord, Māra the enemy: because of evil views that either superimpose or deny, they give rise to various evil and unwholesome deeds such as taking up knives and clubs, fall into idle speculation, are reborn in the bad destinies, and are made to create the conditions for various kinds of evil karma; not so the arhat. Again, fools, as if for a moderate offense, are driven by their lord, Māra the enemy, and made to dwell within the walls of the bondage of sensual craving, unable to escape the suffering of birth in the desire realm; not so the arhat. Again, fools, as if for a light offense, are driven by their lord, Māra the enemy, and made to be born in the form realm and the formless realm, surrounded on every side by the deep moat of ignorance and shut in the prison of the many sufferings of birth-and-death, unable to escape the sufferings of birth and so on; not so the arhat. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows that all fools are ashamed of what they should not be ashamed of and are not ashamed of what they should be ashamed of; that they see fear where there is nothing to fear and see no fear where there is something to fear. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies four things ordinary beings remain bound to that the true sage has entirely relinquished: the "four abodes" of consciousness — the habitual clinging of mind to form, feeling, perception, and mental formation as somehow constituting a stable "me," which keeps rebirth cycling from one life to the next; idle speculation, meaning the wrong views (whether adding false permanence and purity to things that have none, or wrongly denying cause and effect altogether) that keep a being circling without ever landing on the truth; the "wall" of desire-realm craving that keeps beings confined to that lowest, most turbulent realm; and the "moat" of the subtler ignorance that keeps beings confined even within the more refined form and formless realms, unable to cross into genuine freedom despite how peaceful those realms may feel from the inside. Ordinary beings, he explains, remain under the control of what the tradition personifies as four kinds of demonic force — the five aggregates themselves, affliction, death, and the temptations of the highest desire-realm heaven — precisely because they never manage to cross any of these four barriers; the arhat alone has crossed all four completely, and moves through the world with a freedom no onlooker, however devout, can actually perceive.

He explains this invisibility through a double inversion: ordinary people feel ashamed on the arhat's behalf for begging alms rather than keeping their own family wealth, and for pursuing extinction in nirvāṇa rather than a pleasant rebirth in heaven — precisely backward, since it is clinging to wealth and craving further existence that genuinely deserve shame, not their opposites. The same inversion runs through fear: ordinary people find nothing frightening about circling endlessly through saṃsāra, while finding the prospect of nirvāṇa's freedom from self genuinely alarming — again exactly backward, since it is bondage to the four demonic forces that should terrify a thinking person, not release from them. He illustrates how thoroughly conditioned perception can mislead with the well-known story of the Korean monk Wŏnhyo, who drank gratefully from what he took, in pitch darkness, to be a cool spring during a night's journey toward Tang China to study the Dharma — only to discover at dawn a worm-ridden corpse beside the water he had drunk — though at the time he had drunk it gladly and slept soundly, with no ill effect, the water itself unchanged between the two moments. Realizing on the spot that everything is mind-made — the water was neither pure nor foul in itself, only his own perception made it so — he turned back without ever reaching China, having already found, in that single moment of insight, exactly what he had set out so far to find.

13. Sub-Part 18: Four Yokes That Only a Non-Returner Can Truly Cast Off

The eighteenth sub-part explains why identical meditative attainment — even reaching the very peak of conditioned existence — produces two entirely different outcomes depending on whether the meditator is an ordinary being or a genuine non-returner: one remains yoked and eventually falls back to the lower realms, the other is free for good.

Chinese Source

若有熏除諸尋思　於內無餘離分別
超過礙著諸色想　四軛蠲除不往生

Ru-Yi Translation

If one purges every rumination, becomes, within, entirely free of discursive thought, and passes beyond what obstructs and binds and beyond all perceptions of form — the four yokes cast off, one is reborn [in the lower realms] no more.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，已入有學位，未離欲界欲。依初靜慮，熏除欲界諸惡尋思。依第二靜慮，內等清淨心一趣性，初靜慮地所有分別無餘永離，無復分別。依第三靜慮，超過第二靜慮地諸喜礙著；依第四靜慮，超過第三靜慮地諸樂礙著。依無色定，超過一切所有色想。如是漸次，因依諸定，乃至有頂若定、若生，蠲除四軛。何等為四？一、蠲除染污尋思軛，二、蠲除不染污尋思軛，三、蠲除喜樂繫縛軛，四、蠲除一切色想軛。由此因緣，於諸下地不復往生。當知異生雖到有頂若定、若生，猶為四軛所繫縛故，於諸下地還復往生。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，到有邊際有學、異生二種差別。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone has entered the stage of one in training but is not yet free of desire for the desire realm. Relying on the first meditation, they purge the bad thoughts of the desire realm. Relying on the second meditation, with inner clarity and one-pointedness of mind, they leave behind for ever, without remainder, all the discrimination belonging to the stage of the first meditation, and have no further discrimination. Relying on the third meditation, they pass beyond the obstruction and attachment of joy belonging to the stage of the second meditation; relying on the fourth meditation, they pass beyond the obstruction and attachment of happiness belonging to the stage of the third meditation. Relying on the formless attainments, they pass beyond all perceptions of form. Gradually in this way, relying on the concentrations up to the peak of existence, whether in attainment or in rebirth, they cast off the four yokes. What are the four? First, casting off the yoke of defiled thinking; second, casting off the yoke of undefiled thinking; third, casting off the yoke of bondage to joy and happiness; fourth, casting off the yoke of all perceptions of form. For this reason they are not reborn again in the lower stages. It should be understood that ordinary beings, even if they reach the peak of existence in attainment or in rebirth, are still bound by the four yokes, and therefore are reborn again in the lower stages. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the two kinds of difference between those in training and ordinary beings who reach the limit of existence. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the verse onto the successive absorptions: purging defiled rumination over desire, aversion, and harm yields the first absorption — the verse's subject being, in his reading, a trainee at the first or second fruit, not yet free of desire-realm desire, who relies on the first absorption to do this; then purging even that absorption's own wholesome forms of rumination — since these remain, relatively speaking, an agitation — yields the second; then releasing the second absorption's joy yields the third, and releasing the third absorption's pleasure yields the fourth; and finally passing beyond every perception still tied to physical form altogether yields the four formless absorptions, culminating at the very peak of conditioned existence. Named individually, these constitute the "four yokes": the yoke of defiled rumination, the yoke of undefiled rumination, the yoke of attachment to joy-and-pleasure, and the yoke of attachment to form-based perception — cast off, in sequence, on the way from the first absorption to the very summit of saṃsāra.

The crucial distinction the sub-part exists to draw, he stresses, is that a trainee — by this point, he explains, a non-returner — who reaches this same summit has genuinely uprooted the seeds of desire-realm craving along the way, through the wisdom of the four foundations of mindfulness practiced alongside the concentration itself — and so, once yoke-free, stays free, never falling back to a lower realm again. An ordinary meditator who reaches the identical summit through concentration alone, without that accompanying wisdom, has only temporarily suppressed the same afflictions rather than uprooted them; the yokes are loosened, not actually broken, and once the eighty-thousand-eon lifespan available at that peak eventually runs its course, such a being falls right back down into the desire realm exactly as before. Two practitioners can reach the very same meditative peak, in other words, and still end up in entirely different places — one genuinely free, the other simply on an extremely long pause before the same old cycle resumes — a distinction that turns entirely on wisdom, not on depth of concentration.

14. Sub-Part 19: Four Practices That Ripen Pure Faith Into Nirvāṇa

The nineteenth sub-part lays out four progressively deeper practices — generosity, guarding against wrongdoing through patience, sustained cultivation, and the final exhaustion of every affliction — tracing a single continuous path from an ordinary layperson's first steps to an arhat's complete liberation.

Chinese Source

惠施令福增　防非滅怨害　修善捨諸惡　惑盡得涅槃

Ru-Yi Translation

Generosity increases merit; guarding against wrongdoing dissolves enmity and harm; cultivating what is wholesome relinquishes every evil; and the exhaustion of affliction attains nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，於佛所證法毘奈耶獲得正信，雖處居家，而心遠離慳垢纏縛，受持七種依福業事。由此因緣，若行、若住，廣說如經，乃至生長如是福德。若有復能於善說法毘奈耶中清淨出家。既出家已，具足忍力，為護尸羅，雖遭他罵、侵惱、訶責，或以身手、瓦礫、刀杖歐擊傷害；恐壞尸羅，當為障礙，心無惡念，不出惡言，唯緣彼境與慈俱心，於一切方遍滿而住。由此因緣，於現法中自他相續所有怨害，並皆止息；當生無惱樂世界中，無多怨敵，為世欣仰，眾所樂見。如是善修正方便已，依增上戒起增上心，依增上心發增上慧。當於聖諦入現觀時，則能永捨趣惡趣業及諸惡趣。又修如先所得道故，漸次永除所有諸結，於有餘依涅槃界中而般涅槃。如是後時，於無餘依涅槃界中，復般涅槃。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，得淨信者四種正行。一、感財富行；二、感善趣行，三、離惡趣苦，清淨修行；四、離一切苦，清淨修行。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone gains right faith in the Dharma and Vinaya realized by the Buddha; though living in a household, their mind is far from the bondage of the stain of miserliness, and they undertake the seven kinds of meritorious deeds based on material things. For this reason, whether walking or standing, as explained at length in the sutra, such merit grows. Again, there are those who are able to go forth in purity in the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya. Having gone forth, they are endowed with the strength of patience. To guard their precepts, even when others revile them, trouble them or rebuke them, or strike and wound them with hands, potsherds, stones, knives and clubs, they fear damaging their precepts, which would become an obstacle; so they have no evil thought in their mind and utter no evil words, but simply direct toward that object a mind accompanied by loving-kindness and abide pervading all directions with it. For this reason, here and now, all enmity and harm in their own and others' continua comes to rest; and they will be reborn in a happy world free of trouble, without many enemies, admired by the world and delightful for all to see. Having thus cultivated right effort well, relying on higher morality they give rise to higher mind, and relying on higher mind they develop higher wisdom. When they enter direct realization of the noble truths, they are able to abandon for ever the karma that leads to the bad destinies, and the bad destinies themselves. Further, by cultivating the path they had previously attained, they gradually remove for ever all the fetters, and attain final nirvana in the nirvana-element with remainder. Then, later, they attain final nirvana again in the nirvana-element without remainder. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows four kinds of right practice of one who has gained pure faith: first, practice that brings wealth; second, practice that brings a good destiny; third, pure practice that frees from the suffering of the bad destinies; fourth, pure practice that frees from all suffering. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the four lines as four stages on one continuous path. "Generosity increases merit" describes a layperson of genuine faith who cares generously for the seven categories of people they are responsible for — parents, spouse, household help, friends, dependents — free of any stinginess, which steadily builds the wealth and stability that make further practice possible. "Guarding against wrongdoing dissolves enmity" describes someone who has gone forth and, when insulted, struck, or falsely accused, guards their precepts by refusing to answer with anger — meeting the offender instead with the same loving-kindness practiced toward all beings everywhere, since a single moment of hatred can undo a vast accumulation of merit, disturb sleep and appetite alike, and in the worst cases provoke real violence in return; this patience, sustained, brings a peaceful, well-regarded rebirth with few enemies. "Cultivating what is wholesome relinquishes every evil" describes the practitioner who builds concentration on that same foundation of precepts, and wisdom on that concentration, until direct realization of the truths permanently severs the causes and results that lead to the lower realms. And "the exhaustion of affliction attains nirvāṇa" describes the same practitioner's continued cultivation gradually eliminating every remaining fetter, until they enter nirvāṇa with residue while still alive and, at life's end, nirvāṇa without residue at all.

He illustrates the second stage's real difficulty and its reward with a well-known story: a wealthy merchant, returning home unexpectedly late after a long trip, once paid a passing monk a hundred thousand taels of gold for a single verse of advice — "step forward three paces and reflect, step back three paces and consider; when anger rises, pause and weigh it well, and quelling that fire is the most auspicious thing of all" — only to find, entering his darkened bedroom that same night, what looked like two pairs of shoes by his wife's bed. Reaching for a knife in a fury, he suddenly recalled the verse, forced himself to pause and ask first rather than act — and learned that his wife had simply set out an extra pair of shoes for good luck at the new year, missing him terribly, with nothing untoward at all. Master Cháng Bǎi's point is direct: the hundred thousand taels of gold were, in the end, the cheapest thing the merchant ever bought, since the same impulse unchecked would have cost him everything that actually mattered. He closes by summarizing all four lines as one arc — wealth enabling stability, patience enabling harmony, cultivation exhausting the causes of the lower realms, and the complete exhaustion of affliction exhausting suffering itself — the natural order in which pure faith actually ripens into full liberation.

15. Sub-Part 20: The Teaching Shared by No Other Tradition

The twentieth sub-part gives one of Buddhism's single most famous verses — quoted across nearly every branch of the tradition — as the treatise's own answer to what actually sets the Buddha's teaching apart from every other path: not any particular doctrine, but this precise, three-part structure of practice.

Chinese Source

諸惡者莫作　諸善者奉行　自調伏其心　是諸佛聖教

Ru-Yi Translation

Do not commit any evil; uphold and practice every good; tame your own mind completely — this is the teaching of all buddhas.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，於佛所證法毘奈耶獲得正信，於一切種、一切因緣、一切處所所有惡行，皆能斷滅；於善說法毘奈耶中，能善受學尸羅律儀。彼由三相奉行諸善。謂善住尸羅，守別解脫清淨律儀，乃至受學所有學處。依增上戒學發增上心學，依增上心學發增上慧學；彼由此故，於所知境如實知見。如是具足諸善法已，復由三相調伏自心。謂如實知故，能起厭患；由厭患故，能得離染；由離染故，能得解脫。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，三學學果、顯自聖教不與他共。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone gains right faith in the Dharma and Vinaya realized by the Buddha and is able to cut off all bad conduct of every kind, arising from every cause and condition, in every place; in the well-proclaimed Dharma and Vinaya they are able to take up and train well in the restraint of the precepts. They carry out all that is good in three ways. They abide well in the precepts, guarding the pure restraint of the prātimokṣa, and so on up to taking up and training in all the points of training. Relying on the training in higher morality they develop the training in higher mind; relying on the training in higher mind they develop the training in higher wisdom; and because of this they know and see objects of knowledge as they really are. Having thus perfected all wholesome qualities, they further tame their own mind in three ways: because they know as it really is, they are able to give rise to disenchantment; through disenchantment they are able to become free of desire; through freedom from desire they are able to attain liberation. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the three trainings and the fruit of the trainings, and shows that his own noble teaching is not shared with others. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the three lines, with the commentarial gloss (披尋記), as the three trainings and their fruit — the first chiefly precepts, the second all three trainings, the third their fruit — each with its own internal structure. "Do not commit any evil" means someone of genuine faith cutting off every unwholesome action across body, speech, and mind, in every circumstance and toward every kind of object, sentient or otherwise, and then formally taking up the precepts as the concrete expression of that resolve. "Uphold and practice every good" then names six marks of genuinely well-kept precepts — abiding securely in the precepts themselves, guarding each individual rule of the code, perfecting one's conduct and deportment, avoiding disreputable places, treating even minor faults as genuinely alarming, and formally undertaking every training rule — which together form the base from which concentration is built, and concentration in turn the base from which wisdom is built, so that this single line actually contains the whole of the threefold training.

"Tame your own mind completely" then names the fruit of that training as it plays out in three stages: first, genuinely seeing the five aggregates as impermanent, painful, empty, and without self produces real revulsion toward clinging to them as "me" or "mine"; that revulsion, sustained, naturally loosens the grip of craving even without deliberately fighting it moment to moment, since the underlying appetite has already lost its appeal; and that loosened grip, cultivated further, eventually uproots the dormant seeds of affliction entirely rather than merely suppressing their outward expression — genuine liberation, not just good behavior. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in closing is that this compressed sequence — cutting off evil, cultivating good, and taming the mind all the way through to liberation — is precisely what no non-Buddhist path, however rigorous its own ethical code or however deep its own meditative attainment, actually offers as a complete and self-sufficient path, which is why the verse can rightly claim to be, in its own words, the teaching common to every buddha and shared by no other tradition.

16. Sub-Part 21: The Restless Mind, and What Actually Tames It

The twenty-first sub-part names the mind's stubborn, restless attraction to distraction and sense-pleasure as the real obstacle practitioners face — honest about how often practice fails to hold even after real effort — before explaining exactly what steady taming of the mind actually looks like.

Chinese Source

難調伏輕躁　淪墜於諸欲　善調伏其心　心調引安樂

Ru-Yi Translation

Hard to tame, restless, forever sinking into desires — but a mind well tamed, once tamed, brings genuine ease.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂宣說心、若意、若識，長夜愛樂憒鬧雜處，於憒鬧處難得遠離、難可調伏。雖強安處無間修習諸善法中，而不一向能住離貪、離瞋、離癡，亦不一向能住策舉、無掉、寂靜；然復疾疾還生有貪、有瞋、有癡、下劣、掉舉及不寂靜。雖強安處內寂止中，長夜愛樂色聲香味觸故，於五欲境馳趣淪沒。諸聖弟子，於如是等樂著雜染能生苦心，終不縱其令自在轉，亦不隨順；數數思擇成辦遠離，恒修善法心一境性。彼由如是正定心故，能如實知；如實知故，能起厭患；由厭患故，能得離染；由離染故，能得解脫。彼既如是善調伏心盡苦因故，於現法中得安樂住，當來眾苦亦得永盡。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，能不隨順長夜流轉左道之心，及不隨順所得勝利。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains that the mind, also called mentation or consciousness, has for a long time delighted in crowds and bustle, and in the midst of bustle it is hard to keep it apart and hard to tame. Even if it is forcibly settled in the uninterrupted cultivation of wholesome qualities, it cannot invariably abide free of greed, free of anger and free of delusion, nor can it invariably abide roused, without restlessness and at peace; very quickly it again becomes greedy, angry, deluded, sluggish, restless and not at peace. Even if it is forcibly settled in inner calm, because it has for a long time delighted in forms, sounds, smells, tastes and tangibles, it runs toward and sinks into the objects of the five sense pleasures. The noble disciples never let such a mind, which delights in defilement and can give rise to suffering, run free, nor do they go along with it; again and again they reflect, bring about seclusion, and constantly cultivate wholesome qualities and one-pointedness of mind. Through such a rightly concentrated mind they are able to know as it really is; because they know as it really is, they are able to give rise to disenchantment; through disenchantment they are able to become free of desire; through freedom from desire they are able to attain liberation. Because they have thus tamed the mind well and exhausted the causes of suffering, they gain a happy abiding here and now, and future sufferings are also exhausted for ever. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows not going along with the mind that has for a long time drifted along the wrong path, and the benefit gained by not going along with it. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is unusually candid about how honest this verse is regarding the actual experience of practice: even a practitioner sincerely and continuously engaged in wholesome cultivation cannot claim to remain permanently free of craving, aversion, and delusion, or permanently free of dullness and restlessness — the mind, habituated over countless past lives to noise and distraction, keeps sliding back toward craving, aversion, and confusion no matter how strong the initial resolve, and even a mind forcibly settled into stillness for a time keeps drifting back toward the same old attraction to sights, sounds, and sensory pleasure. This is not a sign of failure, he stresses, but simply an accurate description of what an unenlightened mind is actually like — the verse names the problem honestly before offering any solution.

The solution it then gives is the same three-stage sequence seen throughout this fascicle: recognizing this restless, backsliding tendency as itself a source of suffering, refusing to indulge or follow it, and instead repeatedly choosing to cultivate wholesome states and one-pointed concentration — genuine effort sustained specifically through the setbacks, not merely effort applied once and expected to hold. On the foundation of that genuine, settled concentration, seeing the five aggregates as they truly are becomes possible; that seeing produces real revulsion toward clinging to them; that revulsion loosens craving's grip without needing to fight every arising moment by moment; and that loosened grip, sustained further, finally uproots affliction at the root rather than merely suppressing its outward show. Master Cháng Bǎi's closing point is practical: a mind tamed this way, patiently and repeatedly rather than in one decisive stroke, brings genuine ease in the present life and, having exhausted the causes of future suffering along the way, brings that same ease permanently thereafter — the entire path compressed into a single honest observation about how hard the mind actually is to work with, followed by exactly what working with it well requires.

17. Sub-Part 22: Knowing When to Calm, When to Rouse, When to Simply Let Be

The twenty-second sub-part names a specific meditative skill available to a stream-enterer: knowing, moment to moment, whether the mind currently needs calming, rousing, or simply being left in balanced equanimity — a discernment that yields four distinct, describable fruits.

Chinese Source

於心相善知　能餐遠離味　靜慮常委念　受無染喜樂

Ru-Yi Translation

Knowing well the mind's own signs, one savors the taste of seclusion; through meditation, mindfulness constant and thorough, one experiences the ease freed from joy and the untainted ease.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂如有一，有學見迹，能善了知止、舉、捨相。由此因緣，得四功德。謂心住一緣，遠離麁重，能善受用身心安樂；是初功德。又淨定心盡所修故、如所修故，能正審慮諸法道理，獲得內法毘鉢舍那；是第二功德。彼由如是清淨止觀為依止故，於所修習菩提分法，勇猛無間，能常修習、能委修習，無懈無憚；是第三功德。彼由如是無懈憚心，獲得第一正念正知心善解脫，又能受用解脫喜樂及無染樂，於現法中得安樂住；是第四功德。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵此中略示，於相善巧四種功德。謂奢摩他所作、毘鉢舍那所作、無懈憚所作、到究竟所作。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains the following. Someone in training who has seen the track [of the truths] is able to understand well the signs of calm, of exertion and of equanimity. For this reason they gain four virtues. The mind abides on a single object, free of coarseness, and is able to enjoy well the ease of body and mind; this is the first virtue. Again, because the purified concentrated mind has been cultivated to its full extent and in the proper way, they are able rightly to examine and consider the principles of phenomena, and gain insight into inner phenomena; this is the second virtue. Relying on such pure calm and insight, they cultivate the factors of awakening with vigor and without interruption; they are able to cultivate them constantly and thoroughly, without laxity and without weariness; this is the third virtue. Through such a mind without laxity or weariness, they gain foremost right mindfulness and right awareness, their mind is well liberated, and they are also able to enjoy the joy of liberation and untainted happiness, gaining a happy abiding here and now; this is the fourth virtue. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows four kinds of virtue that come from skill in the signs: what calm (śamatha) accomplishes, what insight (vipaśyanā) accomplishes, what freedom from laxity and weariness accomplishes, and what reaching the ultimate accomplishes. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the specific skill this stream-enterer has developed as knowing which of three signs the mind currently calls for: the sign of calming, when the mind needs settling on a stilling object; the sign of rousing, when the mind has grown dull or lax and needs deliberate energizing through an inspiring object; and the sign of equanimity, when the mind is already well-balanced and needs neither adjustment, simply steady, effortless continuation on its object without either excessive effort or excessive slack — like a car already cruising smoothly on the highway, needing neither the brake nor the accelerator. Getting this discernment right, applied moment to moment rather than mechanically, is what separates genuine progress from either force-fed concentration or lazy drift.

He then explains the four resulting fruits in sequence. The first is genuinely tasting the ease of seclusion — the mind, settled on a single object and freed from the coarseness of untrained agitation, able to actually enjoy the bodily and mental calm concentration provides rather than merely enduring the sitting. The second is a genuine, uncontaminated form of insight — examining the specific and general characteristics of experience (each aggregate's own nature, and the shared truth of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self running through all of them) with real clarity rather than borrowed conviction. The third is sustained, wholehearted persistence in cultivating the factors of awakening — continuous and thorough practice free of both laziness and fear, the confidence that comes from having actually seen some of what is being pursued rather than merely hoping it exists. And the fourth, the culmination, is the full six-fold equanimity of complete awakening itself — meeting every sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, and thought without either elation or distress, craving, aversion, and delusion exhausted without remainder, enjoying both the refined pleasure of deep absorption and the undisturbed peace beyond it, genuinely at ease in the present moment. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in laying out all four so precisely is that meditative skill is not vague or unfalsifiable — a practitioner can check their own progress against these four concrete, recognizable milestones rather than simply trusting that something is happening.

18. Sub-Part 23: What a Genuine Monk Actually Looks Like

The twenty-third sub-part draws the arhat's portrait in unusually concrete, everyday terms — how they earn a living, how little they own, how they keep their precepts, what they have permanently stopped being able to do — culminating in a picture not of abstract sanctity but of a specific, describable way of living.

Chinese Source

無工巧活輕自己　樂勝諸根盡解脫
無家無所無希望　斷欲獨行真苾芻

Ru-Yi Translation

No contrived livelihood, holding oneself lightly, delighting in what is excellent, the roots exhausted, and liberated — homeless, possessing nothing, hoping for nothing, desire cut off, walking alone: such is the genuine monk.

Chinese Source

此頌所明，謂成就五支，永斷五支，當知得名真實苾芻。何等為五？謂不依止矯設方便邪活命法，亦不恃賴有勢之家，亦不修治名稱族望，亦不詐受諸佛所說、聖弟子說；猶如依止工巧處所，非法悕求衣服飲食。是名初支。又復減省器物眾具，善棄珍財，衣僅蔽身，食纔充腹，知足歡喜；凡所遊行，必持衣鉢。是第二支。又悕慕沙門、愛樂沙門，悕慕學處、愛樂學處；命難因緣，尚不違越所學禁戒，何況少小利養因緣。是第三支。又彼如是正修方便，淨命喜足，愛樂學處，於諸聖諦未現觀者，能入現觀，得清淨見；或時失念，暫爾發生惡不善尋，引起貪欲、瞋恚、愚癡，遲緩忘念，速復除遣。是第四支。又彼修習如先得道，於諸結、縛、一切隨眠、隨煩惱、纏，心得解脫。是第五支。如是名為成就五支。云何復名永斷五支？謂阿羅漢苾芻，於五處所不復能犯。所謂不能捨所學處，而復退還。又復不能有所貯積，執為己有而受用之，亦不受用諸欲境界。又復不能為財、為命，知而妄語。又復不能棄捨諸欲，行不與取。亦不復能永離貪欲，獨住獨行，而更習近非梵行法，兩兩交會。或計自作而招苦樂，或計他作、或自他作、或非自作亦非他作，不由因生而招苦樂。如是名為五支永斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

This verse explains that one who possesses five factors and has cut off five factors for ever should be understood to be called a genuine monk. What are the five? They do not rely on the wrong livelihood of contrived schemes; they do not depend on powerful families; they do not cultivate fame and reputation for their family line; and they do not falsely take up what was taught by the buddhas and by the noble disciples, using it like a place of crafts to seek clothing and food contrary to the Dharma. This is called the first factor. Again, they reduce their belongings and provisions, give up precious wealth well, wear clothing just enough to cover the body and eat food just enough to fill the belly, and are content and glad; wherever they wander, they always carry their robes and bowl. This is the second factor. Again, they long for the ascetic life and love the ascetic life, long for the points of training and love the points of training; even when their life is in danger they would not transgress the prohibitions they have undertaken to train in, still less for the sake of small gains and honors. This is the third factor. Again, practicing right effort in this way, with pure livelihood and contentment, loving the points of training, they are able to enter direct realization of the noble truths that they have not yet directly realized, and obtain pure vision; and if at times, through loss of mindfulness, bad and unwholesome thoughts briefly arise and stir up greed, anger or delusion, then, though slow to notice through forgetfulness, they quickly remove them again. This is the fourth factor. Again, by cultivating the path previously attained, their mind is liberated from all fetters, bonds, all latent tendencies, secondary afflictions and entanglements. This is the fifth factor. This is called possessing five factors. And how is one called one who has cut off five factors for ever? An arhat monk can no longer transgress in five respects. They cannot give up the points of training and turn back. Again, they cannot store anything up, treating it as their own and using it, nor do they enjoy the objects of sense pleasure. Again, they cannot knowingly tell a lie for the sake of wealth or for the sake of life. Again, having given up sense pleasures, they cannot take what is not given. Nor, being for ever free of sensual greed and dwelling and walking alone, can they again indulge in what is not the holy life, the coupling of two together; or hold that pleasure and pain are brought about by oneself, or by another, or by both oneself and another, or neither by oneself nor by another but arising without a cause. This is called having cut off five factors for ever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes the verse as five things genuinely accomplished and five things permanently given up. Accomplished: a livelihood free of any contrivance — never currying favor with the powerful, never dropping hints about one's own supposed attainment to attract donors, receiving teaching only for the sake of liberation and never to build a reputation; holding oneself lightly — keeping possessions to the barest minimum, robes enough only to cover the body, food enough only to fill the stomach, genuinely content with whatever comes; delighting in what is excellent — cherishing the training rules enough that not even mortal danger, let alone some small material gain, could induce breaking them; the roots exhausted — greed, hatred, and delusion uprooted so thoroughly that even the rare lapse into an unwholesome thought is caught and dismissed almost instantly, the way a drop of water vanishes the moment it lands on a red-hot pan; and liberation itself — the mind genuinely freed from every fetter, every dormant seed, every active manifestation of affliction, through sustained cultivation built on the realization already attained.

Permanently given up: no home, meaning never again abandoning the precepts to return to lay life; no possessions, meaning never again hoarding or claiming anything as genuinely "mine" to enjoy; no hope, meaning never again lying — not even under threat to life or livelihood, since nothing is left to protect by lying; desire cut off, meaning, in the treatise's prose, having relinquished desires, no longer able to take what is not given; and walking alone, meaning dwelling and walking alone, free of craving, no longer able to engage in sexual intercourse (in his reading of the verse itself, also a mind that takes nothing as its companion). He traces the deeper root beneath all five relinquishments to a single correct understanding: the arhat has fully seen through dependent origination and so holds none of the four mistaken theories about where pleasure and pain actually come from — not that a truly existing self produces them, not that another causes them, not that self and other jointly cause them, and not that they arise from no cause at all — the first three what he calls theories of a wrong cause, the fourth a theory of no cause. Only this thoroughgoing insight into non-self, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is what actually makes every one of the five relinquishments effortless and permanent rather than a rule grimly enforced from outside — the whole portrait resolving, in the end, into a single coherent picture of what genuinely seeing through self-clinging looks like in an ordinary daily life of eating, dressing, and walking from town to town.

The twenty-fourth sub-part offers one of the treatise's most striking psychological portraits: the mind across three times, described through four images — traveling immeasurably far, arising utterly alone, having no fixed "body" of its own, and lying dormant like something asleep in a cave — before naming whoever genuinely tames this mind the true "brahmin."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the verse's four images onto mind across the three times. "Traveling far" describes the mind's past: across countless past lives, in an incalculable variety of bodies and circumstances, this same continuum of experience has kept moving forward, driven by karma and craving, with no findable starting point and no single "self" ever actually doing the traveling — dependent arising without an agent behind it. "Traveling alone" and "having no body" together describe the mind's present: unlike the physical body, which stays recognizably the same kind of thing from birth to death, mental states arise strictly one at a time, never two together, each one conditioned into being and then gone the instant its condition passes — sometimes greed, sometimes its opposite, sometimes anger, sometimes calm, with no fixed underlying "nature" that is inherently defiled, since the mind's own baseline is neutral and only becomes defiled when paired, moment by moment, with whatever affliction happens to be present. "Lying dormant in its cave" describes the mind's future: whatever craving and karma have been sown now lie stored, unseen, as latent seeds carried forward — like someone asleep in a cave, present but not yet active — ready to ripen into the next rebirth whenever the right conditions arrive.

He explains the closing two lines as the whole point of walking through these four images in the first place: someone genuinely wise, having clearly seen the mind's past as agentless drift, its present as neutral and momentary rather than fixed, and its future as entirely a function of present choices, uses exactly this understanding to cultivate revulsion toward clinging, freedom from desire, and finally cessation itself — crossing entirely beyond the view of a permanent self and reaching, at last, solid ground. Only such a person, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, deserves the title "brahmin" in its true sense — not by birth or claimed status, but because they have done the one thing genuinely difficult to do: taming a mind that by its very nature never sits still.

20. Sub-Part 25: Five Questions From Maitreya — What Covers the World, and What Finally Ends It

The twenty-fifth sub-part, the longest in the entire fourteenth topic, stages five questions the bodhisattva Maitreya puts to the Buddha directly — drawn from the well-known "Questions of Ajita" chapter — moving from what obscures ordinary beings' minds, through how the resulting affliction actually gets stopped, to exactly what ceases when an arhat enters final nirvāṇa. This section covers the first two exchanges.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three distinct groups behind the first answer's three causes. Non-Buddhist renunciants remain covered by outright ignorance — no genuine grasp of non-self at all, so nothing they do escapes the wheel. Buddhist monastics who have gone forth into the correct teaching but practice half-heartedly remain covered by heedlessness — the wisdom is theoretically available, but laziness keeps it from ever actually shining forth. And laypeople still absorbed in ordinary life remain stained by idle speculation — endless rumination over past, present, and future, fueled equally by ordinary conversation and by private daydreaming, none of it grounded in anything real, all of it generating fresh craving and aversion. All three, left unaddressed, generate the one thing genuinely worth fearing: not any particular hardship, but the suffering of future rebirth itself, since none of these three has yet done anything to stop generating its causes.

The second exchange then asks how this leaking actually gets stopped, and gives two distinct answers operating at two distinct depths: mindfulness — hearing genuine teaching and applying sound reasoning to it — can suppress the leaking in the moment, holding the six sense-gates from spilling into fresh affliction the way a hand might hold back a trickle; but only uncontaminated wisdom, the direct realization that actually uproots the underlying tendency rather than merely restraining its expression, can dam the flood for good. Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to name both as genuinely valuable rather than treating suppression as somehow inferior — sustained mindfulness is what makes the eventual breakthrough to wisdom possible in the first place, the two working as one continuous practice rather than two alternatives to choose between.

The fifth and final exchange turns from the arhat back to the trainee still walking the path, describing a whole graduated sequence: a stream-enterer working through as many as seven more rounds of rebirth, or the intermediate "family to family" grades who need only two or three more, continuing to purify the mind of the five lower fetters — self-view, attachment to rites and rituals, doubt, and desire-realm craving and aversion — until reaching the non-returner's freedom from all five; and a non-returner then continuing to purify the five upper fetters — craving for form and formless-realm experience, restlessness, conceit, and the last residue of ignorance — until nothing remains to purify at all. Master Cháng Bǎi closes the entire sub-part by noting that four of these five exchanges (all but the very first, which simply diagnoses the problem) describe unambiguously wholesome, purifying territory — mindfulness stopping the leak, wisdom damming it for good, and the graduated purification that carries a trainee all the way to an arhat's final, complete cessation — the fullest possible answer, across this fascicle, to what separates a life still generating suffering from one that has genuinely stopped.

Ru-Yi Translation

This is a verse from the Chapter on Meanings (Arthavargīya) concerning sense pleasures. Someone longs for sense pleasures in the future; to obtain them they make diligent efforts, and having obtained them, they cling to them and enjoy them as they come. For this reason, from such longing for and actual enjoyment of the sense pleasures obtained, joy and happiness arise. All this together is called the gratification of sense pleasures. Again, when they lose what they have obtained and enjoyed of the sense pleasures they longed for and actually enjoyed, then, following their attachment to and relishing of those sense pleasures, the arrow of craving enters their heart, as though struck by a poisoned arrow, and they suffer great sorrow and pain, or even die. This is called the danger of sense pleasures. Again, a poisonous snake is a simile for the objects of sense pleasure; the poisonous snake's head is a simile for the gratification in sense pleasures. Fools who relish sense pleasures and cling to enjoying them are like those bitten by a snake. Learned noble disciples keep far from the gratification in sense pleasures as from a poisonous snake's head, and never enjoy them with craving and defilement, and so on at length up to not giving rise to clinging. They are able to tame, cut off and pass beyond all greed and craving for forms, and so on up to all greed and craving for tangibles. This is called the escape from sense pleasures. Again, the nature of sense pleasures is in brief of two kinds: first, desire for objects; second, desire as affliction. Desire for objects is of two kinds. The first is grain; its basis is fields. The second is wealth; its basis is gold, silver and the like. Why? Those who seek grain must seek fields; those who seek wealth must seek gold, silver and the like. Seeking gold, silver and the like is again of two kinds: first, serving a king; second, trading. Seeking grain and seeking fields requires cattle as a means. Seeking wealth by serving a king requires horses as a means. The means of seeking wealth by trading, where connected with gold and silver, are jewels; where of a kind other than gold and silver and not connected with them, they are bracelets and the like. These are mentioned as the foremost examples. Buying and selling, talk and business should be understood likewise. When such wealth and grain have been accumulated, the companions for enjoyment and amusement are women. When they have not yet been accumulated, the helpers in gathering them, guarding them and earning interest are servants. When such wealth and grain have been accumulated on a vast scale, one delights in this place and will not let it go. All this is called desire for objects. Desire as affliction means that, following and relishing desire for objects, relying on a consciousness attached to them, various kinds of greed based on false discrimination arise. Again, with respect to desire for objects, desire as affliction makes the mind sink and become inferior. If those desired objects decay or are scattered and lost, the outflows arise, and lamentation, sorrow, grief and various sufferings and torments wrap around the mind. In this way, overcome here and now by the outflows and without any antidote, like a ship that is breached and gradually fills with water, they bring on the various sufferings and torments of future birth, old age, sickness and so on. If one has already escaped from sense pleasures, one is able to cut off for ever the defiled consciousness that follows the relishing of sense pleasures and gives rise to greed and clinging, like a palm tree with its crown cut off, which grows no further. Again, when objects of desire that are lovely, delightful, and so on up to pleasing, decay, no sorrow or other suffering can rest in the purified consciousness, just as a drop of water does not cling to a lotus leaf. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the threefold nature of sense pleasures: gratification, danger and escape. It also shows that gratification can become a danger, and the virtues of escape from it. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the sequence exactly as the verses present it. The first verse names desire's genuine, undeniable savor — real pleasure follows genuinely getting what one wanted, and that pleasure is fiercely guarded once obtained. The second names the hidden cost: since everything obtained this way is impermanent, its eventual loss produces suffering sharp enough to visibly change a person's complexion, likened to the wound of a poisoned arrow — real grief, not exaggeration, which is why people sometimes take their own lives over lost wealth or lost love. The third names the way out: treating desire's pleasure itself as dangerous as a venomous snake's head (the most lethal part of the animal), a genuinely learned disciple neither indulges nor merely represses craving, but through sustained hearing, reflection, and cultivation actually subdues, severs, and finally transcends attachment to sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch altogether.

The fourth and fifth verses then break desire down into its two working parts, grounded in wholly ordinary economic detail: circumstantial desire — grain, which needs a field and an ox to work it; wealth, pursued either through government service (needing a horse, or today a car, to get around) or through trade (needing money, jewels, or bartered goods, plus the whole exhausting business of travel, hardship, risk, and persuasive talk that trade actually requires) — and, once wealth accumulates, the companions that desire itself recruits: women for pleasure, servants and employees for managing and growing the fortune further, the whole apparatus becoming something a person then can't bear to let go of. And afflictive desire — the actual craving mind riding on top of all that circumstantial desire, which is where the real danger lives: not the field or the gold themselves, but the mind's own grasping at them, which drags a person down into a genuinely degraded mental state and, the moment any of it decays or is lost, floods the mind with grief exactly the way water floods a breached ship, generating the karma that produces still more suffering down the line.

The sixth and final verse names the reward of genuinely cutting this off at the root: like a palm tree whose crown has been severed — which, unlike most trees, never sprouts again — a mind that has truly relinquished desire's underlying craving no longer generates the sorrow that follows its inevitable losses; whatever pleasant or unpleasant circumstance arises, defiled mental states simply find no foothold, sliding off exactly the way a water droplet slides off a lotus leaf rather than soaking in. Master Cháng Bǎi closes by summarizing the whole sequence as building from three foundational insights — desire's savor, its danger, and the path of renunciation — through to the practical payoff: both what indulged desire actually costs, and what its genuine relinquishment actually buys.

23. Sub-Part 27, the Final Sub-Part: The Five Marks That Forge a Genuinely Virtuous Practitioner

The twenty-seventh and final sub-part of the fourteenth topic closes the entire twenty-seven-part "discourse" cycle with a complete map: five marks tracing a single unbroken path from the moment of first going forth to full and unshakeable liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the verse's five marks onto five successive stages of practice. First, no longing: someone who has genuinely gone forth stops looking back at the household life left behind, neither pining for family nor letting the mind wander home out of habit. Second, no craving for the future: the same practitioner stops angling for future reputation or material gain, and no longer seeks rebirth among humans or gods, since chasing tomorrow's recognition is, as the tradition puts it, no different from vomiting up food only to eat it again. Third, thorough observation of the present, itself unfolding in three stages: first coming to genuinely understand the five aggregates of clinging that make up one's own experience — what form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness actually are, not as abstractions but as the concrete basis any practice has to work with; then disciplining body, speech, and mind directly, abandoning the ten unwholesome courses of action and cultivating their wholesome opposites; and finally applying real wisdom to what has been observed and disciplined — seeing the five aggregates, across past, present, and future alike, as impermanent, and therefore painful, and therefore without any findable self to grasp as "me" or "mine."

He illustrates this final stage's stakes with the story — drawn from a well-known documentary film — of a fifteen-year-old girl diagnosed with a rare degenerative disorder, who chose, once told the truth about her prognosis, to spend her remaining time recording each stage of her body's changing relationship to her own will: losing her balance, losing the ability to focus her eyes or thread a needle, eventually losing nearly all voluntary movement. Her own journal entries describe her body coming to feel like it no longer belonged to her — an unintentional but exact description of non-self, arrived at not through philosophy but through direct, unwanted experience. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in bringing her story into this teaching is not the illness itself but what it reveals by contrast: that an ordinary, healthy body able to sit, hold a cup, and think clearly is already an extraordinary convergence of causes and conditions, entirely dependent and impermanent, and worth using well precisely because it cannot be taken for granted.

The fourth and fifth marks then describe what this observation yields once genuinely realized: reaching the direct insight of the path of seeing makes the resulting confidence and accumulated merit and wisdom "unseizable" — no king, no rival teaching, nothing external can take it away, since it was never externally given in the first place; and reaching full arhatship makes the mind "unshakeable" — no affliction, however subtle, can disturb it any longer. Master Cháng Bǎi closes by noting that even a single day and night of practice along these same five marks — genuinely letting go of the past and future, and observing the present with this threefold clarity — already surpasses every other form of spiritual discipline that lacks this precise a map, which is exactly why the treatise places this sub-part last: it is less a new teaching than the whole path, quietly restated in miniature.

Fascicle 19 Complete: All Fourteen Topics of the Essential-Body Verses, Finished

With sub-part 27's close, the fourteenth topic — "discourse," twenty-seven sub-parts in its own right — is finished. And with it, the entire essential-body-verse cycle (建立體義伽他) that opened in fascicle 17, continued through fascicle 18, and concludes here is complete: all fourteen topics, from the opening teaching on avoiding all evil through this closing map of a genuinely virtuous life. The treatise itself marks the occasion with a summary verse naming all fourteen in sequence — evil, teaching, craving, the flood, fear, kinds, renown, the pool, the flood again, craving again, what should be done, toil, attaining benefit, and discourse — the last of the fourteen alone containing the twenty-seven sub-parts making up nearly half the material spread across fascicles 18 and 19 combined. Master Cháng Bǎi closes this entire arc by observing that the "essence" verses (the first thirteen topics) distill the whole of the teaching down to its single most load-bearing line — "do no evil, do only good, purify one's own mind, this is the teaching of all buddhas" — while the "meaning" verses (topic fourteen's twenty-seven sub-parts) unpack that single line's full implications in every direction, primarily oriented, at this stage of the treatise, toward the path of the individual liberation-seeker rather than the bodhisattva path the treatise will take up in far greater depth beginning around fascicle 35.

This fascicle's source material ends here at a genuine and major structural close: with the fourteen-topic essential-body-verse cycle finished in full, the Ground of What Is Attained Through Reflection itself, as Elder Miàojìng notes, is now complete, and fascicle 20 opens the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation.
