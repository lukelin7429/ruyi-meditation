Fascicle 19 · 瑜伽師地論卷第十九 · The Essential-Body Verses, Topic Fourteen Continues

What Makes Speech Genuinely Good

Fascicle 19 continues directly from fascicle 18's stopping point, mid-way through the fourteenth and largest of the essential-body-verse topics — "discourse," twenty-seven sub-parts in all. It opens with the sixth sub-part: what separates a noble one's speech from ordinary speech, and why the wise are said to speak only what is pleasing, undefiling, and wholly good.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 19 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Topic Fourteen, Sub-Part 6: What Makes Speech Genuinely Good

Continuing directly from fascicle 18, the fourteenth topic's sixth sub-part names three qualities that distinguish a noble one's speech from ordinary speech — pleasing, undefiling, and wholly good — then examines a subtlety: these three do not always travel together, and speech genuinely worth calling "good" needs the third far more than the other two.

Chinese Source

賢聖嘗說最善語　愛非不愛語第二
諦非不諦語第三　法非非法語第四

Ru-Yi Translation

The noble ones always speak the most excellent speech: pleasing, not displeasing, first; true, not false, second; in accord with the Dharma, not against it, third.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the three qualities as pleasing speech that genuinely delights the hearer, undefiling speech that is simply true, and wholly good speech that actually leads someone out of an unwholesome state and into a wholesome one — and stresses that only the last of the three is indispensable, since the other two can each occur without it. Flattery that praises a nonexistent virtue is pleasing but neither true nor good, since it only reinforces the hearer's vanity. A harsh rebuke driven by real anger can be entirely true — the fault really was committed — yet be neither pleasing nor good, since it lands as an attack rather than genuine correction, even if the underlying facts are accurate; he notes this differs from a parent's stern correction of a child, which despite its harsh tone is still genuinely aimed at the child's good and so counts as both true and good speech at once. And Dharma-teaching that neither praises nor blames anyone in particular, simply presenting the teaching itself, can be wholly good and genuinely lead someone toward liberation without being calculated to please or aimed at correcting any specific fault.

He illustrates the real-world cost of speaking well without judging the occasion with the well-known story of the monk Mahālla, who begged the wiser Śāriputra to teach him a beautiful verse of blessing after watching it earn Śāriputra two fine blankets from a delighted patron celebrating a run of good fortune — only to recite that same verse, word for word, to the same household on a later visit when tragedy had struck them instead, and to be beaten and driven off for it; his blind attempts afterward to patch the mistake by parroting whatever phrase he'd last been scolded with (offered indiscriminately to a funeral procession, then a wedding party, then a hunter, then a laundress) earned him beating after beating until the Buddha finally explained, gently, that genuine blessing-speech requires reading the occasion correctly, not simply reciting fine words learned by rote. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it is that goodness in speech is inseparable from timing and context — the same words can bless or wound depending entirely on whether they actually fit the moment and the listener in front of you.

2. Sub-Part 7: The Fruit of Genuine Practice, for Renunciant and Householder Alike

The seventh sub-part names five qualities that make up genuine practice — faith, a sense of shame, precepts, generosity, and, for the ordained, the Dharma itself — and the three benefits that follow from them, tracing the path slightly differently for monastics and for laypeople.

Chinese Source

信慚戒施法　善人所稱讚　是名趣天道　能往天世間

Ru-Yi Translation

Faith, a sense of shame, precepts, generosity, and the Dharma — this is what the good praise, this is called the path to the heavens, and by this one is able to go to the heavenly worlds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the five qualities as they unfold for a monastic: genuine confidence in the Buddha's own realized teaching; a sense of shame at remaining a householder at all, since household life is saturated with desire in a way that blocks even the first step toward freedom; taking and keeping the precepts, then living simply and contentedly on whatever robes, food, and bedding come one's way, without demanding more; practicing generosity even as a renunciant, by never privately hoarding any properly acquired offering but sharing it with fellow practitioners; and immersing oneself in the Dharma itself — receiving it through hearing, working it through by reflection, and finally penetrating it through cultivation, then passing it on to others out of genuine compassion. He explains the three resulting benefits: being praised by the Buddhas and their genuinely awakened disciples, since only those who have themselves seen the truth are qualified to recognize it in another; being carried, once the accompanying merit and wisdom are sufficiently complete, toward rebirth among the pure abodes reserved for non-returners; and, even where that fullness isn't yet reached, being guaranteed rebirth in a fortunate realm regardless, since a stream-enterer or once-returner can no longer fall to the three lower realms at all.

For laypeople the same five qualities take a lay shape — faith in the teaching, shame at breaking precepts or clinging to wealth once one understands where such conduct leads, taking whatever precepts fit one's circumstances, and simple generosity — with the Dharma-study and teaching-others piece typically less developed, so the promised fruit is correspondingly the praise of the wise and heavenly rebirth, without the specific promise of the pure abodes reserved for advanced monastic practice. He illustrates lay devotion's power with the well-known story of a servant named Guṇamati, executed by the tyrant king Ajātaśatru simply for cleaning and lighting incense at a neglected memorial stūpa built by his imprisoned father in the Buddha's honor — who was reborn instantly among the gods for that single act of devotion, and upon hearing the Buddha teach in her new heavenly life, immediately attained stream-entry. A believing heart, he notes, is available to anyone regardless of ordination status, and its fruit is no less real for that.

3. Sub-Part 8: How Extensive Learning Leads All the Way to Nirvāṇa

The eighth sub-part traces genuine learning across four stages, from simply knowing the teaching through to nirvāṇa itself — the first two belonging to the mundane path shared by anyone seeking a favorable rebirth, the last two belonging to the supramundane path that actually ends saṃsāra.

Chinese Source

多聞能知法　多聞能遠惡　多聞捨無義　多聞得涅槃

Ru-Yi Translation

Through extensive learning one knows the teaching; through extensive learning one keeps far from evil; through extensive learning one relinquishes what is meaningless; through extensive learning one attains nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first two lines as the mundane path: hearing and genuinely understanding teaching on generosity, precepts, and the causes of favorable rebirth is "knowing the teaching," while actually living by that understanding — steering clear of the unwholesome actions that lead to misfortune and cultivating the ones that lead to a good rebirth — is "keeping far from evil." He stresses that real learning always completes itself in changed conduct; hearing without living accordingly is only information, not the multiple learning the verse has in mind.

The remaining two lines then move to the supramundane path. "Relinquishing what is meaningless" is hearing and genuinely grasping the Four Noble Truths and dependent origination well enough to see that saṃsāra's endless round of rebirth, however elaborated, is ultimately without lasting value compared to nirvāṇa's peace — a shift from merely accumulating merit within saṃsāra to actually wanting out of it. "Attaining nirvāṇa" then plays out two different ways depending on how ripe a practitioner's faculties already are: someone whose faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom are already mature, with the necessary merit and wisdom fully accumulated, can move directly from hearing the teaching into direct realization of the truths, or, if already a stream-enterer, straight through to full liberation; someone whose faculties are not yet mature simply keeps steadily avoiding unwholesome action, building concentration on the foundation of precepts, and building wisdom on the foundation of concentration, trusting — the way an object released from a height is bound to fall — that sustained practice on this path leads to nirvāṇa exactly as surely, even without knowing in advance how long the road will be.

He illustrates the transformative power of hearing the Dharma even from someone hostile to it with the well-known story of a bitter, faithless housewife in Kauśāmbī who slammed the door on a begging monk with contemptuous abuse, only to watch him instantly manifest as a bloated, rotting corpse before her eyes — and, fleeing to fetch her husband, found the "corpse" following her, whole and unharmed, the moment she looked back. When her husband arrived with a blade to deal with the monk, the monk conjured a crystal house no weapon could breach, then asked him to set down not the sword in his hand but the sword still in his heart — a request so uncannily accurate that the man's hostility collapsed into genuine remorse on the spot, and both husband and wife, hearing the Dharma the monk then taught them, attained stream-entry within that same encounter. Master Cháng Bǎi's point is that this same power — described afterward by the Buddha simply as tireless, disciplined, unflagging learning joined to unflagging practice — is available to anyone who commits to it the same way, not a special gift reserved for the already-devout.

4. Sub-Part 9: Unstained as Space, Unshaken as Indra's Banner

The ninth sub-part paints the freed mind in a single, memorable image: untouched by anything the way space itself cannot be stained, unshaken the way the banner of the god Indra never falls regardless of which way the wind blows, and finding no pleasure at all in returning to the "muddy heat" of saṃsāra once it has tasted the cool, clear pool of genuine release.

Chinese Source

智者如空無染汙　不動猶如天帝幢
如泛清涼盈滿池　不樂淤泥生死海

Ru-Yi Translation

The wise one is like space, unstained; unshaken, like the banner of the god of the devas; drifting as though on a cool, brimming pool, taking no pleasure in the muddy sea of birth-and-death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the four images across three complementary lenses. Taken as a portrait of full arhatship alone, the first line describes a mind wholly beyond the eight worldly winds — gain and loss, praise and blame, fame and disgrace, pleasure and pain — untouched the way space itself cannot be dirtied by anything thrown into it; the middle two lines describe a non-returner still advancing toward arhatship, whose mind, settled in the signless concentration through sustained mindfulness practice, can no longer be shaken by self-conceit, by any of those same eight worldly winds, or by any residual longing for desire-realm rebirth — as stable as Indra's own banner, which legend held could never be blown down; and the closing lines describe that same non-returner's own inner motivation, still willing to relinquish even the deep, refined pleasure of form- and formless-realm meditative absorption — itself compared here to inviting but ultimately stifling "hot mud" — for the truly cool, open freedom of complete liberation.

Taken instead as one continuous portrait of the arhat alone, he explains, the same four images simply describe three facets of a single life: undefiled in the ordinary business of eating, speaking, and traveling about, since none of it is grasped at or clung to; unshaken because completely without attachment to any mental image at all; and taking no pleasure in continued existence, since even a body sustained by residual past karma remains, in the end, simply one more thing to be tired of. And taken as a threefold typology of arhats themselves, the four images distinguish the arhat liberated by wisdom alone (undefiled, but not yet master of the deepest absorptions), the advanced trainee who has personally verified the deepest absorption in the body though not yet full liberation (unshaken), and the arhat doubly liberated in both wisdom and concentration, master of cessation-attainment itself (taking no pleasure in continued existence at all) — three real, well-attested profiles of accomplished practice, not competing interpretations so much as three angles on what full freedom from desire actually looks like in practice.

5. Sub-Part 10: Seeing the Buddha's Form Is Not the Same as Knowing the Buddha

The tenth sub-part echoes what would later become one of the best-known verses in the entire Buddhist canon, from the Diamond Sūtra: seeing the Buddha's physical form, or hearing his voice, is not the same as truly knowing him. Five verses lay out four ways of failing to know the Buddha and one way of genuinely succeeding.

Chinese Source

若以色量我　以音聲尋我　欲貪所執持　彼不能知我
若於內了知　於外不能見　由內果觀察　彼音聲所引
若於內無知　於外而能見　由外果觀察　亦音聲所引
若於內無知　於外不能見　彼普障愚夫　亦音聲所引
若於內了知　於外亦能見　英雄出離慧　非音聲所引

Ru-Yi Translation

If one measures me by form, or seeks me by sound — held fast by desire and craving, such a one cannot know me. If one knows within but cannot see without, seeing only through the fruit of inner meditation, such a one is still led by mere sound. If one has no knowledge within yet can see without, seeing only through the fruit of outer sense, such a one is still led by mere sound. If one has no knowledge within and cannot see without, wholly obstructed and confused, such a one too is led by mere sound. But if one knows within and also sees without — the hero possessed of the wisdom of renunciation — such a one is not led by mere sound.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi works through four ways ordinary beings mistake seeing the Buddha for knowing him. The first simply reasons from outward signs — the thirty-two marks of a great being, eloquent teaching, evidently virtuous followers — to the conclusion "this must be a buddha," without any inner realization at all; lacking that realization, such a person remains fully vulnerable to bad company and wrong teaching, and history shows this type does go on to slander the Three Jewels when a persuasive enough false teacher comes along. The second has cultivated the four absorptions and gained the divine eye, so can see the Buddha directly across great distances in meditation — a real attainment, but one still unaccompanied by liberating wisdom, and so just as vulnerable to being talked out of right view by a plausible outsider. The third has only the ordinary physical eye, perhaps living during the Buddha's own lifetime and lucky enough to see him directly — vivid, but equally unprotected without wisdom. The fourth has neither: no meditative sight, no physical sight, whether from blindness or simply from being born after the Buddha's final passing — the most thoroughly obstructed case of all, left with nothing but secondhand report to build any confidence on.

Only the fifth type — a genuine noble one, from the preparatory stages through full realization, whose desire has actually been abandoned and uprooted rather than merely suppressed — truly knows the Buddha, and Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to explain exactly what that means: inwardly, through meditation joined with insight, actually realizing the Buddha's dharma-body — that is, awakening to the same dependent-arising, empty-of-self-nature reality the Buddha himself awakened to, since a buddha's deepest identity was never the physical body at all but this realization itself; and outwardly, still perfectly able to look at a Buddha statue, a stūpa, or a painted image and correctly recognize it as a representation rather than the ultimate reality it points to, without either dismissing it or mistaking the pointer for the moon. Such a person's confidence in the Three Jewels can never be talked away by any outside voice, because it was never secondhand to begin with. He closes with the well-known story of the nun Utpalavarṇā, who used her psychic power to transform into a wheel-turning king so she could push to the very front of the crowd welcoming the Buddha back from teaching his mother in the heavens — only to be told, once she reverted to her own form and bowed, that she had not actually been first to see him at all: the elder Subhūti, who had stayed behind in his cave simply contemplating emptiness rather than joining the crowd, had genuinely seen the Buddha first, precisely because seeing dependent origination itself is what seeing the Buddha actually means.

6. Sub-Part 11: Even Attachment to Meditation Itself Is Still Defilement

The eleventh sub-part turns to a subtler trap than ordinary sense-desire: a practitioner who has genuinely mastered the five sense-based cravings but still clings, however refined the object, to the mind's own experience of concentration — the sixth and most powerful of the six sense-bases, still capable of its own defilement.

Chinese Source

第六增上王　染時染自取　於無染不染　染者名愚夫

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth is the sovereign — when defiled, it defiles by grasping for itself; where there is no defilement, nothing is defiled; whoever is defiled is called a fool.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the sixth" as mind itself — the sixth sense-base governing the other five, called "sovereign" because it is what actually directs and makes use of them. His focus falls on a specific and easily missed case: someone who has already crossed the "flood" of the five sense-desires, mastering attraction to sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and textures well enough to attain genuine meditative absorption, can still remain caught by a sixth, subtler flood — craving the very peace and pleasure of that absorption itself, treating the stillness of concentration as "mine" the same way an untrained mind treats a beautiful object as desirable. This, he stresses, is still craving in every meaningful sense, and it still plants the same seed for future suffering, since attachment even to the most refined states of form- and formless-realm concentration still binds a being to further rebirth within those realms — rebirth that, however pleasant by comparison to the lower realms, remains rebirth all the same.

He explains the antidote as the same fourfold mindfulness practice used everywhere else in the treatise, applied here specifically to the mind observing its own experience of concentration: recognizing craving for absorption as a genuine danger the moment it arises, refusing to let the mind simply follow that craving's pull, and repeatedly training the mind to rest in freedom from craving rather than in the craved object itself — at which point, once this defilement is genuinely uprooted rather than merely suppressed, no further defiled result follows either, since a cause that no longer arises produces no further fruit. Someone who instead simply follows wherever this subtle craving leads, without either restraining it or applying any deliberate remedy, remains — whatever their level of meditative accomplishment — still bound to suffering by that same craving, and the treatise does not hesitate to call such a person, however accomplished their concentration, simply a fool. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this is in some ways a harder trap to escape than ordinary sense-desire, since a meditator confident in their own attainment is often the least willing to hear correction from anyone whose concentration is less advanced than their own — precisely the stubbornness that keeps this particular defilement so persistent.

7. Sub-Part 12: The Fortress of Bone That Locks Wholesome Roots Out

The twelfth sub-part pictures the mind as a fortress — walled with bone, bound with sinew, plastered with flesh — held under siege from within by four defilements: craving and aversion, which trap the householder still absorbed in worldly desire, and conceit and concealment, which trap even someone who has gone forth, if that going-forth was into a mistaken teaching.

Chinese Source

有城骨為牆　筋肉而塗飾　其中有貪恚　慢覆所任持

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a city walled with bone, plastered over with sinew and flesh — and within it, craving and aversion, conceit and concealment, hold their ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the "city" as the mind itself — the eighth consciousness that gathers experience, the seventh that constantly grasps at it as "self," and the six that actively perceive — housed within a body built, quite literally, from bone as its stonework, sinew as its binding cord, and flesh as its plaster. He then explains why four particular defilements are named as the city's occupying forces rather than any others: craving and aversion belong to the ordinary householder still absorbed in sense-pleasure, who clings to whatever is pleasing and resents whatever is not, remaining thereby thoroughly unreceptive to teaching that would ask them to give any of it up. Conceit and concealment belong instead to someone who has gone forth — but into a mistaken teaching, so that whatever confidence or apparent attainment they gain becomes simply one more reason for pride, and whatever fault they commit gets hidden away rather than honestly disclosed, precisely the two habits that make correction from any wiser source impossible to receive even when it is freely offered out of compassion.

He extends the warning to Buddhist practitioners themselves, noting this danger is not confined to outsiders: a monastic who gains a little meditative stability, or simply arrives already carrying unexamined convictions from an earlier life or an earlier teacher, can develop exactly the same conceit and concealment, becoming just as closed to correction as any outsider — the city's walls hold just as firmly whether the fortress belongs to a non-Buddhist ascetic or to someone technically ordained within the Buddha's own teaching. He closes with a well-known verse comparing genuine practice to guarding a walled city: watching for the six sense-thieves by day, staying alert through the night as well, so that when the mind's own general — clear awareness — is truly in command, no weapons are even needed to keep the peace, since a thief who finds no opening simply has nothing to steal.

8. Sub-Part 13: Drawing In Like a Tortoise, Rising Through the Absorptions

The thirteenth sub-part offers one of this fascicle's most vivid images: a practitioner advancing through the absorptions by drawing the mind in exactly as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, growing progressively less disturbed and more genuinely peaceable to live alongside — the sign of real, not merely apparent, progress.

Chinese Source

如龜藏支於自殼　苾芻善攝意尋思
無所依止不惱他　證般涅槃無所謗

Ru-Yi Translation

As a tortoise draws its limbs into its own shell, the monk skillfully draws in the mind's own rumination — relying on nothing, troubling no one — and realizes final nirvāṇa, beyond all reproach.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the tortoise image through the successive absorptions. Attaining the first absorption means abandoning the three unwholesome kinds of rumination — over desire, over aversion, over harm — the way a tortoise pulls all six limbs inside its shell where no predator can reach them; the mind, similarly drawn in from the six sense-objects, becomes correspondingly hard to disturb. But he stresses a practitioner does not stop there: even the first absorption's own wholesome forms of applied and sustained thought are, relatively speaking, still an agitation worth relinquishing, so the same withdrawing motion repeats — releasing the first absorption's rumination to reach the second, then its joy to reach the third, then its pleasure to reach the fourth, and on upward through each of the formless spheres to the very peak of conditioned existence. Progress through the entire ladder, in other words, is really one repeated gesture of letting go rather than a series of unrelated achievements.

The verse's third phrase, "relying on nothing, troubling no one," describes what genuine progress looks like from the outside: a practitioner who does not become attached to any level of concentration attained, remains easy to live alongside, gentle enough to draw others toward wanting to practice near them, and free of the anger that would otherwise make daily community life a source of constant friction. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this social ease is itself a diagnostic — someone whose meditative attainment leaves them harder to get along with, more irritable, or more inclined to look down on others has misunderstood what the practice is actually for, since real freedom from craving naturally expresses itself as freedom from ill will as well. Carried all the way through, this same letting-go finally issues in nirvāṇa itself — a conclusion so thoroughly earned, he notes, that it leaves no honest ground for anyone, of any persuasion, to find fault with it.
