Fascicle 88 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十八 · The Aggregates Section Concludes

Wisdom's Object, and the Final Topics

Fascicle 88 continues directly from fascicle 87, opening the aggregates section's eighth overall topic, wisdom's object (智事攝). Its own four-part verse names right and wrong wisdom together with their common object, the transformation of pleasure and the other formations, sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested, and nirvāṇa's two kinds. This fascicle corresponds to the Saṃyukta Āgama's aggregates recitation, continuing where fascicle 87 left off, and — per Master Cháng Bǎi's own framing across fascicles 85 through 88 — should bring the aggregates section (行擇攝) itself to its full completion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 88 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Aggregates Section Continues — Wisdom's Object Begins

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the aggregates section, the first, part four.

Chinese Source

攝事分中契經事行擇攝第一之四

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the aggregates section, the first, part four.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Fascicle 88 continues directly from fascicle 87, still within the Compendium of Bases' own explanation of the Saṃyukta Āgama — every sūtra concerning the five aggregates gathered and analyzed together under the name "the aggregates section" (行擇攝). This fascicle covers the fourth portion of its eleven particular verses, beginning with the eighth, wisdom's object (智事攝).

2. Section: Wisdom's Object's Own Particular Verse

Further, the summary verse says: the two kinds of wisdom, together with their object; the transformation of pleasure and the other formations; sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested; and nirvāṇa's two kinds.

Chinese Source

復次，嗢柁南曰：二智并其事　樂等行轉變　請無請說經　涅槃有二種

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the summary verse says: the two kinds of wisdom, together with their object; the transformation of pleasure and the other formations; sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested; and nirvāṇa's two kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's eighth overall topic, wisdom's object, unfolds across four further parts: the two kinds of wisdom together with their shared object, the transformation of pleasure and the other formations, sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested, and nirvāṇa's two kinds — each taken up individually in what follows.

3. Section: Two Kinds of Wisdom Named

Wisdom has two kinds. First, right wisdom; second, wrong wisdom.

Chinese Source

智有二種。一者、正智，二者、邪智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wisdom has two kinds. First, right wisdom; second, wrong wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The two kinds of wisdom and their object explains wisdom's object's first topic — the Buddha and outsiders alike observe the same world of sentient beings and container world, yet arrive at entirely different conclusions.

4. Section: Right and Wrong Wisdom Both Arise From the Same Object

Here, right wisdom arises in dependence on a real matter; wrong wisdom likewise does so.

Chinese Source

此中正智依有事生，邪智亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, right wisdom arises in dependence on a real matter; wrong wisdom likewise does so.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha and his noble disciples take the very same world of sentient beings and container world as their object as any outsider does, and from this same object arise both right wisdom and wrong wisdom, depending entirely on who is observing.

5. Section: How Right and Wrong Wisdom Differ in Grasping the Object

Although these two kinds of wisdom both rely on a real matter, right wisdom grasps that matter as it truly is; wrong wisdom, through wrong discrimination, does not grasp that matter as it truly is.

Chinese Source

雖此二智俱依有事，然正智如實取事，邪智、邪分別不如實取事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although these two kinds of wisdom both rely on a real matter, right wisdom grasps that matter as it truly is; wrong wisdom, through wrong discrimination, does not grasp that matter as it truly is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Right wisdom recognizes the aggregates as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, grasping them exactly as they truly are; wrong wisdom, like the outsider Pūraṇa Kāśyapa observing people's sudden anger and equally sudden calm and concluding that affliction and purity arise entirely without cause, grasps the very same object wrongly.

6. Section: What Precedes Right and Wrong Wisdom

Through having right teaching and wise attention as the leading factor, right wisdom is able to arise regarding the known object; through having wrong teaching and unwise attention as the leading factor, wrong wisdom is able to arise regarding the known object.

Chinese Source

由有正教、如理作意為前行故，於所知境正智得生；由有邪教、非理作意為前行故，於所知境邪智得生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through having right teaching and wise attention as the leading factor, right wisdom is able to arise regarding the known object; through having wrong teaching and unwise attention as the leading factor, wrong wisdom is able to arise regarding the known object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The same object, observed through the noble teaching and wise attention, yields right wisdom; observed through a wrong teaching and unwise attention, yields wrong wisdom — the very same event interpreted two entirely different ways depending on what precedes the observation.

7. Section: Right Wisdom's Own Nature — Marked, and Illustrated by a Lamp

Right wisdom does not arise by destroying the known object; it is only that, regarding this object, one relinquishes wrong grasping, and right wisdom thereby arises. Just as, when a bright lamp arises within a dark room, it does not destroy the form present there, but is only able to illuminate it — one should know this same meaning applies here as well.

Chinese Source

非正智生壞所知境，但於此境捨於邪執，而起正智。如闇中色，明燈生時不壞此色，但能照了。當知此義亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Right wisdom does not arise by destroying the known object; it is only that, regarding this object, one relinquishes wrong grasping, and right wisdom thereby arises. Just as, when a bright lamp arises within a dark room, it does not destroy the form present there, but is only able to illuminate it — one should know this same meaning applies here as well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A lamp lit in a dark room does not destroy the table sitting there — it only reveals what was already present, unseen. Right wisdom works the same way: it does not annihilate the five aggregates, only illuminates them as they truly are, impermanent and suffering, freeing the mind from clinging rather than from the aggregates themselves.

8. Section: The Transformation of Formations Begins — At the Stage of Feeling

Further, formations that accord with pleasant feeling are joined together with the mark of impermanence; if they reach the stage of pain, this is then named the pressure of harm and affliction; if they reach the stage of neither pain nor pleasure, only then, regarding the suffering of formation itself, is this named the pressure of suffering.

Chinese Source

復次，隨順樂受諸行，與無常相共相應故，若至苦位，爾時說名損惱迫迮；若至不苦不樂位，爾時方於行苦名苦迫迮。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, formations that accord with pleasant feeling are joined together with the mark of impermanence; if they reach the stage of pain, this is then named the pressure of harm and affliction; if they reach the stage of neither pain nor pleasure, only then, regarding the suffering of formation itself, is this named the pressure of suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The transformation of formations explains wisdom's object's second topic. Pleasant feeling, being impermanent, inevitably shifts toward pain, and even neutral feeling, escaping neither, carries the pervasive suffering of formation itself — the deepest, most ultimate kind of suffering, known only to the noble.

9. Section: Refuting the Notion That Pleasant Feeling Never Transforms

If it did not reach those other stages, it would then, without exception, be confined to pleasant feeling alone, never reaching any other stage.

Chinese Source

若不至彼位，便應畢竟唯順樂受，勿至餘位。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it did not reach those other stages, it would then, without exception, be confined to pleasant feeling alone, never reaching any other stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Were pleasant feeling truly permanent, as outsiders claim of all phenomena, it could never shift into pain or neutrality at all — yet plainly it does, proving no formation ever holds still.

10. Section: The Stage of Birth, Aging, and the Rest

Further, formations followed by the dharmas of birth, aging, and the rest are, every one, suffering. If they reach the stage of illness, this is named the pressure of harm and affliction; if they reach the stage of the suffering of birth and the rest, this is named the pressure of suffering.

Chinese Source

又生老等法所隨諸行，皆悉是苦。彼若至疾病位，說名損惱迫迮；若至生等苦位，名苦迫迮。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, formations followed by the dharmas of birth, aging, and the rest are, every one, suffering. If they reach the stage of illness, this is named the pressure of harm and affliction; if they reach the stage of the suffering of birth and the rest, this is named the pressure of suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates carry birth, aging, sickness, death, and separation from the loved forward without pause; health gives way to illness, and continued craving guarantees a further birth carrying the same suffering forward again.

11. Section: Refuting the Notion That This, Too, Never Transforms

If it did not reach those stages, then, among the formations, the cause of birth's own suffering and the rest, though it follows along, would never reach its fruit.

Chinese Source

若不至彼位，於諸行中，生等苦因之所隨逐，勿至果位。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it did not reach those stages, then, among the formations, the cause of birth's own suffering and the rest, though it follows along, would never reach its fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Were self and world truly permanent, as outsiders claim, youth would never fade into old age or death — yet plainly it does.

12. Section: Distinguished by Many Conditions — the Principle Explained

Further, formations, by their very own nature, are produced from many conditions, and so have no freedom of their own, and no ruling master.

Chinese Source

又本性諸行眾緣生故，不得自在，亦無宰主。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, formations, by their very own nature, are produced from many conditions, and so have no freedom of their own, and no ruling master.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Search the body, the feelings, the thoughts, the will, or consciousness itself, and no ruling master can ever be found anywhere among them — every one simply shifts according to whatever conditions happen to be present.

13. Section: Refuting a Ruling Master, Closing "The Transformation of Formations"

If there were a ruling master, then, although every formation's own nature is impermanent, it should flow on unceasingly wherever that master delights, or else not arise at all — extensively explained, up through death.

Chinese Source

若有宰主，彼一切行雖性無常，應隨所樂流轉不絕；或不令生，廣說乃至於死。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there were a ruling master, then, although every formation's own nature is impermanent, it should flow on unceasingly wherever that master delights, or else not arise at all — extensively explained, up through death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

If a true master existed within the aggregates, it could direct rebirth to heaven or hell at will, or even halt the entire process of birth and death outright, simply by wishing it — yet no one, anywhere, actually has this power, proving the master was never there to begin with, only conditions arising and passing. With this, the transformation of pleasure and the other formations, corresponding to sūtras 143-146, is complete — together with the two kinds of wisdom and their object, corresponding to sūtras 139-142, both fully explained through the same repeated pattern: observing each aggregate as impermanent, hence suffering, hence without self or what-belongs-to-self, across past, future, present, near and far, leading through weariness and freedom from desire to liberation.

14. Section: Sūtras Spoken Upon Request or Unrequested — Two Kinds Named

Further, there are two kinds of sūtra. First, spoken because of a request; second, spoken not because of a request.

Chinese Source

復次，有二種契經。一、因請而說，二、不因請說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are two kinds of sūtra. First, spoken because of a request; second, spoken not because of a request.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested explains wisdom's object's third topic, corresponding to sūtras 147-148.

15. Section: Spoken Because of a Request

Spoken because of a request means that, if there is a person to be tamed through such teachings on the formations' own marks, then, because of that person's request, one turns and teaches such marks of the formations accordingly.

Chinese Source

因請說者，謂若有補特伽羅，由此諸行相教而調伏者，因彼請故，為轉如是諸行相教。

Ru-Yi Translation

Spoken because of a request means that, if there is a person to be tamed through such teachings on the formations' own marks, then, because of that person's request, one turns and teaches such marks of the formations accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium for Elucidation (fascicle 81)

Whenever a disciple, seeking to tame their own affliction, requests teaching on the aggregates, the elements, the sense-spheres, dependent origination, what is and is not possible, or the foundations of mindfulness, the Buddha teaches accordingly.

16. Section: Spoken Not Because of a Request, Closing "Sūtras Spoken Upon Request or Unrequested"

Spoken not because of a request means that, if within a great assembly of several hundred, one speaks a wondrous teaching through immeasurable approaches, or, for the great teacher's own close attendant disciple Ānanda and the like, one speaks in this way — this is in order that the true dharma may long abide.

Chinese Source

不因請說者，謂若於彼多百眾中，以無量門作美妙說，或為大師近住弟子阿難陀等作如是說，為令正法得久住故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Spoken not because of a request means that, if within a great assembly of several hundred, one speaks a wondrous teaching through immeasurable approaches, or, for the great teacher's own close attendant disciple Ānanda and the like, one speaks in this way — this is in order that the true dharma may long abide.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Some teachings matter too much to wait for a question — the Buddha offers them unbidden, whether to a large assembly or to a close disciple like Ānanda, precisely so the true dharma endures long after his own passing. With this, sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested, corresponding to sūtras 147-148, is complete.

17. Section: Nirvāṇa's Two Kinds Begin — Complete Nirvāṇa, Embraced Through Three Parts

Further, one should know that, through three parts, complete nirvāṇa is embraced. First, through according with instruction. Second, through rightly observing every formation. Third, through permanently cutting off every affliction.

Chinese Source

復次，當知由三分故，攝受圓滿涅槃。一、由隨順教授故；二、由正觀察一切行故；三、由永斷一切煩惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, one should know that, through three parts, complete nirvāṇa is embraced. First, through according with instruction. Second, through rightly observing every formation. Third, through permanently cutting off every affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Nirvāṇa's two kinds explains wisdom's object's fourth and final topic. Complete nirvāṇa is reached by according with a teacher's own instruction, rightly observing the aggregates, sense-elements, and sense-spheres accordingly, and thereby permanently cutting off every affliction.

18. Section: According With Instruction — Three Kinds of Miraculous Transformation Named

According with instruction means being subsumed under prophetic speech, admonition, and supernormal power.

Chinese Source

隨順教授者，謂記說、教誡、神變所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

According with instruction means being subsumed under prophetic speech, admonition, and supernormal power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground (fascicle 27)

The Buddha teaches through three kinds of miraculous transformation: supernormal power (moving faithless disciples to respect and attention), prophetic speech (reading another's mind and declaring it), and admonition (teaching each disciple exactly what their own capacity and progress require) — together, complete instruction.

19. Section: Prophetic Speech — Three Aspects of Reading Another's Mind

The Tathāgata, wishing to declare another's mind, through his own settled concentration, illumines that other mind comprehensively through three aspects — whether it is a mind that ceased long ago, in succession; or a mind that ceased in the immediately preceding moment; or a mind presently turning toward its object. Rising from concentration, recollecting and discriminating accordingly, he reflects on the other mind received within concentration. Exactly as it was received, he then declares it thus: you had such a mind — namely, one that ceased long ago; such an intention — namely, one that ceased in the immediately preceding moment; such a consciousness — namely, one presently occurring.

Chinese Source

如來隨欲記說彼心，由自定意，以三行相遍照他心，若展轉久遠滅心、若無間滅心、若於現在所緣轉心。從定起已，隨念分別，思惟定內所受他心。如其所受，即如是記：汝有如是心，謂久遠滅者；如是意，謂無間滅者；如是識，謂現在者。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata, wishing to declare another's mind, through his own settled concentration, illumines that other mind comprehensively through three aspects — whether it is a mind that ceased long ago, in succession; or a mind that ceased in the immediately preceding moment; or a mind presently turning toward its object. Rising from concentration, recollecting and discriminating accordingly, he reflects on the other mind received within concentration. Exactly as it was received, he then declares it thus: you had such a mind — namely, one that ceased long ago; such an intention — namely, one that ceased in the immediately preceding moment; such a consciousness — namely, one presently occurring.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Entering concentration, the Buddha can read a disciple's mind across three depths: thoughts from the distant past, the very thought at a past death, or a thought presently arising — and, rising from concentration, declares exactly what he perceived, mapped respectively onto distant-past mind, immediately-preceding-moment intention, and present consciousness.

20. Section: What Is Declared — By Category, Not By Instant, Closing "Prophetic Speech"

This is based on category, not based on the instant.

Chinese Source

此據種類，不據剎那。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is based on category, not based on the instant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Distant-past mind, immediately-preceding mind, and present mind are named according to the wholesome or unwholesome category of mind involved across whole lifetimes, not according to literal moment-to-moment succession. With this, prophetic speech is complete.

21. Section: Admonition — Three Places Named

Relying precisely on such prophetic speech as a basis, one then gives admonition regarding three places. First, regarding the present object within the place of activity, permitting wise attention and forbidding unwise attention. Second, regarding the place of dwelling, forbidding wrong discursive thought and permitting right discursive thought. Third, regarding the place of diligently cultivating calm and insight, permitting the cutting off of formations not yet cut off, and causing affliction to be permanently released from bondage, thereby realizing nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

即以如是記說神變為依止故，於其三處而為教誡。一、於行處現前境界，開許如理作意，遮止不如理作意。二、於住處，遮止不正尋思，開許正尋思。三、於止觀勤修行處，開許令斷未斷諸行，及令煩惱永得離繫，而證涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Relying precisely on such prophetic speech as a basis, one then gives admonition regarding three places. First, regarding the present object within the place of activity, permitting wise attention and forbidding unwise attention. Second, regarding the place of dwelling, forbidding wrong discursive thought and permitting right discursive thought. Third, regarding the place of diligently cultivating calm and insight, permitting the cutting off of formations not yet cut off, and causing affliction to be permanently released from bondage, thereby realizing nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having read a disciple's mind, the Buddha then admonishes accordingly: guarding the senses with wise attention in daily activity, replacing the eight kinds of distracting thought with right reflection on no-self during formal stillness, and, in diligent cultivation itself, permitting the actual cutting off of remaining affliction all the way to nirvāṇa.

22. Section: The Purpose of Such Teaching, Closing "According With Instruction"

Teaching in this way causes the mind's secondary afflictions arising from three places to become purified — namely, from the place of activity, the place of dwelling, and the place relied upon.

Chinese Source

如是宣說，令從三處諸隨煩惱心得清淨。謂從行處、住處、依處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Teaching in this way causes the mind's secondary afflictions arising from three places to become purified — namely, from the place of activity, the place of dwelling, and the place relied upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, according with instruction — prophetic speech, admonition, and supernormal power together — complete nirvāṇa's first of three embracing parts, is entirely complete.

23. Section: Rightly Observing Every Formation

Further, rightly observing the formations of the past, the future, and the present is named rightly observing every formation.

Chinese Source

又正觀察過去、未來、現在諸行，名正觀察一切諸行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, rightly observing the formations of the past, the future, and the present is named rightly observing every formation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Observing the aggregates across past, future, and present, inner and outer, coarse and fine, as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, is complete nirvāṇa's second embracing part.

24. Section: Permanently Cutting Off Every Affliction Begins — Three Contaminations Named

Further, there are three contaminations; with these three contaminations leading the way, desire and harm arise; with desire and harm leading the way, discursive thought's own burning affliction arises; with discursive thought's burning affliction leading the way, the anguish of seeking arises.

Chinese Source

又有三漏；三漏為先，而有欲害；欲害為先，而有尋思熱惱；尋思熱惱為先，而有追求憂惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are three contaminations; with these three contaminations leading the way, desire and harm arise; with desire and harm leading the way, discursive thought's own burning affliction arises; with discursive thought's burning affliction leading the way, the anguish of seeking arises.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 89

The three contaminations are desire (every desire-realm affliction apart from ignorance itself), existence (every form- and formless-realm affliction apart from ignorance), and ignorance (the outsider's own settled wrong view, whether mistaking sensory pleasure or a meditative absorption for ultimate truth, obscuring the mind regardless of whether desire has actually been left behind).

25. Section: Permanent Cutting Off Explained, Closing "Complete Nirvāṇa's" Three-Part Embrace

When all such things are permanently cut off, this is named permanently cutting off every affliction. Thus abiding, the mind well liberated, dwelling in signless ease, without fear — within this present life, this is named entering the category of complete parinirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

如是一切皆永斷故，說名永斷一切煩惱。如是安住心善解脫，無相樂住無恐怖時，於現法中，名入圓滿般涅槃數。

Ru-Yi Translation

When all such things are permanently cut off, this is named permanently cutting off every affliction. Thus abiding, the mind well liberated, dwelling in signless ease, without fear — within this present life, this is named entering the category of complete parinirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Cutting off desire first, then existence, then ignorance itself, the mind rests in signless ease, wholly unafraid of no-self — nirvāṇa with residue, realized within this very life. With this, permanently cutting off every affliction, and complete nirvāṇa's three embracing parts in full, are complete.

26. Section: The Basis Revealed — Three Dharmas Named

Further, relying on three dharmas: relying on one's own meaning is named dwelling in refuge; relying on another's meaning is named dwelling on an island. What are the three? First, relying on inward wise attention as the leading factor, conduct according with the dharma. Second, relying on hearing the true dharma spoken by the Buddha. Third, relying on drawing near the good persons within the true dharma, not drawing near any unwholesome persons outside the true dharma among outsiders.

Chinese Source

又依三法，依止自義，名住歸依；依止他義，名住洲渚。何者為三？一、依內如理作意為先法隨法行；二、依佛聽聞所說正法；三、依親近正法內善士，不依親近餘正法外一切外道諸不善士。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, relying on three dharmas: relying on one's own meaning is named dwelling in refuge; relying on another's meaning is named dwelling on an island. What are the three? First, relying on inward wise attention as the leading factor, conduct according with the dharma. Second, relying on hearing the true dharma spoken by the Buddha. Third, relying on drawing near the good persons within the true dharma, not drawing near any unwholesome persons outside the true dharma among outsiders.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground (fascicle 25) and Bodhisattva Ground (fascicles 38, 44)

A worthy companion is marked by eight qualities (settled in precepts, sharp innate wisdom, realization through calm and insight, tireless benefit to others, fearless eloquence, patience under contempt, sustained clear exposition, and language that draws listeners toward the ultimate truth without ever distorting it), and is drawn near through four means (attending them in health and illness alike, greeting and serving them respectfully, supporting them with the four requisites, and following their teaching faithfully, without wavering, whatever hardship it may bring).

27. Section: The Reason for These Three Dharmas Revealed, Closing "Complete Nirvāṇa" Entirely

One should know that such three dharmas reveal the four most productive dharmas among humans — namely, drawing near a good person, hearing the true dharma, wise attention, and conduct according with the dharma.

Chinese Source

如是三法，當知顯示人中四種多所作法，謂親近善士、聽聞正法、如理作意、法隨法行。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that such three dharmas reveal the four most productive dharmas among humans — namely, drawing near a good person, hearing the true dharma, wise attention, and conduct according with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These are the four limbs of stream-entry — the classic sequence any practitioner follows toward the noble path. With this, complete nirvāṇa, nirvāṇa's first kind, is entirely complete.

28. Section: Partial Nirvāṇa Begins — Three Conditions and Five Marks Named

Further, through three conditions and five marks, one should know partial nirvāṇa is realized. What are the three conditions? First, through thoroughly knowing suffering. Second, through deeply seeing the danger in every practice that accords with suffering. Third, through transcending sorrow and every other kind of suffering.

Chinese Source

復由三緣及五種相，當知證得彼分涅槃。何等三緣？一、遍知苦故；二、深見一切隨順苦行諸過患故；三、超過愁等一切苦故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through three conditions and five marks, one should know partial nirvāṇa is realized. What are the three conditions? First, through thoroughly knowing suffering. Second, through deeply seeing the danger in every practice that accords with suffering. Third, through transcending sorrow and every other kind of suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Partial nirvāṇa — nirvāṇa's second kind — means partial, not complete, realization. Knowing the aggregates' suffering as urgent as a fire already at one's own head, seeing the danger each kind of feeling breeds (craving from pleasure, aversion from pain, delusion from neutrality), and thereby finding the strength to press on past sorrow toward the path itself — these three conditions together make partial realization possible.

29. Section: Five Marks Named — The First, Thoroughly Knowing Its Own Nature

What are the five marks? First, knowing that when the many kinds of suffering come into contact, sorrow and the rest arise — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's own nature.

Chinese Source

云何五相？一、知苦種類相交涉時，發生愁等，是名於彼遍知自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the five marks? First, knowing that when the many kinds of suffering come into contact, sorrow and the rest arise — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Recognizing the aggregates' own many kinds of suffering — birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from the loved, meeting the unloved, not getting what is sought, and countless further sufferings — as they touch the mind and provoke sorrow, is the first step toward genuine realization; without it, one cannot even recognize suffering as suffering, let alone transcend it.

30. Section: The Remaining Four Marks

Second, knowing that there is a seed from which that dharma is able to arise — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's causal nature. Third, knowing one's own conduct and the object one knows — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's conditioning nature. Fourth, observing that clinging to what belongs to self and to self are both formations that accord with every kind of suffering — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's own formation-nature. Fifth, observing the danger present in the formations bound to the desire realm across the three times, one is able to cut off every kind of sorrow and the rest.

Chinese Source

二、知有種子彼法得生，是名於彼遍知因性。三、知自所行所知境界，是名於彼遍知緣性。四、隨觀執著我所及我，皆是能順眾苦諸行，是名於彼遍知行性。五、隨觀三世欲界所繫諸行過患，能斷一切愁等諸苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, knowing that there is a seed from which that dharma is able to arise — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's causal nature. Third, knowing one's own conduct and the object one knows — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's conditioning nature. Fourth, observing that clinging to what belongs to self and to self are both formations that accord with every kind of suffering — this is named thoroughly knowing that suffering's own formation-nature. Fifth, observing the danger present in the formations bound to the desire realm across the three times, one is able to cut off every kind of sorrow and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Suffering's manifestation traces to a seed (cause), ripening through contact between the senses and their objects (condition), and expressing itself through the deep, unconscious habit of relating everything back to self and what-belongs-to-self (its own formation-nature) — seeing all this across past, future, and present finally supplies the will to actually cut sorrow off at the root.

31. Section: Concluding — Three Conditions and Five Marks Together Yield Partial Nirvāṇa

One should know that, through these three conditions and five marks, such partial nirvāṇa is attained.

Chinese Source

當知由此三緣五相，獲得如是彼分涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, through these three conditions and five marks, such partial nirvāṇa is attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All three conditions and five marks together remain squarely within the four noble truths — knowing the three realms' fruit as suffering, tracing it to self-view and self-love, and cultivating the path that alone transcends it. With this, what can be realized is complete.

32. Section: Sorrow and the Rest Explained — By Their Own Marks

Through a loved matter's impermanent change, sorrowful and grieving at heart, this is named sorrow. Through this, uttering words of sighing and lament, this is named lament. Through this, beating one's own breast, this is named pain. Harboring a knot of injustice within, this is named grief. Through this, becoming confused and disordered, this is named vexation.

Chinese Source

由可愛事無常轉變，悲傷心慼，故名為愁。由彼發言咨嗟歔欷，故名為歎。因此拊膺，故名為苦。內懷冤結，故名為憂。因茲迷亂，故名為惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through a loved matter's impermanent change, sorrowful and grieving at heart, this is named sorrow. Through this, uttering words of sighing and lament, this is named lament. Through this, beating one's own breast, this is named pain. Harboring a knot of injustice within, this is named grief. Through this, becoming confused and disordered, this is named vexation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Sorrow, lament, pain, grief, and vexation form a natural progression — an inner ache deepening first into audible sighing, then physical anguish, then unresolved resentment, then a mind thrown into genuine confusion.

33. Section: Sorrow and the Rest, by Stages, Closing "Partial Nirvāṇa"

Further, upon the sudden presence of any one matter — the loss of wealth, of health, of a relative — grief and vexation first arise; this is named sorrow. Relying on this, one then utters words of sorrowful lament and injustice, the whole body burning with agitation; this is named the stage of lament and pain. After this sorrow and lament, the body's burning agitation subsides, yet, though the outer matter has quieted, an inner fire still burns and the mind remains unsettled; this is named the stage of grief. After the first day, or the second, third, fifth, or tenth day or month, through that same cause and condition, the mind is still not at peace; this is named vexation.

Chinese Source

又以喪失財寶、無病、親戚等事隨一現前，創生憂惱，說名為愁。由依此故，次乃發言哀吟悲冤，舉身煩熱，名歎苦位。過此愁歎身煩熱已，內燒外靜，心猶未平，說名憂位。過初日已，或二三五十日夜月，由彼因緣，意尚未寧，說名為惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, upon the sudden presence of any one matter — the loss of wealth, of health, of a relative — grief and vexation first arise; this is named sorrow. Relying on this, one then utters words of sorrowful lament and injustice, the whole body burning with agitation; this is named the stage of lament and pain. After this sorrow and lament, the body's burning agitation subsides, yet, though the outer matter has quieted, an inner fire still burns and the mind remains unsettled; this is named the stage of grief. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All of this ultimately traces to clinging on self and what-belongs-to-self; remove that clinging and, even when loss still occurs, the mind no longer suffers over it. With this, partial nirvāṇa is complete.

34. Section: Background — Sūtras 147-148 Retold, Closing "Wisdom's Object" and the Aggregates Section's Coverage in Fascicles 85 Through 88's Final Topic Boundary

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's sūtras 147-148

At Bamboo Grove, the Buddha, reading the minds of three newly ordained monks — Aniruddha, Nandika, and Kimbila — through prophetic speech, admonished them on guarding attention in activity, dwelling, and cultivation, and taught them to observe the aggregates as impermanent, hence suffering, leading through weariness and freedom from desire to nirvāṇa, with the three contaminations of desire, existence, and ignorance permanently cut off. All three monks attained liberation on the spot.

The Buddha then taught the monks to dwell as their own island, self-reliant, dharma-reliant, relying on nothing else — never blaming another for one's own affliction, since no one else can plant the seed that only one's own mind already carries. Asked further what gives rise to sorrow and grief, the Buddha explained that clinging to the aggregates as genuinely real is the cause: without such clinging, their inevitable change would provoke no sorrow at all, but with it, reflection upon reflection only deepens the anguish further. Sixteen monks, following this teaching through insight meditation on the spot, cut off every affliction and attained liberation. With this, wisdom's object — the two kinds of wisdom, the transformation of formations, sūtras spoken upon request or unrequested, and nirvāṇa's two kinds, corresponding to sūtras 139-148 — and the aggregates section's eighth overall topic, are entirely complete.

35. Section: Contention Begins — Its Own Seven-Topic Verse

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the summary verse says: contention, the sprout, view as the great defilement; the one direction, training, the four fears; and, within the well-taught compared with the wrongly taught, the distinction in recollecting past lives.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With wisdom's object entirely closed, the aggregates section's ninth overall topic, contention, opens with its own seven-part verse: contention (why the Tathāgata is free of it, sūtras 149-150), the sprout (how further existence's sprout arises, sūtra 151), view as the great defilement (self-view as the greatest of all defilements, sūtra 152), the one direction (the single direction every practitioner's training follows from ordinary being to noble one, sūtra 153), training (what should be learned from the ordinary stage through the stage of training, sūtra 154), the four fears (four fears obstructing nirvāṇa, sūtras 155-156), and the distinction in recollecting past lives within well-taught versus wrongly-taught traditions (sūtras 157-158).

36. Section: The Tathāgata's Freedom From Contention Begins — Four Causes Named

Through four causes and conditions, the Tathāgata does not engage in hostile contention with the world's own confused clinging; yet the world, through its own wrong discrimination, takes this as hostile contention. What are the four? First, through teaching the meaning of principle. Second, through teaching the meaning of truth. Third, through teaching the meaning of benefit. Fourth, through sometimes according with the world's own turning.

Chinese Source

由四因緣，如來不與世間迷執共為怨諍，然彼世間起邪分別，謂為怨諍。何等為四？一者、宣說道理義故；二者、宣說真實義故；三者、宣說利益義故；四者、有時隨世轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through four causes and conditions, the Tathāgata does not engage in hostile contention with the world's own confused clinging; yet the world, through its own wrong discrimination, takes this as hostile contention. What are the four? First, through teaching the meaning of principle. Second, through teaching the meaning of truth. Third, through teaching the meaning of benefit. Fourth, through sometimes according with the world's own turning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Contention explains the aggregates section's ninth topic. The Buddha never seeks conflict — he simply teaches according to sound principle, plain truth, and genuine benefit, sometimes even using the world's own conventional language, yet confused beings still perceive disagreement as hostility.

37. Section: Teaching the Meaning of Principle — Four Kinds of Principle

Here, the Tathāgata, relying on four kinds of principle, teaches the true dharma — as before, namely the principle of dependence, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of what is naturally so. Through this, the Tathāgata is named one who speaks according to dharma.

Chinese Source

此中如來依四道理，宣說正法。如前所謂觀待道理、作用道理、因成道理、法爾道理。由此如來名法語者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the Tathāgata, relying on four kinds of principle, teaches the true dharma — as before, namely the principle of dependence, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of what is naturally so. Through this, the Tathāgata is named one who speaks according to dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground (fascicle 25)

The principle of dependence shows every dharma as arising only in dependence on conditions, hence without any real self-nature of its own; the principle of function recognizes that each dharma, though empty, still has its own real function — wind moves, water moistens, the eye sees; the principle of proof establishes truths like impermanence and no-self through authoritative teaching, inference, and direct perception together; and the principle of what is naturally so accepts certain facts (earth is solid, fire is hot) simply as reality's own nature, not further explicable. Teaching through these four is what makes the Tathāgata's own speech genuinely "according to dharma."

38. Section: Revealing the Matter of Non-Contention — The Tathāgata Never Seeks It Out

The Tathāgata never deliberately goes to another's place, seeking to stir up a matter of contention.

Chinese Source

如來終不故往他所，求興諍事。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgata never deliberately goes to another's place, seeking to stir up a matter of contention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha teaches from settled principle, truth, and benefit, never from a wish to provoke disagreement — any conflict that arises is entirely the world's own confused reaction, not his intention.
