Fascicle 42 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十二 · The Seven Remaining Kinds of Precept Close Out Discipline

Difficult Practices to Purity

Continuing directly from fascicle 41's complete account of the forty-three minor precepts, the treatise now closes the whole chapter on the perfection of discipline by re-describing that same body of precepts through seven further classical lenses — difficult practices, comprehensive-gateway, virtuous-person, comprehensive-kind, fulfilling-requests, both-worlds-happiness, and purity precepts — before opening an entirely new chapter: the perfection of patience.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 42 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Opening — and the Three Difficult Practices

Fascicle 42 opens by locating itself precisely within the treatise's larger architecture — still within the Bodhisattva Stage's chapter on discipline, continuing directly from fascicles 40 and 41's account of the precepts' intrinsic nature and their comprehensive detail — and announces that this fascicle will describe the remaining seven ways of viewing that same body of precepts, beginning with the three difficult practices: a bodhisattva's willingness to give up great worldly wealth and standing to take up the precepts in the first place, to hold them without the slightest compromise even facing mortal danger, and to hold them, across a whole lifetime of activity, without ever once losing mindfulness.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處戒品第十之三。云何菩薩難行戒？當知此戒略有三種。謂諸菩薩，現在具足大財、大族自在增上，棄捨如是大財、大族自在增上，受持菩薩淨戒律儀。是名菩薩第一難行戒。又諸菩薩受淨戒已，若遭急難乃至失命，於所受戒尚無少闕，何況全犯。是名菩薩第二難行戒。又諸菩薩，如是如是遍於一切行住作意，恆住正念，常無放逸，乃至命終於所受戒無有誤失，尚不犯輕，何況犯重。是名菩薩第三難行戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the discipline of the initial yoga foundation, part three. What is a bodhisattva's difficult practice with regard to discipline? One should know this precept is, in brief, of three kinds. Bodhisattvas who presently possess great wealth, an eminent family, and genuine worldly power and independence, yet give up all of that wealth, family standing, and independence in order to take up and hold the bodhisattva precepts — this is called a bodhisattva's first difficult practice. Bodhisattvas who, having received the precepts, encounter sudden mortal danger, even facing the loss of life itself, and yet find not the slightest compromise in the precepts they hold, let alone an outright violation — this is called a bodhisattva's second difficult practice. Bodhisattvas who, in this way and that, across every moment of walking, standing, and all other activity, constantly abide in mindfulness, remaining forever free of carelessness, right up until the moment of death, finding no error at all in the precepts they hold — not violating even a minor precept, let alone a grave one — this is called a bodhisattva's third difficult practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that this fascicle continues the same eighteen-chapter architecture of the Bodhisattva Stage already established, still within the tenth chapter on discipline — this being its third and final installment, following fascicle 40's treatment of discipline's intrinsic nature and comprehensive scope, and fascicle 41's complete account of the forty-three minor precepts. What remains, he notes, is not new content so much as seven different classical lenses through which that same body of precepts can be viewed and appreciated — a technique the treatise uses throughout to help practitioners genuinely internalize material already covered, by returning to it from fresh angles rather than simply repeating it. He also flags, as a preview, that this fascicle will go on to open two entirely new chapters after discipline finally closes: the nine kinds of patience, and the nine kinds of diligence.

On the three difficult practices themselves, he stresses what makes each one genuinely difficult rather than merely notable: giving up considerable wealth and high family standing for the precepts is difficult because it runs directly against the strongest of ordinary human motivations — accumulated comfort and social position are, for most people, precisely what a lifetime is spent building, not discarding. Holding the precepts with zero compromise even under mortal threat is difficult because fear of death is, by most accounts, the single most powerful instinct a person carries, and yet the bodhisattva's discipline must remain completely intact regardless. And holding unbroken mindfulness across an entire lifetime of ordinary activity — walking, standing, sitting, lying down, working, resting — right up to the final moment, is difficult simply because sustained attention of that kind, moment after moment for an entire lifetime, is something almost no one manages to do without significant lapses. He notes the treatise's own technical gloss connecting this passage to an earlier five-part framework of "diligence free of carelessness" spanning past, present, and future action — here specifically the two components concerning deliberate prior resolve and continuous present follow-through, both of which a bodhisattva must hold simultaneously to genuinely never lapse. This closes the three difficult practices; the treatise turns next to the comprehensive-gateway precepts, describing the four doorways through which the precepts as a whole are entered and sustained.

2. Milestone: The Four Comprehensive-Gateway Precepts — Formal Reception, Innate Nature, Habituation, and Skillful Means

The second of the seven lenses: the comprehensive-gateway precepts, describing the four distinct ways a bodhisattva actually comes to hold the precepts — formally receiving them through proper ordination, possessing them already as an innate disposition prior to formal reception, carrying them forward through habituation cultivated across past lifetimes, and actively applying them through the four specific skillful means of gathering and guiding other beings.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切門戒？當知此戒略有四種。一者、正受戒，二者、本性戒，三者、串習戒，四者、方便相應戒。正受戒者，謂諸菩薩受先所受三種菩薩淨戒律儀。即律儀戒、攝善法戒、饒益有情戒。本性戒者，謂諸菩薩住種性位，本性仁賢，於相續中，身語二業恆清淨轉。串習戒者，謂諸菩薩昔餘生中，曾串修習如先所說三種淨戒。由宿因力所任持故，於現在世一切惡法不樂現行，於諸惡法深心厭離；樂修善行，於善行中深心欣慕。方便相應戒者，謂諸菩薩依四攝事，於諸有情身語意業恆相續轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-gateway precept? One should know this precept is, in brief, of four kinds. First, the precept of formal reception; second, the precept of innate nature; third, the precept of habituation; fourth, the precept of skillful means. The precept of formal reception is when bodhisattvas properly receive the three bodhisattva precepts already described — namely, the precept of discipline, the precept of gathering wholesome dharmas, and the precept of benefiting sentient beings. The precept of innate nature is when bodhisattvas, abiding within the bodhisattva lineage prior even to formal reception, are by innate nature kind and virtuous, their bodily and verbal conduct remaining constantly pure throughout the continuity of their being. The precept of habituation is when bodhisattvas, having already practiced and habituated themselves to these same three precepts across former lifetimes, find that the power of that prior cause now sustains them, such that in the present life they take no pleasure at all in unwholesome conduct, feel deep aversion toward it, and instead delight in wholesome conduct, feeling deep and genuine enthusiasm for it. The precept of skillful means is when bodhisattvas, relying on the four means of gathering beings, keep their bodily, verbal, and mental conduct constantly and continuously directed toward benefiting all beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two kinds of spiritual lineage at work in the second and third precepts here: "innate nature" describes what the tradition calls lineage abiding by nature (本性住種性) — a disposition present since beginningless time, requiring no prior cultivation at all, simply the way this particular being's mind is constituted, naturally kind and easy to get along with even before ever formally encountering the Buddhist path; "habituation," by contrast, describes lineage accomplished through practice (習所成種性) — a disposition built up through genuine effort across countless past lifetimes of actually taking up and practicing the precepts, whose accumulated momentum now expresses itself as a strong, spontaneous-feeling aversion to wrongdoing and delight in virtue in the present life, even though it was, in fact, earned rather than simply given. Both kinds of lineage, he notes, are genuinely valuable, and a given bodhisattva may possess either, or both, in different measure.

He unpacks the four skillful means named in the fourth precept with real care, since they recur throughout the treatise's account of how a bodhisattva actually engages other beings: "accommodating means" (隨攝方便), corresponding to generosity, is the necessary first step of simply giving material support to build genuine goodwill — "first draw them in with what they desire, then lead them into the Buddha's wisdom afterward," as the classical saying goes; "capturing means" (能攝方便), corresponding to loving speech, is using genuinely meaningful, skillfully chosen words to help a confused being see through to the actual workings of cause and effect they had not understood; "inducting means" (令入方便), corresponding to beneficial conduct, is the concrete work of actually guiding someone, once genuinely receptive, into real training in discipline, concentration, and wisdom; and "accompanying means" (隨轉方便), corresponding to working alongside others, is leading by example — a bodhisattva who asks others to cultivate faith, discipline, learning, generosity, and wisdom must be seen genuinely living out those same qualities themselves, working alongside the student rather than merely directing from a distance. This closes the four comprehensive-gateway precepts; the treatise turns next to the five virtuous-person precepts.

3. Milestone: The Five Virtuous-Person Precepts — What Makes Someone a Genuine Spiritual Friend

The third lens: the virtuous-person precepts, naming the five marks of someone genuinely worth calling a good spiritual friend with respect to discipline — holding the precepts oneself, actively encouraging others to take them up, genuinely praising their benefits, feeling real joy at seeing fellow practitioners keep them well, and, should one's own conduct ever lapse, promptly and properly confessing and correcting it.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩善士戒？當知此戒略有五種。謂諸菩薩自具尸羅，勸他受戒，讚戒功德，見同法者深心歡喜，設有毀犯如法悔除。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's virtuous-person precept? One should know this precept is, in brief, of five kinds: bodhisattvas themselves genuinely hold the precepts; they encourage others to receive the precepts; they praise the precepts' genuine benefits; seeing fellow practitioners who hold the same precepts, they feel deep and genuine joy; and should a lapse ever occur in their own conduct, they properly confess and correct it without delay.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that these five marks together describe exactly what makes someone a genuine spiritual friend specifically with respect to discipline — not simply a good person in some general sense, but someone whose entire relationship to the precepts qualifies them to actually help others along the same path: they walk the walk themselves, actively invite others onto it rather than keeping their practice private, can articulate why it genuinely matters rather than merely modeling it silently, take real delight rather than quiet indifference or competitive comparison when others succeed at the same practice, and handle their own inevitable lapses with honest correction rather than concealment or denial. Any one of these alone would be admirable; together, they describe someone capable of genuinely guiding others rather than merely practicing in isolation.

He passes along the modern teacher Venerable Miao-Jing's own observation about the second mark — genuinely praising the precepts' benefits — noting two complementary angles from which this praise can be offered: through the lens of cause and effect, explaining plainly that unwholesome action leads toward lower realms of suffering while wholesome action leads toward higher realms of genuine happiness; or through the lens of compassion itself, since every one of the ten unwholesome deeds the precepts guard against is, at root, a failure of compassion — killing, stealing, and the rest all involve real harm to another being, so praising the precepts' benefit can equally well be framed as praising the compassion that not harming others actually embodies. Master Cháng Bǎi treats compassion as, in fact, the deepest logic underlying discipline as such: holding the precepts is ultimately just a concrete, practical expression of not wishing to harm any being, which is why someone who genuinely delights in seeing others hold the precepts well is, in the fullest sense, delighting in the spread of compassion itself. This closes the five virtuous-person precepts; the treatise turns next to the thirteen comprehensive-kind precepts.

4. Milestone: The Comprehensive-Kind Precepts Open — Six Ways the Precepts Themselves Are Complete

The fourth and most elaborate lens yet: the comprehensive-kind precepts, comprising thirteen further ways of describing the same body of precepts — six naming the precepts' own intrinsic qualities, and seven naming the different fruits they produce — beginning with six qualities: dedication toward supreme awakening, breadth spanning every area of training, freedom from both self-indulgence and self-punishment, permanence across an entire lifetime, unshakeable firmness against any pressure to abandon them, and the genuinely universal ornament they provide.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切種戒？當知此戒以要言之，六種、七種，總十三種。言六種者，一、迴向戒，迴向大菩提故。二、廣博戒，廣攝一切所學處故。三、無罪歡喜處戒，遠離耽著欲樂、自苦二邊行故。四、恆常戒，雖盡壽命亦不棄捨所學處故。五、堅固戒，一切利養恭敬、他論、本隨煩惱不能伏故、不能奪故。六、尸羅莊嚴具相應戒，具足一切戒莊嚴故。尸羅莊嚴如聲聞地應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-kind precept? One should know this precept, in brief, comes to thirteen kinds altogether — six of one sort and seven of another. The six are: first, the precept of dedication, since it is dedicated toward supreme awakening. Second, the precept of breadth, since it comprehensively includes every area of training. Third, the precept of blameless joy, since it steers clear of both indulgent attachment to sensory pleasure and self-punishing austerity. Fourth, the precept of permanence, since it is never abandoned even at the cost of one's own life. Fifth, the precept of firmness, since it cannot be overcome or stolen away by any offering of support and reverence, any rival doctrine, or any root or secondary affliction. Sixth, the precept that functions as an ornament, since it fully possesses the qualities of every kind of adornment a precept can offer — a nature of adornment one should understand in the same way as described in the Śrāvaka Stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the six with genuine care. Dedication (迴向) he describes through its three classical directions: dedicating cause toward fruit, as when the merit of holding discipline in the present is directed toward the future attainment of supreme awakening; dedicating phenomena toward principle, as when the very act of holding a precept is directed toward the direct realization of emptiness itself, freeing it from any grasping at self or phenomena; and dedicating oneself toward others, as when the entire merit of one's own practice — including confession, requesting the teaching, and every other wholesome act — is offered outward to all beings throughout the dharma-realm, that all might realize awakening together, precisely echoing the great vow "may this merit extend universally to all, that we and every being might together complete the buddha path." Breadth (廣博) simply names the precepts' own comprehensive scope, spanning discipline, gathering wholesome dharmas, and benefiting beings together, leaving nothing genuinely relevant to the path untouched.

Blameless joy (無罪歡喜處) he treats as the treatise's own quiet declaration of the middle way in miniature: a bodhisattva neither indulges attachment to fine food, clothing, or comfortable surroundings, always examining their genuine drawbacks and knowing how to release them, nor adopts the kind of self-inflicted suffering some outside traditions practice — imitating animal behavior, covering oneself in ash, and similar ascetic extremes — since the Buddha's own discipline runs directly down the middle, attached to neither extreme. Permanence (恆常) and firmness (堅固) he treats as closely related: permanence describes holding the precepts across an entire lifetime without ever setting them aside, while firmness describes an inner stability so well-established that no external inducement — wealth, flattery, a persuasive rival argument, or the pull of one's own remaining affliction — can actually shake it loose, a firmness built on both genuine strength of resolve and a settled, well-grounded understanding that cannot be argued away. And the ornament of discipline (尸羅莊嚴具), he notes, drawing on the treatise's own cross-reference to the Śrāvaka Stage, is unlike ordinary jewelry, which only flatters those already young and attractive — genuine moral discipline adorns absolutely anyone who wears it, young or old, sick or healthy, at any hour of the day, which is precisely why the Buddha called it an ornament unlike any other. This closes the six qualities; the treatise turns next to the seven fruits the precepts produce.

5. Milestone: Seven Fruits the Precepts Produce — Culminating in a Heart That Naturally Loves Beings

The second half of the comprehensive-kind precepts: seven further qualities describing what genuinely disciplined conduct actually produces — stopping harm, actively doing good, guarding that twofold achievement, the physical marks of a great being, a settled and joyful mind, favorable future rebirth, and, as the crowning fruit, a heart that has come to love and care for beings as its own spontaneous nature.

Chinese Source

言七種者，一、止息戒，遠離一切殺生等故。二、轉作戒，攝一切善故，饒益有情故。三、防護戒，隨護止息轉作戒故。四、大士相異熟戒。五、增上心異熟戒。六、可愛趣異熟戒。七、利有情異熟戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

The seven are: first, the precept of ceasing, since it steers entirely clear of killing and every other harmful act. Second, the precept of actively doing, since it comprehensively gathers every wholesome deed and genuinely benefits sentient beings. Third, the precept of guarding, since it protects and sustains both the precept of ceasing and the precept of actively doing. Fourth, the precept that ripens into the marks of a great being. Fifth, the precept that ripens into a settled, elevated mind. Sixth, the precept that ripens into rebirth in genuinely favorable circumstances. Seventh, the precept that ripens into a heart that benefits sentient beings as its own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first three as corresponding directly to the three-part structure this entire section of the treatise has followed all along: ceasing (止息) matches the precept of discipline itself, actively doing (轉作) matches gathering wholesome dharmas and benefiting beings together, and guarding (防護) is simply the ongoing vigilance that keeps both of the first two genuinely intact rather than eroding over time — stopping harm without also actively cultivating good would be incomplete, and either without ongoing guarding would eventually slip.

The remaining four he treats as a genuine chain of maturing fruit, each building on the last. The marks of a great being (大士相異熟) names the eight classical marks of excellent karmic ripening a bodhisattva's disciplined conduct produces — a full lifespan, an excellent physical form, noble family standing, genuine independence, trustworthy speech, great influence, a favorable human nature, and great strength — laid out more fully in an earlier chapter of the treatise. A settled, elevated mind (增上心異熟) follows naturally from genuinely clean conduct: a mind with nothing to regret feels no anxious remorse, a mind without remorse settles into genuine ease, and a mind at ease naturally deepens into concentration — discipline itself functioning as the foundation concentration is built upon. Favorable rebirth (可愛趣異熟) is the natural consequence of that same clean conduct extended across lifetimes: an ordinary practitioner is reborn into families of genuine standing and means, while a bodhisattva who has already reached the grounds is reborn, appropriately to each stage from the first ground of great joy through the tenth and final ground, always in circumstances suited to their continued practice.

And the seventh, crowning fruit — a heart that benefits beings as its own nature (利有情異熟) — he treats as the deepest point in this whole progression: a bodhisattva who has spent countless past lifetimes cultivating discipline alongside the eleven classical forms of benefiting others eventually reaches a point where compassionate concern for beings is no longer an effort at all, but simply how the mind spontaneously moves, arising as naturally as breathing rather than through deliberate exertion. Master Cháng Bǎi calls this the truest mark of a mature practitioner: someone whose very first instinct, in any situation, is concern for how others are faring — not because they are trying to be compassionate, but because sustained practice has genuinely reshaped what compassion now costs them to feel. This closes all thirteen comprehensive-kind precepts — six qualities and seven fruits together; the treatise turns next to the fulfilling-requests precepts.

6. Milestone: The Fulfilling-Requests Precepts — the Golden Rule as Formal Discipline

The fifth lens, and perhaps the most immediately relatable of the nine categories: the fulfilling-requests precepts, grounding discipline in a strikingly direct piece of moral reasoning — a bodhisattva reflects honestly on the eight kinds of harm they themselves would never wish to suffer, recognizes that every other being wishes the exact same thing, and resolves, even at the cost of their own life, never to be the one who inflicts on someone else what they would never wish inflicted on themselves.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩遂求戒？當知此戒略有八種。謂諸菩薩自諦思惟：如我希求勿彼於我現行斷命，不與而取，穢欲邪行，虛妄，離間，麤惡，綺語，手塊杖等諸非愛觸加害於我。我求是已，他若相違而現行者，我求不遂、我意不悅。如我希求，他亦如是。勿我於彼現行斷命，廣說乃至惡觸加害；彼求是已，我若相違而現行者，彼求不遂、彼意不悅。我之所作，若有令他所求不遂、意不悅者，何現行為。菩薩如是審思惟已，命難因緣亦不於他現行八種所求不遂、不悅意事。如是八種，說名菩薩遂求戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's fulfilling-requests precept? One should know this precept is, in brief, of eight kinds. Bodhisattvas honestly reflect in this way: "Just as I myself hope that no one will take my life, take what is not given to them, engage in defiled and improper sexual conduct toward me, speak falsely to me, speak divisively of me, speak harshly to me, speak idle and meaningless words at me, or strike, stone, or beat me, or inflict any other unwelcome physical harm upon me — having wished for this, if another acts against my wish, what I hoped for is not fulfilled, and my mind is unhappy. Just as I myself hope for this, so too does every other being. They too hope that I will never take their life," and so on in full detail down to inflicting unwelcome physical harm, "and having wished for this, if I act against their wish, what they hoped for is not fulfilled, and their mind is unhappy. If anything I do leaves another being's hope unfulfilled and their mind unhappy, why would I ever act this way?" Having reflected honestly in this way, bodhisattvas do not, even under threat to their own life, inflict upon others these eight kinds of unfulfilled hope and unhappiness. These eight, taken together, are called a bodhisattva's fulfilling-requests precept.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes how the treatise deliberately grounds this entire precept not in an external commandment but in a piece of reasoning any honest person can walk through for themselves — precisely the structure of what is often called the golden rule, arrived at here through careful, first-person reflection rather than simple assertion. The first seven items named map directly onto the classical ten unwholesome deeds' first seven — the three bodily (killing, stealing, sexual misconduct) and four verbal (lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle talk) — while the eighth, unwelcome physical contact, opens into its own further eight-fold list drawn from an earlier chapter of the treatise: being struck by a hand, struck with a thrown stone or clod of earth, beaten with a rod, cut with a blade, and — a broader, more everyday reading he draws out explicitly — subjected to excessive cold or heat through someone else's indifference (being refused a blanket in the cold, or refused a fan in summer heat), or left hungry and thirsty through someone else's neglect. The text names only the first three explicitly and lets "and so on" stand in for the remaining five, trusting the reader to recognize the full pattern.

What makes this a formal precept rather than merely good advice, he stresses, is the resolve that follows the reasoning: having genuinely walked through this reflection, a bodhisattva commits to never inflicting any of these eight harms on another being, holding to that commitment even under threat to their own life — the same uncompromising standard already established in the earlier difficult practices. He treats this precept as, in a sense, the simplest possible restatement of the entire discipline chapter: everything the many precepts already covered in fascicles 40 and 41 ultimately reduces to this one honest question, asked and answered with complete sincerity — would I want this done to me? This closes the eight fulfilling-requests precepts; the treatise turns next to the nine both-worlds-happiness precepts.

7. Milestone: The Both-Worlds-Happiness Precepts Open — Four Precepts of Discerning Judgment

The sixth lens, naming discipline by what it actually produces: happiness both in this present life and in lives to come. The treatise first names four precepts of discerning judgment — correctly discerning what must be restrained, what may rightly be permitted, which beings should be actively drawn in, and which beings require firmer correction — all carried out, in every case, with body and speech that remain genuinely, unwaveringly pure.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩此世他世樂戒？當知此戒略有九種。謂諸菩薩為諸有情於應遮處而正遮止，於應開處而正開許，是諸有情應攝受者正攝受之，應調伏者正調伏之，菩薩於中身語二業常清淨轉，是則名為四種淨戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's precept of happiness in this world and the next? One should know this precept is, in brief, of nine kinds. For the sake of beings, bodhisattvas correctly restrain what should genuinely be restrained, and correctly permit what should genuinely be permitted; among beings, they correctly draw in those who should genuinely be drawn in, and correctly correct those who genuinely require correction — bodhisattvas conducting themselves throughout with bodily and verbal action that remains constantly and genuinely pure. These are called the four kinds of pure precept.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the four categories of judgment this passage asks a bodhisattva to exercise wisely rather than mechanically. "What should be restrained" names conduct the Buddha himself forbade — action driven by intense affliction that leads toward a lower rebirth, action that blocks a favorable one, dishonest livelihood, action that endangers one's own life, or simply indulging the five senses so completely that liberation itself becomes unreachable; "what should be permitted" is everything on the opposite side — the ten wholesome deeds, the six perfections, and every other genuinely beneficial practice. A bodhisattva must be able to tell these apart clearly and act accordingly, neither permitting what should be restrained nor needlessly restraining what should be permitted.

"Those who should be drawn in" he divides into two related senses: a bodhisattva ruling as a king or otherwise wielding real authority who guides an entire population toward the ten wholesome deeds through uncorrupted leadership, and, more personally, a bodhisattva who gathers students not for private advantage but genuinely wishing them to flourish and mature in the teaching — the same non-attached gathering of disciples already discussed in fascicle 41. "Those who require correction" covers beings who have genuinely lost mindfulness and violated the precepts, matched to the same three-tier response already established earlier: verbal reprimand for a lesser fault, formal disciplinary correction for a middling one, and outright expulsion for a serious one. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses the passage's own closing qualification as the whole point of naming these four together: whichever of the four judgments a given situation calls for — restraining, permitting, drawing in, or correcting — a bodhisattva carries it out never through deception, always through honest and sincere speech, entirely free of harming, binding, striking, or coercing anyone, and, even where reprimand or expulsion is genuinely necessary, still holding real affection and freedom from any wish to injure. Correction delivered this way looks, from the outside, sometimes fierce and sometimes gentle, yet remains, in every case, genuinely pure at its root. This closes the four precepts of discerning judgment; the treatise turns next to five further precepts, each pairing discipline with one of the remaining five perfections.

8. Milestone: Five Precepts Pairing Discipline With Every Other Perfection — Both-Worlds-Happiness Closes

The second half of the both-worlds-happiness precepts: five further precepts, each showing how genuine discipline never stands alone but is always accompanied by one of the other five perfections in action — generosity, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom — bringing the total to nine, and closing with the treatise's own reminder that this entire ninefold precept is what actually secures genuine happiness for oneself and others, both now and in lives to come.

Chinese Source

復有所餘施、忍、精進、靜慮、般若波羅蜜多俱行淨戒，則為五種。總說名為九種淨戒。如是菩薩所有淨戒，能令自他現法後法皆得安樂，是故說名菩薩此世他世樂戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, pure precepts that proceed together with the perfections of generosity, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom — these make five more kinds. Together with the four already named, these are called, in total, the nine kinds of pure precept. In this way, all of a bodhisattva's pure precepts enable both oneself and others to attain genuine ease in this present existence and in lives to come — and it is for this reason that this is called a bodhisattva's precept of happiness in this world and the next.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the five pairings as a concrete illustration of how the six perfections interpenetrate one another — discipline practiced fully is never discipline alone. Paired with generosity: making offerings to the three jewels and never taking what belongs to others is discipline functioning as material generosity; leading by upright personal example while genuinely teaching the dharma to whoever seeks it is discipline functioning as the gift of the teaching; and holding firm in non-killing, together with genuine compassion for even those who have themselves broken precepts, is discipline functioning as the gift of freedom from fear. Paired with patience: not retaliating against an enemy, promptly accepting another's sincere apology, holding no grudge against someone who has wronged you, and governing any students one has without craving their service, are all discipline expressing itself as patience in action.

Paired with diligence: not indulging excessive sleep, not wasting the day in idle talk, and steadily clearing away carelessness in pursuit of the path are discipline expressing itself as sustained effort. Paired with meditative concentration: actively seeking a qualified teacher's guidance in settling the mind, releasing the five hindrances rather than tolerating them, and not becoming attached to the pleasure of absorption itself, are discipline expressing itself as genuine meditative discipline. And paired with wisdom: holding genuine right view grounded in the great vehicle's own understanding of selflessness, skillfully training in the five classical fields of knowledge, penetrating both the lesser and greater vehicles' scriptures, never denigrating a profound teaching one does not yet understand, conducting oneself with genuine integrity, refusing to praise oneself while disparaging others, regularly attending genuine dharma teaching, and never disrespecting the teacher, are all discipline expressing itself as wisdom in practice. Every one of these five pairings, he notes, directly echoes specific precepts already detailed in fascicle 41 — this passage's real contribution is showing how those same precepts, examined again through this ninefold lens, reveal discipline as never truly separable from any of the other five perfections. With this, all nine both-worlds-happiness precepts are complete, closing with the treatise's own plain restatement of why the whole ninefold precept earns its name: because it genuinely delivers happiness, for oneself and for others, in this life and in every life to come. The treatise turns next to the seventh and final lens — the ten purity precepts.

9. Milestone: The Ten Purity Precepts Open — From a Genuinely Pure Motive to a Precisely Balanced Mind

The seventh and final lens: the ten purity precepts, opening with the motive that must ground everything else — taking up the precepts purely for the sake of the teaching and supreme awakening, never merely to secure a livelihood — followed by four further qualities of a genuinely well-tuned practice: neither excessive self-reproach over a lapse nor needless guilt over what was never actually wrong, freedom from indulging the pleasures of sleep and rest, freedom from carelessness in every direction of time, and a properly ordered aspiration untainted by any wish for worldly gain or a pleasant heavenly rebirth.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's purity precept? One should know this precept is, in brief, of ten kinds. First, the precept of a genuinely good initial reception — taken up purely for the sake of the renunciant path and supreme awakening, never merely to secure a livelihood. Second, the precept free of excessive withdrawal — steering clear of only a faint, insufficient regret at an actual violation; together with the precept free of excessive agitation — steering clear of feeling regret and shame in situations where none is actually warranted. Third, the precept free of laziness — not indulging the pleasure of sleep, of reclining, or of lying down, but diligently cultivating every wholesome practice day and night. Fourth, the precept encompassed by freedom from every kind of carelessness — cultivating the five-part diligence already described earlier. Fifth, the precept of properly ordered aspiration — free of any craving for gain or reverence, with no wish for heavenly rebirth, resolved instead purely on cultivating the genuinely pure conduct that leads to liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first purity precept as the foundation every other precept in this list ultimately rests on: a bodhisattva whose original motive for taking up ordination and the precepts was genuinely for the dharma and supreme awakening, never for mere survival or comfort, carries that pure original intention forward into everything that follows — someone who took up the precepts for the wrong reason to begin with will struggle to sustain any of the other nine no matter how carefully they are individually practiced. He notes with a certain plainness that a monastic lacking this genuine aspiration risks becoming, in his own words, little more than "a shaved-headed layperson" — someone outwardly ordained but inwardly still living an ordinary, unexamined life, precisely the outcome this first precept exists to guard against.

The second precept he treats as a genuinely important piece of psychological balance, cutting in two directions at once: someone who violates a precept and feels only a faint, easily-dismissed regret has not taken the violation seriously enough, quietly allowing real laxity to creep in over time; but someone who develops the opposite habit — feeling intense guilt and repeated, anxious regret over matters that were never actually violations at all, or replaying an already-confessed and cleared violation over and over — has let regret itself become an obstacle, since this kind of excessive, misplaced remorse actively blocks the calm, settled mind that meditative practice requires. Genuine discipline, he stresses, requires knowing accurately what actually calls for regret and what does not, and calibrating one's response precisely to match. The remaining three in this opening group he treats more briefly: freedom from indulging sleep and freedom from carelessness both directly echo precepts already detailed in fascicle 41, restated here as marks of purity rather than merely as rules; and properly ordered aspiration insists that even a wish as apparently modest as favorable heavenly rebirth is still, in the end, a form of craving that has no place in a bodhisattva's actual motivation, which must aim only at genuine liberation itself. This closes the first five purity precepts; the treatise turns next to the remaining five, closing out the entire ninefold system of precept-lenses that has run since fascicle 40.

10. Milestone: Five More Purity Precepts Close the Entire Nine-Category System — and the Śrāvaka Stage's Own Ten Marks of Impairment

The remaining five purity precepts: genuinely proper conduct across every daily activity, an honest livelihood entirely free of the manipulative techniques already condemned in fascicle 41, steering clear of both self-indulgence and self-punishment, complete freedom from the wrong view of a fixed, independently existing self, and, finally, simply never having lost or damaged the very precepts already received — together closing not only the purity precepts but the entire nine-category system of precept-lenses this fascicle and its two predecessors have built, matched precisely against the Śrāvaka Stage's own ten classical marks of discipline's impairment.

Chinese Source

Sixth, the precept encompassed by fully proper conduct — carrying out every posture, every daily task, and every wholesome practice with genuinely excellent and complete propriety, body and speech consistently manifesting exactly as the dharma prescribes. Seventh, the precept encompassed by a fully pure livelihood — free of deceptive posturing and every other fault of dishonest livelihood. Eighth, the precept free of both extremes — steering clear of both indulgence in sensory pleasure and self-punishing austerity. Ninth, the precept of genuine, lasting release — entirely free of every non-Buddhist view of a fixed self. Tenth, the precept of no loss with respect to what was already received — the bodhisattva precepts once properly received suffering no deficiency and no breakdown whatsoever. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sixth and seventh precepts as restating, in this new context, ground already covered: proper conduct across walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, and across every daily monastic task, following the classical image of "walking like the wind, sitting like a bell, lying like a bow, standing like a pine" — dignified, settled comportment in every posture — while an honest livelihood means avoiding the five manipulative techniques for extracting donations already condemned at length in fascicle 41. The eighth, freedom from both extremes, restates the middle way already introduced earlier in this fascicle under the comprehensive-kind precepts. The ninth, genuine and lasting release, he treats as a doctrinally important clarification: non-Buddhist ascetics may, through sheer effort, temporarily suppress craving and even reach the very peak of the formless meditative attainments, yet remain bound by the wrong view of a fixed, independently existing self and so never achieve any genuine, final liberation at all — only a bodhisattva who has actually released that wrong view, grounded in pure discipline and building genuine concentration and wisdom upon it, achieves release that is truly lasting rather than merely temporary. The tenth is simply the negative confirmation of everything already positively established: the precepts once received have suffered no deficiency, no abandonment, and no change of heart.

He closes by drawing out the treatise's own striking cross-reference: the Śrāvaka Stage names exactly ten corresponding factors by which moral discipline becomes impaired — an improperly motivated original ordination, excessive withdrawal, excessive agitation, carelessness and laziness, a wrongly ordered aspiration, impairment of proper conduct, impairment of pure livelihood, falling into either extreme, an inability to achieve genuine release, and outright loss or breakdown of what was received — and these ten purity precepts are simply their direct positive mirror image, differing only in how the second through fourth items happen to be grouped (what the Śrāvaka Stage lists as three separate impairments, this passage combines into two precepts, folding excessive withdrawal and excessive agitation together while keeping carelessness distinct). Because this framework is shared directly with the śrāvaka path rather than unique to the bodhisattva precepts, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, it is precisely why this closing lens can rightly still be called, in full, a bodhisattva's own purity precept — the bodhisattva's precepts include and build upon everything the śrāvaka path already establishes, never discarding it. With this, all ten purity precepts are complete, and with them the entire nine-category system spanning the precepts' intrinsic nature, comprehensive scope, difficult practices, comprehensive gateways, virtuous persons, comprehensive kinds, fulfilling requests, both-worlds happiness, and purity — the full architecture of the bodhisattva precepts first opened in fascicle 40 finally, completely closed. The treatise turns next to a concluding summary of how this entire "great treasury of discipline" functions as a whole.

11. Milestone: Five Lasting Benefits of Holding the Precepts — Dying Without Fear, Like Setting Down a Poisoned Cup

The treatise steps back to summarize what holding this entire "great treasury of discipline" actually secures for a bodhisattva, right up until supreme awakening is finally reached: constant protection by the buddhas of all directions, genuine joy rather than fear at the moment of death, rebirth alongside genuine spiritual companions of equal or greater discipline, an ever-growing store of merit that steadily completes the perfection of discipline, and, most remarkably, a purity of precept that eventually becomes simply who the bodhisattva is, rather than something separately maintained.

Chinese Source

乃至未證無上菩提，依此無量菩薩戒藏正勤修習，常能獲得五種勝利。一者、常為十方諸佛護念。二者、將捨命時，住大歡喜。三者、身壞已後，在在所生，常與淨戒若等若增諸菩薩眾為其同分、為同法侶、為善知識。四者、成就無量大功德藏，能滿淨戒波羅蜜多。五者、現法後法常得成就自性淨戒，戒成其性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Right up until supreme awakening is realized, relying on this immeasurable treasury of the bodhisattva precepts and diligently practicing it brings five constant benefits. First, being constantly held in the protective awareness of the buddhas of the ten directions. Second, at the moment of death, abiding in great joy. Third, after this body has perished, wherever one is subsequently born, constantly finding oneself among bodhisattvas of equal or greater discipline, as genuine companions on the same path, comrades in the same practice, and true spiritual friends. Fourth, accomplishing an immeasurable, vast treasury of merit, thereby fulfilling the perfection of discipline. Fifth, in this present life and in every life to come, constantly coming to possess purity of discipline as one's own innate nature, discipline itself becoming one's very character.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers especially on the second benefit — genuine joy at the moment of death — since it addresses what is, for most people, the single greatest source of fear. A bodhisattva who has genuinely kept the precepts knows with real confidence, not mere hope, that their next rebirth will be favorable rather than a fall into a lower realm, and this settled knowledge itself is what produces the joy, not denial of death's reality. He cites the classical verse: "when pure conduct has been beautifully established, and the noble path well cultivated, one greets the ending of this life with genuine joy — like setting down a container that held poison." The five aggregates of body and mind, he explains, are themselves that poisoned container — clung to by most people out of sheer habit and fear of the unknown, yet a bodhisattva who has genuinely practiced knows this particular container was never something to hold onto in the first place, and setting it down at death feels, in his vivid phrase, like trading a copper coin for gold — something to welcome, not dread.

He also draws attention to the fifth benefit's most advanced expression, cross-referenced to a bodhisattva who has reached the second ground, called the ground of stainless purity: at that stage, discipline is no longer something actively maintained through effort at all, but has become so thoroughly integrated into the bodhisattva's own nature that not even the subtlest fault arises — "discipline becoming one's very character" describes exactly this transition from effortful practice to effortless, spontaneous purity. He notes the treatise's own closing plain statement here, worth taking at face value: this — the nine categories of precept, their five benefits, and their threefold function — is genuinely the complete and entire content of the bodhisattva precepts, with nothing further to add and nothing here to subtract, a claim made not out of overconfidence but out of Maitreya's own settled authority as the treatise's source. This closes the five lasting benefits; the treatise turns next to a final summary of how the entire threefold precept structure functions as a whole, closing the discipline chapter for good.

12. Milestone: The Discipline Chapter Closes — Three Precepts, Three Tasks, One Complete System — and the Perfection of Patience Opens

The treatise brings the entire discipline chapter to its formal close: all nine categories of precept ultimately reduce to the same three precepts already familiar from fascicle 40 — discipline, gathering wholesome dharmas, and benefiting beings — which together accomplish exactly three tasks for a bodhisattva: settling the mind in genuine meditative ease, maturing one's own qualities as a buddha-to-be, and maturing the beings one is working to benefit; and the treatise states plainly that this is the complete content of the bodhisattva precepts, studied identically by every bodhisattva of the past, present, and future throughout the ten directions — before turning, in its very next line, to an entirely new chapter: the perfection of patience.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

All nine kinds of discipline described above — from intrinsic nature onward — should be understood as encompassed within three precepts: the precept of discipline, the precept of gathering wholesome dharmas, and the precept of benefiting sentient beings. In brief, these three bodhisattva precepts accomplish three tasks for a bodhisattva: the precept of discipline settles the mind; the precept of gathering wholesome dharmas matures one's own qualities as a buddha; and the precept of benefiting sentient beings matures other beings. In this way, every task a bodhisattva needs to accomplish is fully encompassed: dwelling in genuine ease in this present life with a settled mind, body and mind free of weariness, maturing one's own buddha-qualities, and maturing other beings. A bodhisattva's precepts are exactly this much, their benefits are exactly this much, and their function is exactly this much — there is nothing beyond this, and nothing to add to it. Bodhisattvas of the past who sought supreme awakening have already trained in exactly this; bodhisattvas of the future who seek supreme awakening will train in exactly this; and bodhisattvas presently existing throughout the boundless worlds of the ten directions who seek supreme awakening are training in exactly this even now. From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter eleven: patience.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing passage as a genuinely satisfying piece of architecture: after fascicles 40 through 42 have opened the precepts out into nine distinct categories and dozens of individual precepts, the treatise now folds everything back down to the same simple threefold structure it started with — discipline settles the mind (providing the stability meditation itself depends on), gathering wholesome dharmas matures the bodhisattva's own qualities (the entire work of the six perfections), and benefiting beings matures others (the entire work of genuinely helping beings toward liberation). Every one of the forty-plus precepts detailed across three full fascicles, he stresses, was never really more than an elaboration of these same three functions from a different angle — nothing new in principle, only ever greater specificity in practice.

He closes by noting the significance of the treatise's own confident closing claim — that this is genuinely the complete content of the bodhisattva precepts, held identically by every bodhisattva across all of time and space — attributing this confidence to the treatise's traditional source, the bodhisattva Maitreya himself, whose authority as a future buddha grounds the claim rather than mere scholarly assertion. With this line, the discipline chapter that opened in fascicle 40 — covering the four root violations, the forty-three minor precepts, the eight classical exceptions, the mechanics of confession and self-ordination, and finally these nine ways of viewing the same body of precepts — comes to its complete and formal close. And in the very next line, without any further transition, the treatise opens an entirely new chapter: the eleventh chapter of the Bodhisattva Stage's foundational section, on the perfection of patience — a subject Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise will explore through nine distinct kinds of patience, just as discipline was explored through nine kinds of precept, beginning in the section that follows.

The chapter on the perfection of patience opens with the identical mnemonic verse already used to organize the entire discipline chapter — intrinsic nature, comprehensive scope, difficult practice, comprehensive gateways, virtuous persons, comprehensive kinds, fulfilling requests, both-worlds happiness, purity — now reapplied to patience itself, followed by a brief overview naming what each of the nine categories will cover.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this same nine-part verse organizes every one of the six perfections throughout the treatise — generosity, discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom are each explored through the identical structure, which is worth memorizing precisely because it provides a single reusable map for understanding all six at once, rather than six entirely separate frameworks to learn independently. 14. Milestone: The Intrinsic Nature of Patience — Three Classical Grades, from Simple Tolerance to Effortless Realization

What patience actually is at its root: either a capacity grounded in genuine, deliberate reflection, or an innate temperamental capacity to endure hostility, or, ideally, both together — in every case free of any trace of aversion and motivated purely by genuine compassion, spanning the classical three grades of patience from simple tolerance of others' harm through enduring the ordinary hardships of existence to the deepest, effortless patience born of directly realizing that nothing ultimately arises at all.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩自性忍？謂諸菩薩或思擇力為所依止，或由自性堪忍怨害，遍於一切皆悉堪忍、普於一切皆能堪忍。由無染心，純悲愍故，能有堪忍。當知此則略說菩薩忍之自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's patience of intrinsic nature? Bodhisattvas rely either on the power of deliberate reflection, or on an innate temperamental capacity to endure hostility and harm, and in either case remain tolerant of everything, universally able to endure everything. Because the mind is free of any defiling affliction, and moved purely by genuine compassion, they are able to remain patient. One should understand this as, in brief, a bodhisattva's patience of intrinsic nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats patience as simultaneously a form of wisdom, a form of strength, and a form of character — never merely passive endurance. He distinguishes the two roots named here: patience grounded in "the power of deliberate reflection" arises from genuinely wise consideration, actively thinking through why enduring a given difficulty is the right response; patience arising from "innate capacity" is simply a temperamental disposition some beings already carry, tolerating hostility without needing to reason their way there each time. A bodhisattva ideally holds both together, and it is specifically this reflective capacity that opens onto the classical three grades of patience worth holding in mind throughout this entire chapter: patience toward beings (生忍), the ordinary tolerance needed simply from living in close contact with other people, since any two bodies existing together will inevitably create some friction; patience toward phenomena (法忍), enduring impersonal hardship — extreme heat or cold, illness, the discomforts of aging — none of which involves any other being's malice at all, simply the nature of conditioned existence itself; and patience toward the unborn nature of phenomena (無生法忍), the deepest and most complete grade, arising only through genuine wisdom that directly realizes emptiness — whether through the Consciousness-Only school's analysis of the three natures and their absence of a fixed essence, the Madhyamaka school's analysis of dependent origination and emptiness, or the Buddha-nature teachings' own approach, all converging on the same direct realization that phenomena never truly arise at all.

He stresses why this third grade is called the most complete: once a bodhisattva has genuinely realized that nothing ultimately arises, there is, in the deepest sense, no longer anything left to "endure" at all — no self standing over against a hardship demanding effortful tolerance, since patience of this kind requires no straining exertion whatsoever, unlike the first two grades, which, however genuine, always retain something being actively endured and can eventually wear thin under enough pressure. A patience that still requires visible effort to maintain is, he notes bluntly, not yet the genuine article — real patience is thorough enough that it no longer costs anything to sustain. He closes by unpacking the treatise's own gloss distinguishing "universally tolerant of everything" (achieved through reflective power, spanning all phenomena, all suffering, and all hostility) from "constantly able to endure everything" (achievable even through mere innate temperament alone, but limited to suffering and hostility specifically, since realizing the emptiness of all phenomena requires the deliberate reflective power that innate temperament by itself cannot provide) — a subtle but genuinely important distinction between what raw disposition can accomplish on its own and what only genuine wisdom can complete. This closes the intrinsic nature of patience; the treatise turns next to comprehensive patience and its classical three-fold division.

Master Cháng Bǎi offers a preview of each of the three kinds that will structure the remainder of this chapter, since together they form the classical backbone of the entire patience perfection. Patience toward hostility and harm (耐他怨害忍), also called "patience toward beings," is cultivated chiefly through five specific contemplations applied toward friend, enemy, and stranger alike, across every gradation of relative status and circumstance — the subject the treatise takes up first and treats at the greatest length. Patience calmly bearing suffering (安受眾苦忍), also called "patience toward phenomena," covers the ordinary hardships built into simply having a body and living in the world: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, the discomforts of daily posture, the fatigue of serving the three jewels and one's teachers, an ordained practitioner's own hardships in going out to beg for food, the exertion of diligent practice across the six perfections, the wearing effort of genuinely working to benefit others, and the plain tedium of ordinary daily tasks. And the patience of resolute understanding (法思勝解忍), also called "the patience of the unborn," is the capacity to think through and correctly discern the nature of all phenomena, arriving at a settled, confident understanding — through genuine, careful reflection — of the qualities of the three jewels and everything else genuinely worth understanding and practicing.

He notes that both lay and ordained bodhisattvas cultivate all three kinds identically in their essential structure, though the specific circumstances calling for patience will naturally differ between a householder's daily life and a monastic's — a lay bodhisattva's hostility-endurance might be tested by family conflict or business dealings, while an ordained bodhisattva's might be tested within the more contained world of monastic community life, yet the underlying discipline being cultivated is exactly the same in both cases. This closes the overview of comprehensive patience's three-fold structure; the treatise turns next to the first and most extensively developed of the three — patience toward others' hostility and harm, opening with the reflective groundwork a bodhisattva must lay before genuine tolerance becomes possible at all.

16. Milestone: Before the Five Contemplations — Three Preliminary Reflections on Karma, Shared Suffering, and the Śrāvaka's Own Example

Before turning to the five specific contemplations for enduring others' hostility, the treatise lays essential groundwork: a bodhisattva who has genuinely accepted rebirth in saṃsāra out of compassion must expect real hostility from confused beings along the way, and should meet it first by recognizing the suffering as the fruit of one's own past karma, then by recognizing that retaliation only compounds suffering that both parties already inescapably share, and finally by recognizing that if even śrāvakas — seeking liberation for themselves alone — manage genuine patience, a bodhisattva working for every being's benefit has still less excuse not to.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩猛利無間種種長時從他怨害所生眾苦現在前時，應如是學：如此是我自業過耳，由我先世自造種種不淨業故，今受如是種種苦果。我今於此無義利苦若不忍者，復為當來大苦因處。我若於此大苦因法隨順轉者，便為於己自作非愛，便為於己自生結縛，便為於己自興怨害，非是於他。又自他身所有諸行，一切皆用性苦為體。彼無知故，於我身中性苦體上更增其苦；我既有知，何宜於彼性苦體上重加其苦。又諸聲聞多分唯修自義利行，尚不應起能生自他眾苦不忍，何況我今正為勤修他義利行而生不忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

When intense, unrelenting, varied, and long-lasting suffering arising from another's hostility and harm presents itself, bodhisattvas should train in this way: "This is simply my own karmic fault. Because I myself created various impure actions in past lives, I now receive this kind of painful result. If I fail to endure this meaningless, worthless suffering now, it will only become the cause of great suffering yet to come. If I now go along with this cause of great suffering, I only create something genuinely unwelcome for myself, only bind myself in further affliction, only bring hostility and harm upon myself — not upon anyone else." Furthermore, every activity of both my own body and another's body has, as its very essence, the nature of suffering. Out of sheer ignorance, the other person only adds further suffering onto a body already suffering by its very nature; since I already know better, how could it possibly be fitting for me to pile still more suffering onto a body that is, by nature, already suffering? Furthermore, śrāvakas, for the most part, cultivate practice purely for their own benefit, and yet even they should not give rise to intolerance that produces further suffering for both self and other — how much less should I, who am now diligently cultivating practice for the benefit of others, give rise to such intolerance?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the very first reflection as essential mental preparation for anyone genuinely taking up the bodhisattva path: having chosen, out of compassion, to remain bound to birth and death rather than exit into personal liberation, a bodhisattva must expect real hostility from beings still confused by ignorance along the way — coming to help beings inevitably means, at times, being hurt by the very beings one came to help. Rather than seeing this hostility as an unjust surprise, the first reflection reframes it honestly as the ripening of one's own past karma, and stresses the stakes of the response: refusing to endure it now, and instead meeting it with retaliatory anger, does not actually harm the other person at all — it only plants a fresh seed of future suffering in one's own mind, since it is the bodhisattva's own intolerance, not the other's original act, that becomes the operative cause going forward.

The second reflection he treats as a genuinely startling reframe: both bodies — the bodhisattva's own and the hostile other's — already share the exact same basic nature of suffering, simply by virtue of being conditioned, impermanent existence at all. The other person, not understanding this, adds still more suffering onto a body already suffering by its very constitution; a bodhisattva who does understand this has correspondingly less excuse to add yet more suffering back onto that same already-suffering being through retaliation. The third reflection appeals to a kind of moral logic from the lesser to the greater: śrāvakas, whose entire practice is oriented toward their own liberation alone, are still expected to show genuine patience rather than compounding suffering through retaliation — and if even a practice aimed purely at self-benefit demands this much tolerance, a bodhisattva whose entire practice is explicitly oriented toward benefiting every other being has, if anything, even less justification for intolerance. This closes the three preliminary reflections; the treatise turns next to the first, and richest, of the five specific contemplations — recognizing every hostile being as a former kinsman across countless past lives.

17. Milestone: The First Contemplation — Every Enemy Was Once Your Own Mother, Father, or Sibling

The first and, in the tradition's own estimation, most powerful of the five contemplations for enduring hostility: a bodhisattva reflects that across the literally beginningless span of past lifetimes, there is no being anywhere who has not, at some point, been one's own mother, father, sibling, teacher, or honored elder — meaning every hostile stranger encountered today is, in the deepest and most literal sense the tradition can offer, already family, simply unrecognized behind an unfamiliar face.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩應如是學：非易可得少分有情，經歷長世昔餘生中，未曾為我若父、若母、兄弟、姊妹、親教、軌範、尊似尊等。如是如理正思惟故，於有怨害諸有情所捨怨憎想，住親善想。依親善想，於諸怨害悉能堪忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas should train in this way: "It would be no easy thing to find even a small handful of beings who, across the long span of countless past lives, have never once been my father, my mother, my brother, my sister, my ordination preceptor, my instructing teacher, or an honored elder to me." Through this kind of genuine, principled reflection, bodhisattvas set aside any sense of hostility or resentment toward beings who have done them harm, and abide instead in a sense of genuine kinship. Relying on this sense of kinship, they are able to endure every kind of hostility and harm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi grounds this contemplation directly in the Buddha's own words, quoted in the treatise's cross-reference: "I have never seen any being of this kind that could actually be found" — meaning the Buddha himself could not point to a single being, across the whole of beginningless time, who had never once occupied one of these close family roles toward oneself. Given infinite past lifetimes, the sheer statistical improbability of any two beings never having crossed paths as close kin becomes, in effect, a certainty — today's stranger, today's enemy, was very likely yesterday's mother or brother, simply wearing an unfamiliar face after countless rounds of rebirth erased the memory.

He then offers his own extended, strikingly physical illustration of just how literally interconnected all beings actually are, worth preserving in full: think of water — what you drink today is eventually excreted, flows eventually to the sea, evaporates into cloud, falls again as rain, is filtered and drawn again as someone else's drinking water or bathwater; every being who drinks water, human or animal, shares in the same continuously cycling water, so that quite literally, "you are in my water, and I am in yours." The same holds for earth: food eaten becomes waste returned to soil, which nourishes the plants that become food again for others across the world — "you are in my earth, and I am in yours." Fire and warmth make no fundamental distinction between one body's heat and another's. And breath, most intimately of all: the air you exhale is drawn in by someone else's next breath, and what you now inhale already carries traces of countless other beings' own exhalations — in the fullest sense, he says, all beings form a single, genuinely inseparable whole. He adds a deeper doctrinal layer to the same point: every being and every circumstance encountered is, according to the Consciousness-Only teaching, a manifestation of one's own storehouse consciousness — the very enemy troubling you today was never really "found" from outside at all, but appeared precisely because your own karma manifested them, the way a dream figure is never anyone but your own mind at work; as the awakened masters say, "once you truly recognize mind itself, the great earth holds not one inch of separate ground." Cultivated regularly, he stresses, this contemplation genuinely dissolves the instinct to feel wronged and vengeful when difficulty arises, since the very category of "a stranger who attacked me for no reason" simply stops making sense once its premise — that the other person is genuinely a stranger — has been seen through. This closes the first contemplation; the treatise turns next to the second — recognizing that, in the deepest sense, there was never really "a person" doing the harming at all.

18. Milestone: The Second Contemplation — There Was Never Really "Someone" Doing the Harming

The second contemplation reaches for a subtler and more thoroughgoing release: examined through the lens of dependent origination, there is, in the strictest sense, no fixed self, no abiding being, no true agent anywhere in the exchange — only impersonal phenomena arising and passing according to conditions — meaning there was never genuinely "someone" who scolded, struck, or mocked, and never genuinely "someone" who was scolded, struck, or mocked either.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩應如是學：依託眾緣，唯行唯法，此中都無我及有情、命者、生者，是其能罵、能瞋、能打、能弄、能訶，或是所罵、所瞋、所打、所弄、所訶。如是如理正思惟故，於有怨害諸有情所捨有情想，住唯法想。依唯法想，於諸怨害悉能堪忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas should train in this way: "Arising in dependence on manifold conditions, there is only impersonal activity, only impersonal phenomena; within all this there is no fixed self, no abiding being, no life-possessor, no producer of anything at all — no one who scolds, who grows angry, who strikes, who mocks, who reproaches; and no one who is scolded, made angry at, struck, mocked, or reproached." Through this kind of genuine, principled reflection, bodhisattvas set aside the notion of "a being" doing harm, and abide instead in the recognition of mere impersonal phenomena. Relying on this recognition, they are able to endure every kind of hostility and harm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the classical analysis of dependent origination underlying this contemplation: everything that exists arises through the four conditions and unfolds through the twelve links running from ignorance through aging and death, and careful reflection on this chain — across past, present, and future — reveals only phenomena giving rise to further phenomena, with no fixed, unchanging agent directing the process and no fixed, unchanging recipient receiving its results. There is only "such-and-such arising because such-and-such arose before it," ignorance conditioning formations, formations conditioning consciousness, and so on down the chain, phenomenon after phenomenon, with nothing anywhere that could genuinely be pointed to as an independent, controlling "self" behind any of it.

Applied to a moment of being scolded or struck, this means there was, in the strictest analysis, no truly existing "I" being harmed and no truly existing "other" doing the harming — only, as the treatise puts it starkly, "mere sound, mere name." When someone calls you a fool, he notes, there is no actual entity called "foolishness" sitting anywhere inside that syllable — examine the word itself and nothing solid can be found there at all; seeing this clearly, the sting simply has nowhere left to land. He contrasts this with the first contemplation's approach: where recognizing everyone as former kin softens hostility through a kind of expanded love, this second contemplation dissolves it more radically, by seeing through the very categories — self, other, insult, injury — that made hostility possible as a concept in the first place. He offers the classical image of being struck by an empty, unmanned boat drifting downriver: no one gets genuinely angry at a boat with no one steering it, because there is plainly no one to be angry at — and the deepest Mahāyāna teaching, he adds, goes even further still, since not only is there no person to find in the exchange, but, examined with sufficiently thorough wisdom, not even the phenomena themselves — the "boat," the "blow," the "insult" — can be found to possess any fixed, independent nature of their own either, leaving, in the end, genuinely nothing left worth getting angry about. This closes the second contemplation; the treatise turns next to the third — reflecting on the sheer impermanence already dissolving both parties moment by moment.

19. Milestone: The Third and Fourth Contemplations — Already Dying Moment by Moment, and Already Suffering by Nature

Two further contemplations: the third reflects that every being's life is already ending, moment by moment, in a continuous, unstoppable process of arising and passing — so that deliberately taking a life only accomplishes what is already happening on its own, making even the wish to harm someone, let alone the act itself, simply beside the point; the fourth reflects that every being, however outwardly fortunate, already suffers constantly under three pervasive kinds of suffering, and that adding to an already-suffering being's burden is the last thing a bodhisattva, once this is truly seen, could wish to do.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩應如是學：諸有有情若生若長，一切無常，皆是死法。極報怨者，謂斷彼命。是諸有情命念念斷，智者何緣復欲更斷。如是生死性無常法諸有情上，其有智者尚不應起有染濁心，況當以手、塊杖加害，何況一切永斷其命。……謂諸菩薩應如是觀：若諸有情大興盛者，尚為三苦常所隨逐，所謂行苦、壞苦、苦苦，況諸有情住衰損者。如是觀已，應如是學：我今於此苦常隨逐諸有情所，應勤方便令離眾苦，不應於彼重加其苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas should train in this way: "All beings, whether just born or already grown, are entirely impermanent, subject in every case to death. The most extreme act of vengeance one could take is to end another's life — yet these beings' lives are already ending, moment by moment, on their own; why would anyone wise wish to end them further? Given that the very nature of birth and death is already impermanence itself, even a wise person should not give rise to even a defiled thought toward such beings, let alone actually harm them with a hand, a stone, or a rod — how much less end their life entirely?" ... Bodhisattvas should reflect in this way: "Even beings of great flourishing and prosperity are still constantly pursued by the three kinds of suffering — the suffering of conditioned existence itself, the suffering of change, and the suffering of outright pain — how much more so beings already living in decline and hardship." Having reflected in this way, they should further train: "Toward these beings, constantly pursued by suffering, I should diligently apply skillful means to free them from suffering, not add further suffering onto them."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the impermanence contemplation as a kind of quiet logical trap for anger itself: the most severe harm anyone could wish on another is death, yet death is already underway in every being, every single moment, entirely without anyone's help — cells and moments of consciousness alike constantly arising and immediately perishing. Wishing harm on someone already dying by nature is, seen clearly, simply redundant; there is nothing left for hostility to actually accomplish that impermanence was not already accomplishing on its own. He pairs this with the classical verse: "there is no need to argue over right and wrong, no need to debate who has more or less merit — the affairs of this world are inherently flawed by nature; study the path, seek what is genuinely true, and be free of impermanence's grip" — a reminder that the entire dispute over who wronged whom evaporates once both parties are seen as already, moment by moment, ceasing to be the people they were a moment ago.

On the suffering contemplation, he unpacks the classical three kinds carefully: the suffering of conditioned existence itself (行苦) is the subtlest and most pervasive, simply the built-in instability of any conditioned body and mind, present even in ordinary neutral experience and largely invisible to anyone without genuine insight; the suffering of change (壞苦) is the pain of watching anything pleasant — a beloved friend, a cherished possession, a happy circumstance — inevitably deteriorate or vanish; and the suffering of outright pain (苦苦) is the most obvious kind, ordinary physical and emotional distress, illness, loss, and unmet longing. Even a being at the height of wealth and good fortune remains subject to all three, which is precisely the point: recognizing that literally every being — including whoever is presently causing harm — is already suffering this deeply and this constantly makes deliberately adding to that burden through retaliation feel, once truly seen, almost unthinkable; the only fitting response left is the wish to help relieve suffering, never to compound it. This closes the third and fourth contemplations; the treatise turns next to the fifth and final contemplation — embracing every being, even a hostile one, as already one's own.

20. Milestone: The Fifth Contemplation — Embracing Every Being as One's Own Kin — Patience Toward Hostility and Harm Closes

The fifth and final contemplation: a bodhisattva who took up the bodhisattva vow specifically for the sake of every being has already, in that very act, embraced all beings as genuine kin, and so recognizes that meeting hostility with intolerance would directly contradict the vow's own original purpose — followed by the treatise's own compact definition of what "patience" actually means, and the formal close of this first of the three kinds of patience.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩應如是學：我為一切有情之類發菩提心，攝受一切有情之類皆為親眷，我應為彼作諸義利。我今不應本於有情欲作義利，而當於彼不忍怨害，作非義利。如是如理正思惟故，於有怨害諸有情所滅除他想，住攝受想。依攝受想，於諸怨害悉能堪忍。云何名忍？自無憤勃，不報他怨，亦不隨眠流注恆續，故名為忍。是名菩薩耐他怨害忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas should train in this way: "I gave rise to the aspiration for awakening for the sake of every kind of being, embracing every kind of being as my own kin; I ought to act for their genuine benefit. Having originally resolved to act for beings' benefit, I should not now, faced with their hostility, become intolerant and act instead for their harm." Through this kind of genuine, principled reflection, bodhisattvas dissolve the sense of the other as separate, and abide instead in a sense of genuine embrace. Relying on this sense of embrace, they are able to endure every kind of hostility and harm. What is meant by patience? Being personally free of indignant anger, not retaliating against another's hostility, and not letting it settle into a continuously flowing undercurrent of resentment — this is what is called patience. This is called a bodhisattva's patience toward others' hostility and harm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fifth contemplation as circling back to ground the whole path in its own original commitment: a bodhisattva's very first act on this path was to vow benefit for every being without exception, and hostility met with retaliation would be a direct, self-contradicting betrayal of that original vow — not a new failure so much as an abandonment of what one already promised at the very start. He notes the treatise's own deeper gloss here, tying this contemplation back to the Consciousness-Only teaching already introduced in the first contemplation: since all beings are, in the fullest sense, manifestations of one's own mind, embracing them as kin can be taken even further, as embracing them as literally part of one's own being — the same way a hand instinctively moves to brush away a mosquito biting one's own foot, without any deliberation or resentment toward the mosquito, simply because foot and hand are unmistakably part of the same body. A bodhisattva who has genuinely internalized this sees a suffering or hostile being the same way, moved to help rather than retaliate as naturally as the hand moves to the foot.

He closes by drawing out the treatise's compact three-part definition of patience itself, worth holding as a checklist against which to measure genuine practice: first, no actual anger arising in the mind at all, not merely anger suppressed and hidden behind a calm exterior; second, no retaliation in action, regardless of the internal state; and third — the subtlest and easiest to miss — no lingering resentment quietly persisting underneath an outwardly forgiving surface, continuing to flow beneath consciousness like an underground current even after the incident itself has passed. Genuine patience requires all three together; someone who merely refrains from visible retaliation while nursing private resentment has not yet achieved it. With this, the first of the chapter's three kinds of patience — enduring others' hostility and harm, cultivated through careful preliminary reflection and these five specific contemplations — reaches its complete close. The treatise turns next to the second kind: patience calmly enduring the ordinary sufferings built into existence itself.
