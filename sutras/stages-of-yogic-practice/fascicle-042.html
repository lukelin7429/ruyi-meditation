Fascicle 42 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十二 · The Seven Remaining Kinds of Precept Close Out Discipline

Difficult Practices to Purity

Continuing directly from fascicle 41's complete account of the forty-three minor precepts, the treatise now closes the whole chapter on the perfection of discipline by re-describing that same body of precepts through seven further classical lenses — difficult practices, comprehensive-gateway, virtuous-person, comprehensive-kind, fulfilling-requests, both-worlds-happiness, and purity precepts — before opening an entirely new chapter: the perfection of patience.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 42 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Opening — and the Three Difficult Practices

Fascicle 42 opens by locating itself precisely within the treatise's larger architecture — still within the Bodhisattva Stage's chapter on discipline, continuing directly from fascicles 40 and 41's account of the precepts' intrinsic nature and their comprehensive detail — and announces that this fascicle will describe the remaining seven ways of viewing that same body of precepts, beginning with the three difficult practices: a bodhisattva's willingness to give up great worldly wealth and standing to take up the precepts in the first place, to hold them without the slightest compromise even facing mortal danger, and to hold them, across a whole lifetime of activity, without ever once losing mindfulness.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處戒品第十之三。云何菩薩難行戒？當知此戒略有三種。謂諸菩薩，現在具足大財、大族自在增上，棄捨如是大財、大族自在增上，受持菩薩淨戒律儀。是名菩薩第一難行戒。又諸菩薩受淨戒已，若遭急難乃至失命，於所受戒尚無少闕，何況全犯。是名菩薩第二難行戒。又諸菩薩，如是如是遍於一切行住作意，恆住正念，常無放逸，乃至命終於所受戒無有誤失，尚不犯輕，何況犯重。是名菩薩第三難行戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the discipline of the initial yoga foundation, part three. What is a bodhisattva's difficult practice with regard to discipline? One should know this precept is, in brief, of three kinds. Bodhisattvas who presently possess great wealth, an eminent family, and genuine worldly power and independence, yet give up all of that wealth, family standing, and independence in order to take up and hold the bodhisattva precepts — this is called a bodhisattva's first difficult practice. Bodhisattvas who, having received the precepts, encounter sudden mortal danger, even facing the loss of life itself, and yet find not the slightest compromise in the precepts they hold, let alone an outright violation — this is called a bodhisattva's second difficult practice. Bodhisattvas who, in this way and that, across every moment of walking, standing, and all other activity, constantly abide in mindfulness, remaining forever free of carelessness, right up until the moment of death, finding no error at all in the precepts they hold — not violating even a minor precept, let alone a grave one — this is called a bodhisattva's third difficult practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that this fascicle continues the same eighteen-chapter architecture of the Bodhisattva Stage already established, still within the tenth chapter on discipline — this being its third and final installment, following fascicle 40's treatment of discipline's intrinsic nature and comprehensive scope, and fascicle 41's complete account of the forty-three minor precepts. What remains, he notes, is not new content so much as seven different classical lenses through which that same body of precepts can be viewed and appreciated — a technique the treatise uses throughout to help practitioners genuinely internalize material already covered, by returning to it from fresh angles rather than simply repeating it. He also flags, as a preview, that this fascicle will go on to open two entirely new chapters after discipline finally closes: the nine kinds of patience, and the nine kinds of diligence.

On the three difficult practices themselves, he stresses what makes each one genuinely difficult rather than merely notable: giving up considerable wealth and high family standing for the precepts is difficult because it runs directly against the strongest of ordinary human motivations — accumulated comfort and social position are, for most people, precisely what a lifetime is spent building, not discarding. Holding the precepts with zero compromise even under mortal threat is difficult because fear of death is, by most accounts, the single most powerful instinct a person carries, and yet the bodhisattva's discipline must remain completely intact regardless. And holding unbroken mindfulness across an entire lifetime of ordinary activity — walking, standing, sitting, lying down, working, resting — right up to the final moment, is difficult simply because sustained attention of that kind, moment after moment for an entire lifetime, is something almost no one manages to do without significant lapses. He notes the treatise's own technical gloss connecting this passage to an earlier five-part framework of "diligence free of carelessness" spanning past, present, and future action — here specifically the two components concerning deliberate prior resolve and continuous present follow-through, both of which a bodhisattva must hold simultaneously to genuinely never lapse. This closes the three difficult practices; the treatise turns next to the comprehensive-gateway precepts, describing the four doorways through which the precepts as a whole are entered and sustained.

2. Milestone: The Four Comprehensive-Gateway Precepts — Formal Reception, Innate Nature, Habituation, and Skillful Means

The second of the seven lenses: the comprehensive-gateway precepts, describing the four distinct ways a bodhisattva actually comes to hold the precepts — formally receiving them through proper ordination, possessing them already as an innate disposition prior to formal reception, carrying them forward through habituation cultivated across past lifetimes, and actively applying them through the four specific skillful means of gathering and guiding other beings.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切門戒？當知此戒略有四種。一者、正受戒，二者、本性戒，三者、串習戒，四者、方便相應戒。正受戒者，謂諸菩薩受先所受三種菩薩淨戒律儀。即律儀戒、攝善法戒、饒益有情戒。本性戒者，謂諸菩薩住種性位，本性仁賢，於相續中，身語二業恆清淨轉。串習戒者，謂諸菩薩昔餘生中，曾串修習如先所說三種淨戒。由宿因力所任持故，於現在世一切惡法不樂現行，於諸惡法深心厭離；樂修善行，於善行中深心欣慕。方便相應戒者，謂諸菩薩依四攝事，於諸有情身語意業恆相續轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-gateway precept? One should know this precept is, in brief, of four kinds. First, the precept of formal reception; second, the precept of innate nature; third, the precept of habituation; fourth, the precept of skillful means. The precept of formal reception is when bodhisattvas properly receive the three bodhisattva precepts already described — namely, the precept of discipline, the precept of gathering wholesome dharmas, and the precept of benefiting sentient beings. The precept of innate nature is when bodhisattvas, abiding within the bodhisattva lineage prior even to formal reception, are by innate nature kind and virtuous, their bodily and verbal conduct remaining constantly pure throughout the continuity of their being. The precept of habituation is when bodhisattvas, having already practiced and habituated themselves to these same three precepts across former lifetimes, find that the power of that prior cause now sustains them, such that in the present life they take no pleasure at all in unwholesome conduct, feel deep aversion toward it, and instead delight in wholesome conduct, feeling deep and genuine enthusiasm for it. The precept of skillful means is when bodhisattvas, relying on the four means of gathering beings, keep their bodily, verbal, and mental conduct constantly and continuously directed toward benefiting all beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two kinds of spiritual lineage at work in the second and third precepts here: "innate nature" describes what the tradition calls lineage abiding by nature (本性住種性) — a disposition present since beginningless time, requiring no prior cultivation at all, simply the way this particular being's mind is constituted, naturally kind and easy to get along with even before ever formally encountering the Buddhist path; "habituation," by contrast, describes lineage accomplished through practice (習所成種性) — a disposition built up through genuine effort across countless past lifetimes of actually taking up and practicing the precepts, whose accumulated momentum now expresses itself as a strong, spontaneous-feeling aversion to wrongdoing and delight in virtue in the present life, even though it was, in fact, earned rather than simply given. Both kinds of lineage, he notes, are genuinely valuable, and a given bodhisattva may possess either, or both, in different measure.

He unpacks the four skillful means named in the fourth precept with real care, since they recur throughout the treatise's account of how a bodhisattva actually engages other beings: "accommodating means" (隨攝方便), corresponding to generosity, is the necessary first step of simply giving material support to build genuine goodwill — "first draw them in with what they desire, then lead them into the Buddha's wisdom afterward," as the classical saying goes; "capturing means" (能攝方便), corresponding to loving speech, is using genuinely meaningful, skillfully chosen words to help a confused being see through to the actual workings of cause and effect they had not understood; "inducting means" (令入方便), corresponding to beneficial conduct, is the concrete work of actually guiding someone, once genuinely receptive, into real training in discipline, concentration, and wisdom; and "accompanying means" (隨轉方便), corresponding to working alongside others, is leading by example — a bodhisattva who asks others to cultivate faith, discipline, learning, generosity, and wisdom must be seen genuinely living out those same qualities themselves, working alongside the student rather than merely directing from a distance. This closes the four comprehensive-gateway precepts; the treatise turns next to the five virtuous-person precepts.

3. Milestone: The Five Virtuous-Person Precepts — What Makes Someone a Genuine Spiritual Friend

The third lens: the virtuous-person precepts, naming the five marks of someone genuinely worth calling a good spiritual friend with respect to discipline — holding the precepts oneself, actively encouraging others to take them up, genuinely praising their benefits, feeling real joy at seeing fellow practitioners keep them well, and, should one's own conduct ever lapse, promptly and properly confessing and correcting it.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩善士戒？當知此戒略有五種。謂諸菩薩自具尸羅，勸他受戒，讚戒功德，見同法者深心歡喜，設有毀犯如法悔除。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's virtuous-person precept? One should know this precept is, in brief, of five kinds: bodhisattvas themselves genuinely hold the precepts; they encourage others to receive the precepts; they praise the precepts' genuine benefits; seeing fellow practitioners who hold the same precepts, they feel deep and genuine joy; and should a lapse ever occur in their own conduct, they properly confess and correct it without delay.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that these five marks together describe exactly what makes someone a genuine spiritual friend specifically with respect to discipline — not simply a good person in some general sense, but someone whose entire relationship to the precepts qualifies them to actually help others along the same path: they walk the walk themselves, actively invite others onto it rather than keeping their practice private, can articulate why it genuinely matters rather than merely modeling it silently, take real delight rather than quiet indifference or competitive comparison when others succeed at the same practice, and handle their own inevitable lapses with honest correction rather than concealment or denial. Any one of these alone would be admirable; together, they describe someone capable of genuinely guiding others rather than merely practicing in isolation.

He passes along the modern teacher Venerable Miao-Jing's own observation about the second mark — genuinely praising the precepts' benefits — noting two complementary angles from which this praise can be offered: through the lens of cause and effect, explaining plainly that unwholesome action leads toward lower realms of suffering while wholesome action leads toward higher realms of genuine happiness; or through the lens of compassion itself, since every one of the ten unwholesome deeds the precepts guard against is, at root, a failure of compassion — killing, stealing, and the rest all involve real harm to another being, so praising the precepts' benefit can equally well be framed as praising the compassion that not harming others actually embodies. Master Cháng Bǎi treats compassion as, in fact, the deepest logic underlying discipline as such: holding the precepts is ultimately just a concrete, practical expression of not wishing to harm any being, which is why someone who genuinely delights in seeing others hold the precepts well is, in the fullest sense, delighting in the spread of compassion itself. This closes the five virtuous-person precepts; the treatise turns next to the thirteen comprehensive-kind precepts.

4. Milestone: The Comprehensive-Kind Precepts Open — Six Ways the Precepts Themselves Are Complete

The fourth and most elaborate lens yet: the comprehensive-kind precepts, comprising thirteen further ways of describing the same body of precepts — six naming the precepts' own intrinsic qualities, and seven naming the different fruits they produce — beginning with six qualities: dedication toward supreme awakening, breadth spanning every area of training, freedom from both self-indulgence and self-punishment, permanence across an entire lifetime, unshakeable firmness against any pressure to abandon them, and the genuinely universal ornament they provide.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切種戒？當知此戒以要言之，六種、七種，總十三種。言六種者，一、迴向戒，迴向大菩提故。二、廣博戒，廣攝一切所學處故。三、無罪歡喜處戒，遠離耽著欲樂、自苦二邊行故。四、恆常戒，雖盡壽命亦不棄捨所學處故。五、堅固戒，一切利養恭敬、他論、本隨煩惱不能伏故、不能奪故。六、尸羅莊嚴具相應戒，具足一切戒莊嚴故。尸羅莊嚴如聲聞地應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-kind precept? One should know this precept, in brief, comes to thirteen kinds altogether — six of one sort and seven of another. The six are: first, the precept of dedication, since it is dedicated toward supreme awakening. Second, the precept of breadth, since it comprehensively includes every area of training. Third, the precept of blameless joy, since it steers clear of both indulgent attachment to sensory pleasure and self-punishing austerity. Fourth, the precept of permanence, since it is never abandoned even at the cost of one's own life. Fifth, the precept of firmness, since it cannot be overcome or stolen away by any offering of support and reverence, any rival doctrine, or any root or secondary affliction. Sixth, the precept that functions as an ornament, since it fully possesses the qualities of every kind of adornment a precept can offer — a nature of adornment one should understand in the same way as described in the Śrāvaka Stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the six with genuine care. Dedication (迴向) he describes through its three classical directions: dedicating cause toward fruit, as when the merit of holding discipline in the present is directed toward the future attainment of supreme awakening; dedicating phenomena toward principle, as when the very act of holding a precept is directed toward the direct realization of emptiness itself, freeing it from any grasping at self or phenomena; and dedicating oneself toward others, as when the entire merit of one's own practice — including confession, requesting the teaching, and every other wholesome act — is offered outward to all beings throughout the dharma-realm, that all might realize awakening together, precisely echoing the great vow "may this merit extend universally to all, that we and every being might together complete the buddha path." Breadth (廣博) simply names the precepts' own comprehensive scope, spanning discipline, gathering wholesome dharmas, and benefiting beings together, leaving nothing genuinely relevant to the path untouched.

Blameless joy (無罪歡喜處) he treats as the treatise's own quiet declaration of the middle way in miniature: a bodhisattva neither indulges attachment to fine food, clothing, or comfortable surroundings, always examining their genuine drawbacks and knowing how to release them, nor adopts the kind of self-inflicted suffering some outside traditions practice — imitating animal behavior, covering oneself in ash, and similar ascetic extremes — since the Buddha's own discipline runs directly down the middle, attached to neither extreme. Permanence (恆常) and firmness (堅固) he treats as closely related: permanence describes holding the precepts across an entire lifetime without ever setting them aside, while firmness describes an inner stability so well-established that no external inducement — wealth, flattery, a persuasive rival argument, or the pull of one's own remaining affliction — can actually shake it loose, a firmness built on both genuine strength of resolve and a settled, well-grounded understanding that cannot be argued away. And the ornament of discipline (尸羅莊嚴具), he notes, drawing on the treatise's own cross-reference to the Śrāvaka Stage, is unlike ordinary jewelry, which only flatters those already young and attractive — genuine moral discipline adorns absolutely anyone who wears it, young or old, sick or healthy, at any hour of the day, which is precisely why the Buddha called it an ornament unlike any other. This closes the six qualities; the treatise turns next to the seven fruits the precepts produce.

5. Milestone: Seven Fruits the Precepts Produce — Culminating in a Heart That Naturally Loves Beings

The second half of the comprehensive-kind precepts: seven further qualities describing what genuinely disciplined conduct actually produces — stopping harm, actively doing good, guarding that twofold achievement, the physical marks of a great being, a settled and joyful mind, favorable future rebirth, and, as the crowning fruit, a heart that has come to love and care for beings as its own spontaneous nature.

Chinese Source

言七種者，一、止息戒，遠離一切殺生等故。二、轉作戒，攝一切善故，饒益有情故。三、防護戒，隨護止息轉作戒故。四、大士相異熟戒。五、增上心異熟戒。六、可愛趣異熟戒。七、利有情異熟戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

The seven are: first, the precept of ceasing, since it steers entirely clear of killing and every other harmful act. Second, the precept of actively doing, since it comprehensively gathers every wholesome deed and genuinely benefits sentient beings. Third, the precept of guarding, since it protects and sustains both the precept of ceasing and the precept of actively doing. Fourth, the precept that ripens into the marks of a great being. Fifth, the precept that ripens into a settled, elevated mind. Sixth, the precept that ripens into rebirth in genuinely favorable circumstances. Seventh, the precept that ripens into a heart that benefits sentient beings as its own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first three as corresponding directly to the three-part structure this entire section of the treatise has followed all along: ceasing (止息) matches the precept of discipline itself, actively doing (轉作) matches gathering wholesome dharmas and benefiting beings together, and guarding (防護) is simply the ongoing vigilance that keeps both of the first two genuinely intact rather than eroding over time — stopping harm without also actively cultivating good would be incomplete, and either without ongoing guarding would eventually slip.

The remaining four he treats as a genuine chain of maturing fruit, each building on the last. The marks of a great being (大士相異熟) names the eight classical marks of excellent karmic ripening a bodhisattva's disciplined conduct produces — a full lifespan, an excellent physical form, noble family standing, genuine independence, trustworthy speech, great influence, a favorable human nature, and great strength — laid out more fully in an earlier chapter of the treatise. A settled, elevated mind (增上心異熟) follows naturally from genuinely clean conduct: a mind with nothing to regret feels no anxious remorse, a mind without remorse settles into genuine ease, and a mind at ease naturally deepens into concentration — discipline itself functioning as the foundation concentration is built upon. Favorable rebirth (可愛趣異熟) is the natural consequence of that same clean conduct extended across lifetimes: an ordinary practitioner is reborn into families of genuine standing and means, while a bodhisattva who has already reached the grounds is reborn, appropriately to each stage from the first ground of great joy through the tenth and final ground, always in circumstances suited to their continued practice.

And the seventh, crowning fruit — a heart that benefits beings as its own nature (利有情異熟) — he treats as the deepest point in this whole progression: a bodhisattva who has spent countless past lifetimes cultivating discipline alongside the eleven classical forms of benefiting others eventually reaches a point where compassionate concern for beings is no longer an effort at all, but simply how the mind spontaneously moves, arising as naturally as breathing rather than through deliberate exertion. Master Cháng Bǎi calls this the truest mark of a mature practitioner: someone whose very first instinct, in any situation, is concern for how others are faring — not because they are trying to be compassionate, but because sustained practice has genuinely reshaped what compassion now costs them to feel. This closes all thirteen comprehensive-kind precepts — six qualities and seven fruits together; the treatise turns next to the fulfilling-requests precepts.
