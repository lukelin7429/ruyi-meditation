Fascicle 98 · 瑜伽師地論卷第九十八 · The Fourth Particular Verse of the Foundations of Mindfulness Begins

The Fourth Particular Verse Begins

Fascicle 98 continues directly from fascicle 97, which closed the first three particular verses of the foundations of mindfulness (念住攝) in their entirety. This fascicle opens the fourth and final particular verse, naming eight matters: the false teacher's self-serving dwelling, the snow mountain's parable of the monkey and the hunter, exhortation, being bound to purity, the gradual sequence, the precepts' fullness, the ear of grain, and accomplishment. Once this verse closes, the foundations of mindfulness (the first of the factors of enlightenment's nine great topics) will stand complete, and this fascicle will continue into the remaining eight topics — right effort, the bases of supernormal power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of enlightenment, the eightfold path, mindfulness of breathing, the trainee and the adept beyond training, and the four confirmed purities — all previously confirmed, per fascicle 97's own framing, to fall entirely within this fascicle.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 98 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Fascicle's Own Title — Continuing the Embrace of the Factors of Enlightenment

Within the compendium of bases, sūtra matters, the embrace of the factors of enlightenment, the fourth, part two.

Chinese Source

攝事分中契經事菩提分法擇攝第四之二

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the compendium of bases, sūtra matters, the embrace of the factors of enlightenment, the fourth, part two.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This is the fascicle's own chapter title, marking its place as the second part ("第四之二") of the fourth and final great division of sūtra matters (契經事) within the compendium of bases (攝事分, spanning fascicles 85 through 100). Master Cháng Bǎi recaps the whole division's structure: the aggregates' embrace (行擇攝), the sense-bases' embrace (處擇攝), the dependent-origination-food-truths-elements embrace (緣起食諦界擇攝), and the factors of enlightenment's embrace (菩提分法擇攝) — this last spanning fascicles 97 and 98, with fascicle 97 having already closed the general summary verse and the foundations of mindfulness's first three particular verses in their entirety, and this fascicle now continuing with the foundations of mindfulness's fourth and final particular verse.

3. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — The False Teacher's Dwelling Begins — Manifesting Exclusive Self-Interest Begins — Marking

There are various outside paths who, among their assembly of disciples, establish themselves as teacher, yet exclusively seek gain, exclusively seek veneration, and exclusively seek their own benefit.

Chinese Source

有諸外道，於弟子眾自立為師，專求利養、專求恭敬、專求自利。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are various outside paths who, among their assembly of disciples, establish themselves as teacher, yet exclusively seek gain, exclusively seek veneration, and exclusively seek their own benefit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the long-form explanation (子二長行釋8), an eightfold structure matching the verse's eight matters, beginning with the false teacher's dwelling (丑一邪師住2), named by the verse's first phrase "the false teacher's dwelling, the snow mountain" (邪師住雪山), beginning with manifesting exclusive self-interest (寅一明專自利2), beginning with marking (卯一標) — an outside-path teacher who sets himself up as a guide to disciples while exclusively chasing gain, veneration, and self-benefit is, at root, a false teacher regardless of outward appearance.

4. Section: Explaining Begins — Raising the Two Speeches Begins — The Outside-Path Teacher

Meeting a conjunction of conditions, there is a son of good family who goes forth under him. He then says to him: "You and I have not previously had any resources or requisites to share and use in common. You should, for my sake, go to another place, praise my virtues, and conceal my faults; I, too, will do the same for you. The two of us, taking turns relying on and protecting one another, will thereby obtain much gain and veneration from kings, from those equal to kings, and even from every great merchant lord." If a teacher speaks in this way, the various outside teachers are called exclusively seeking their own benefit.

Chinese Source

遇緣和合，有族姓子投其出家。因而謂曰：汝之與我先無一切資身眾具可共受用，汝應為我往詣他處，褒讚我德，掩藏我失，我亦為汝行如是事。我等二人迭相依護，當於諸王若與王等乃至一切大商主邊，多獲利養及以恭敬。若作是言，諸外道師名專自利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Meeting a conjunction of conditions, there is a son of good family who goes forth under him. He then says to him: "You and I have not previously had any resources or requisites to share and use in common. You should, for my sake, go to another place, praise my virtues, and conceal my faults; I, too, will do the same for you. The two of us, taking turns relying on and protecting one another, will thereby obtain much gain and veneration from kings, from those equal to kings, and even from every great merchant lord." If a teacher speaks in this way, the various outside teachers are called exclusively seeking their own benefit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining (卯二釋2), the second of manifesting exclusive self-interest's two matters, beginning with raising the two speeches (辰一舉二言2), beginning with the outside-path teacher (巳一外道師) — a newly-ordained disciple is instructed to trade mutual flattery for worldly gain: each praising the other's virtues and concealing the other's faults before patrons, so that both profit from the arrangement.

5. Section: The Disciple, Closing "Raising the Two Speeches" Entirely

Yet his disciple then speaks in protest: "Do not hold this view! Such protection as this is not called self-protection from the fault of going to an unfortunate destiny; only guarding against this fault is called self-protection. Therefore you should protect yourself as before, and I too shall separately protect myself in another way. Since I am unable to protect you, you likewise need not protect me."

Chinese Source

然其弟子便發抗言：勿為此見！如是護者，未名自護往惡趣失；若防此失，乃名自護。是故汝應如前自護，我亦當自別為餘護。我既不能護汝，汝亦不須護我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet his disciple then speaks in protest: "Do not hold this view! Such protection as this is not called self-protection from the fault of going to an unfortunate destiny; only guarding against this fault is called self-protection. Therefore you should protect yourself as before, and I too shall separately protect myself in another way. Since I am unable to protect you, you likewise need not protect me."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

With this, the disciple (巳二彼弟子) closes entirely, closing raising the two speeches (辰一舉二言2: 外道師/彼弟子) entirely — the disciple sees through the arrangement: mutual flattery protects neither party from an unfortunate rebirth; only guarding one's own conduct against that fault deserves the name "self-protection," so each should attend to their own practice rather than trading favors. The 披尋記 confirms "self-protection as before" refers back to guarding against the very fault of an unfortunate destiny.

6. Section: Distinguishing Merit and Fault, Closing "Manifesting Exclusive Self-Interest" Entirely

Within this meaning, one should know that the disciple is one who speaks in accordance with principle, one who is wise, since he gives weight to what is to come; one should know that his teacher is one who speaks contrary to principle, one who is foolish, since he gives weight only to the present.

Chinese Source

於此義中，當知弟子是如理語者，是聰慧者，重當來故。應知其師是非理語者，是愚癡者，重現在故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this meaning, one should know that the disciple is one who speaks in accordance with principle, one who is wise, since he gives weight to what is to come; one should know that his teacher is one who speaks contrary to principle, one who is foolish, since he gives weight only to the present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, distinguishing merit and fault (辰二辨德失) closes entirely, closing **manifesting exclusive self-interest (寅一明專自利2: 標/釋), the first of the false teacher's dwelling's two matters, entirely** — weighing future consequence over present convenience marks the wise; weighing present convenience over future consequence, however dressed up as mutual aid, marks the fool — and, in this case, the false teacher.

7. Section: Manifesting the Fault of Failing to Protect Begins — Marking the Fault

Further, there is defilement that touches and disturbs another; because of this defilement, one is unable to protect oneself; and because of thereby disturbing another, this is not called protecting another.

Chinese Source

復有雜染觸惱於他，由雜染故，不能自護；因此惱他，不名護他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there is defilement that touches and disturbs another; because of this defilement, one is unable to protect oneself; and because of thereby disturbing another, this is not called protecting another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens manifesting the fault of failing to protect (寅二明不護失2), the second of the false teacher's dwelling's two matters, beginning with marking the fault (卯一標失) — a mind still defiled by affliction, when it disturbs another, has failed to protect itself, and disturbing another can never be mistaken for genuinely protecting them.

8. Section: Explaining the Meaning Begins — One Should Protect Oneself

Here, as said before, severing every affliction through drawing near a good teacher and the rest is called protecting oneself.

Chinese Source

此中如前由親近等斷諸煩惱，名當自護。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, as said before, severing every affliction through drawing near a good teacher and the rest is called protecting oneself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

This opens explaining the meaning (卯二釋義2), the second of manifesting the fault of failing to protect's two matters, beginning with one should protect oneself (辰一當自護) — genuine self-protection, as established throughout this treatise, comes through drawing near a good spiritual friend, hearing the true dharma, giving principled attention, and practicing in accordance with the dharma, severing affliction at its root; the 披尋記 confirms this same fourfold sequence as the very cause of guarding against an unfortunate destiny's fault.

9. Section: One Should Protect Others Begins — Marking the Name

From this point on, taking this severance as cause, not disturbing others and the rest is called protecting others.

Chinese Source

從此已後，由斷為因，不惱他等，名當護他。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point on, taking this severance as cause, not disturbing others and the rest is called protecting others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens one should protect others (辰二當護他2), the second of explaining the meaning's two matters, beginning with marking the name (巳一標名) — protecting oneself through severing affliction naturally flows outward into protecting others as well, since a mind no longer disturbed by its own defilement no longer disturbs those around it.

10. Section: Explaining the Meaning Begins — Through Being Without Aversion and Without Harmfulness

One should know that, here, being without aversion and without harmfulness is the meaning of being without disturbance.

Chinese Source

應知此中，無瞋無害，是無惱義。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, here, being without aversion and without harmfulness is the meaning of being without disturbance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining the meaning (巳二釋義2), the second of one should protect others' two matters, beginning with through being without aversion and without harmfulness (午一由無瞋無害) — a mind free of aversion and free of the wish to harm is, by that very freedom, incapable of disturbing another.

11. Section: Through Sympathetic Concern for All Begins — Directly Manifesting This Meaning

Giving rise, without any personal condition, to the two minds of benefit and ease; giving rise, without any personal condition, to the two minds of loving-kindness and compassion — one should know that this is the meaning of sympathetic concern. Because of this sympathetic concern, one does not disturb others.

Chinese Source

無緣而起利樂二心，無緣而起慈悲二心，當知如此是哀愍義。由哀愍故，不惱於他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Giving rise, without any personal condition, to the two minds of benefit and ease; giving rise, without any personal condition, to the two minds of loving-kindness and compassion — one should know that this is the meaning of sympathetic concern. Because of this sympathetic concern, one does not disturb others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens through sympathetic concern for all (午二由一切哀愍2), the second of explaining the meaning's two matters, beginning with directly manifesting this meaning (未一正顯此義) — toward hostile, neutral, and dear beings alike, without regard to any personal cause, one gives rise to the wish for their benefit and ease, and to loving-kindness and compassion — the very meaning of sympathetic concern, which by its nature cannot disturb another. Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes three levels of loving-kindness: sentient-being-conditioned kindness (an ordinary being's initial practice, regarding every being as a former parent across beginningless rebirths); dharma-conditioned kindness (a śrāvaka's realization that beings are merely the five aggregates, with no real striker or struck, beater or beaten); and unconditioned great kindness (a bodhisattva's embrace of every being as not separate from one's own mind — the same-essence great compassion).

12. Section: Setting Out Its Reversal, Closing "Through Sympathetic Concern for All," "Explaining the Meaning," "One Should Protect Others," "Explaining the Meaning," "Manifesting the Fault of Failing to Protect," and "The False Teacher's Dwelling" Entirely

Therefore one should know that sympathetic concern for all beings is the very reverse of that defiled disturbance.

Chinese Source

是故當知，一切哀愍與彼相違。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore one should know that sympathetic concern for all beings is the very reverse of that defiled disturbance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra of the acrobat and his apprentice

With this, setting out its reversal (未二出彼相違) closes entirely, closing through sympathetic concern for all (午二由一切哀愍2: 正顯此義/出彼相違) entirely, closing explaining the meaning (巳二釋義2: 由無瞋無害/由一切哀愍) entirely, closing **one should protect others (辰二當護他2: 標名/釋義) entirely**, closing explaining the meaning (卯二釋義2: 當自護/當護他) entirely, closing **manifesting the fault of failing to protect (寅二明不護失2: 標失/釋義) entirely**, closing **the false teacher's dwelling (丑一邪師住2: 明專自利/明不護失), the first of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, entirely** — sympathetic concern for every being stands in direct contrast to the defiled disturbance that opened this section. Master Cháng Bǎi traces this teaching to a companion sūtra: an acrobat tells his apprentice that each should watch out for the other while performing atop a pole, so both profit from their shared act; the apprentice objects that each should instead watch out for himself alone, so each descends safely — and the Buddha confirms the apprentice's wisdom, teaching that protecting oneself from affliction is itself the truest way of protecting others, since one free of affliction cannot disturb anyone, and calling every monk to cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness as both self-protection and protection of others at once.

13. Section: The Snow Mountain Begins — Comparing to Skillful Liberation Begins — Marking and Listing the Three Grounds

Further, one should know that the snow mountain is a metaphor for the Buddha's well-taught dharma and discipline; here, briefly, three parts can be found: first, the ground of the one beyond training; second, the ground of the trainee; third, the ground of the ordinary being.

Chinese Source

復次，應知雪山喻佛善說法毗奈耶，此中略有三分可得。一、無學地，二、有學地，三、異生地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, one should know that the snow mountain is a metaphor for the Buddha's well-taught dharma and discipline; here, briefly, three parts can be found: first, the ground of the one beyond training; second, the ground of the trainee; third, the ground of the ordinary being.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the snow mountain (丑二雪山2), the second of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, named by the verse's first phrase's second half "the snow mountain" (邪師住雪山), beginning with comparing to skillful liberation (寅一喻善解脫2), beginning with marking and listing the three grounds (卯一標列三地) — the Buddha's own well-taught dharma is likened to a great snow mountain, its three elevations corresponding to the arhat's ground beyond training (the summit, where neither monkey nor hunter can reach), the trainee's ground (the middle slopes, where the monkey but not the hunter can still be found), and the ordinary being's ground (the foothills, where both freely roam).

14. Section: Correlating and Explaining Their Marks Begins — Within the Ground of the One Beyond Training

The monkey is a metaphor for the various minds conjoined with unprincipled attention; the hunter is a metaphor for Māra. Within the ground of the one beyond training, neither of these is able to operate.

Chinese Source

猿猴喻彼非理作意諸相應心，獵人喻魔，於無學地俱不能行。

Ru-Yi Translation

The monkey is a metaphor for the various minds conjoined with unprincipled attention; the hunter is a metaphor for Māra. Within the ground of the one beyond training, neither of these is able to operate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens correlating and explaining their marks (卯二配釋其相3), the second of comparing to skillful liberation's two matters, a threefold structure, beginning with within the ground of the one beyond training (辰一於無學地) — the monkey's restless leaping stands for unprincipled attention's every conjoined mind; the hunter stalking it stands for Māra, disturbing body and mind, obstructing wholesome dharmas, and ruining flourishing states. The arhat, having attained the wisdom of exhaustion and the wisdom of no-further-arising, is reached by neither.

15. Section: Within the Ground of the Trainee

Within the ground of the trainee, up through the non-returner, only the monkey-like mind conjoined with unprincipled attention is alone able to go there; it is not a place where the hunter-like Māra is able to operate.

Chinese Source

於有學地乃至不還，唯有非理作意相應猿猴喻心獨一能往，非獵人喻魔所能行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the ground of the trainee, up through the non-returner, only the monkey-like mind conjoined with unprincipled attention is alone able to go there; it is not a place where the hunter-like Māra is able to operate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens within the ground of the trainee (辰二於有學地), the second — the trainee, from stream-entry through non-return, has already directly realized the noble truths and attained non-discriminating wisdom, severing every discursively-arisen affliction, but the innate afflictions still remain, like a lone monkey able to roam the mountain's deep middle slopes where no hunter ever ventures.

16. Section: Within the Ground of the Ordinary Being

Within the ground of the ordinary being, both of these are able to operate.

Chinese Source

於異生地二俱能行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the ground of the ordinary being, both of these are able to operate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens within the ground of the ordinary being (辰三於異生地) — an ordinary being, unable to give principled attention when facing objects and still subject to Māra's manifest activity, dwells at the mountain's very foothills, where both monkey and hunter freely appear, mind not yet purified. Reading continues past this point to confirm what follows — comparing to skillful liberation's own further matter (寅二, presumably setting out the absence of liberation) — before further closure is claimed.

17. Section: Setting Out the Absence of Liberation, Closing "The Snow Mountain" Entirely

Further, the various foolish ones must observe a further object in order to leave a given object; pursuing one further object after another, they remain bound by that further object — therefore, toward these objects, they do not attain liberation.

Chinese Source

又諸愚夫，要觀餘境能出餘境，追求餘境餘境所縛，是故於境不得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the various foolish ones must observe a further object in order to leave a given object; pursuing one further object after another, they remain bound by that further object — therefore, toward these objects, they do not attain liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra on the monkey caught by pitch

With this, setting out the absence of liberation (寅二出不解脫) closes entirely, closing **the snow mountain (丑二雪山2: 喻善解脫/出不解脫), the second of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, entirely** — an ordinary being pursuing the mundane path of meditation must always observe a still-higher, purer object (the first dhyāna's own faults driving one toward the second, and so on) merely to leave the ground below, yet remains bound within the three realms regardless of how high the ascent goes, since the underlying view of a real body is never actually severed. Master Cháng Bǎi elaborates the mountain's three elevations once more (the arhat's summit reached by neither monkey nor hunter; the trainee's middle slopes reached only by the monkey; the ordinary being's foothills reached by both), then turns to the companion sūtra's own vivid image: a foolish monkey, its hand caught in sticky pitch, tries to free itself by stamping with its feet, only to become caught by all four limbs at once — just as an ordinary being, failing to give principled attention, guards neither the sense-doors within nor the body without, becomes bound by the five desires, follows wherever Māra leads, and abandons the very contemplation of the four foundations of mindfulness that alone could free it. The Buddha calls every being to progress, through the four foundations rightly practiced, from the trainee's own attainment onward to the arhat's deep, mountain-summit liberation.

18. Section: Exhortation Begins — Setting Out the Four Meanings

Further, through hearing the true dharma, upholding it, observing its meaning, and practicing in accordance with the dharma, in their proper sequence, one should know that exhortation and transformation establish four meanings.

Chinese Source

復次，由於正法聽聞、受持、觀察義理、法隨法行，如其次第，應知勸化安立四義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through hearing the true dharma, upholding it, observing its meaning, and practicing in accordance with the dharma, in their proper sequence, one should know that exhortation and transformation establish four meanings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Foundation Section, fascicle 43

This opens exhortation (丑三勸勉2), the third of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, named by the verse's second phrase "exhortation, and being bound to purity" (勸勉繫屬淨), beginning with setting out the four meanings (寅一出四義) — four terms correlate exactly to the fourfold sequence of hearing, upholding, observing meaning, and practicing accordingly: exhortation (urging a being to hear the true dharma), transformation (taming affliction as the dharma is upheld in mind), settling (placing the mind within the object of observed meaning), and establishing (founding the pure cause and effect of practicing in accordance with the dharma, accomplishing nirvāṇa's own merit) — the same fourfold structure the Foundation Section, fascicle 43, identifies as a bodhisattva's own natural conduct of benefiting others.

19. Section: Elaborating the Three Powers Begins — Marking and Listing

There are, further, three dharmas which are able to sever every supreme, sublime bond of sexual craving, let alone the various baser bonds of sense-desire craving. What are the three? First, the power of diligence; second, the power of freedom from heedlessness; third, the power of the remedy.

Chinese Source

復有三法，尚能斷除一切勝妙婬欲貪纏，況乎鄙劣諸欲貪纏。何等為三？一、精進力，二、不放逸力，三、對治力。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, three dharmas which are able to sever every supreme, sublime bond of sexual craving, let alone the various baser bonds of sense-desire craving. What are the three? First, the power of diligence; second, the power of freedom from heedlessness; third, the power of the remedy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens elaborating the three powers (寅二廣三力3), the second of exhortation's two matters, beginning with marking and listing (卯一標列) — three powers, if genuinely accomplished, are strong enough to sever even the most refined bond of sexual craving, let alone lesser sense-desires: the power of diligence, the power of freedom from heedlessness, and the power of the remedy.

20. Section: Correlating and Explaining

Through the power of diligence, what has already arisen is made not to abide firmly. Through the remaining two powers, what has not yet arisen is made not to arise.

Chinese Source

由精進力，其已生者令不堅住。由餘二力，其未生者令不得生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the power of diligence, what has already arisen is made not to abide firmly. Through the remaining two powers, what has not yet arisen is made not to arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens correlating and explaining (卯二配釋), the second of elaborating the three powers' three matters — diligence uproots evil already present before it can take firm hold, while freedom from heedlessness and the remedy together forestall evil not yet arisen and sustain wholesome dharmas already present or newly arising — the same fourfold task the four right efforts name.

21. Section: The Concluding Statement Manifesting the Meaning, Closing "Exhortation" Entirely

Thus the practitioner diligently cultivates right conduct, in order to sever what evil has already arisen, and in order to make what has not yet arisen not arise.

Chinese Source

如是行者勤修正行，為欲斷除已生惡故，及未生者令不生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the practitioner diligently cultivates right conduct, in order to sever what evil has already arisen, and in order to make what has not yet arisen not arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing three companion sūtras (including one on the young monk Udayī, and one of the Buddha's teaching prompted by the sight of the courtesan Āmrapālī)

With this, the concluding statement manifesting the meaning (卯三結顯) closes entirely, closing elaborating the three powers (寅二廣三力3: 標列/配釋/結顯) entirely, closing **exhortation (丑三勸勉2: 出四義/廣三力), the third of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, entirely** — diligent cultivation of the four foundations, sustained by these three powers, severs both present and future evil. Master Cháng Bǎi traces this teaching to three companion sūtras: one in which the Buddha instructs Ānanda that young monks, trainees, and even arhats alike should all be guided toward the four foundations of mindfulness; one occasioned by the Buddha's own sighting of the courtesan Āmrapālī from a distance, in which he warns the assembly, through the simile of a man forced to carry a bowl brimful of oil past a beautiful dancer under threat of instant beheading should a single drop spill, that a monk must guard the mind just as single-mindedly, never letting the five sense-objects distract from the body's own contemplation; and a closing verse urging the assembly to hold the four foundations as one holds that same oil bowl, since only sustained, undistracted attention — never a heedless one — can reach the sublime, subtle state beyond craving, aversion, and delusion.

29. Section: Distinguishing Its Stages, Closing "Elaborating on Purity," "Distinguishing by Way of Persons," "Manifesting Being Bound," and "Being Bound to Purity" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, here, the higher trainees are called pure, and the lower trainees are called impure, since they, through the path of cultivation, are not yet pure.

Chinese Source

餘如前說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The rest is as said before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記 (twice) and a substantial cluster of companion sūtras (including the monk Utkaṭa and the monk Vāhika)

With this, distinguishing its stages (巳二辨其分位) closes entirely, closing elaborating on purity (辰二廣清淨2: 出四證淨/辨其分位) entirely, closing **distinguishing by way of persons (卯二約有情辨2: 出差別/廣清淨) entirely**, closing **manifesting being bound (寅二明繫屬2: 約三界辨/約有情辨) entirely**, closing **being bound to purity (丑四繫屬2: 指修相/明繫屬), the fourth of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, entirely** — even among trainees, purity admits degrees: the higher trainee (the non-returner) is called pure, while the lower trainees (stream-enterer and once-returner), not yet free of desire-realm affliction through the path of cultivation, are called impure relative to the non-returner — though both remain pure relative to the ordinary being, and both remain impure relative to the arhat's own bright, final purity, per the 披尋記's own distinction of upper and lower trainee stages within the four confirmed purities. Master Cháng Bǎi traces this teaching to a substantial cluster of companion sūtras: the monk Utkaṭa's threefold petition to the Buddha, answered with the instruction to first purify one's own precepts and straighten one's own view before taking up the four foundations of mindfulness, after which he attained arhatship; the monk Vāhika's parallel teaching, adding that this same purification overcomes every class of Māra in turn (the aggregates, affliction, death, and the heavenly Māra); Aniruddha's question on how a trainee still short of security should proceed (through the very same four foundations); and Ānanda's own exchange with Udāyin on why the Buddha, despite having realized ultimate truth directly, nonetheless dwells at length on the merit of precepts — because purified precepts are themselves the necessary foundation for the four foundations of mindfulness's own cultivation. Several shorter companion sūtras follow in immediate succession, each simply reaffirming that sustained cultivation of the four foundations yields non-retrogression, purification, crossing to nirvāṇa's far shore, and, ultimately, arhatship itself — echoing the teaching that all the Buddha's teachings, however many, can be summarized as the four foundations of mindfulness alone.

Chinese Source

復次，修四念住，應知略有五種漸次。一、信增上力，清淨出家；二、戒律儀；三、根律儀；四、樂遠離；五、蓋清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness, one should know there are, briefly, five gradual stages: first, the increasing power of faith, pure going forth; second, restraint through precepts; third, restraint through the sense-faculties; fourth, delight in seclusion; fifth, purity from the hindrances.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens purity's gradual sequence (丑五淨漸次2), the fifth of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, named by the verse's second phrase's third word and the third phrase's first word "purity...the gradual sequence" (勸勉繫屬淨...漸次戒圓滿), beginning with marking and listing the five kinds (寅一標列五種) — five stages unfold in order: faith's increasing power leading to a pure going-forth (never fleeing debts or taxes, but genuinely aspiring to seek and to save); precepts received, studied, and upheld (monks devoting their first five years, nuns their first six, chiefly to the monastic code, purifying body, speech, and mind); restraint of the six sense-faculties (guarding the mind, the source from which every dharma arises or ceases, through sustained right knowledge in every activity); delight in seclusion (bodily, mental, and physical seclusion — dwelling apart from crowds, free of scattered thought, cultivating only wholesome, beneficial attention); and purity from the five hindrances (craving, ill will, torpor-and-sleep, restlessness-and-worry, doubt — severed, then progressively purified, enabling calm-abiding and, within it, insight into ultimate truth).

31. Section: Distinguishing "Not for the Householder," Closing "Purity's Gradual Sequence" Entirely

Chinese Source

諸在家者，雖復數數修諸念住，獲得淨信，諸蓋清淨，然闕學處，當知所修不得圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Householders, though they may repeatedly cultivate the foundations of mindfulness, attain pure faith, and become pure of the hindrances, nevertheless lack the precincts of training; one should know that their cultivation does not reach completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

With this, distinguishing "not for the householder" (寅二簡非在家) closes entirely, closing **purity's gradual sequence (丑五淨漸次2: 標列五種/簡非在家), the fifth of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, entirely** — a householder may indeed attain pure faith and freedom from the hindrances, but, lacking the precincts of monastic training that householders are not bound by, cannot bring this gradual sequence to full completion. Master Cháng Bǎi traces each of the five stages to its own companion sūtra passage: pure faith leading to going forth, free of desire's entangling cords; precepts as the traffic light that tells a practitioner when to go and when to stop; the six sense-faculties guarded so that whatever the eyes see becomes an occasion for mindfulness of the Three Jewels; delight in seclusion, sitting joyfully apart under a tree or in an empty room; and the five hindrances purified by stages — first severed, then progressively refined into full purity.

35. Section: Explaining Begins — Fullness of Conduct

Fullness of conduct means being entirely free from buying and selling, up through harming, binding, severing limbs, striking, and massaging others, and every such matter.

Ru-Yi Translation

Fullness of conduct means being entirely free from buying and selling, up through harming, binding, severing limbs, striking, and massaging others, and every such matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground, fascicle 22

This opens explaining (寅四釋3), the fourth and final of the precepts' fullness's matters, beginning with fullness of conduct (卯一所行圓滿) — a monk's conduct is full when free of commerce and of every violent or improper act toward others' bodies. The Śrāvaka Ground, fascicle 22, frames this instead as avoiding five improper places (a herald's house, a courtesan's house, a wine-seller's house, a king's palace, and an outcaste's or executioner's house) while otherwise moving about blamelessly at the right time — Master Cháng Bǎi notes this passage's own "fullness of conduct" corresponds instead to that other passage's "moving about blamelessly at the right time," while the Śrāvaka Ground's own "fullness of conduct" corresponds to avoiding the five improper places named there.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Ground of Investigation and Analysis, fascicle 68

This opens fullness of what is taken up (卯二攝取圓滿), the second — keeping livestock (elephants, horses, cattle, sheep, chickens, pigs, dogs, cats) easily breeds attachment and is therefore forbidden to monastics; taking up unhusked grain, requiring hours of hulling before it can be eaten, likewise wastes practice time. Neither adorns the path of precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and both are therefore impure to take up — detailed further, per Master Cháng Bǎi, in the Ground of Investigation and Analysis's own treatment of the Śrāvaka and Solitary-Realizer Grounds, fascicle 68, under "unadorned."

38. Section: The Ear of Grain Begins — Marking the Distinction

Further, body and the rest's four dharmas are like four great roads; the unprincipled attention that arises toward them is like wrongly praying for an ear of rice or wheat grain; the principled attention that arises toward them is like rightly praying for an ear of rice or wheat grain.

Chinese Source

復次，身等四法如四大路，於彼所生非理作意，如邪祈願稻穀麥穗；於彼所生如理作意，如正祈願稻穀麥穗。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, body and the rest's four dharmas are like four great roads; the unprincipled attention that arises toward them is like wrongly praying for an ear of rice or wheat grain; the principled attention that arises toward them is like rightly praying for an ear of rice or wheat grain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 攝異門分, fascicle 84

This opens the ear of grain (丑七穗2), the seventh of the fourth particular verse's eight sections, named by the verse's fourth phrase's first word "the ear of grain" (穗成就為後), beginning with marking the distinction (寅一標差別) — body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are the shared ground everyone stands on, like four great roads; misperceiving them (taking the impure body for pure, the selfless for possessing self) is like praying wrongly for a harvest — even with seed in the ground, without sun, air, water, and fertilizer, no ripened ear of grain will ever appear. 攝異門分, fascicle 84, is cited on affliction itself as a stump or stubble left in the field, causing ever-deepening, self-perpetuating growth and rendering body and mind incapable, like salt-poisoned soil unfit for any planting at all. Conversely, principled attention toward these same four is like praying rightly: the causes for purity are properly gathered, and a pure fruit is certain to follow — a practitioner who instead clings to unprincipled thought only continues circling further into saṃsāra.

39. Section: Manifesting the Outflows, Closing "The Ear of Grain" Entirely

One should know that the desire realm is the unfixed ground, just like the skin; the form and formless realms are both fixed grounds, just like the flesh; ignorance is like the blood. Within the three realms, through the three outflows, there is the meaning of trickling.

Chinese Source

當知欲界是不定地，猶如其皮；色無色界俱是定地，猶如其肉；無明如血。於三界中，由三種漏有淋漏義。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that the desire realm is the unfixed ground, just like the skin; the form and formless realms are both fixed grounds, just like the flesh; ignorance is like the blood. Within the three realms, through the three outflows, there is the meaning of trickling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Ground with Both Investigation and Analysis (fascicle 8) and 攝異門分 (fascicle 84), and the 披尋記

With this, manifesting the outflows (寅二明有漏) closes entirely, closing **the ear of grain (丑七穗2: 標差別/明有漏) entirely** — the desire realm's affliction, coarse and readily severed through principled attention, is like grime on the skin; the form and formless realms' subtler, meditation-linked affliction (desire's outflow only temporarily suppressed, its seed unsevered) sinks deeper, like flesh; and ignorance, pervading every one of the three realms alike, is deepest of all, like blood filling the whole body — the three outflows (of desire, of existence, of ignorance) that keep every unliberated being trickling, waterlogged, unable to dwell at ease, so long as their seeds remain. The Ground with Both Investigation and Analysis, fascicle 8, defines an outflow simply as that which keeps the mind flowing outward; 攝異門分, fascicle 84, as that which is able to give rise to future birth — together naming affliction's power to detain beings within the three realms and obstruct their approach to liberation. The 披尋記 confirms the skin-flesh-blood simile maps precisely onto desire's outflow (欲漏), existence's outflow (有漏), and ignorance's outflow (無明漏) in turn, graded by their coarseness or subtlety, both within and across the three realms.
