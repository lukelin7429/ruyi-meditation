Fascicle 100 · 瑜伽師地論卷第一百 · Thorough Knowledge Continues

Thorough Knowledge Begins

Fascicle 100 is the treatise's final fascicle, continuing directly from fascicle 99, which closed six of matters of monastic discipline's (調伏事) nine great matters entire. This fascicle opens with the seventh matter, thorough knowledge (遍知) — five things a monastic diligently training in discipline must fully understand: the basic constituents of experience, the anatomy of any offense committed, the character of persons, the threefold aim of guiding others, and the five kinds of present affliction ordinary beings bring upon themselves. It closes the first two of these five entire: thoroughly knowing the basic constituents (the five aggregates, elements, sense-fields, dependent origination, and sound versus unsound reasoning), and thoroughly knowing offense itself, down to its finest distinctions of cause, manner of arising, object, effort, and completion. Per Master Cháng Bǎi's own framing, this fascicle's first half continues matters of monastic discipline (毗奈耶事), while its second half will open the third and final great topic of the embrace of the bases (攝事分): matters of the foundational treatises (摩怛履迦事), also called the embrace of distinguishing dharma-characteristics (分別法相).

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 100 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Treatise's Final Fascicle Begins — The General Survey Embrace, Second Part

Within the embrace of the bases, the general survey embrace of matters of monastic discipline, the second part.

Chinese Source

攝事分中調伏事擇攝第五之二

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the embrace of the bases, the general survey embrace of matters of monastic discipline, the second part.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This is the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra's own final fascicle. The embrace of the bases, spanning fascicles 85 through 100, distinguishes the three matters in detail: sūtra matters' foundational treatise (fascicles 85-98, explaining the Āgamas), discipline matters' foundational treatise (fascicles 99 through this fascicle's first half, explaining the vinaya), and, in this fascicle's second half, a further foundational treatise gathering and re-organizing the treatise's own teachings as distinguished dharma-characteristics (分別法相). This fascicle continues discipline matters' general survey embrace's unfinished portion from fascicle 99, entering now its seventh section.

2. Section: Thorough Knowledge Begins — Marking

Further, a monk diligently training in discipline should rightly and thoroughly know five matters.

Chinese Source

復次，依毗奈耶勤學苾芻，於其五處應正遍知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, a monk diligently training in discipline should rightly and thoroughly know five matters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens **thorough knowledge (庚七遍知4), the SEVENTH of the nine matters**, named by the verse's own word — thorough knowledge (遍知, also 遍智) means wisdom that fully penetrates and understands its object — beginning with marking (辛一標).

Ru-Yi Translation

4. Section: Listing

First, thorough knowledge of the basic constituents; second, thorough knowledge of offense; third, thorough knowledge of persons; fourth, thorough knowledge of drawing beings toward genuine benefit; fifth, thorough knowledge of affliction.

Chinese Source

一、事遍知，二、罪遍知，三、補特伽羅遍知，四、引攝義利遍知，五、損惱遍知。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, thorough knowledge of the basic constituents; second, thorough knowledge of offense; third, thorough knowledge of persons; fourth, thorough knowledge of drawing beings toward genuine benefit; fifth, thorough knowledge of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens listing (辛三列), the third of thorough knowledge's four sections — the basic constituents are the five aggregates, the eighteen elements, and the twelve sense-fields underlying experience; offense concerns knowing what constitutes a genuine transgression; persons concerns understanding a given being's own character; drawing beings toward benefit concerns three ways of leading others toward genuine practice; and affliction concerns the five kinds of self-inflicted present distress ordinary beings bring on themselves.

5. Section: Explaining Begins — Thorough Knowledge of the Basic Constituents

What is thorough knowledge of the basic constituents? It means the five matters of the aggregates and the rest, as already explained in the Śrāvaka Grounds.

Chinese Source

云何事遍知？謂蘊等五事。如聲聞地已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is thorough knowledge of the basic constituents? It means the five matters of the aggregates and the rest, as already explained in the Śrāvaka Grounds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Grounds, fascicle 27, and the 披尋記

This opens explaining (辛四釋5), a fivefold structure matching the five matters just listed, beginning with thorough knowledge of the basic constituents (壬一事遍知) — the Śrāvaka Grounds names this fivefold skill: skill in the aggregates, skill in the elements, skill in the sense-fields, skill in dependent origination, and skill in sound versus unsound reasoning — the last distinguishing an account of cause and effect that genuinely accords with dependent origination (sound) from one that does not (unsound). Mastering all five requires sustained study, detailed further in the embrace of the alternate approach's own treatment of the five-consciousness-body-conjoined ground and the mental ground, fascicles 53 through 57.

9. Section: Explaining Begins — Thoroughly Knowing the Cause and Condition of the Offense

Thoroughly knowing the cause and condition of the offense means: whether by the cause and condition of craving, or of aversion, or of delusion, one transgresses and commits a host of offenses.

Chinese Source

遍知犯罪因緣者，謂或貪因緣、或瞋因緣、或癡因緣，毀犯眾罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thoroughly knowing the cause and condition of the offense means: whether by the cause and condition of craving, or of aversion, or of delusion, one transgresses and commits a host of offenses.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining (癸四釋5), a fivefold structure matching the five marks just listed, beginning with thoroughly knowing the cause and condition of the offense (子一遍知犯罪因緣) — every offense ultimately traces to one, two, or all three of the root afflictions, since afflictive defilement is what drives karmic defilement.

10. Section: Thoroughly Knowing How the Offense Arose Begins — Setting Forth the Distinctions

Thoroughly knowing how the offense arose means: there is an offense that arises through the body, not through speech, not through mind; further, there is an offense that arises through speech, not through the body, not through mind; further, there is an offense that arises through mind, not through the body, not through speech; further, there is an offense that arises through the body and through mind, not through speech; further, there is an offense that arises through speech and through mind, not through the body; further, there is an offense that arises through the body and through speech, not through mind; further, there is an offense that arises through the body, through speech, and through mind.

Chinese Source

遍知犯罪等起者，謂或有罪由身等起，非語、非心；或復有罪由語等起，非身、非心；或復有罪由心等起，非身、非語；或復有罪由身、由心等起，非語；或復有罪由語、由心等起，非身；或復有罪由身、由語等起，非心；或復有罪由身、由語、由心等起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thoroughly knowing how the offense arose means: there is an offense that arises through the body, not through speech, not through mind; further, there is an offense that arises through speech, not through the body, not through mind; further, there is an offense that arises through mind, not through the body, not through speech; further, there is an offense that arises through the body and through mind, not through speech; further, there is an offense that arises through speech and through mind, not through the body; further, there is an offense that arises through the body and through speech, not through mind; further, there is an offense that arises through the body, through speech, and through mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens thoroughly knowing how the offense arose (子二遍知犯罪等起2), beginning with setting forth the distinctions (丑一出差別) — seven possible combinations of body, speech, and mind exhaust every way an offense can actually arise.

11. Section: Distinguishing Disclosure, Closing "Thoroughly Knowing How the Offense Arose" Entirely

There is no offense committed by mind alone that requires disclosure and repentance to another; one need only earnestly and deeply guard against it oneself. As with a monk who gives rise to various unwholesome discursive thoughts of desire and the like.

Chinese Source

無獨由心所犯眾罪，應從他處發露悔除，唯當懇誠深自防護。如有苾芻，發起種種欲尋思等不善尋思。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no offense committed by mind alone that requires disclosure and repentance to another; one need only earnestly and deeply guard against it oneself. As with a monk who gives rise to various unwholesome discursive thoughts of desire and the like.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, distinguishing disclosure (丑二簡發露) closes, closing **thoroughly knowing how the offense arose (子二遍知犯罪等起2: 出差別/簡發露) entirely** — of the seven combinations, only the one arising by mind alone needs no formal disclosure to another; it is instead guarded against through calm-abiding and clear-seeing, remedying discursive thoughts of desire, ill will, harm, homeland, kin, mortality, family standing, and contempt for others. Any offense actually manifesting through body or speech, however, does require disclosure proportional to its gravity.

12. Section: Thoroughly Knowing the Offense's Own Object

Thoroughly knowing the offense's own object means: the object of an offense is, in brief, of two kinds. First, a matter numbered among sentient beings; second, a matter numbered among insentient things.

Chinese Source

遍知所犯罪事者，謂犯罪事略有二種。一者、有情數事，二者、無情數事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thoroughly knowing the offense's own object means: the object of an offense is, in brief, of two kinds. First, a matter numbered among sentient beings; second, a matter numbered among insentient things.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens thoroughly knowing the offense's own object (子三遍知犯罪事) — killing or quarreling involves a sentient-being object; mishandling robes and bowl, or wrongly bounding or purifying ground, involves an insentient object.

13. Section: Thoroughly Knowing the Effort Behind the Offense

Thoroughly knowing the effort behind the offense means: what is committed has two kinds of effort. First, the effort of an undertaking that should not be done; second, the effort of an undertaking that should be done.

Chinese Source

遍知犯罪加行者，謂所犯罪有二加行。一、非所應作事業加行，二、是所應作事業加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thoroughly knowing the effort behind the offense means: what is committed has two kinds of effort. First, the effort of an undertaking that should not be done; second, the effort of an undertaking that should be done.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens thoroughly knowing the effort behind the offense (子四遍知犯罪加行) — doing what should not be done is an offense of commission; failing to do what should be done — neglecting the precepts, samādhi, and wisdom one ought diligently to cultivate — is an offense of omission.

14. Section: Thoroughly Knowing the Offense's Completion Begins — Marking the Meaning

Thoroughly knowing the offense's completion means: where the means toward it were set in motion, it is there that completion is reached, with no reversal along the way. By reason of this, what was committed is fulfilled in full.

Chinese Source

遍知犯罪究竟者，謂於是處施設方便，即於是處而得究竟，非於中間有其退轉。以是緣故，所犯圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thoroughly knowing the offense's completion means: where the means toward it were set in motion, it is there that completion is reached, with no reversal along the way. By reason of this, what was committed is fulfilled in full.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens thoroughly knowing the offense's completion (子五遍知犯罪究竟2), beginning with marking the meaning (丑一標義) — an offense carried through to its full completion, without interruption, is graver than one abandoned partway; the treatise distinguishes a preparatory ("cause") offense from the completed ("fruit") offense — an attempted killing that fails to kill incurs only the preparatory offense, lighter than the completed offense of an actual killing.

15. Section: Distinguishing and Deliberating Begins — The Class an Offense Belongs To

Every gathering-coarse offense — among defeat and the remainder's own preparatory acts, one commits a downfall or a wrongdoing; within that very preparatory act, and within its own class, completion is reached; and within downfall's own preparatory acts, one likewise commits a wrongdoing.

Chinese Source

諸集麤罪，他勝眾餘方便中犯隕墜惡作，於彼方便及自聚中而得究竟，於隕墜罪諸方便中亦犯惡作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every gathering-coarse offense — among defeat and the remainder's own preparatory acts, one commits a downfall or a wrongdoing; within that very preparatory act, and within its own class, completion is reached; and within downfall's own preparatory acts, one likewise commits a wrongdoing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 披尋記

This opens distinguishing and deliberating (丑二料簡2), beginning with the class an offense belongs to (寅一所犯罪聚) — the embrace of the bases, fascicle 99, already named the five classes of offense (defeat/remainder/downfall/individual-confession/wrongdoing); a preparatory act toward a defeat or remainder-class offense that falls short of completion still incurs its own lesser offense (downfall or wrongdoing class) as a derivative rather than root offense, and this gradation of gravity continues down even within the downfall class's own preparatory acts.

16. Section: Alternate Names for What Is Committed Begins — Offense with Remainder and Offense without Remainder

The four classes of offense are called offense with remainder; the class of defeat is called offense without remainder.

Chinese Source

四種罪聚，名有餘罪；他勝罪聚，名無餘罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

The four classes of offense are called offense with remainder; the class of defeat is called offense without remainder.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens alternate names for what is committed (寅二所犯異名6), the second of distinguishing and deliberating's two sections, beginning with offense with remainder and offense without remainder (卯一有餘罪無餘罪) — the four lesser classes still leave room for repentance; defeat, gravest of all, leaves none, at least under the ordinary rule for the śrāvaka precepts.

17. Section: Not Accumulating and Not Failing to Accumulate

If an offense committed is, by reason of wisdom, not accumulated; or is also not accumulated by reason of being disclosed through another — contrary to this, it does not fail to accumulate.

Chinese Source

若所犯罪，由有智故，名不積集；或復從他而顯發故，亦不積集。與此相違，非不積集。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an offense committed is, by reason of wisdom, not accumulated; or is also not accumulated by reason of being disclosed through another — contrary to this, it does not fail to accumulate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens not accumulating and not failing to accumulate (卯二不積聚非不積聚) — wisely handled and swiftly disclosed, an offense does not compound into further derivative offenses through ongoing concealment; concealed and left undisclosed, it accumulates a fresh offense for each day it remains hidden.

18. Section: Already Disclosed and Not Yet Disclosed

If an offense committed has already been lawfully disclosed to another, and its means of repentance set in motion — this is called already disclosed. Contrary to this, it is called not yet disclosed.

Chinese Source

若所犯罪，已從於他如法發露，方便悔除，名已顯說。與此相違，名未顯說。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an offense committed has already been lawfully disclosed to another, and its means of repentance set in motion — this is called already disclosed. Contrary to this, it is called not yet disclosed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens already disclosed and not yet disclosed (卯三已顯說未顯說) — not yet disclosed describes concealment: a genuine offense one dares not admit and has taken no lawful step toward repenting.

19. Section: With Resolve and without Resolve

If an offense committed is provisionally held, with the resolve to repent it in due course — this is called with resolve. Contrary to this, it is called without resolve.

Chinese Source

若所犯罪，權持當悔，名有期願。與此相違，名無期願。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an offense committed is provisionally held, with the resolve to repent it in due course — this is called with resolve. Contrary to this, it is called without resolve.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens with resolve and without resolve (卯四有期願無期願) — some offenses, being grave, cannot be repented on the spot and require a larger quorum to gather; disclosing the offense now while genuinely resolving to complete repentance once the occasion allows still counts as with resolve, distinct from harboring no intention to repent at all.

20. Section: With Formal Establishment and without Formal Establishment

If an offense committed is one the buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, established as an offense within the individual-liberation discipline — this is called with formal establishment. Contrary to this, it is called without formal establishment.

Chinese Source

若所犯罪，諸佛世尊於別解脫毗奈耶中，建立為犯，名有制立。與此相違，名無制立。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an offense committed is one the buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, established as an offense within the individual-liberation discipline — this is called with formal establishment. Contrary to this, it is called without formal establishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens with formal establishment and without formal establishment (卯五有制立無制立) — some transgressions are explicitly named as training-grounds within the vinaya's own text; others, though not individually codified, still incur an offense in terms of karmic cause and effect even without an explicit precept naming them.

21. Section: Equally Applied and Not Equally Applied, Closing "Alternate Names for What Is Committed," "Distinguishing and Deliberating," "Thoroughly Knowing the Offense's Completion," "Explaining," and "Thorough Knowledge of Offense" Entirely

If an offense committed, whether taken with respect to a single kind of person, or with respect to time, is not fixed, and is established beforehand as a general mark without distinction — one should know this offense is called equally applied. Contrary to this, it is called not equally applied.

Chinese Source

若所犯罪，或約一類補特伽羅，或復約時而不決定，先無差別總相制立，當知此罪名為等運。與此相違，名非等運。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an offense committed, whether taken with respect to a single kind of person, or with respect to time, is not fixed, and is established beforehand as a general mark without distinction — one should know this offense is called equally applied. Contrary to this, it is called not equally applied.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, equally applied and not equally applied (卯六等運非等運) closes, closing **alternate names for what is committed (寅二所犯異名6: 有餘罪無餘罪/不積聚非不積聚/已顯說未顯說/有期願無期願/有制立無制立/等運非等運), six kinds, entirely**, closing **distinguishing and deliberating (丑二料簡2: 所犯罪聚/所犯異名) entirely**, closing **thoroughly knowing the offense's completion (子五遍知犯罪究竟2: 標義/料簡), the fifth of thorough knowledge of offense's five marks, entirely**, closing **explaining (癸四釋5: 遍知犯罪因緣/遍知犯罪等起/遍知所犯罪事/遍知犯罪加行/遍知犯罪究竟), five marks, entirely**, closing **thorough knowledge of offense (壬二罪遍知4: 徵/標/列/釋), the SECOND of thorough knowledge's five matters, entirely** — offenses committed collectively, without singling out a particular person or moment (as when an entire assembly jointly confesses unspecified lesser lapses accrued over a half-month), may be resolved together as a class; individually committed offenses tied to a specific person and moment must be repented individually.

It means one should know the distinctions through five marks.

謂由五相應知差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

It means one should know the distinctions through five marks.

23. Section: Listing

First, by reason of distinctions in mental conduct; second, by reason of distinctions among the assemblies; third, by reason of distinctions in what is heightened or diminished; fourth, by reason of distinctions in attainment; fifth, by reason of distinctions in observation.

Chinese Source

一、由行差別故；二、由眾差別故；三、由增減差別故；四、由證得差別故；五、由觀察差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, by reason of distinctions in mental conduct; second, by reason of distinctions among the assemblies; third, by reason of distinctions in what is heightened or diminished; fourth, by reason of distinctions in attainment; fifth, by reason of distinctions in observation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens listing (癸三列), the third of thorough knowledge of persons' four sections — the same offense carries different gravity depending on who commits it (a monk's offense is graver than a novice's for the same act), on the offender's own inherent and cultivated qualities, on their level of realization, and on how carefully any charge against them is weighed before being raised.

24. Section: Explaining Begins — By Reason of Distinctions in Mental Conduct

By reason of distinctions in mental conduct: it means being able to thoroughly know that, by reason of distinctions among the conduct of craving and the rest, that person's distinctions follow. This should be known in its marks as in the Śrāvaka Grounds.

Chinese Source

由行差別者，謂能遍知，由貪等行有差別故，彼有差別。如聲聞地應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

By reason of distinctions in mental conduct: it means being able to thoroughly know that, by reason of distinctions among the conduct of craving and the rest, that person's distinctions follow. This should be known in its marks as in the Śrāvaka Grounds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Grounds, fascicle 26, and the 披尋記 at length

This opens explaining (癸四釋5), a fivefold structure matching the five marks just listed, beginning with distinctions in mental conduct (子一由行差別) — the Śrāvaka Grounds establishes persons' distinctions across eleven angles altogether (by sharp or dull faculty; by the seven assemblies; by mental conduct; by vow — śrāvaka, solitary-realizer, or great vehicle; by the path traversed — painful-slow, painful-swift, pleasant-slow, pleasant-swift; by the eight stages of realization; by faith-following or dharma-following preparatory practice; by cessation-attainment; by the eightfold rebirth-pattern of the first three fruits; by reversible or irreversible liberation; and by which obstruction, afflictive or meditative, remains). Here only mental conduct is unpacked in full, yielding seven kinds of person: one in whom craving predominates, one in whom aversion predominates, one in whom delusion predominates, one in whom pride predominates, one in whom discursive thought predominates, one of equal proportion (all five present without any one predominating), and one of thin defilement (all five present but faint) — the first five sharing the further marks of being both fierce and long-enduring.

25. Section: By Reason of Distinctions Among the Assemblies

By reason of distinctions among the assemblies: it means being able to thoroughly know that, by reason of the distinctions among monks, nuns, and the rest of the seven assemblies, that person's distinctions follow.

Chinese Source

由眾差別者，謂能遍知，由苾芻、苾芻尼等七眾別故，彼有差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

By reason of distinctions among the assemblies: it means being able to thoroughly know that, by reason of the distinctions among monks, nuns, and the rest of the seven assemblies, that person's distinctions follow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens by reason of distinctions among the assemblies (子二由眾差別) — the same act's gravity, and the standard for judgment, differs across monks, nuns, novices, novice-nuns, probationary nuns, laymen, and laywomen.

26. Section: By Reason of Distinctions in What Is Heightened or Diminished Begins — Distinguishing Begins — With Respect to Innate Endowment

By reason of distinctions in what is heightened or diminished: it means, as with one kind of person who goes forth from noble lineage, or goes forth from wealthy lineage, or has a dignified countenance; while another kind is not like this.

Chinese Source

由增減差別者，謂如一類補特伽羅，或貴族出家，或富族出家，或顏容端正。其餘一類則不如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

By reason of distinctions in what is heightened or diminished: it means, as with one kind of person who goes forth from noble lineage, or goes forth from wealthy lineage, or has a dignified countenance; while another kind is not like this.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens **by reason of distinctions in what is heightened or diminished (子三由增減差別2), the third of thorough knowledge of persons' five marks**, beginning with distinguishing (丑一辨2), beginning with respect to innate endowment (寅一約俱生辨) — those born to noble lineage, wealth, or fine appearance need careful guidance to temper any resulting pride and bring them to keep the precepts on equal footing with everyone else; those of ordinary background need encouragement instead, to draw out their own potential.

27. Section: With Respect to Cultivated Effort

Further, there is a kind of person who is broadly learned and well-informed, whose speech is fully accomplished, of great wisdom and great merit, who firmly and valiantly guards pure moral discipline, has few transgressions, gives rise readily to wrongdoing-remorse, and skillfully understands both offense and emergence from it; while another kind is not like this.

Chinese Source

復有一類補特伽羅，多聞博識，語具圓滿，大智大福，於淨尸羅堅猛防護，少有所犯，多生惡作，於犯、於出能善了知。其餘一類則不如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ru-Yi Translation

By reason of distinctions in observation: it means that a person raising a charge should skillfully observe the one against whom the charge is raised, and only then should raise it, doing so for the sake of a genuine reminder. It means observing whether that person holds affection and reverence toward oneself or not — broadly stated as in the sūtra, this should be known in its marks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Saṃyukta Āgama, discourses 496 and 497

This opens **by reason of distinctions in observation (子五由觀察差別2), the fifth of thorough knowledge of persons' five marks**, beginning with distinguishing (丑一辨2), beginning with observing the one who raises a charge (寅一觀察所舉) — when Śāriputra asked the Buddha what conditions a monk must examine in himself before raising a charge against another, the Buddha named five: whether the charge is factually true; whether the timing is right; whether it is genuinely meant to benefit, not merely to embarrass; whether it can be delivered gently rather than harshly; and whether it comes from a compassionate mind free of aversion. Discourse 497 further describes how a genuinely upright monk — free of deceit, possessed of faith, precepts, shame, mindfulness, samādhi, and wisdom, diligent, reverent toward the precepts, valuing renunciant conduct, honoring nirvāṇa, and having gone forth for the dharma rather than mere livelihood — receives a charge with joy, like drinking nectar; a monk of the opposite character, deceitful and indifferent to the precepts, is far more likely to react with anger or even retaliation, so the one raising a charge must weigh in advance whether doing so risks provoking real harm, a lasting grudge, or even a schism in the saṅgha, and refrain if so.

謂有一類，傷悼死亡，以無量門而自煎迫，傷淪喪者。是名第一現法損惱，凡夫所趣，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a kind who, mourning the dead, presses and torments themselves through countless means, grieving over the one who has passed. This is called the first present affliction — what ordinary beings resort to, broadly stated as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Saṃyukta Āgama and the 披尋記, and several traditional narratives

This opens explaining (子三釋2), beginning with listing the afflictions (丑一列損惱5), a fivefold structure, beginning with the first affliction (寅一第一損惱) — excessive grief at a loved one's death, even to the point of losing one's reason, is common among the unenlightened; the noble ones, understanding impermanence, feel loss without succumbing to it — Ānanda, still at the first fruit when the Buddha entered final nirvāṇa, wept, while those of the third fruit and above, craving already severed, did not. Master Cháng Bǎi tells of a man reborn, through his wife's clinging grief at his deathbed, as a worm in her own mucus, saved only when an arhat intervened; of a family who mourned a father for three years until the father, now blissfully reborn in a heaven, appeared to them disguised as a herdsman weeping over a dead ox, to shock them into recognizing their own folly; and of Milarepa, whose own mother's grief and desire for revenge first drove him toward black magic, before his eventual realization allowed him to genuinely liberate his parents through meditative power — the point in each case being that indulged grief neither helps the dead nor serves the living, while diligent cultivation and dedicated merit genuinely can.

56. Section: The Second Affliction

Further, there is a kind who, though fortunate enough to have other easy means of livelihood remaining, instead, at a crossroads or in a busy marketplace, dismembers their own limbs and joints, feigning that their life is nearly spent, forcing themselves through this false suffering merely to survive. This is called the second present affliction — what ordinary beings resort to, broadly stated as before.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there is a kind who, though fortunate enough to have other easy means of livelihood remaining, instead, at a crossroads or in a busy marketplace, dismembers their own limbs and joints, feigning that their life is nearly spent, forcing themselves through this false suffering merely to survive. This is called the second present affliction — what ordinary beings resort to, broadly stated as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the second affliction (寅二第二損惱) — deliberately maiming oneself to solicit pity and alms, when honest livelihood remains genuinely available, trades real self-harm for a shortcut that yields nothing of lasting value.

57. Section: The Third Affliction Begins — Marking the Kind

Further, there is a kind who, by nature stingy and greedy, veiled by the defilement of stinginess, though fortunate enough to have every kind of means to sustain life, lives instead in great hardship and toil. This is called the third present affliction — what ordinary beings resort to, broadly stated as before.

Chinese Source

復有一類，為性慳貪，慳垢所蔽，幸有種種養命資緣，而大艱辛以自存活。是名第三現法損惱，凡夫所趣，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there is a kind who, by nature stingy and greedy, veiled by the defilement of stinginess, though fortunate enough to have every kind of means to sustain life, lives instead in great hardship and toil. This is called the third present affliction — what ordinary beings resort to, broadly stated as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a traditional narrative

This opens **the third affliction (寅三第三損惱2), beginning with marking the kind (卯一標種類)** — a genuinely wealthy person who denies themselves and their own family any real comfort, hoarding rather than using what they have, exemplifies this affliction. Master Cháng Bǎi tells of one such miser whom Śakra sought to reform by taking his exact double form, entering his house, and lavishly giving away the entire household fortune; when the real miser returned and found his double already installed, no one — not even his own family — could tell them apart until a hidden birthmark settled the matter in court, and even then his wife declared she preferred the generous double to her own real husband. Ashamed, the miser repented, abandoned his stinginess, and took up genuine generosity himself.
