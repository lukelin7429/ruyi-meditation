Fascicle 17 · 瑜伽師地論卷第十七 · The Verses on the Essential Body of the Teaching

Fourteen Topics Distilling the Dharma's Core Content

Fascicle 17 continues directly from fascicle 16's stopping point mid-commentary on the three trainings, then turns to the third and final set of verses in this ground's account of reflection: fourteen topics — beginning with the classic teaching to avoid all evil — distilling the entire teaching's essential content into a single connected sequence of verse and commentary.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 17 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. The Essential-Body Verses Open: Avoid All Evil, Abandon All Desire

Fascicle 17 opens mid-sentence, continuing fascicle 16's prose commentary on the three trainings exactly where it left off — then, partway through, turns to the third and final set of verses in this ground's account of reflection: fourteen topics distilling the entire teaching into a single connected sequence. The first topic opens with Buddhism's most famous single verse, on avoiding evil altogether, and its own reflection on what abandoning desire actually requires.

Chinese Source

已釋意趣義聖教伽他。今當建立體義伽他。宣說法要，是名為體。開顯理趣，是名為義。嗢柁南曰：惡、說、貪、流、怖，類、譽、池、流、貪，作、劬勞、得義，論議十四種。

Ru-Yi Translation

The verses on essential intent have now been explained. Now the verses on the essential body of the teaching should be established. Proclaiming the Dharma's essentials is called the body; disclosing its underlying meaning is called its significance. A summary verse states: evil, teaching, craving, flow, dread, kind, renown, pool, flow, craving, action, toil, attainment of benefit, and discourse — fourteen topics in all.

Chinese Source

於身語意諸所有，一切世間惡莫作，由念正知離諸欲，勿親能引無義苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever belongs to body, speech, and mind — let none of the world's evil be done; through mindfulness and clear comprehension, abandon every desire; do not draw close to what brings suffering devoid of benefit.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，所言惡者，謂諸惡行。於一切種、一切因緣、一切處所所有惡行皆不應作。云何於一切種不作惡耶？謂由身語意不造眾惡故。云何於一切因緣不作惡耶？謂由貪瞋癡所生諸惡終不造作故。云何於一切處所不作惡耶？謂依有情事處及非有情事處不造眾惡故。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, "evil" refers to evil conduct of every kind. Evil should never be done in any type, from any cause, or in any place whatsoever. What is meant by not doing evil in any type? Never producing evil through body, speech, or mind. What is meant by not doing evil from any cause? Never producing the evils that arise from craving, aversion, and delusion. What is meant by not doing evil in any place? Never producing evil in relation to sentient beings or to non-sentient things.

Chinese Source

云何由念正知遠離諸欲？謂斷事欲及斷煩惱欲故。云何斷事欲？謂如有一，於如來所證正法毗奈耶中得清淨信，了知居家迫迮，猶如牢獄，思求出離……由正信心，捨離家法，趣入非家，然於欲貪猶未永離。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by abandoning desire through mindfulness and clear comprehension? It means severing desire's objects and severing desire's affliction. What is severing desire's objects? It means one who, gaining pure faith in the Dharma and discipline realized by the Tathāgata, recognizes household life to be as confining as a prison, and seeks to leave it... through this pure faith, one abandons household life and enters homelessness — though the craving of desire is not yet entirely gone.

Chinese Source

云何斷煩惱欲？謂彼既出家已，為令欲貪無餘斷故，往趣曠野山林，安居邊際臥具，或住阿練若處，乃至或在空閑靜室。於諸事欲所起一切煩惱欲攝妄分別貪，為對治故，修四念住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is severing desire's affliction? It means that, having gone forth, in order to eliminate craving entirely, one goes to a wilderness or forest, settles with the most minimal bedding, dwells in a secluded place, or in a quiet, empty room; and in order to counteract every affliction of desire — every false conception of craving arising in connection with desire's objects — one cultivates the four foundations of mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this fourteen-topic summary verse as a genuinely condensed table of contents for everything the rest of fascicles 17-18 will unpack — evil, right teaching, craving, the flow of rebirth, the fear that drives renunciation, and so on through fourteen headings, each pointing toward a specific block of verses and commentary. He reads the opening verse itself as, in effect, the entire Buddhist ethical teaching in four lines: the famous "cease all evil" instruction, glossed here with real precision — evil is barred across every possible category (the three doors of body, speech, and mind), every possible motive (the three root afflictions of craving, aversion, and delusion), and every possible object (whether the evil concerns another sentient being, as in killing or stealing, or a non-sentient thing, as in mishandling property) — leaving no loophole through type, cause, or circumstance.

On abandoning desire, he distinguishes two layers that must both be addressed: desire's actual objects (the concrete situations of household and sensory life) and desire's underlying affliction (the mental habit of craving itself, which can persist long after the objects are physically out of reach). Renunciation — literally leaving home, recognizing ordinary household life as confining as a prison — handles the first layer, but Cháng Bǎi stresses the treatise's own honest admission that ordination alone does not extinguish craving; a newly ordained monastic still carries the mental habit inward, which is exactly why cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness (observing the body, feelings, mind, and mental objects) in a genuinely solitary setting is named as the actual remedy for the second, subtler layer. He notes the treatise goes on, in material this section compresses, to detail how a monastic re-enters a village for alms without losing this hard-won inward stillness — guarding the body from physical danger, guarding the senses from inflaming sights, and keeping mindfulness steady throughout, material the treatise itself cross-references at length to the Śrāvaka Ground's own fuller treatment. With evil conduct and desire's two layers both addressed, the treatise turns to the remainder of this first topic: what genuinely harmful austerity looks like, and why it should not be pursued either.

2. Topic One Closes: Freedom From Desire's Fruit, and the Harm of Self-Torment

Completing the first of fourteen topics, the treatise names what sustained mindfulness and clear comprehension actually accomplish — freedom from the five hindrances, culminating in the first meditative absorption — then turns to the verse's closing line: a direct warning against the non-Buddhist practice of self-mortification, mistaken by its practitioners for a shortcut to liberation.

Chinese Source

又即以彼正知而住為依止故，遠離諸蓋，身心調暢，有所堪能，熾然方便修斷寂靜。彼由如是念及正知為依止故，便能證得煩惱欲斷，遠離諸欲，乃至於初靜慮具足而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

And, resting on that same abiding in clear comprehension, one becomes free of the hindrances; body and mind grow pliant and capable, and one applies oneself with ardent effort to cultivating their cessation, and to stillness. Resting on mindfulness and clear comprehension in this way, one is able to realize the severing of desire's affliction, becoming free of desire, up to and including dwelling fully in the first meditative absorption.

Chinese Source

何等名為引無義苦？謂如有一，若諸沙門、或婆羅門，行自苦行，於現法中，以種種苦自逼自切，周遍燒惱。自謂我今由現法苦所逼惱故，解脫當苦。雖求是事而自煎逼，彼於此事終不能得，然更招集大損惱事。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by bringing on suffering devoid of benefit? It refers to one — whether an ascetic or a brahmin — who practices self-torment, afflicting and scourging themselves in every way in this very life, thinking: through the suffering that afflicts me now, I will be freed from future suffering. Though seeking this goal, and tormenting themselves in pursuit of it, they never actually attain it — and instead bring upon themselves great further harm.

Chinese Source

諸聖弟子，能於如是受用自苦行邊能引非聖無義苦法善了知已，遠而避之，不親、不近，亦不承事。

Ru-Yi Translation

The noble disciples, having thoroughly understood this practice of self-torment as an ignoble teaching bringing suffering devoid of benefit, keep their distance from it, neither drawing close to it, nor associating with it, nor paying homage to those who practice it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the transition from mindfulness to clear comprehension as marking real progress: mindfulness alone applies the four foundations as a technique, but sustained clear comprehension — continuously and accurately knowing one's own present state — is what actually clears away the five hindrances (craving, ill will, torpor, restlessness, and doubt, already met in fascicle 16's account of concentration), yielding the pliancy and capability that make genuine meditative absorption possible. He notes the verse's own logic is cumulative: mindfulness and clear comprehension together sever desire's underlying affliction, which in turn opens the first meditative absorption — not a separate achievement, but the natural fruit of the very process just described.

On the verse's closing warning, he reads self-mortification as the opposite error from indulgence, but no less mistaken: some non-Buddhist ascetics reasoned that inflicting present suffering on themselves would somehow discharge future suffering in advance, as if pain itself were a kind of currency that could be spent down — a view the treatise flatly rejects, since suffering caused this way accomplishes nothing toward actually uprooting its cause (craving and ignorance) and only adds fresh harm on top of what already exists. He notes the treatise's instruction to noble disciples is unusually direct: not merely to decline this practice personally, but to keep a real distance from those who teach or practice it, neither honoring nor associating with them, since proximity to a compelling but mistaken teaching risks drawing a practitioner toward it. With evil conduct, desire's two layers, and harmful austerity all addressed, the treatise's exposition of the opening verse is complete, and it turns to a further reflection recasting the same ground in terms of what a being's fulfillment actually requires.

3. Topic One's Summary: Two Kinds of Fulfillment Every Being Seeks

Before moving to the second topic, the treatise steps back and recasts everything just covered in terms of what any being's fulfillment actually consists in — a favorable rebirth, or decisive excellence culminating in nirvāṇa — then maps the opening verse's four lines directly onto these two goals, closing the first of the fourteen topics.

Chinese Source

復次，今當略辯上所說義。云何略辯？謂諸有情有二種滿。一、增上生滿，二、決定勝滿。增上生滿者，謂往善趣。決定勝滿者，謂愛盡、離欲、寂滅、涅槃。於此二滿及與障礙，能斷能證，是名略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, let what has been said above now be briefly explained. What is this brief explanation? It is that sentient beings seek two kinds of fulfillment: first, the fulfillment of a favorable rebirth; and second, the fulfillment of decisive excellence. The fulfillment of a favorable rebirth means proceeding to a good realm of rebirth. The fulfillment of decisive excellence means the exhaustion of craving, freedom from desire, cessation, and nirvāṇa. Being able to sever the obstacles to, and realize, both of these fulfillments — this is the brief explanation.

Chinese Source

若於一切種、一切因緣、一切處所不作惡行，彼便能斷增上生滿所有障礙，亦能證得增上生滿。若於受用欲樂行邊及於受用自苦行邊決定遠離，彼便能斷決定勝滿所有障礙，亦能證得決定勝滿。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one refrains from evil conduct of every type, from every cause, in every place, one thereby severs every obstacle to the fulfillment of a favorable rebirth, and also realizes that fulfillment. If one resolutely keeps clear of both the indulgence of sensual pleasure and the practice of self-torment, one thereby severs every obstacle to the fulfillment of decisive excellence, and also realizes that fulfillment. Know that this is the brief explanation given here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this two-fold framework — favorable rebirth and decisive excellence — as a lens the treatise will apply repeatedly across the remaining thirteen topics, worth understanding precisely here at its first appearance: a favorable rebirth is simply a good outcome within saṃsāra (human or heavenly birth, secured by ordinary ethical conduct), while decisive excellence names the qualitatively different achievement of actually exhausting craving, becoming free of desire, reaching cessation, and realizing nirvāṇa itself — the trajectory that leaves saṃsāra behind rather than merely improving one's position within it. He notes the four technical terms making up "decisive excellence" trace a single deepening process rather than four separate states: exhaustion of craving marks the end of hoping for a better future existence; freedom from desire marks the end of delighting in present sense-objects; cessation marks the ending of whatever afflictions remain beyond craving itself; and nirvāṇa, strictly speaking, names only the state without any remainder at all, after death — so that exhaustion of craving and freedom from desire describe liberation while the body still lives, while cessation and nirvāṇa proper describe its completion.

The closing correspondence he reads as elegantly exact: avoiding evil across every type, cause, and place (the verse's first two lines) removes every obstacle to a favorable rebirth and thereby secures it, while resolutely avoiding both indulgence and self-torment (the verse's last two lines) removes every obstacle to decisive excellence and thereby secures that too — meaning the single opening verse, rightly understood, already contains everything needed for both a good rebirth and full liberation. With this summary, the first of the fourteen topics on the essential body of the teaching — evil conduct and its remedy — is complete, and the treatise turns to the second topic: what should be taught, focused specifically on the five sensory pleasures and why a renunciant's happiness genuinely exceeds an indulgent one's.

4. Topic Two Opens: The Five Sensory Pleasures, and How Craving Traps a Being

The second of the fourteen topics, "what should be spoken of," takes up the five sensory pleasures directly — why they are called this, and how an ordinary being's unexamined attachment to them, left unaddressed, becomes the very engine of samsaric wandering. Four verses frame the topic; this section covers the first, and the reasoning behind why desire deserves to be spoken of at all.

Chinese Source

應說想眾生，依應說安住，不了知應說，而招集生死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beings conceive what should be spoken of, and dwell resting upon it; not understanding what should be spoken of, they thereby accumulate birth and death.

Chinese Source

今此義中，說妙五欲以為應說。又妙五欲，諸餘沙門、婆羅門等，從施主邊以言求索，故名應說。又諸君主於妙五欲，從僕使等，以言呼召而受用之；由是因緣，亦名應說。又諸受欲者，於妙五欲不能自然善知過患，唯除諸佛及佛弟子，為其宣說彼過患已，乃能了知；由是因緣，亦名應說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the sublime five sensory pleasures are what is meant by "what should be spoken of." Other ascetics and brahmins must ask for them from their donors in words — hence they are called "what should be spoken of." Rulers, too, summon their servants with words in order to enjoy them — by this same reason, they too are called "what should be spoken of." And those who indulge in them cannot, on their own, come to know their faults; only the buddhas and their disciples, by actually teaching those faults, enable this understanding — for this reason too, they are called "what should be spoken of."

Chinese Source

又於諸欲不如實知有眾過患。所謂諸欲無常、虛、偽、空無有實，敗壞之法，猶如幻事誑惑愚夫，甚少愛味，多諸過患。亦不如實了知如是少味多患諸欲出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nor do they truly understand desire's many faults — that desires are impermanent, hollow, false, empty of any real substance, subject to decay, deceiving the foolish just as a conjurer's illusion does, offering only the barest sweetness while carrying many faults. Nor do they truly understand how to be free of desires so slight in their sweetness and so heavy with fault.

Chinese Source

彼既如是不見過患、不知出離而受諸欲；由是因緣，便於欲界生為根本所有諸行深起樂著。又復造作生為根本所有業已，受欲界生，生已死滅，生已殞歿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not seeing these faults, not knowing how to be free of them, one indulges in desire regardless — and by this cause, one becomes deeply attached to whatever conditioned states take rebirth in the desire realm as their root. Having performed the actions rooted in such rebirth, one takes birth in the desire realm again, and having been born, dies; having been born, perishes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three distinct reasons the five sensory pleasures earn the name "what should be spoken of": ordinary renunciants and brahmins must actually ask their donors for what sustains them; even a king summons servants with spoken commands to enjoy the same pleasures; and — the deepest reason — no one indulging in sense-pleasure discovers its faults on their own, since the buddhas and their disciples are the ones who must actually put those faults into words for anyone to understand them. Desire, in other words, is a topic that has to be taught, not one that teaches itself.

He traces the verse's causal chain step by step: an ordinary mind first grasps a pleasing object's overall impression, then elaborates it into ever more particular attractive features (he gives the example of noticing a person is beautiful, then admiring the eyes, then the nose, each admiration a fresh layer of the same craving); this generates attachment, indulgence, and eventually — in the treatise's own vivid vocabulary — gluttonous absorption, bewildered bemusement, and stubborn immersion, all simply different names for the same underlying craving intensifying by degrees. Because ordinary beings never see the four faults hidden beneath desire's surface — desires only appear stable but are impermanent; only appear self-directed but are without any real controlling self; only appear clean but are in fact impure; only appear pleasurable but are in fact suffering — they remain trapped by what the verse calls "a conjurer's illusion," and, not knowing the sevenfold sequence of mental cultivation that actually leads out of this trap (already introduced in fascicle 16's account of wisdom), simply continue producing the karma that keeps rebirth in the desire realm running: birth, death, and rebirth in an unbroken cycle powered by nothing more than unexamined attachment to what was never worth the cost. With the first verse's exposition complete, the treatise turns to the second: what happens once a being does come to understand.

5. Verse Two: Renouncing Without Reward-Seeking, and Why the Wise Face No Valid Criticism

The second verse turns from the trap of unexamined desire to what genuine understanding produces: renunciation undertaken with no wish even for a pleasant future rebirth, and the settled confidence that follows — a confidence the treatise grounds not in indifference to criticism but in the simple fact that a rightly motivated renunciant has given critics nothing accurate to say.

Chinese Source

若了知應說，於說者無慮，由無有此故，他不應譏論。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one understands what should be spoken of, one who speaks of it need have no concern; because this is absent, others should not criticize.

Chinese Source

若遇善士，得聞正法，如理作意，則於諸欲如實了知過患出離……彼於如來所證正法毗奈耶中得清淨信，便於諸欲深見過患轉復增勝；遂能捨離若少若多財寶、庫藏、眷屬、遊從，以正信心，捨離家法，趣於非家。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one encounters a good teacher, hears the true Dharma, and applies proper reflection, one comes to truly understand desire's faults and how to be free of them... gaining pure faith in the Dharma and discipline realized by the Tathāgata, one's insight into desire's faults grows ever deeper and stronger; one thereby becomes able to give up wealth and property great or small, storehouses, kin, and companions, and with a mind of pure faith, abandons household life and enters homelessness.

Chinese Source

如是出家，無所願求修行梵行。謂我由此持戒精進修梵行故，當得生天，或異天處。彼無如是邪祈願故，於己不見、不恐不慮他所譏論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Going forth in this way, one cultivates the holy life with no wish for any reward — not thinking, "because I keep these precepts and diligently practice the holy life, I shall be reborn in heaven, or in some other exalted realm." Being free of any such misguided aspiration, one sees nothing in oneself to fear or worry over regarding others' criticism.

Chinese Source

此即成就清淨尸羅及清淨見。何以故？由見顛倒，發起於慢；慢所持故，與餘沙門、婆羅門等共興諍論。由此因緣，說如是見為諍根本。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is precisely the accomplishment of pure precepts and pure view. Why is this so? Because a distorted view gives rise to pride; and, held by that pride, one enters into dispute with other ascetics and brahmins. For this reason, such a view is called the very root of dispute.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this verse as answering a question the prior verse leaves open: given how deeply desire traps an ordinary being, what actually breaks the pattern? His answer follows the text closely — not willpower alone, but the specific sequence of meeting a genuine teacher, hearing the true Dharma from them, and then actually reflecting on it correctly, which together produce real understanding of desire's faults rather than mere intellectual assent. He stresses the treatise's own insistence that genuine renunciation cannot be motivated even by a wish for heavenly rebirth — desiring a better birth is still desire, operating on the same basic mechanism the treatise has spent this whole topic dismantling — so a rightly motivated renunciant seeks nothing at all within the three realms, high or low.

The closing point he treats as this verse's real payoff: such a person faces criticism with settled confidence not because they are indifferent to what others think, but because there is genuinely nothing accurate left for a critic to say — no secret desire for status, no hidden wish for future comfort. He traces the deeper mechanism behind why criticism arises at all: distorted view (imagining a fixed self where none exists) breeds pride, and pride, in turn, is what actually produces dispute with people of other views — which is why the treatise names view itself, not merely temperament, as dispute's true root. A renunciant who has genuinely uprooted self-view has thereby also uprooted the pride that fuels arguments in the first place, leaving both pure conduct and pure view intact together.

6. Verse Three: Comparison's Trap, and Debating From Compassion Rather Than Pride

The third verse names the specific mechanism behind most dispute — measuring oneself as equal, superior, or inferior to others — and shows how a noble disciple who has genuinely seen no-self is simply no longer available to be moved by it, before addressing a natural objection: if disputing is rooted in pride, why do the Buddha's own disciples still debate outsiders at all?

Chinese Source

若計等勝劣，彼遂興諍論，於三種無動，等勝劣皆無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever reckons themselves equal, superior, or inferior thereby falls into dispute; unmoved with respect to all three, neither equal, superior, nor inferior remain.

Chinese Source

依止我勝、我等、我劣三種慢類，立己為勝、或等、或劣。若聖弟子，非我、我所、我慢所動……了知諸行皆眾緣生，於諸行中唯見法性，尚不以己校量於他為勝等劣，況起見慢而興諍論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on the three kinds of pride — I am superior, I am equal, I am inferior — one establishes oneself as superior, equal, or inferior. But a noble disciple is moved by none of these — not by self, not by what is mine, not by pride... understanding that all conditioned things arise from many causes, seeing in them only the nature of things themselves, such a one does not even measure themselves against another as superior, equal, or inferior — how much less would they give rise to views and pride and fall into dispute.

Chinese Source

彼聖弟子，雖於他所顯揚自宗，摧伏他論；然於諸法，唯為法性緣於慈悲。謂當云何若有於我所說妙義一句領解，如是如是正修行者，令彼長夜獲得大義利益安樂，亦令如來正法久住。不依見慢及為利養恭敬因緣，而興諍論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though the noble disciples do proclaim their own tradition before others and refute rival positions, this is done resting only on the nature of things, in dependence on compassion — thinking: if only someone might grasp even a single phrase of the sublime meaning I teach, and practice accordingly, they would gain great benefit and ease throughout the long night of ignorance, and the Tathāgata's true Dharma would long endure. They do not enter dispute resting on view and pride, nor for the sake of gain and veneration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "three kinds of pride" as all stemming from the same root: the moment a person imagines a fixed self, they cannot help placing that self somewhere on a ladder relative to others — above, level with, or below — and it is precisely this placement, not any particular outcome of the comparison, that generates dispute. A noble disciple who has genuinely realized that conditioned things arise from causes and conditions, with only the bare nature of things visible underneath, simply has no fixed self left to place on the ladder at all; the comparison itself becomes unavailable, not merely resisted through discipline.

He then addresses the obvious objection this raises: doesn't the Buddha's own community engage in exactly this kind of debate with rival ascetics and brahmins? The answer he draws out is a distinction of motive rather than of behavior — the noble disciples do refute other positions, sometimes vigorously, but the refutation rests entirely on compassion for those who might benefit from even a fragment of genuine understanding, and on the wish that the true teaching endure for others' sake, never on pride in one's own view or a desire for material support and recognition. He connects this explicitly to the Bodhisattva Ground's teaching on "Dharma-based compassion" — compassion directed at beings understood only as provisional aggregates rather than as fixed persons, a mode of compassion the treatise notes bodhisattvas share with śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas but non-Buddhist practitioners do not. With comparison's trap named and compassionate debate distinguished from prideful dispute, the treatise turns to the fourth and final verse: what actual liberation looks like once craving, pride, and clinging are genuinely gone.

7. Verse Four: Craving as Smoke Before the Fire, and the Arhat Untouched by Gain or Loss

The fourth and final verse of topic two describes what liberation actually looks like once it is complete — tracing craving toward the five aggregates through the stages from stream-entry to full arhatship, then describing craving itself through the treatise's own memorable image of smoke rising before a fire, and closing with a portrait of an arhat genuinely unmoved whether treated generously or shabbily by others.

Chinese Source

斷名色愛慢，無著煙寂靜，無惱希不見，此彼天人世。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having severed craving and pride toward name-and-form, without attachment, the smoke stilled; free of affliction, free of expectation — such a one is not to be found among either gods or humans, in this world or the next.

Chinese Source

言名色者，謂五取蘊。若有於彼觀見為苦，當諦現觀於五取蘊盡見苦時，於五取蘊所有貪愛，由意樂故皆說為斷，非隨眠故。彼若即如已所得道轉更修習，於其我慢無餘斷滅，成阿羅漢，諸漏永盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Name-and-form" refers to the five aggregates of clinging. One who observes them to be suffering, upon directly realizing the truths and seeing the five aggregates entirely as suffering, is said to have severed all craving toward them — in disposition, though not yet in every latent trace. Continuing to cultivate the very path already attained, one's residual pride is finally extinguished without remainder, and one becomes an arhat, all defilements forever exhausted.

Chinese Source

如世間煙，是火前相，能損眼根，便為擾亂，令不安住。愛亦如是，是貪瞋癡火之前相，能損慧眼，亂心相續。謂能引發無義尋思。彼於此愛已斷已知，乃至令其於當來世成不生法，名煙寂靜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as, in the world, smoke is the precursor of fire, injuring the eyes and disturbing them, leaving them unsettled — craving is just the same: it is the precursor of the fire of greed, hatred, and delusion, injuring the eye of wisdom and disturbing the mind's continuity, giving rise to thoughts devoid of benefit. One who has severed this craving and fully understood it, so that it will never arise again in future — this is called "the smoke stilled."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the verse's opening line across the full arc of the path: a stream-enterer's direct realization of the four truths severs craving toward the five aggregates "in disposition" — the felt pull toward clinging genuinely subsides — but the deeper latent tendency remains, capable of resurfacing under the wrong conditions, exactly as an arhat's occasional flash of irritation extinguishes itself almost as fast as it appears (he cites the classical comparison to oil in a hot pan, evaporating almost the instant it touches down). Only the continued cultivation belonging to the path of cultivation proper — the same path already attained, simply carried further — finally exhausts pride's last latent trace and yields full arhatship, defilements entirely spent, with nothing left to reawaken.

He reads the "smoke" image as one of the treatise's most vivid similes: ordinary smoke stings the eyes and clouds vision before any flame is even visible, exactly the way craving clouds the eye of wisdom and stirs the mind into restless, unproductive thinking before the actual fires of greed, hatred, and delusion fully ignite — sever the smoke completely, in other words, and the fire itself is preempted. He also draws out a subtlety worth noting: an arhat still needs food, robes, and shelter to sustain the body, and does seek them out, but seeks without the taint of craving — genuinely wanting the sustenance while genuinely not craving it, a distinction the treatise insists is coherent rather than contradictory.

The remainder of the verse's exposition — deliberately organized around the three roots of affliction — shows what this actually looks like in daily encounters with donors: whether a benefactor gives generously and promptly or begrudgingly and late, the arhat neither delights in the first nor resents the second, remaining free of greed's disturbance in either case; whether treated well or poorly by a fellow practitioner or by a donor, the arhat harbors no wish to harm and no resentment, remaining free of hatred's disturbance; and whether the goods received are fine or coarse, the arhat sees clearly through their impermanence and their real cost, remaining free of delusion's disturbance, steadily mindful throughout. Cháng Bǎi reads this threefold portrait as the verse's real answer to what "free of affliction, free of expectation" concretely means: not a numb indifference to circumstance, but a mind so thoroughly free of greed, hatred, and delusion at the root that circumstance simply no longer has the power to disturb it.

8. Verse Four Closes: Free of Expectation, and a Virtue Found in No Other Realm

The verse's final phrase, "free of expectation," is unpacked through two objects an ordinary mind never stops hoping for — fine possessions and continued life — before the treatise closes topic two with a sweeping claim: this particular constellation of freedom appears in no god's or human's causal path or resulting state, anywhere in this world-system or any other.

Chinese Source

彼不擎鼻內懷貪願，往趣居家……我當從彼獲得上妙應所噉食，乃至財寶、衣服、餚膳、諸坐臥具、病緣醫藥供身什物。如是追求及與受用，於此財物都無希望。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such a one does not go to a household harboring a craving wish, as an elephant lifts its trunk toward what it wants... thinking, "I shall obtain from them the finest food, wealth, clothing, delicacies, bedding and seating, and medicine for illness." In seeking out and making use of such things, there is no expectation toward this wealth at all.

Chinese Source

又彼恆常安住死想。謂過夜分入晝分中，復過晝分還入夜分，於其中間我有無量應死因緣，如經廣說。所謂發風，乃至非人之所恐怖。由此因緣，所為追求、所為受用所有財物，於此壽命亦無希望。

Ru-Yi Translation

And such a one constantly abides in the perception of death — knowing that, whether passing from night into day or from day back into night, there exist in the interval countless conditions by which one might die, exactly as the sutras describe at length: a disturbance of the wind-element within the body, and so on, up to being terrified by non-human beings. For this reason, in seeking out and making use of any wealth, there is no expectation toward life itself either.

Chinese Source

如是無著、煙寂靜、無燒惱、無希望故，於此天人、帝釋、自在、世主天等所有因中都不可見。於彼天人諸因果中，亦不可見。又於此四洲天人世間，及彼餘處都不可見。又於此世界天人世間，及彼餘處都不可見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being thus free of attachment, the smoke stilled, free of affliction, and free of expectation — this is nowhere to be found within the causal paths of gods, humans, Śakra, the lord of the Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven, or the great Brahmā king; nor is it found within their resulting states. Nor is it to be found among the gods and humans of these four continents, or in any place beyond them. Nor is it to be found among the gods and humans of this world-system, or in any place beyond it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "free of expectation" as reaching deeper than ordinary contentment: an arhat still seeks food, clothing, and medicine as genuinely needed, but without the elephant's-trunk grasping reach toward more — and, more strikingly, holds no expectation even toward continued life itself, precisely because constant, lived awareness of death's countless causes (a ruptured wind-element, a stray accident, even fright from a non-human presence, all drawn from the sutras' own catalogue) has already done the work ordinary people spend a lifetime avoiding. He cites the same simile met earlier in this ground (fascicle 16, section 2): one who has genuinely internalized impermanence in advance, like someone who has already seen the lightning, is no more shaken by the thunderclap of actual loss than by its rehearsal.

On the closing sweep across every possible realm, he reads the specific figures named — Śakra ruling the Heaven of the Thirty-Three, the lord of the Heaven of Free Enjoyment of Others' Creations, and the great Brahmā king himself — as deliberately naming the very highest attainments any being can reach through ordinary merit or worldly meditation alone: even these exalted rulers, in both their causal cultivation (the ten wholesome courses of action, or the four immeasurables) and their resulting states, never developed genuine freedom from craving's root, because none of their practices actually uprooted the underlying afflictions the way a renunciant's realization of no-self does. The claim sweeping across every continent, every world-system, and their gods and humans without exception is, in his reading, the strongest possible way of saying that liberation of this specific, complete kind belongs to renunciant practice alone — not merely rare, but categorically absent everywhere else.

9. Topic Two's Summary Closes: Karma's Own Timing, and Why the Deva Needed the Buddha

Before moving on, the treatise passes back over all four verses twice more — first mapping them onto a simple wrongdoing-and-result pattern, then adding one genuinely new point: why desired things take time to ripen even after real effort. It closes by explaining the frame story directly: why the young monk himself could not satisfy the deva's question, and why only the Buddha could.

Chinese Source

若於先世不作福者，今雖用功，於所樂欲不能果遂。或唯今世造作福者，即於此時，其所樂欲亦不諧偶。由此因緣，後方成辦，所以諸欲名曰待時。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one performed no merit in a former life, then however much effort one now applies, one's desired object still fails to be realized. Or, if one performs merit only in this present life, the desired object still does not come together at this very time. Because of this, its accomplishment must wait for a later time — this is why desires are said to "require their proper time."

Chinese Source

此中前三頌，顯示世尊為諸天說，苾芻不能顯揚如來聖教大義，而我獨能。說是語時，彼既領悟，於苾芻所生陵懱心及於自身心生憍慢皆得除滅。第四頌中，廣顯如來聖教大義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the first three verses show the Blessed One speaking directly to the deity: the monk himself could not unfold the great meaning of the Tathāgata's teaching — only I can. Upon hearing this, the deity awakened to understanding, and both the contempt felt toward the monk and the pride felt in themselves were entirely dispelled. The fourth verse broadly discloses the great meaning of the Tathāgata's teaching itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats most of this closing summary as a second pass over ground already covered — the four verses mapped once as "desire requiring solicitation → wrong conduct toward desire → wrong conduct's result," and mapped again with slightly more precision onto renunciation's motives and the stages from stream-entry through arhatship — genuinely useful confirmation of the reading already given, but not new doctrine. The one point he flags as a real addition is the explanation of why desired things "require their proper time": merit performed in a past life may only ripen now, merit performed only in this present life may not ripen until a future one, and either way the gap between effort and result is simply how karma actually operates — a plain, practical answer to the common frustration of working hard without the expected reward, and the deeper reason the treatise gave for calling desire "something requiring solicitation" back in verse one.

He reads the closing explanation of the frame story as tying the whole topic back to where it began, in fascicle 17's opening pages: the deity's original question — why abandon present pleasure for something unseen — could not be answered convincingly by the young monk alone, however sincere, because unfolding "the great meaning of the Tathāgata's teaching" requires the Buddha's own complete realization, not merely correct practice; only once the Buddha himself spoke the first three verses did the deity's scorn for the monk, and pride in their own status, genuinely dissolve, and only the fourth verse, spoken last, discloses the teaching's full weight. With this, the second of fourteen topics — covering the five sensory pleasures, why they trap the unwary, and what it means to actually see through them — is complete in every respect, and the treatise turns to the third: craving-desire itself.

10. Topic Three Opens: A Burning Mind, and the Buddha's Fourfold Remedy

The third of fourteen topics, craving-desire, opens with another deity's direct plea — a mind crushed and set ablaze by lust — and the Buddha's four-verse answer naming the complete remedy: correcting distorted perception, cultivating meditation on the body's impurity, and observing suffering, non-self, and signlessness until pride itself is destroyed. This section covers the opening exchange and the first remedy: seeing through the perceptual distortion that makes an attractive appearance seem worth clinging to.

Chinese Source

欲貪所摧蔽，我心遍燒然，唯大仙哀愍，為說令寂靜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Crushed and obscured by craving-desire, my mind is entirely ablaze; great sage, have compassion — teach me how to make it still.

Chinese Source

由汝想顛倒，令心遍燒然，是故常遠離，引貪淨妙相。汝當修不淨，常定於一境，為貪火速滅，數數應澆灌。觀非妙諸行，為苦為無我，亦繫念於身，多修習厭離。修習於無相，壞慢及隨眠，由於慢現觀，當證苦邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is your own distorted perception that sets your mind entirely ablaze; therefore always keep far from the alluring, seemingly pure appearance that provokes craving. You should cultivate meditation on impurity, keeping the mind constantly fixed on a single object; to quickly extinguish craving's fire, you should pour water on it again and again. Observe conditioned things — not beautiful, but suffering, but without self; keep mindfulness fixed on the body as well, and cultivate revulsion extensively. Cultivate the signless, and destroy pride along with its latent tendency; through direct realization concerning pride, you shall reach the very limit of suffering.

Chinese Source

云何想顛倒？謂於不淨境，捨不淨相；不正思惟，取淨妙相及取隨好。云何遠離引貪淨相？謂如有一，見少盛色應可愛樂諸母邑已，便攝諸根而不隨念。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is distorted perception? It means, regarding an impure object, discarding its impure mark; through improper reflection, taking up instead a seemingly pure, alluring mark, along with its attractive particulars. What is meant by keeping far from the alluring mark that provokes craving? It refers to one who, having seen a young woman in the bloom of youth, lovely and desirable, at once withdraws the senses and does not dwell on what was seen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the deity's opening plea as more visceral than the previous topic's — not merely trapped by desire in the abstract, but actively burning, which is why the Buddha's answer moves immediately to a concrete remedy rather than further analysis. The four verses lay out a complete sequence: first correct the perceptual error at desire's root, then apply meditation on impurity as an active antidote, then deepen understanding by seeing the object of craving as suffering and as without self, and finally cultivate the signless concentration that uproots pride's latent tendency entirely — reaching, in the verse's own phrase, "the very limit of suffering," the last possible experience of it before liberation.

He glosses "distorted perception" precisely: the body is, by its actual nature, impure — an ordinary mind simply discards this accurate impure mark and substitutes a seemingly clean, attractive one instead, then elaborates that substitution into ever more particular admired features, exactly the escalating pattern already traced in the previous topic's account of craving. The remedy he describes as disarmingly simple in principle, demanding in practice: the instant an attractive appearance is noticed, withdraw the senses before the mind has a chance to dwell on it and elaborate further — not suppressing the initial perception, which cannot be helped, but refusing to feed it with continued attention, since (as the treatise notes elsewhere) an attractive appearance encountered but not lingered over cannot damage the holy life the way one dwelt upon inevitably will. With perceptual distortion named and its immediate remedy given, the treatise turns to the active cultivation that follows: meditation on the body's impurity in full detail.

11. Meditation on Impurity: The Corpse Outside, the Body Within

Where withdrawing the senses is a first line of defense, meditation on impurity is the active cultivation that follows — training the mind on a body's actual decay from without, and then turning the same unflinching attention inward, to what any living body, one's own included, is actually made of.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，先以巧便取於賢善三摩地相，所謂青瘀乃至白骨，或骨瑣相。即以此相，於現所得可愛境界，繫念思惟；如前所取，後亦如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who first skillfully grasps the mark belonging to wholesome concentration — the mark of a corpse turning livid, and on through to bare bones, or a scattered skeleton. Taking up this same mark, one fixes mindfulness upon it in reflecting on whatever presently appears as an attractive object; just as one grasped it before, so one continues to grasp it afterward.

Chinese Source

又於內身或自、或他，觀察種種不淨充滿。謂此身中有髮有爪，乃至便利種種不淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

And, regarding the body within — one's own or another's — one observes it as filled with impurity of every kind: within this body there is hair, there are nails, and on through to excrement and every other kind of impurity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "wholesome concentration" as the classical progression of corpse-contemplation stages already touched on in earlier fascicles — a body turning livid, bloating, being consumed by scavengers, discoloring, decomposing, and finally reduced to bare bones or a scattered skeleton — a sequence the Mahāprajñāpāramitā treatise elsewhere renders in eleven verses of graphic detail. The crucial instruction, he notes, is what happens next: a practitioner does not merely contemplate a stranger's corpse in isolation, but deliberately transfers that same decaying image onto whatever living body currently appears attractive, in meditation and in ordinary encounters alike — training the mind to see straight through an alluring surface to the same decay any body is inevitably heading toward.

The internal contemplation he reads as completing the picture from the inside: rather than waiting for death and decay to reveal a body's real composition, a practitioner inventories what is already there beneath the skin right now — hair, nails, organs, and waste — applying the meditation equally to one's own body as to another's, since the same "thin skin stretched over impurity," as the treatise elsewhere puts it, describes every living body without exception. He stresses this is not intended as revulsion for its own sake, but as a precise corrective to distorted perception's specific error: once a practitioner has genuinely trained the mind to see a body's actual composition and trajectory, the false "pure, attractive mark" that fuels craving simply has nothing left to stand on. With both the outer and inner dimensions of impurity meditation established, the treatise turns to the third and fourth remedies: observing conditioned things as suffering, and as without any self at all.

12. Observing Suffering and Non-Self, and Sustained Practice for the Strongly Afflicted

The fourth verse's remaining ground covers observing conditioned things as suffering and as without self, then turns practical: how a practitioner whose craving runs unusually strong must apply the same remedies not once but repeatedly, and how much of this affliction actually remains present even in advanced practitioners short of full arhatship.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，作是思惟：見少盛色應可愛樂諸母邑已，所生貪愛受用希望，即是集諦，為眾苦因。由此故生，生已老死，愁歎憂苦種種擾惱從此而生。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who reflects thus: the craving, indulgence, and longing that arise upon seeing a young woman in the bloom of youth, lovely and desirable — this itself is the truth of origination, the cause of every kind of suffering. From this, birth arises; having been born, aging and death follow, and sorrow, lamentation, grief, and every kind of affliction arise from this in turn.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，作是思惟：於我身形、女身形中，都無有我及有情等，誰能受用，誰所受用；唯是諸行，唯是諸法，從眾緣生。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who reflects thus: within my own bodily form, or a woman's bodily form, there exists no self and no being of any kind — who, then, could be the one enjoying, and who the one enjoyed? There is only what is conditioned, only these phenomena, arising from a coming-together of many causes.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，性是猛盛欲貪種類。由是猛盛欲貪類故，雖攝諸根，然被貪欲損壞其心；雖復作意思惟不淨、苦及無我，亦為欲貪損壞其心。由此因緣，彼依不淨、或苦、無我作意思惟，權時厭毀、違逆、不順……親近、修習、若多修習。彼由多住如是行故，便能斷此猛盛欲貪。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one whose disposition belongs to the fiercely intense type of craving-desire. Because their craving is so intensely strong, even withdrawing the senses still leaves the mind damaged by desire; even applying the mind to impurity, suffering, and non-self, the mind is still damaged by desire. For this reason, relying on these same reflections on impurity, suffering, and non-self, one must repeatedly reject what has already arisen, resist what is now arising, and refuse what is about to arise... drawing near to this practice, cultivating it, and cultivating it extensively. Only by dwelling in such practice at length is one able to sever this fiercely intense craving-desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "observing suffering" as tracing craving forward to its actual cost: the very moment of attraction is already the truth of origination in miniature, setting in motion the same causal chain — craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and the aging, death, and grief that inevitably follow — that structures the four noble truths as a whole; nothing about desire's pleasure is separable from this trajectory. "Observing non-self" then removes the last apparent justification for craving by asking directly who is actually enjoying and who is actually being enjoyed — finding, on close examination, no self anywhere in either party, only conditioned processes meeting conditioned processes.

He treats the passage on "fierce" craving as the treatise's own honest acknowledgment that these remedies do not work instantly or equally for everyone: some practitioners, by disposition, carry unusually strong craving, strong enough that even correct technique — withdrawing the senses, applying the antidotes — still leaves the mind bruised by desire's pull. For this type, the treatise prescribes sustained repetition across past, present, and future moments of arising desire (rejecting what already arose, resisting what is now arising, refusing what is about to arise) and distinguishes three depths of practice already met in fascicle 16's account of the trainings — familiarity through hearing, genuine cultivation through reflection, and extensive cultivation through meditation proper — stressing that only the last, sustained over real time, actually severs the affliction's active manifestation, though its underlying latent tendency requires the deeper wisdom of non-self to remove entirely. He adds a sobering technical note the treatise draws out next: even a once-returner or non-returner, well advanced on the path, still carries measurable craving — the non-returner has fully severed only the five lower fetters binding one to the sense-desire realm (craving, aversion, self-view, attachment to wrong precepts, and doubt), while the five higher fetters (craving for the form and formless realms, restlessness, pride, and ignorance) remain until arhatship finally removes every trace. With the fourth verse's full analysis complete, the treatise turns to the fifth and final verse: the signless concentration that destroys pride itself.

13. Topic Three's Verses Close: Pride's Final Extinction, and Craving's Five Causes

The fifth verse's exposition closes with pride's complete extinction and full arhatship — the very limit of suffering finally reached. The treatise then steps back, as it did after topic one, to reframe everything just covered in a single clarifying pass: five specific causes that make craving-desire arise in the first place.

Chinese Source

又即於此多修習故，永斷三界修斷我慢。由此斷故，說名無學離三界欲，上下貪斷，已得安隱。一切苦因皆捨離故，證得一切眾苦邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

And by extensively cultivating this same signless concentration, one permanently severs the pride belonging to the path of cultivation across all three realms. By this severing, one is called "beyond training," free of desire for all three realms, both lower and higher craving cut off, having attained genuine peace. Because every cause of suffering has been abandoned entirely, one realizes the very limit of all suffering.

Chinese Source

復次，今當略辯上所說義。謂顯貪欲由是而生，由是寂靜及彼寂靜，當知是名此中略義。云何貪欲由是而生？謂五因故。一、由淨妙想，二、由欣樂樂，三、由有情想，四、由猛盛貪，五、由隨眠有餘未盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, let what has been said above now be briefly explained. It shows how craving-desire arises, and how it is stilled, and its stillness fully realized — know this to be the brief explanation given here. How does craving-desire arise? From five causes: first, from perceiving something as pure and lovely; second, from delight taken in that perception; third, from conceiving of a being as such; fourth, from craving already intensely habituated; and fifth, from the latent tendency not yet entirely exhausted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the fifth verse's close as the whole topic's actual destination: the non-returner's continued cultivation of signless concentration finally severs pride's last latent trace belonging to the path of cultivation (the deepest, hardest layer of self-clinging, distinguished throughout this ground from the more superficial afflictions already cut at stream-entry), yielding full arhatship — "beyond training" in the technical sense that nothing further remains to be learned or practiced toward liberation itself, every cause of future suffering exhausted at the root.

He reads the five causes of craving's arising as a genuinely useful diagnostic list, moving from coarsest to subtlest: perceiving something as pure and attractive (the same "distorted perception" already named in verse two) is the initial spark; delight taken in that perception is what actually engages craving rather than letting the perception pass; conceiving of another as a desirable being specifically (rather than an object in general) sharpens the craving further; habituation built up through repeated indulgence over time intensifies it into something automatic; and, subtlest of all, the latent tendency that remains even after its active manifestation has been suppressed can still resurface given the right conditions, which is exactly why the treatise insisted earlier that severing craving's present activity is not the same as severing its root. With craving's five causes now named, the treatise turns to the mirror list: the five causes by which it is genuinely stilled.

14. Topic Three Closes: Five Causes of Stillness, and Two Depths of Freedom

Matching the five causes that make craving arise, the treatise names five practices — one for one — that still it, then distinguishes two depths of that stillness: freedom from craving's present manifestation, achieved by the first four, and permanent severance of its underlying tendency, achieved only by the fifth. With this, topic three is complete.

Chinese Source

云何欲貪生已，由是寂靜？謂五因故。一、由作意思惟不淨，二、由作意思惟於苦，三、由作意思惟無我，四、由繫念多修厭離，五、由隨眠無餘永滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, once craving-desire has arisen, is it stilled? From five causes: first, from applying the mind to reflect on impurity; second, from applying the mind to reflect on suffering; third, from applying the mind to reflect on non-self; fourth, from sustained mindfulness and extensive cultivation of revulsion; and fifth, from the latent tendency being permanently extinguished without remainder.

Chinese Source

云何寂靜？謂此寂靜略有二種。一者、現行寂靜；二者、永斷隨眠，當來不起。由前四種寂靜因緣，成初寂靜；由第五因，第二成就。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is stillness? Briefly, this stillness has two kinds: first, stillness of present manifestation; and second, the permanent severance of the latent tendency, such that it never arises again in future. The first four causes accomplish the first kind of stillness; the fifth cause alone accomplishes the second.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the five stilling causes directly onto the topic's own verses, each answering its matching cause of arising: reflecting on impurity displaces the false "pure, lovely" perception; reflecting on suffering displaces the delight taken in it; reflecting on non-self displaces the sense of a desirable being to crave; sustained mindfulness and extensive cultivation of revulsion overcomes even deeply habituated craving; and only the fifth — permanent extinction of the underlying latent tendency — reaches deep enough to prevent any future recurrence at all.

He treats the two-level distinction as the topic's real payoff, worth remembering well beyond this single topic: a practitioner can appear to have overcome craving — no active manifestation, no felt pull toward the object — while the deeper latent tendency remains fully intact, capable of resurfacing the moment conditions align and mindfulness lapses. Only genuine severance of that latent tendency, achieved through the deep wisdom already traced across this topic's five verses, secures what the treatise calls "decisive excellence" (the same term met in topic one) rather than a merely temporary, reversible calm. With craving-desire's causes, remedies, and the two depths of its stillness fully mapped, topic three is complete, and the treatise turns to its fourth topic: the raging flood that sweeps ordinary beings through saṃsāra.

15. Topic Four Opens: Crossing the Raging Flood

The fourth topic takes up a new image for the same underlying problem: the "flood" — a set of afflictions so overwhelming that ordinary beings are simply swept along by it. A deity asks the Buddha directly how a monastic actually crosses six such floods, and the Buddha answers in three parts, addressing the five that bind a being to the sense-desire realm, the sixth that operates through the mind alone, and the specific method for a practitioner already advanced but not yet fully free.

Chinese Source

云何苾芻多所住，越五暴流當度六？云何定者能度廣，欲愛而未得腰舟？

Ru-Yi Translation

Abiding in what, does a monastic cross the five raging floods, and go on to cross the sixth? How does one who has attained concentration, having already crossed the vast flood of craving, still lack the "waist-float" that would carry them the rest of the way?

Chinese Source

身輕安心善解脫，無作繫念不傾動。了法修習無尋定，憤愛惛沈過解脫。如是苾芻多所住，越五暴流當度六；如是定者能度廣，欲愛而未得腰舟。

Ru-Yi Translation

With body at ease and mind well liberated, mindfulness unmoving, engaged in no contrivance; understanding the Dharma, cultivating concentration free of examination, one passes beyond rage, craving, and torpor to liberation. Abiding thus, a monastic crosses the five raging floods, and goes on to cross the sixth; thus does one who has attained concentration cross the vast flood, though craving and the "waist-float" are not yet attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "five raging floods" with the five lower fetters already met in the previous topic — craving, aversion, self-view, attachment to wrong precepts, and doubt, all binding a being specifically to the sense-desire realm — and the "sixth" with the mental afflictions belonging to the five higher fetters (craving for form and formless existence, restlessness, pride, and ignorance), crossed only through the mind's own deepest concentration. He reads the deity's third question as pointedly specific: a practitioner may already be a non-returner, having crossed the vast flood of desire itself, yet still lack the "waist-float" — a vivid image for the final buoyancy that alone completes the crossing — because the higher fetters, subtler and mental rather than sensory, remain.

The Buddha's answer, he notes, proceeds in the same three-part structure as the question: bodily ease and a mind genuinely liberated (through the four foundations of mindfulness, already covered at length in topic one) crosses the first five; unmoving mindfulness engaged in "no contrivance" — resting in signless concentration without effortful construction, the same attainment closing topic three — crosses the sixth; and the specific technique for a non-returner still short of arhatship is spelled out directly: first understand the four truths, then cultivate concentration free of examination and analysis (progressively subtler stages of absorption, beginning by clearing the eight kinds of distracting thought already named in earlier fascicles — desire, ill will, harm, and thoughts of family, homeland, immortality, contempt, and status — and deepening from the access concentration bordering the first absorption into the second absorption and beyond), through which rage, craving, and torpor are finally surpassed. With the flood named and the method for crossing it given in outline, the treatise turns to unpacking each element of the Buddha's answer in full.
