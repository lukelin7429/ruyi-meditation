Fascicle 41 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十一 · The Minor Bodhisattva Precepts Begin

The Forty-Three Minor Precepts

Continuing directly from the four gravest root violations already covered, the treatise now turns to discipline's own comprehensive detail: forty-three specific minor precepts, thirty-two supporting the six perfections and eleven supporting the four means of embrace — beginning with six honest ways to check oneself for violation, and the very first precept itself: never letting a single day pass without genuine reverence for the three jewels.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 41 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle Forty-One Opens — The Structure of the Forty-Three Minor Precepts

Directly continuing from the four root violations already covered, the treatise now details the comprehensive body of minor precepts supporting the remaining two storehouses of discipline: thirty-two precepts under gathering wholesome practice, organized around progress through each of the six perfections in turn, and eleven precepts under benefiting beings, organized around the four means of embrace — together with six honest, practical ways a bodhisattva can check whether any given lapse genuinely counts as a violation at all.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於有違犯及無違犯，是染非染、軟中上品，應當了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts should clearly understand, regarding any given matter, whether it constitutes a violation or not, whether it is tainted or untainted by affliction, and whether it falls into a mild, moderate, or severe degree.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the overall structure: the thirty-two precepts under gathering wholesome practice divide into six clusters matching each perfection in turn — seven concerning giving, seven concerning discipline, five concerning patience, two concerning diligence, three concerning meditative concentration, and eight concerning wisdom; while the eleven precepts under benefiting beings divide around the four means of embrace, addressing companionship, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and shared work in turn. He notes traditional enumerations of this same material vary slightly between forty-one, forty-two, and forty-three total precepts depending purely on how certain closely related items are grouped or split, with no actual difference in content.

He then lays out six honest, practical ways a bodhisattva can examine whether a given lapse genuinely constitutes a fault: a contemptuous mind, disrespecting some aspect of the teaching without ever genuinely trying to understand it; a lazy mind, neglecting to do what should be done through simple laziness; a mind covered by affliction, one of the five hindrances or a related affliction currently clouding judgment; a weary mind, genuinely worn out after sustained, vigorous effort; a mind affected by illness, including genuine mental illness; and a mind obstructed by excessive socializing, drawn into idle conversation that serves no one's genuine benefit. In each case, he notes, the treatise's own consistent principle applies: if the underlying state is recognized and not indulged or accepted as acceptable, there is no fault; only continuing to tolerate and indulge the underlying laxity, rather than correcting it, turns an understandable lapse into a genuine violation. This closes the overview of the whole system; the very first of the forty-three precepts, requiring daily reverence for the three jewels, follows next.

2. Milestone: Precept One — Never a Day Without Genuine Reverence for the Three Jewels

The first minor precept requires a bodhisattva to show genuine reverence toward the Buddha, the teaching, and the noble community every single day — through offerings ranging from abundant to modest, but at an absolute minimum through a single bow, a single four-line verse of praise, or, at the very least, a single moment of pure, remembering faith — since letting an entire day pass with no reverence shown at all constitutes a genuine violation of this foundational precept.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於日日中，若於如來、或為如來造制多所；若於正法、或為正法造經卷所；若於僧伽，謂十方界已入大地諸菩薩眾。若不以其或少、或多諸供養具而為供養，下至以身一拜禮敬，下至以語一四句頌讚佛法僧真實功德，下至以心一清淨信隨念三寶真實功德。空度日夜，是名有犯，有所違越。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, every single day, toward the Tathāgata or a monument built for the Tathāgata, toward the true teaching or a scripture recording it, and toward the noble community — the bodhisattvas already established on the noble grounds across the ten directions — should make some offering, whether modest or abundant; at the very least, a single bow of respect, a single four-line verse praising the genuine qualities of the Buddha, the teaching, and the community, or, at the least, a single moment of pure faith calling those genuine qualities to mind. Letting an entire day and night pass with none of this at all constitutes a violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses this precept sets an extraordinarily low floor precisely so that no genuine excuse — busyness, poverty, distance from a temple — can ever justify complete neglect: even a single bow, a single remembered verse, or a single moment of sincere, believing recollection satisfies it entirely. He explains genuine reverence properly extends to the entire community of practitioners on the bodhisattva path, not merely those already fully realized — since every ordinary monastic is, in truth, a future buddha still in training, just as every being without exception carries the same potential, dismissing reverence for the imperfect while reserving it only for the already-accomplished misses the point of genuine devotion entirely.

He cites the classical verse from a foundational treatise as a model worth memorizing: praising the Buddha's own attainment of complete liberation, the teaching's own fearless, undefiled realization now handed down, and the genuine disciples of the Great Vehicle who faithfully transmit it — noting the verse deliberately honors all three jewels together in a single, brief recitation exactly of the kind this precept requires as a bare minimum. He explains that whether this daily lapse counts as a tainted violation, carrying real affliction, or an untainted one depends entirely on the underlying cause: disrespect or laziness taints it with genuine affliction, while a single, honest lapse of mindfulness amid otherwise sincere practice does not. This closes the precept's basic requirement; the specific circumstances under which no fault at all is incurred, including a striking account of how advanced bodhisattvas naturally never lapse in this way, follow next.

3. Milestone: When There Is No Violation — Genuine Illness, and an Advanced Bodhisattva's Natural, Unbroken Reverence

Two circumstances incur no fault at all under this precept: genuine mental illness or derangement, since a mind not functioning normally cannot meaningfully be said to violate anything at all; and, at the opposite extreme, a bodhisattva who has already reached the first ground and beyond, whose settled realization and enduring aspiration mean reverence for the three jewels has become entirely spontaneous and unbroken, requiring no deliberate effort to sustain at all.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂心狂亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one whose mind is genuinely deranged.

Chinese Source

若已證入淨意樂地，常無違犯。由得清淨意樂菩薩，譬如已得證淨苾芻，恆時法爾於佛法僧以勝供具承事供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a bodhisattva have already entered the ground of purified aspiration, there is never any violation at all — a bodhisattva of such purified aspiration, exactly as a monastic who has attained genuine, verified confidence, naturally and spontaneously serves and reveres the Buddha, the teaching, and the community with the finest offerings at every moment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a bodhisattva who has reached the first ground, the ground of great joy, actually beholds buddhas directly through two conditions: a genuinely settled faith, confident enough to trust that buddhas across the ten directions truly exist even without having personally verified it, and a genuine, resolute vow to actually behold one — citing the model of the fourth-century teacher Huiyuan, who, through sustained devotional practice and firm aspiration, reportedly beheld Amitābha directly in meditation on several occasions before his own death, a vivid illustration of how faith and resolve together can bring what might otherwise seem merely aspirational into genuinely lived experience.

He notes such a bodhisattva has attained what the tradition calls the four confirmed certainties regarding the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and discipline itself — a settled confidence so thoroughly rooted that it persists unshaken across every future rebirth without exception, meaning reverence for the three jewels no longer requires deliberate practice at all, having become as natural and spontaneous as breathing. This closes the two circumstances under which this first precept incurs no fault; the second precept, addressing excessive craving for others' offerings, opens next.

4. Milestone: Precept Two Closes — Struggling Against Deep-Rooted Craving Is Not Yet a Fault

The second precept addresses craving others' offerings and never feeling satisfied, incurring no fault at all when a beginning practitioner is genuinely trying to overcome that craving through sustained, sincere effort — even if the same innate, habitual craving, carried over from countless past lives, still occasionally breaks through despite real effort — since the treatise recognizes such deep-rooted tendencies cannot always be immediately mastered, however genuine the underlying wish to overcome them.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂為斷彼，生起樂欲，發勤精進攝彼對治。雖勤遮遏，而為猛利性惑所蔽，數起現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who, wishing to overcome this craving, diligently applies themselves to its antidote — even if, despite this sincere effort, the intense, innate affliction still repeatedly breaks through and manifests.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "innate affliction" as habitual tendency carried forward from countless past lives rather than a fault newly created in this one — some practitioners simply carry a stronger seed of craving or aversion than others, entirely apart from anything done wrong in this present life. He notes this leniency applies specifically to a beginning practitioner still building genuine strength against long-entrenched habit — someone who has practiced for decades and still shows no real progress against the same affliction cannot claim quite the same excuse. This closes the second precept; the third, addressing genuine courtesy toward respected elders and fellow practitioners, follows next.

5. Milestone: Precept Three — Welcoming Elders and Fellow Practitioners With Genuine Courtesy

The third precept requires genuine courtesy toward respected elders and fellow practitioners on the path — rising to greet them, offering the better seat, and engaging warmly in conversation — a fault, and a tainted one, when neglected out of arrogance or resentment, but only an untainted fault when neglected merely through laziness, forgetfulness, or simple unfamiliarity with proper etiquette; a long list of genuine circumstances, from illness to actively protecting a teacher's own concentration or the wider community's peace, incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，見諸耆長有德可敬同法者來，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不起承迎，不推勝座。……是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, seeing a respected elder or genuinely admirable fellow practitioner arrive, who, overcome by arrogance and harboring resentment or irritation, do not rise to greet them or offer them the better seat ... this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

非憍慢制，無嫌恨心、無恚惱心，但由懶惰、懈怠、忘念、無記之心，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should this arise not from arrogance or resentment, but simply from laziness, forgetfulness, or genuine unfamiliarity with what to do, this is an untainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes genuine social awkwardness or an upbringing that simply never taught proper hospitality from actual disrespect — someone who never learned how to welcome a guest is not thereby holding contempt for that guest, and the fault, while real, carries no genuine affliction behind it. He lists the treatise's own extensive no-fault circumstances: genuine illness or mental confusion; being asleep while a visitor mistakenly believes otherwise; being actively engaged in teaching or hearing the dharma oneself; deliberately withholding a greeting in order to protect a teacher's own concentration while listening, or to protect a whole community's shared quiet; using silence itself as a skillful means of correcting someone who needs it; and observing a monastic community's own formal discipline toward someone under sanction. This closes courtesy toward visitors already present; accepting genuine invitations extended by others follows next.

6. Milestone: Precept Four — Accepting Genuine Invitations

The fourth precept requires accepting a sincere invitation to receive support — refusing out of arrogance or resentment denies the host their own chance to build merit and constitutes a tainted violation, while refusing merely from laziness is untainted — with a substantial list of genuine circumstances, from illness and danger on the road to actively protecting one's own meditative practice or shielding others from a host acting in bad faith, incurring no fault whatsoever.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他來延請，或往居家、或往餘寺，奉施飲食及衣服等諸資生具，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不至其所，不受所請，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, invited to a home or another temple to receive food, clothing, or other necessities, who, overcome by arrogance and harboring resentment, refuse to go or accept the invitation — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，或有疾病；或無氣力；或心狂亂；或處懸遠；或道有怖；或欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；或餘先請；或為無間修諸善法，欲護善品令無暫廢……或復知彼懷損惱心，詐來延請；或為護他多嫌恨心；或護僧制；不至其所，不受所請，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who is ill, without strength, mentally deranged, invited somewhere too distant, facing genuine danger on the road, declining in order to skillfully correct the host instead, already committed elsewhere, protecting an unbroken course of meditative practice ... aware the invitation is offered in bad faith, protecting others from resentment, or observing the community's own formal discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes an invitation extended purely to build the host's own merit from one extended specifically to hear teaching in pursuit of genuine liberation — refusing either without real cause denies the host precisely what they sought by inviting a bodhisattva in the first place. He walks through the treatise's own extensive list of legitimate reasons for declining: genuine illness or exhaustion, an unreasonably distant location, real danger along the way, a prior commitment already made, an unbroken retreat or course of study a bodhisattva is unwilling to interrupt, a first direct breakthrough toward genuine realization still being consolidated, sensing the invitation is offered with hidden ill intent, or protecting either an individual or the wider community from an association that would cause more harm than good. This closes accepting genuine invitations; receiving valuable gifts and offerings, where refusal out of resentment carries a striking additional weight, follows next.

7. Milestone: Precept Five — Accepting Valuable Gifts, Where Refusal Out of Resentment "Abandons the Being"

The fifth precept concerns accepting valuable offerings — refusing them out of resentment or irritation constitutes a tainted violation described in unusually strong terms, since it amounts to abandoning the very being making the offering, while refusing merely from laziness remains only an untainted lapse; a substantial list of legitimate reasons — including the risk of one's own attachment, the donor's likely future regret, or the offering's own questionable or already-spoken-for origin — incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他持種種生色可染、末尼、真珠、琉璃等寶，及持種種眾多上妙財利供具殷勤奉施，由嫌恨心、或恚惱心，違拒不受，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯，捨有情故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, offered fine jewels or abundant, excellent material support with genuine sincerity, who refuse it out of resentment or irritation — this constitutes a tainted violation, since it amounts to abandoning the very being making the offering.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，或心狂亂；或觀受已心生染著；或觀後時彼定追悔；或復知彼於施迷亂；或知施主隨捨隨受，由是因緣定當貧匱；或知此物是僧伽物、窣堵波物；或知此物劫盜他得；或知此物由是因緣多生過患，或殺、或縛、或罰、或黜、或嫌、或責；違拒不受，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who is mentally deranged, who foresees genuine attachment would arise from accepting it, who foresees the giver would surely come to regret it, who recognizes the giver is confused about the nature of giving, who knows accepting would leave the giver genuinely impoverished, who recognizes the object belongs to the community or a monument, who knows it was obtained through theft, or who foresees accepting it would lead to real harm — being killed, bound, punished, removed from office, resented, or blamed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the striking phrase "abandoning the being" as underscoring how personal a refusal driven by resentment actually is — declining a gift for practical reasons is one thing, but declining it specifically to spite the giver rejects the person behind the gift, not merely the object itself. He walks through the treatise's own detailed exceptions: recognizing that accepting a particular object would itself feed one's own attachment; foreseeing that the giver, perhaps acting impulsively, would likely regret the gift once given; recognizing the giver does not actually understand what genuine giving even means; knowing acceptance would leave the giver in genuine hardship; recognizing the object already belongs to the wider community or a sacred monument rather than to the giver personally; knowing it was stolen; or foreseeing that accepting it would expose the giver to real danger, whether violence, punishment, or social consequence. This closes accepting valuable gifts; sharing the teaching with genuine seekers, a precept already anticipated at length in the earlier chapter on giving, follows next.

8. Milestone: Precept Six — Sharing the Teaching With Genuine Seekers

The sixth precept requires sharing the teaching itself, whether spoken instruction or written scripture, with anyone genuinely seeking it — refusing out of resentment or jealousy toward a seeker's own future understanding constitutes a tainted violation, while refusing merely from laziness remains untainted — and, echoing the giving chapter's own extensive treatment, a substantial list of genuine circumstances, from a hostile questioner seeking only to find fault to a seeker likely to develop wrong view from teaching too advanced for them, incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他來求法，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，嫉妒變異，不施其法，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, approached by a genuine seeker of the teaching, who, out of resentment, irritation, or jealousy, refuse to give it — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂諸外道伺求過短；或有重病；或心狂亂；或欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；或於是法未善通利；或復見彼不生恭敬，無有羞愧，以惡威儀而來聽受；或復知彼是鈍根性，於廣法教得法究竟深生怖畏，當生邪見，增長邪執，衰損惱壞；或復知彼，法至其手轉布非人；而不施與，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation toward a hostile outsider seeking only to find fault; one who is genuinely ill or mentally deranged; one being deliberately corrected through this withholding; a teaching not yet genuinely mastered oneself; a seeker showing no respect or shame in their manner; one whose limited capacity would only give rise to wrong view and worsening harm from teaching too advanced for them; or one likely to pass the scripture on to unworthy hands.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes jealousy here specifically concerns withholding a written text out of fear the seeker's own resulting wisdom might eclipse one's own standing, while a more general shift in disposition covers simply losing the will to keep teaching consistently once some private irritation has set in — both are genuine, tainted faults, since both put self-interest ahead of a seeker's genuine benefit. He recalls the no-fault list as already familiar from the earlier chapter on giving: an outsider seeking only to win an argument, genuine incapacity to teach a subject not yet mastered, a seeker's own visible disrespect, and, most delicately, a seeker whose limited capacity means teaching that is technically correct could still do them genuine harm by provoking confusion or wrong view rather than insight — withholding in such cases is compassion, not stinginess. This closes sharing the teaching; the remaining precept of this cluster follows in the treatise's own account still ahead.

9. Milestone: Precept Seven Closes — Never Abandoning Even a Violent Wrongdoer

The seventh precept closes this cluster by requiring compassion even toward violently precept-breaking beings — abandoning such a being out of resentment constitutes a tainted violation, since the treatise insists such beings deserve, if anything, even greater compassion than those already practicing well, exactly as a mother's deepest concern falls on whichever child is struggling most rather than the one already thriving.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於諸暴惡犯戒有情，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，由彼暴惡犯戒為緣，方便棄捨，不作饒益，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, harboring resentment or irritation toward a violently precept-breaking being, deliberately abandon them and refuse to help — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

何以故？非諸菩薩於淨持戒、身語意業寂靜現行諸有情所，起憐愍心，欲作饒益；如於暴惡犯戒有情，於諸苦因而現轉者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because a bodhisattva's compassion toward beings of already calm, disciplined conduct is not meant to exceed their compassion toward violently precept-breaking beings actively creating the very causes of their own future suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the treatise's own striking logic here: a being already practicing calmly and well needs relatively little additional concern, while a being actively generating the causes of future suffering through violent, undisciplined conduct needs it most urgently — a bodhisattva who reserves compassion only for those already doing well has the priority exactly backward. He compares this to a mother's own instinctive concern falling hardest on whichever child is struggling or unwell, not because the healthy child matters less, but because urgent need itself calls for urgent compassion. The no-fault exceptions here largely echo those already described — genuine mental illness, deliberately withholding help as a skillful means of correction, protecting a wider community's peace, and observing formal monastic discipline. This closes the entire first cluster of seven giving-related precepts; the second cluster, concerning discipline itself, opens next.

10. Milestone: Discipline-Progression Opens — Observing the Restrictive Precepts Shared With Monastics

The eighth precept opens a new cluster of seven concerning discipline itself, requiring a bodhisattva to observe the same restrictive precepts the Buddha established for monastics — governing proper conduct and etiquette rather than genuinely wrong acts in themselves — precisely because even monastics focused primarily on their own liberation never abandon protecting others' growing faith through visibly disciplined conduct, so a bodhisattva, whose entire priority is benefiting others, should uphold these same restrictive precepts all the more diligently, without exception.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，如薄伽梵於別解脫毗奈耶中，將護他故，建立遮罪，制諸聲聞令不造作。諸有情類，未淨信者令生淨信，已淨信者令倍增長。於中菩薩與諸聲聞應等修學，無有差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts should observe exactly as the Blessed One established within the individually liberating monastic code — restrictive precepts established in order to protect others, so that those without faith come to have it, and those with faith have it deepened further. In this matter, bodhisattvas should train exactly as monastics do, without any difference.

Chinese Source

何以故？以諸聲聞自利為勝，尚不棄捨將護他行，為令有情未信者信、信者增長，學所學處，何況菩薩利他為勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because monastics, who prioritize their own liberation, still do not abandon protecting others through their own visible conduct, training in these precepts so that faith arises and deepens in those who witness them — how much more so, then, should bodhisattvas, who prioritize benefiting others above all, uphold this same practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the six classical qualities that earn a buddha the title "Blessed One" — genuine mastery free of affliction, wisdom blazing like fire that burns away every obscuration, physical majesty adorned with the classical excellent marks, a name and reputation known throughout every world, an auspicious presence everyone delights to approach, and complete, tireless virtue benefiting all beings — and distinguishes restrictive precepts, governing outward conduct and etiquette, from precepts against genuinely wrong acts, noting these restrictive precepts exist purely to inspire confidence in onlookers through visibly disciplined behavior, quite apart from whether the underlying act would otherwise be considered wrong in itself.

He outlines the four components traditionally used to organize the full monastic code — formally receiving ordination itself, then undertaking to observe every specific precept received, then observing proper decorum and appropriate conduct in daily life so as to protect others' confidence, and finally holding even the smallest fault in genuine dread, quick to confess and correct it — and stresses a bodhisattva trains in the third of these, proper decorum protecting others' confidence, in exactly the same way a monastic does, since the underlying logic applies with even greater force to someone whose entire purpose is benefiting beings. This closes the precept requiring shared observance of restrictive conduct; the very next precept, strikingly, marks a point where bodhisattvas and monastics diverge entirely.

11. Milestone: Precept Nine — Why a Bodhisattva May Accumulate What a Monk Must Renounce

Unlike the shared precept just described, this next one marks a genuine, deliberate divergence: while monastics cultivate few possessions, few undertakings, and minimal desire through the classical twelve ascetic practices, a bodhisattva, precisely because their purpose is drawing beings toward the teaching rather than personal renunciation alone, may rightly accumulate abundant clothing, alms bowls, fine silk bedding, and even vast quantities of gold and silver — not for private comfort, but as the very means of generosity that first attracts beings before ever leading them toward wisdom.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，如薄伽梵於別解脫毗奈耶中，為令聲聞少事、少業、少希望住，建立遮罪，制諸聲聞令不造作。於中菩薩與諸聲聞不應等學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, unlike monastics, are not bound by the restrictive precepts the Blessed One established within the monastic code specifically to cultivate few possessions, few undertakings, and minimal desire. In this matter, bodhisattvas should not train exactly as monastics do.

Chinese Source

何以故？以諸聲聞自利為勝，不顧利他……如是菩薩為利他故，從非親里長者、居士、婆羅門等及恣施家，應求百千種種衣服……生色可染百千俱胝，復過是數亦應取積。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because monastics prioritize their own liberation, not the benefit of others ... whereas bodhisattvas, for the sake of benefiting others, may rightly request hundreds and thousands of garments even from unrelated donors and generous households ... and may accumulate even vast quantities of gold and silver beyond that, and rightly gather it all in.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes the classical twelve ascetic practices this precept exempts a bodhisattva from — four concerning food, such as begging door to door without preference and never eating after midday; two concerning clothing, wearing only patchwork robes and never more than the three basic garments; and six concerning dwelling, living in genuine solitude, moving beneath a different tree every few days to avoid even subtle attachment to a favorite spot, and sitting upright without lying down — practices whose entire purpose is uprooting craving for material comfort in a practitioner focused solely on personal liberation.

He explains a bodhisattva's own exemption rests on an entirely different logic: generosity is itself one of the six perfections, and a bodhisattva unable to gather abundant resources cannot actually practice generosity on the scale their vow requires, nor draw beings toward the teaching through the classical principle of first meeting a genuine worldly need before ever introducing something more profound. This closes the precept marking this genuine divergence; the practical consequence of holding such abundance responsibly, distinguished through four classical ways of actually using what is given, follows next.

12. Milestone: Four Ways of Using What Is Given — Thief, Debtor, Heir, or Master

Whether accumulating abundant resources like a bodhisattva or living simply like a monastic, anyone receiving material support falls into one of four classical categories: using it like a thief, having no genuine merit to warrant it at all; using it like a debtor, technically entitled to it yet consumed without the reflection that would make it genuinely wholesome; using it like an heir, the way an advanced practitioner already assured of eventual buddhahood rightly receives what is, in a sense, already their own inheritance; or using it like a true master, the way a fully realized arhat or buddha receives anything at all with complete equanimity, entirely untouched by craving whether the object is fine or humble.

Chinese Source

盜賊受用……欠債受用……繼承者的受用……主人受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Using support like a thief ... using it like a debtor ... using it like an heir ... using it like a true master.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains thief-like use as consuming what generous donors offer while possessing no genuine virtue at all to warrant it — a serious matter, since accepting support given in good faith without any real practice behind it quietly incurs a debt never actually earned. Debtor-like use describes someone genuinely entitled to receive support through their monastic or bodhisattva standing, yet consuming it without the reflection that would make the consumption genuinely wholesome — the treatise recommends two specific contemplations here: first, analyzing whatever is received, whether food, clothing, or shelter, as merely a temporary composite of basic elements and sensory qualities, with no fixed, independent nature of its own; and second, contemplating it as genuinely impure, since even a fine robe becomes something to release attachment toward once its function is properly understood, precisely to prevent craving from quietly attaching itself to the gift.

Heir-like use he describes as belonging to practitioners already advanced enough — a bodhisattva established on the first ground and beyond, or, more broadly, any of the seven kinds of noble disciple already assured of eventual complete liberation — that receiving support is less like being given something new and more like an heir finally receiving what was, in a real sense, always destined to become theirs. And master-like use belongs only to a fully realized arhat or buddha, whose complete freedom from craving means they receive anything at all, fine or humble, with the same untroubled equanimity, genuinely free rather than merely restrained. He notes the treatise's own practical advice for ordinary practitioners in between: formal practices like consecrating money as impure before use, or reflecting on the five contemplations before a meal, exist precisely to help maintain this same mindful awareness even before genuine mastery has been reached. This closes the four ways of using what is given; the treatise's own most philosophically significant teaching on discipline — when genuine compassion may override an ordinarily wrong act entirely — opens next.

13. Milestone: When Compassion Overrides an Ordinary Precept — The Classical Doctrine of Skillful Exception

The treatise now introduces one of its most consequential and carefully qualified teachings: a bodhisattva who, through genuinely skillful judgment and purely out of compassion for beings, occasionally and only in small degree performs what would ordinarily constitute one of the classically wrong deeds, incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts whatsoever and instead generates substantial merit — since what actually completes a genuinely wrong deed requires five factors together, including affliction itself, and a bodhisattva's own act, however similar in outward form, lacks that affliction entirely, motivated purely by love and compassion for the being involved.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，善權方便，為利他故，於諸性罪少分現行，由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, through skillful judgment and purely for the sake of benefiting others, occasionally and only in small degree perform what would ordinarily constitute one of the classically wrong deeds — through this very cause, they incur no violation of the bodhisattva precepts at all, and instead generate substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that any of the classically wrong deeds only fully completes as genuine unwholesome karma when five factors are all present together: a genuine object the deed is directed toward; a matching perception, correctly identifying that object as intended; genuine willing desire to carry out the act, not mere coercion by another; real affliction — craving, aversion, or delusion — driving it; and an actual, completed action carrying the intention through to its conclusion. A bodhisattva's own rare exercise of this exception may well involve a genuine object, a matching perception, and even a deliberate, completed action, yet crucially lacks the fourth factor entirely — no affliction whatsoever drives it, only genuine love and compassion for the being involved — which is precisely why it does not complete as the same kind of unwholesome deed at all, however similar its outward form.

He surveys three classical scholarly positions on exactly who may rightly invoke this exception: one view restricts it strictly to bodhisattvas who have already reached the first ground and directly realized emptiness, since only such settled realization can guarantee genuinely selfless motivation; a second view extends it to any bodhisattva who has genuinely realized the emptiness of a fixed self, even prior to the first ground, since it is precisely the absence of self-interest that makes the exception valid; and a third, more cautious position holds it may extend even to an ordinary, still-unrealized bodhisattva acting from genuine humility and compassion in a truly unavoidable circumstance — but only ever as a response forced by circumstance never actively sought out, approached with real trepidation rather than casual confidence, and never treated as an accomplishment to be proud of. This closes the general principle and its careful qualifications; the treatise's own specific account of this exception applied to killing — illustrated through the classical story of a single life taken to save five hundred — opens next.

14. Milestone: The Exception Applied to Killing — Better That I Go to Hell Than He

The treatise's first and most famous application of the skillful-exception doctrine: a bodhisattva who sees a bandit about to murder many beings, harm a genuinely accomplished practitioner, or commit one of the five gravest possible deeds — the kind that ripens into rebirth in the uninterrupted hell — may, out of pure compassion and after careful reflection, kill that person first rather than let the deed complete, reasoning that a single lifetime of suffering the killer's own karmic consequence is a far smaller harm than the immeasurable suffering that beings' completed crime would bring upon them.

Chinese Source

謂如菩薩見劫盜賊，為貪財故，欲殺多生，或復欲害大德聲聞、獨覺、菩薩，或復欲造多無間業。見是事已，發心思惟：我若斷彼惡眾生命，墮那落迦，如其不斷，無間業成，當受大苦；我寧殺彼墮那落迦，終不令其受無間苦。如是菩薩意樂思惟，於彼眾生或以善心、或無記心，知此事已，為當來故深生慚愧，以憐愍心而斷彼命。由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose a bodhisattva sees a bandit who, driven by greed for wealth, intends to kill many beings, or intends to harm an accomplished monastic, solitary realizer, or bodhisattva, or intends to commit one of the deeds that ripen into the uninterrupted hell. Seeing this, the bodhisattva reflects: "If I take this evil being's life, I myself will fall into hell. But if I do not, his uninterrupted-hell-ripening deed will be completed, and he will suffer greatly for it. I would rather kill him and fall into hell myself than let him suffer the uninterrupted torment." Reflecting with this resolve, and knowing the matter clearly through a wholesome or karmically neutral mind, the bodhisattva — feeling deep and genuine shame at what is to come, and moved purely by compassion — takes that being's life. Through this very cause, the bodhisattva incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and instead generates substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi devotes real care to what counts as "the deeds ripening into the uninterrupted hell": traditionally five direct acts — killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing an arhat, causing a schism in the harmonious monastic community, or drawing a buddha's blood — plus a wider category of deeds "of the same class," such as slandering the true dharma or violating a nun who has attained the fruits of the path, all of which share the same terrible character: suffering with literally no interruption in time, no interruption in space (the hell is always full, however many beings enter), no interruption in the intensity of pain, no distinction of who suffers it, and no interruption across countless cycles of dying and being instantly reborn to suffer again. Weighing the killer's single, bounded lifetime of consequence against a victim's incalculably longer torment is precisely what moves the bodhisattva to act. He also notes the *Yogācārabhūmi Commentary*'s important restriction here: this exception presupposes a bodhisattva who has reached the grounds and possesses genuine mind-reading power — only such a bodhisattva can actually know, with certainty rather than mere suspicion, that the other person's murderous intention is real and otherwise unstoppable. An eighth-ground-or-above bodhisattva like Avalokiteśvara, dwelling constantly in the direct realization of reality without any effortful application, acts only from wholesome mind in such cases; bodhisattvas from the first through seventh grounds, still moving in and out of meditative absorption, may act from either wholesome or karmically neutral mind. An ordinary, pre-ground bodhisattva simply cannot invoke this exception at all, since there is no way to genuinely know another person's murderous intent with the certainty the exception requires.

Vasubandhu's commentary on the *Compendium of the Great Vehicle* frames the same passage slightly differently, and Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the authoritative gloss on what "wholesome mind or karmically neutral mind" actually means in the root text: it is precisely the mind-reading cognition itself, arising either from settled meditative concentration (called wholesome mind) or from supernormal power (called karmically neutral mind) — two descriptions of the same underlying faculty, not two different kinds of motivation. Vasubandhu adds the striking image of the bodhisattva as a skilled physician: inflicting a small, immediate pain — cutting the diseased limb — in order to spare the patient a much greater, much longer suffering. Killed with this beneficial intention, the wrongdoer incurs almost no fault upon the bodhisattva and generates abundant merit instead, precisely because the bodhisattva's own future suffering is willingly accepted on the wrongdoer's behalf; and it is this very willingness, arising from a heart of genuine benefit, that swiftly propels the bodhisattva toward unsurpassed complete awakening. This closes the killing exception; the treatise turns next to a second, related exception involving political power — removing a tyrannical ruler or minister from office.

15. Milestone: Deposing a Tyrannical Ruler or Minister

A second, closely related exception: a bodhisattva who sees a king or a powerful minister ruling with extreme cruelty, utterly without compassion for the people under their power, may — motivated purely by compassion for those being oppressed, and to whatever extent their own standing and ability allow — act to have that ruler or minister deposed or removed from office, without incurring any violation of the bodhisattva precepts at all.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見有增上增上宰官，上品暴惡，於諸有情無有慈愍，專行逼惱。菩薩見已，起憐愍心，發生利益安樂意樂，隨力所能，若廢、若黜增上等位。由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees a supremely powerful king or a supremely powerful minister acting with extreme, top-grade cruelty, utterly without compassion for any being, devoted entirely to oppressing and tormenting others. Seeing this, the bodhisattva gives rise to a heart of compassion, generates the resolve to bring benefit and ease, and, to whatever extent their own strength and ability permit, has that ruler or minister deposed or removed from their position of power. Through this very cause, the bodhisattva incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and instead generates substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the doubled character "supremely powerful, supremely powerful" (增上增上) as naming two distinct offices in one breath: the first "supremely powerful" names the king, whose authority governs every official and every commoner beneath him and so ranks highest of all; the second names a powerful minister — a prime minister or chief counselor — who ranks highest specifically among the other officials. Both share the same essential danger: whoever holds this much concentrated power, if genuinely without compassion, can inflict oppression on an entire population at once, which is precisely why the exception belongs here, directly after the killing exception, rather than being treated as an unrelated matter of politics that the śrāvaka precepts never had occasion to address.

He is careful to note exactly how this differs from ordinary anger-driven harm. Deposing the tyrant certainly resembles committing the karma of hatred — the bodhisattva is, after all, deliberately working to harm this person's position and standing. But it only resembles it: since the bodhisattva's own mind carries no actual aversion, only compassion for the ruler's countless present and future victims, and since acting resembles a violation of the great compassion that grounds the whole of the bodhisattva path without actually being one, the text can truthfully say there is no violation at all, only merit generated. He adds a plain, practical justification: a single ruler or minister governs the fate of an entire territory's worth of beings, so removing one such person from power, however aversive it looks from the outside, serves the welfare of the many — precisely the calculus a bodhisattva, unlike an ordinary person acting from private grievance, is equipped to make correctly. This closes the political exception; a third exception, concerning the forcible reclaiming of stolen property, follows next.

16. Milestone: Forcibly Reclaiming What Belongs to the Three Jewels

A third exception, applied to the precept against taking what is not given: a bodhisattva who sees bandits, or corrupt stewards entrusted with monastic property, treating stolen or misappropriated goods belonging to the monastic community or to a stupa as their own private possession may, purely to spare them the long, meaningless suffering that comes from wrongfully consuming what belongs to the Three Jewels, forcibly take it back — and must then return it precisely to its rightful owner: to the community if it was the community's, to the stupa if it was the stupa's, to the individual being if it belonged to a being.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見劫盜賊奪他財物，若僧伽物、窣堵波物，取多物已執為己有，縱情受用。菩薩見已，起憐愍心，於彼有情發生利益安樂意樂，隨力所能，逼而奪取……由此因緣，所奪財寶，若僧伽物，還復僧伽；窣堵波物，還窣堵波；若有情物，還復有情。……又見眾主、或園林主，取僧伽物、窣堵波物，言是己有，縱情受用。菩薩見已，思擇彼惡，起憐愍心，勿令因此邪受用業，當受長夜無義無利，隨力所能廢其所主。菩薩如是雖不與取，而無違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees a bandit seize another's property — property belonging to the monastic community, or to a stupa — and, having taken a great deal of it, treats it as personal possession, consuming it freely. Seeing this, the bodhisattva gives rise to a heart of compassion, generates the resolve to bring that being benefit and ease, and, to whatever extent their strength permits, forcibly takes it back... Through this cause, the reclaimed wealth is returned precisely to its owner: if it belonged to the community, it is returned to the community; if to a stupa, to the stupa; if to a being, to that being. ...Suppose too the bodhisattva sees a community steward or a groundskeeper of monastic property take goods belonging to the community or a stupa, declare them personal possessions, and consume them freely. Seeing this, the bodhisattva weighs the wrongness of the act, gives rise to a heart of compassion, and — so that this wrongful consuming will not bring the steward long, meaningless suffering — to whatever extent their strength permits, removes that person from their position of trust. Though the bodhisattva has in this way taken what was not given, there is no violation, and substantial merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi points out how strikingly contemporary this exception feels. A monastic passes away without having properly arranged the disposition of monastery-owned property; lay relatives, unaware of or indifferent to its status, inherit it and simply use it as their own. If those relatives cannot be persuaded to return what genuinely belongs to the Three Jewels, a bodhisattva is permitted to reclaim it by force rather than let the family go on wrongfully consuming what was never truly theirs — the same long, meaningless suffering described throughout this section of exceptions is what such wrongful consumption is understood to bring about, and it is purely to spare the family that consequence, not out of any personal claim on the property, that the bodhisattva acts.

He also distinguishes the two kinds of dishonest custodian named in the second scene: a "community steward" (眾主) is specifically one entrusted with property belonging to the monastic community as a whole, while a "groundskeeper" or "garden-master" (園林主) is entrusted specifically with a stupa's property or grounds. Both scenarios share the identical structure and identical justification — reclaim what is misappropriated, restore it to its rightful owner, and, where the wrongdoer holds an ongoing position of trust, remove them from it so the wrong cannot continue. Master Cháng Bǎi notes a genuinely modern parallel: temples where a lay caretaker effectively controls the resident monastic's finances, or even makes demands on temple funds — the same corrective applies. This closes the third exception; a fourth, restricted specifically to lay bodhisattvas, follows next.

17. Milestone: An Exception for Lay Bodhisattvas Only — Sexual Misconduct

A fourth exception, explicitly restricted to a bodhisattva living at home rather than one who has taken ordination: if an unattached woman, belonging to no one else, has become genuinely infatuated with the bodhisattva and seeks intimacy, and the bodhisattva judges that refusing her would provoke deep resentment and much unwholesome karma while accepting would allow her to be skillfully guided toward wholesome roots, the bodhisattva may, motivated purely by compassion rather than desire, engage in the act without incurring any violation — a door the text closes immediately and explicitly for any bodhisattva who has taken ordination.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩處在居家，見有母邑現無繫屬，習婬欲法，繼心菩薩求非梵行。菩薩見已，作意思惟：勿令心恚，多生非福，若隨其欲，便得自在，方便安處令種善根，亦當令其捨不善業。住慈愍心，行非梵行。雖習如是穢染之法，而無所犯，生多功德。……出家菩薩，為護聲聞聖所教誡，令不壞滅，一切不應行非梵行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva living at home sees a woman presently belonging to no one else, given over to habitual sensual craving, who becomes infatuated with the bodhisattva and seeks intimacy. Seeing this, the bodhisattva reflects: "If I do not do as she wishes, resentment will arise in her heart and much that is unwholesome will follow; if I comply with her wish, she will find ease, and I can skillfully settle her in a way that plants wholesome roots, and eventually lead her to abandon the unwholesome altogether." Abiding in a heart of compassion, the bodhisattva engages in the act. Though this involves what is ordinarily an impure and defiling deed, there is no violation, and substantial merit is generated. ... An ordained bodhisattva, however, in order to protect the śrāvaka precepts' own sacred instruction from being undermined, must never engage in such conduct under any circumstance whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is precise about the conditions that must all be present before this exception can apply: the woman must be "presently unattached" — not already married, and not under anyone else's protection or claim — and her feeling must be genuine, habitual infatuation rather than a passing impulse. Her attachment forming specifically toward the bodhisattva, and her explicit seeking of intimacy, together set up the dilemma the bodhisattva must weigh: refusal risks real resentment and the substantial unwholesome karma that resentment toward a bodhisattva generates (just as, he notes, drawing near a bodhisattva in devotion generates immeasurable merit, so pushing a bodhisattva away in resentment generates a correspondingly immeasurable harm to oneself); compliance, by contrast, opens a door through which the bodhisattva can skillfully plant the seeds of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom in her, and in time lead her away from the unwholesome pattern altogether. "Wholesome roots" here means precisely those seeds; "abandon the unwholesome" means she eventually gives up seeking such encounters altogether, having been genuinely helped rather than merely indulged.

The explicit closing line restricting this entirely to lay bodhisattvas is, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, not incidental but essential: an ordained bodhisattva's visible conduct is itself a support for other people's faith in the monastic community as a whole, and the śrāvaka precepts' prohibition on sexual conduct exists specifically to protect that visible integrity — a protection a bodhisattva must never undermine regardless of any private compassionate calculation, however genuine. This closes the fourth exception, and with it the two exceptions concerning bodily conduct proper; the treatise turns next to four parallel exceptions concerning speech, beginning with false speech told to save lives.

18. Milestone: Lying to Save Lives

The first of four speech-related exceptions: a bodhisattva who could, through deliberate false speech, free many beings from mortal danger, imprisonment, mutilation, or similarly brutal punishment will knowingly and deliberately speak falsely to save them — even though, the text stresses, the bodhisattva would never tell such a lie to save their own life — because the bodhisattva's concern throughout is purely for what genuinely benefits others, never anything self-interested.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩為多有情解脫命難、囹圄縛難、刖手足難、劓鼻刵耳剜眼等難，雖諸菩薩為自命難，亦不正知說於妄語，然為救脫彼有情故，知而思擇，故說妄語。以要言之，菩薩唯觀有情義利，非無義利，自無染心，唯為饒益諸有情故，覆想正知而說異語。說是語時，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva, in order to free many beings from mortal danger, imprisonment, or the danger of having their hands and feet cut off, their nose or ears sliced off, or their eyes gouged out, will — though such a bodhisattva would never knowingly speak falsely even to save their own life — for the sake of rescuing those beings, knowingly and deliberately choose to speak falsely. In short, a bodhisattva considers only what genuinely benefits beings, never what lacks benefit, and, the mind itself entirely free of any self-interested motive, purely to bring beings benefit, deliberately sets aside their own true perception and knowingly speaks words other than the truth. In speaking such words, the bodhisattva incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and instead generates substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a direct line to a passage later in the treatise, in the discussion of the bodhisattva's "difficult practices": there, it says that even facing sudden mortal danger, a bodhisattva who has taken the precepts will not compromise them in the slightest, let alone violate them outright — which is precisely why this passage insists a bodhisattva would never lie merely to save their own life. The exception exists exclusively for the sake of others, never for the bodhisattva's own benefit, and this asymmetry is the entire point: a bodhisattva who lied to save themselves would be acting from ordinary self-preservation, indistinguishable from anyone else's fear; a bodhisattva who lies to save someone else is acting from the same compassionate calculation found throughout this whole section of exceptions.

He also explains the technical term the text uses for this kind of speech — "different words" (異語) rather than simply "false words." The distinction matters: it names speech that differs from the bodhisattva's own genuine perception and genuine knowledge of the facts, deliberately and knowingly set aside for a specific compassionate purpose, rather than speech born of confusion, self-deception, or any actual departure from truthful character. Master Cháng Bǎi offers a vivid recent-history example: resistance figures during the Japanese occupation who told invading soldiers deliberate falsehoods in order to protect civilians from harm — precisely the kind of "different words" this passage has in mind, and precisely the kind of act a bodhisattva may rightly perform. This closes the exception for false speech; two related exceptions concerning divisive and harsh speech, each aimed at freeing a being from harmful company or open wrongdoing, follow next.

19. Milestone: Divisive Speech to Break a Harmful Friendship, and Harsh Speech to Correct Open Wrongdoing

Two further speech exceptions, closely paired: a bodhisattva who sees beings caught in the grip of a harmful friendship — kept close by someone who will lead them toward great and meaningless future suffering — may deliberately speak words designed to drive the two apart; and a bodhisattva who sees beings behaving in ways that violate ordinary decency and worldly propriety may address them with harsh, forceful reproach, in both cases purely in order to draw the being out of an unwholesome situation and back toward what is wholesome.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見諸有情為惡朋友之所攝受，親愛不捨。菩薩見已，起憐愍心，發生利益安樂意樂，隨能隨力說離間語，令離惡友，捨相親愛……菩薩如是以饒益心說離間語，乖離他愛，無所違犯，生多功德。又如菩薩，見諸有情為行越路，非理而行，出麤惡語，猛利訶擯，方便令其出不善處安立善處。菩薩如是以饒益心，於諸有情出麤惡語，無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees beings taken in by a harmful friend, bound to that friend by affection they will not let go. Seeing this, the bodhisattva gives rise to a heart of compassion, generates the resolve to bring benefit and ease, and, to whatever extent their ability allows, speaks words designed to divide the two, freeing the being from the harmful friend and severing the mutual attachment... Speaking such divisive words with a heart of genuine benefit in this way, and thereby severing another's affection, the bodhisattva incurs no violation, and substantial merit is generated. Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees beings acting outside the bounds of proper conduct, behaving unreasonably, and addresses them with harsh, forceful reproach, skillfully drawing them out of an unwholesome place and settling them in a wholesome one. Speaking such harsh words to beings with a heart of genuine benefit in this way, the bodhisattva incurs no violation, and substantial merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "harmful friend" of the first exception specifically with non-Buddhist teachers who set themselves up as masters over their own followers purely to extract material support, honor, and personal advantage — teachers who, precisely because they care only for the present and never for their followers' future welfare, draw their students toward exactly the kind of long, meaningless suffering this whole section keeps returning to. A bodhisattva who sees a being closely attached to such a figure is permitted to speak words that deliberately create distance between them — an act that would ordinarily count as the karma of divisive speech, since it does produce a rift, but is entirely free of the malice that makes divisive speech a fault, being motivated purely by concern for where the friendship is leading.

The harsh-speech exception he treats as its direct counterpart, aimed not at a bad companion but at the wrongdoer's own conduct: someone visibly violating ordinary worldly decorum and behaving against reason may need forceful, even stinging reproach — precisely the kind of speech a bodhisattva would never otherwise use — to be shaken out of the pattern and redirected toward what is wholesome. Both exceptions share the same underlying logic already established for lying: the outward form resembles a fault (division sown, harshness spoken) but the governing motive is entirely compassionate benefit, with no ill will present at all, which is exactly why neither incurs any actual violation. This closes both exceptions; the eighth and final exception, concerning idle or frivolous talk used to build genuine rapport, follows next.

20. Milestone: Idle Talk as Skillful Rapport — The Eighth Exception Closes the Set

The eighth and final exception, and in some ways the most socially striking: a bodhisattva genuinely skilled in worldly entertainments, songs, and the ordinary gossip people love to discuss — talk of rulers, of thieves, of food, of romance, of whatever the street corner is presently absorbed in — may deliberately engage in exactly this kind of idle talk with beings drawn to it, not for personal enjoyment but purely to win their genuine trust and affection, so that once that bond is formed, the bodhisattva can skillfully guide them out of an unwholesome way of living and into a wholesome one.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見諸有情信樂倡妓、吟詠、歌諷，或有信樂王、賊、飲食、婬蕩街衢無義之論，菩薩於中皆悉善巧，於彼有情起憐愍心，發生利益安樂意樂，現前為作綺語相應種種倡妓、吟詠、歌諷、王、賊、飲食、婬衢等論，令彼有情歡喜引攝，自在隨屬，方便獎導出不善處安立善處。菩薩如是現行綺語，無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees beings who delight in performance, song, and verse, or who delight in talk of rulers, of bandits, of food and drink, of romance, or the meaningless chatter of the street corner. Genuinely skilled in all of it, the bodhisattva, moved by compassion for these beings and the resolve to bring them benefit and ease, engages right there in exactly this kind of idle talk — performance, song, verse, talk of rulers, bandits, food, and romance — so that these beings, delighted, are drawn in and freely attach themselves to the bodhisattva, who then skillfully guides and leads them out of an unwholesome way of living and into a wholesome one. Engaging in idle talk in this way, the bodhisattva incurs no violation, and substantial merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that classical commentary divides idle talk into exactly two categories, both named in this passage: playful, entertaining speech — song, dance-accompanying verse, musical composition, whatever falls under sheer amusement — and ordinary crowd conversation, the kind of talk that dominates any gathering of people: politics, crime, cuisine, drink, fashion, romance, current events, history, geography, even sailors' tales of the sea's treasures. None of it bears any real connection to the four noble truths or the path to liberation, which is precisely why, in an absolute sense, virtually all worldly conversation qualifies as idle talk — yet the bodhisattva is explicitly permitted, even expected, to be genuinely skilled in all of it.

The purpose he stresses is entirely instrumental and entirely compassionate: beings are drawn toward a bodhisattva who can speak their own language and share their own interests in a way they find genuinely delightful, and it is precisely this delight, this voluntary and easy attachment, that gives the bodhisattva an opening to eventually redirect them toward the wholesome — an opportunity that would never arise if the bodhisattva simply refused, out of misplaced propriety, to ever discuss anything but the dharma directly. This closes the full set of eight exceptions to the classically wrong deeds — four concerning bodily conduct (killing, deposing a tyrant, taking back what is not given, and, for lay bodhisattvas only, sexual misconduct) and four concerning speech (lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle talk) — together constituting one of the most distinctive features separating the bodhisattva precepts from the śrāvaka precepts: where the latter permit no exception whatsoever to these eight classically wrong deeds, the bodhisattva precepts recognize that compassion, entirely free of affliction, can transform the very same outward act into a source of merit. The treatise turns next to an entirely different kind of precept: the prohibition against wrong livelihood.

21. Milestone: Wrong Livelihood — Five Classical Forms of Dishonest Solicitation

The treatise now leaves the eight exceptions behind and returns to ordinary violation: a bodhisattva who resorts to deception, false claims, staged appearances of poverty, badgering pressure, or shaming manipulation in order to extract donations, and who does so shamelessly and refuses to give the pattern up, commits a tainted violation — dishonest livelihood the classical tradition analyzes into five distinct manipulative techniques, each aimed at the same end: getting others to give more than genuine merit would otherwise earn.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，生起詭詐虛談現相，方便研求假利求利，味邪命法無有羞恥，堅持不捨；是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，若為除遣生起樂欲，發勤精進，煩惱熾盛蔽抑其心，時時現起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who resort to deceptive posturing, false claims, staged appearances, badgering pressure, or shaming manipulation for gain, and who indulge this dishonest livelihood without any shame, holding fast to it and refusing to give it up — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who genuinely wishes to eliminate the pattern and applies diligent effort toward doing so, even if the affliction is so intense that it still, from time to time, overpowers and obscures the mind and recurs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the five classical manipulations named here, drawn from the treatise's own Śrāvaka Stage: deceptive posturing (詐威儀) — affecting an outward appearance of advanced spiritual attainment purely so that others, believing it genuine, will feel moved to offer support; false claims (邪妄語) — directly asserting attainments one does not have, such as claiming meditative absorption or realization of the path one has never actually reached; staged appearances (假現相) — deliberately wearing tattered clothing or displaying an exaggerated appearance of want, even while genuinely lacking nothing, purely to prompt others into offering fine replacements; badgering pressure (苦研逼) — refusing to leave a prospective donor's presence, wearing them down with persistent, even harsh insistence until they give simply to be rid of the pressure; and shaming manipulation (依利求利) — publicly disparaging a modest gift already received, comparing the donor unfavorably to some other, wealthier person's generosity, precisely to shame them into a larger one. All five share the same underlying structure: using dishonesty, manufactured appearances, or emotional pressure to obtain more than one's genuine merit and genuine need would otherwise draw forth.

He also brings in the parallel four-fold classification found in the *Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise* and the *Sutra of the Buddha's Bequeathed Teaching*, describing wrong livelihoods specifically forbidden to the ordained: "lowered-mouth eating," earning one's food through farming or preparing medicines; "raised-mouth eating," earning it through astrology and divination by the stars; "sideways-mouth eating," earning it by flattering the powerful and currying favor with the wealthy; and "roundabout-mouth eating," earning it through incantations and fortune-telling. An ordained person is forbidden all of these — no trade, no farming, no stargazing, no cultivating the powerful, no fortune-telling — and a bodhisattva, whether ordained or not, is likewise forbidden all of them, together with the five manipulative techniques above, whenever practiced shamelessly and without any genuine effort to stop. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that the no-fault clause exists specifically for the beginning practitioner: someone still genuinely struggling against a deeply ingrained habit, applying real and diligent effort against it, is not at fault merely because the habit — not yet mastered — still occasionally overpowers them and resurfaces despite that effort. This closes wrong livelihood; the treatise turns next to frivolous, undignified conduct.

22. Milestone: Restlessness and Frivolous Conduct — Loud Laughter, Games, and Horseplay

A bodhisattva who, stirred by restlessness, cannot settle the mind, has no taste for stillness, and instead indulges in loud laughter, noisy games, boisterous commotion, and physical horseplay purely to provoke others' amusement commits a tainted violation — though the treatise carves out a wide range of exceptions where the very same behavior, undertaken purely to skillfully connect with or console another being, is entirely blameless.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，為掉所動心不寂靜，不樂寂靜，高聲嬉戲諠譁紛聒，輕躁騰躍望他歡笑；如此諸緣，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若忘念起，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若為除遣生起樂欲，廣說如前；若欲方便解他所生嫌恨令息；若欲遣他所生愁惱；若他性好如上諸事，方便攝受，敬慎將護，隨彼而轉；若他有情猜阻菩薩，內懷嫌恨，惡謀憎背，外現歡顏表內清淨；如是一切，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, stirred by restlessness, cannot find a settled mind, take no pleasure in stillness, and instead indulge in loud laughter, noisy games, boisterous commotion, and reckless, playful horseplay purely to draw out others' laughter — all such conduct constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If it arises merely from a lapse of mindfulness, it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation: for one genuinely working to eliminate the pattern, as described earlier; for one skillfully using such behavior to dissolve another's resentment or grudge; for one seeking to relieve another's grief and distress; for one whose companion is simply inclined by nature to such things, and who joins in respectfully and carefully in order to protect the relationship and stay connected to them; and for one who, sensing that another secretly harbors suspicion, resentment, or even ill intent, nonetheless displays an outwardly cheerful face precisely to show that their own heart remains genuinely undisturbed and clear. In all such cases, there is no violation whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the underlying affliction here as restlessness (掉舉) — traditionally associated with the mind's tendency to keep circling back over past events, unable to settle — which here manifests outwardly as an aversion to genuine stillness and a taste instead for constant noisy company. The specific games named in the classical commentary, "shuanglu," "chupu," and "playing with beads," were popular board and dice games of the era — shuanglu resembling a form of backgammon played with colored pieces raced across a divided board according to dice throws, chupu a simpler game of colored-stick casting akin to modern dice, "playing with beads" a further Tang-dynasty pastime — the modern equivalent, he notes dryly, being card games or online gaming: none of it becoming someone who has taken up the aspiration for supreme awakening. The horseplay described — clasping arms, pulling at each other's hair, touching, embracing, exchanging glances, and the like — is called out specifically because it stirs the mind into precisely the kind of agitated, leaping restlessness that blocks any approach to genuine stillness.

The generous list of exceptions, however, shows this precept is not really about the outward behavior at all but about the motive behind it. A beginning bodhisattva still working against the habit is not at fault for its occasional recurrence, as established in the previous precept. More strikingly, a bodhisattva may deliberately join in exactly this kind of laughter and play as a skillful means — to dissolve someone's resentment, to comfort someone in genuine grief, or simply to stay warmly connected to a companion whose own temperament runs toward such things, joining in respectfully rather than aloofly refusing and thereby risking the relationship itself. And when someone secretly harbors suspicion or even hostility toward the bodhisattva, showing an outwardly untroubled, cheerful face — rather than visibly reflecting the tension back — is itself treated as skillful, demonstrating that the bodhisattva's own inner clarity remains genuinely undisturbed by another's ill will. This closes the precept on frivolous conduct; the treatise turns next to one of its doctrinally richest passages — the wrong view that a bodhisattva should feel aversion toward nirvana itself.

23. Milestone: The Wrong View That a Bodhisattva Should Turn Away from Nirvana

A genuinely doctrinally significant precept: a bodhisattva who holds and teaches the view that bodhisattvas should not delight in nirvana but should instead feel aversion toward it, and should not fear or work to eliminate the afflictions since three incalculable eons of continued rebirth are required to reach supreme awakening, commits a tainted violation — since, the treatise insists, bodhisattvas in fact love and draw near to nirvana even more deeply than śrāvakas do, precisely because their compassion extends to every being rather than only to themselves, and they must continue working to overcome the afflictions even while deliberately forgoing the śrāvaka's rush to realize nirvana immediately.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，起如是見，立如是論：菩薩不應忻樂涅槃，應於涅槃而生厭背；於諸煩惱及隨煩惱不應怖畏而求斷滅，不應一向心生厭離；以諸菩薩三無數劫流轉生死，求大菩提。若作此說，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。何以故？如諸聲聞於其涅槃忻樂親近，於諸煩惱及隨煩惱深心厭離；如是菩薩於大涅槃忻樂親近，於諸煩惱及隨煩惱深心厭離，其倍過彼百千俱胝。以諸聲聞唯為一身證得義利勤修正行，菩薩普為一切有情證得義利勤修正行。是故菩薩當勤修集無雜染心，於有漏事隨順而行，成就勝出諸阿羅漢無雜染法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who form and propound the view that bodhisattvas should not delight in nirvana but should feel aversion toward it, and should not fear the afflictions and seek to eliminate them, and should not turn wholly away from them, on the grounds that bodhisattvas must pass three incalculable eons wandering through birth and death in pursuit of supreme awakening — anyone who teaches this commits a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. Why is this so? Just as śrāvakas delight in and draw near to nirvana, and turn deeply away from the afflictions, so too do bodhisattvas delight in and draw near to great nirvana, and turn deeply away from the afflictions — and do so a hundred-thousand-koti times more intensely than śrāvakas. For śrāvakas diligently cultivate the true path in pursuit of benefit for one body alone, while bodhisattvas diligently cultivate the true path in pursuit of benefit for every being universally. A bodhisattva must therefore diligently cultivate a mind free of defilement, act in accordance with worldly, conditioned affairs, and thereby accomplish an undefiled quality that surpasses even that of the arhats.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a direct correction of a plausible-sounding but genuinely mistaken inference: since a bodhisattva's path famously requires three incalculable eons of continued rebirth to reach supreme awakening, one might conclude that a bodhisattva should therefore feel indifferent or even averse toward nirvana, and should stop worrying about eliminating the afflictions, since eliminating them too thoroughly would seem to risk exiting saṃsāra prematurely, before the work of universal liberation is complete. The treatise flatly rejects this. Because a bodhisattva has directly realized the emptiness of all phenomena, saṃsāra and nirvana are, for such a mind, ultimately not two separate, opposed things — unlike the śrāvaka path, which observes conditioned existence through impermanence and suffering and consequently regards saṃsāra as a burning house to be fled and nirvana as the safety to be reached, with a correspondingly urgent wish to realize it in this very lifetime. A bodhisattva, by contrast, genuinely sees nirvana's subtle and supreme excellence — loves and delights in it, in fact, more intensely than any śrāvaka — yet deliberately declines to rush toward realizing it, "enduring without directly realizing it," precisely in order to complete the accumulation of merit and wisdom on behalf of every being, not only their own.

Crucially, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, this restraint from *rushing toward* nirvana is entirely different from *not working to eliminate the afflictions* — a bodhisattva must still actively subdue and eliminate craving, aversion, delusion, pride, doubt, and every secondary affliction that follows from them, since compassionate activity carried out through an afflicted mind becomes merely "love-view great compassion" — a counterfeit compassion still tangled in self-interest, not the genuine article. What distinguishes the bodhisattva is holding both halves together at once: genuinely eliminating affliction while genuinely declining to abide fixedly in either saṃsāra or nirvana, "not dwelling in either extreme," which is precisely why the text can say a bodhisattva's undefiled achievement surpasses even the arhat's — an arhat having mastered only the single half of not being tainted by saṃsāra, while remaining unable to release the corresponding attachment to nirvana itself. This closes the precept on wrong view regarding nirvana; the treatise turns next to how a bodhisattva should respond when slandered.

24. Milestone: Failing to Address a Slander That Is Actually True

A bodhisattva whose own genuine misconduct — failing to properly keep the precepts, failing to maintain proper monastic decorum — gives rise to real, damaging rumor and reputation, and who neither corrects the underlying fault nor takes any step to guard against the harm it causes, commits a tainted violation when the accusation is in fact true; but the treatise carves out a substantial list of situations, including when the slander is simply false and not worth dignifying with a response, where no fault at all is incurred.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於自能發不信重言，所謂惡聲、惡稱、惡譽，不護不雪。其事若實而不避護，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若事不實而不清雪，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若他外道；若他憎嫉；若自出家，因行乞行、因修善行，謗聲流布；若忿弊者；若心倒者；謗聲流布，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who give rise, through their own conduct, to words that undermine others' trust and respect — harsh talk, harsh reputation, harsh renown — and who neither guard against it nor clear it up, commit, if the underlying accusation is in fact true and they still fail to guard against it, a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If the accusation is false and they simply fail to clear it up, this too constitutes a violation and a transgression, but it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation whatsoever: when the slanderer is a non-Buddhist outsider; when the slanderer is simply jealous; when the rumor arises from the bodhisattva's own ordained life of begging for alms or diligent spiritual practice, which naturally attracts misunderstanding; when the slanderer acts from sheer malice and bad character; or when the slanderer's mind is simply deranged.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the crucial distinction the passage rests on: this violation splits into two grades depending purely on whether the underlying accusation is actually true. If the bodhisattva has genuinely failed to keep the precepts properly, failed to maintain the four proper aspects of monastic decorum, and this failure is what has caused others to lose faith and speak ill of them, then failing both to correct the actual fault and to guard their own conduct going forward is a tainted violation — the fault lies not in being spoken of badly, but in doing nothing about the real problem generating the talk. If, by contrast, the accusation is simply untrue — the bodhisattva has done nothing wrong at all — then declining to clear their name is still technically a violation (since, as the classical principle "let the truth speak for itself rather than arguing" might suggest, silence in the face of falsehood can still let harm spread unchecked), but it carries no taint, since there is no real fault underlying it to begin with.

The no-fault list, he notes, covers the situations where responding would simply be pointless or beside the point: rumor from a non-Buddhist rival who starts from an entirely different framework and cannot be reasoned with on Buddhist terms; rumor born of straightforward jealousy of the bodhisattva's own genuine virtue; rumor that arises simply because ordained life — begging for food, renouncing all worldly pleasure and entertainment for the sake of practice — looks strange or suspicious to those who don't understand it, through no fault of the bodhisattva's own conduct; and rumor from someone acting purely out of malice or outright derangement, where the accusation is plainly not a genuine attempt at truthful correction at all. In every one of these cases, there is simply nothing constructive to be gained by engaging, so no fault attaches to leaving it alone. This closes the precept on slander; the treatise turns next, and closes out the discipline-progression cluster, with the precept against withholding deserved correction.

25. Milestone: Withholding Deserved Harsh Correction Out of Misplaced Kindness — Discipline-Progression Closes

The seventh and final precept of the discipline-progression cluster: a bodhisattva who sees that a being genuinely needs stern physical discipline or forceful verbal rebuke in order to receive real benefit, but withholds it purely to spare that being momentary distress, commits a non-tainted violation — unless the bodhisattva has correctly judged that, for this particular person, such correction would in fact accomplish very little good while provoking a great deal of needless resentment, in which case withholding it is entirely blameless.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，見諸有情，應以種種辛楚加行、猛利加行而得義利，護其憂惱而不現行，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。……無違犯者，觀由此緣，於現法中少得義利，多生憂惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who see that certain beings would genuinely benefit from stern bodily discipline or forceful verbal correction, but who, wishing only to spare them distress, withhold such correction and fail to act — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, though it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation when the bodhisattva judges that, in this particular case, such correction would bring about very little genuine benefit in this present life while giving rise to a great deal of needless distress and resentment instead.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two channels of correction named here: "stern accompaniment" (辛楚加行), meaning bodily discipline such as an assigned penance or physical correction, and "forceful accompaniment" (猛利加行), meaning verbal reproach and stern rebuke. A bodhisattva who sees clearly that a being needs exactly this kind of firm hand to actually change course, but holds back purely because inflicting the correction would cause the being distress in the moment, has let a shallow, sentimental kindness override the deeper kindness of actually helping — precisely the failure this precept names as a violation, though a comparatively lighter, non-tainted one, since the motive is at least a genuine if misapplied wish to spare suffering rather than any actual malice or indifference.

The no-fault clause, however, gives real weight to the bodhisattva's own judgment of the individual case. Some people, Master Cháng Bǎi observes, respond well to a firm hand and genuinely need it to break a harmful pattern; others, of a more fragile or resistant temperament, would simply be driven away entirely by the same treatment — some lay students would just stop coming back, some ordained students would disrobe altogether — gaining almost nothing from the correction while losing the relationship, and with it any future chance of being helped at all. A bodhisattva who has genuinely weighed this and judged that firm correction would do this particular person more harm than good is not at fault for choosing gentleness instead; the precept asks for wisdom in each individual case, not a blanket rule that correction must always be delivered regardless of its actual effect. This closes the seventh and final precept of the discipline-progression cluster — 戒漸次攝 — completing the second of the six perfection-based clusters within the thirty-two precepts of gathering wholesome dharmas. The treatise turns next to the third cluster: five precepts supporting the perfection of patience, beginning with how a bodhisattva should respond to another's hostility.

26. Milestone: The Patience-Progression Cluster Opens — Returning Abuse with Abuse

The third of the six perfection-based clusters within the thirty-two precepts of gathering wholesome dharmas: five precepts supporting the perfection of patience, since the very world bodhisattvas work within is traditionally named for its demand for endurance. The first and most direct: a bodhisattva who meets another's verbal abuse with abuse in return, another's anger with anger, another's blows with blows, or another's mockery with mockery, has failed at patience entirely and commits a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他罵報罵，他瞋報瞋，他打報打，他弄報弄，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, when abused, abuse in return; when met with anger, respond with anger; when struck, strike back; when mocked, mock in return — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening precept of the patience-progression cluster as deliberately blunt precisely because it names the world's most instinctive response to hostility — meeting force with equal force, tit for tat — and rules it out completely for anyone who has taken up the bodhisattva path. The world named "sahā," the world of endurance, is precisely the world where beings constantly provoke one another, and the entire cluster that follows exists because genuine patience, unlike a merely passive temperament, has to be deliberately cultivated against exactly this instinct.

He notes that aversion is, of all the afflictions, the one the bodhisattva precepts treat with the greatest seriousness, since the overwhelming majority of actual precept violations trace back to anger rather than to craving or confusion — a bodhisattva's entire training in compassion is undone the moment hostility is returned in kind, since compassion cannot coexist, even briefly, with the wish to hurt someone back. This closes the first patience precept; the treatise turns next to a bodhisattva's own failure to apologize when at fault.

27. Milestone: Refusing to Apologize — Pride Versus Mere Laziness

A bodhisattva who has genuinely wronged another, or who is simply suspected of having done so, and who — out of resentment or, more seriously, out of pride — refuses to offer a proper, sincere apology and instead dismisses the matter with contempt, commits a tainted violation; the very same failure, arising merely from laziness or carelessness rather than pride, is a lesser, non-tainted one — though the treatise also grants a substantial list of situations where withholding the apology is entirely appropriate.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於他有情有所侵犯，或自不為彼疑侵犯，由嫌嫉心，由慢所執，不如理謝而生輕捨，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若由懶惰、懈怠、放逸，不謝輕捨，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；若是外道；若彼希望要因現行非法有罪方受悔謝；若彼有情性好鬥諍，因悔謝時倍增憤怒；若復知彼為性堪忍，體無嫌恨；若必了他因謝侵犯，深生羞恥；而不悔謝，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who have genuinely wronged another being, or are simply suspected of having done so, and who, gripped by resentment or by pride, fail to apologize properly and instead dismiss the matter with contempt — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If the failure to apologize and the contemptuous dismissal arise instead merely from laziness, carelessness, or negligence, this too constitutes a violation and a transgression, but it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation: when the bodhisattva withholds the apology purely as a skillful means to guide and subdue the other, drawing them out of an unwholesome place and into a wholesome one; when the other is a non-Buddhist outsider [demanding a genuinely improper concession as the price of accepting an apology]; when the other genuinely delights in conflict, and apologizing would only double their anger; when the bodhisattva knows the other is by nature genuinely tolerant and holds no resentment at all; or when the bodhisattva is certain the other would feel deep embarrassment at being formally apologized to — in all such cases, there is no violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to separate the two grades of this fault. Pride is what makes it tainted: looking down on the other person as lowly in status, unattractive, forgetful, unpracticed, or unintelligent, and letting that contempt override the plain duty to make amends — this is, he says bluntly, simply the mountain of self-regard standing too tall to bow. Mere laziness or carelessness, by contrast, involves no such contempt at all, only ordinary negligence, and so is a genuine but lesser fault; he notes the commentary's own reminder that laziness, carelessness, and negligence are really one and the same root cause described from slightly different angles, not three separate faults.

The no-fault list, he stresses, again turns on genuine skillful judgment rather than avoidance: a bodhisattva may rightly decline to apologize if doing so is itself the better way to guide the other person toward the wholesome; if the other party is a non-Buddhist rival who would only accept an apology on the condition that the bodhisattva actually commit some further genuinely wrong or unlawful act, which the bodhisattva of course will never do; if the other person's temperament is so combative that any apology would only inflame rather than resolve the conflict; if the bodhisattva already knows the other holds no resentment whatsoever and would find a formal apology unnecessary; or if a formal apology would actually embarrass rather than comfort the other person. In every one of these cases, the underlying motive remains genuine care for the other's wellbeing, which is precisely what distinguishes them from pride-driven refusal. This closes the precept on failing to apologize; its natural counterpart, refusing to accept someone else's genuine apology, follows next.

28. Milestone: Refusing to Accept Another's Genuine Apology

The mirror image of the previous precept: a bodhisattva who has been genuinely wronged, and who is offered a sincere and proper apology, but refuses to accept it — whether out of a wish to keep hurting the other person or simply because their own temperament cannot tolerate letting the matter go — commits a violation; the classical commentaries differ, interestingly, on whether the second case, refusal from mere ill temper rather than actual malice, counts as tainted or not.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他所侵犯，彼還如法平等悔謝。懷嫌恨心，欲損惱彼，不受其謝，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。雖復於彼無嫌恨心，不欲損惱，然由稟性不能堪忍，故不受謝，亦名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, having been wronged by another, are offered a sincere and proper apology in return, yet refuse to accept it out of a lingering resentment and a wish to keep hurting the other person, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. Even where the bodhisattva harbors no actual resentment and has no wish to hurt the other person, but simply cannot, by native temperament, let the matter go, and so still refuses the apology — this too constitutes a violation and a transgression, and, according to the Taishō text, remains a tainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi flags a genuinely interesting textual divergence at exactly this point. The Taishō edition, as translated by Xuanzang, states plainly that refusing another's sincere apology is a tainted violation in both cases described — whether the refusal comes from actual malicious resentment or merely from an inability to let go rooted in one's own difficult temperament. But Dharmakṣema's earlier translation of the bodhisattva precepts renders the second case differently: where there is no resentment and no wish to hurt the other, but the refusal comes purely from an inborn inability to tolerate and forgive, Dharmakṣema's version treats this as a violation that is not tainted. Master Cháng Bǎi does not resolve the discrepancy but presents it honestly as a genuine point where the two classical translations diverge, leaving the more severe Taishō reading as the primary teaching while noting the alternative for the record.

Whichever grading applies, the underlying point holds either way: refusing a genuine apology, for whatever reason, keeps a wound open that both parties have already agreed to close, and a bodhisattva's own difficult temperament is no more an acceptable excuse for that than genuine malice would be — it simply describes a lighter, more habitual kind of failure rather than an active, deliberate one. The no-fault clause here mirrors the previous precept exactly: declining to accept an apology remains blameless when it serves as a genuine skillful means to help the other person, or when the apology offered is not actually sincere or proper to begin with. This closes the precept on refusing another's apology; the treatise turns next to harboring an unresolved grudge more generally.

29. Milestone: Harboring a Grudge — And Master Cháng Bǎi on Learning to Let Go

A bodhisattva who holds onto anger toward another being, keeping the grievance alive and refusing, over time, to release it, commits a tainted violation — since aversion held and nursed rather than actively worked through is, the treatise makes clear, one of the single greatest dangers to the bodhisattva path, and Master Cháng Bǎi's own extended reflection here turns the classical teaching toward the practical, lifelong work of genuinely letting old resentments go.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於他懷忿，相續堅持，生已不捨，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，為斷彼故生起樂欲，廣說如前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who harbor anger toward another being, holding it fast and continuing to nurse it, never releasing it once it has arisen — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who genuinely wishes to eliminate the grudge and works diligently toward doing so, as described earlier.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the technical description precisely: this is a beginning bodhisattva who keeps recalling and replaying the memory of some harm done to them, continuously and firmly reinforcing that resentful intention in the mind, never applying the four foundations of mindfulness or the calming and insight meditations that would actually dissolve it — and it is this failure to even attempt the antidote, not merely the initial arising of the feeling, that makes the violation tainted. Aversion, he stresses again, is the single greatest hazard on the bodhisattva path, since the great majority of actual precept violations trace back to it, which is exactly why a bodhisattva must work actively to strengthen compassion and dissolve resentment rather than simply tolerating its presence.

He then offers an unusually personal and extended reflection worth preserving closely to how he gave it: younger people, he observes, genuinely struggle to release the sting of conflict and unpleasantness with others — but as one grows older, gains more life experience, or faces illness and diminished capacity, something shifts. One loses some of the sheer energy needed to hold a grudge, and also loses some of the rigid certainty that a person who has once done wrong is permanently and only "bad," or that goodness and badness are fixed, unchanging labels. He gives concrete examples: someone who once scolded you may still be the one who drives you to the hospital when you fall ill; someone who once needled you with a few sharp words may still be the one who quietly helps when you genuinely need it. Living long enough in community with others, one learns that everyone can, in principle, live and practice alongside everyone else, provided each person is genuinely working to subdue their own afflictions — friction, when it happens, is simply ordinary, the same way one's own teeth sometimes bite one's own tongue; how could two separate people never, ever come into any friction at all? Seeing this, one comes to feel real affection even for difficult people, precisely because one will not be around them, or they around oneself, for very much longer — none of us knows whether tomorrow or the next breath comes first. Held closely, this reflection on life's brevity makes it far easier to set down any grievance, to let old scores soften and fade, and to practice the path together with absolutely anyone. This closes the precept on harboring a grudge; the treatise turns next, and closes the patience-progression cluster, with the precept against craving to be served and attended to by disciples.

30. Milestone: Craving to Be Served by Disciples — the Buddha's Three Kinds of Students and Cunda's Final Offering — Patience-Progression Closes

The fifth and final patience precept: a bodhisattva whose genuine merit has begun to attract followers, but who governs them from a craving, attachment-driven desire to be served and attended to rather than genuine compassion — playing favorites with the obedient, growing cold toward the disobedient, ignoring the indifferent — commits a tainted violation; the same authority exercised purely to train disciples properly, without any craving for their service, is entirely blameless.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，貪著供事增上力故，以愛染心管御徒眾，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，不貪供侍，無愛染心管御徒眾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, driven by the intensifying force of craving to be served and attended to, govern their following with a mind of attached, craving affection — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who does not crave being served or attended to, and governs the community without any mind of attached, craving affection.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a vivid contrast with how the Buddha himself related to three different kinds of disciples, offering it as the model this precept asks bodhisattvas to emulate. Toward the diligent disciple who genuinely practices as taught, the Buddha felt no special favoritism at all, since he understood through the contemplation of impermanence that even this disciple's diligence was not guaranteed to last — no telling when resentment or backsliding might arise — so there was nothing to become attached to. Toward the deeply flawed, afflicted disciple who could not manage to practice properly, the Buddha responded not with disappointment but with heightened compassion, seeing clearly that this person was simply overwhelmed by their own afflictions and did not yet know how to do otherwise. And toward the disciple who wavered — sometimes diligent, sometimes lazy — the Buddha related through the contemplation of no-fixed-characteristic, neither clinging to their good moments as proof of permanent virtue nor condemning their lapses as proof of permanent failure, since such a person's nature was simply changeable, subject to shift in either direction. Through impermanence, compassion, and the absence of fixed characteristics, the Buddha's heart remained genuinely equal toward all three kinds of student.

He then tells the well-known story of Cunda, who made the Buddha's final offering of food before the parinirvāṇa: though countless others also wished to make offerings that day, the Buddha accepted only Cunda's, prompting jealous onlookers to say to Cunda, "Look — the Buddha loves you best of anyone, refusing everyone else's offering but yours." Cunda's own reply, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is the whole teaching in miniature: "Don't say such things — the Buddha regards every being exactly as he regards his own son Rāhula; he accepted my offering today for a particular causal reason, not out of any special fondness for me." This example, he says, is precisely what every practitioner should aspire toward: relating to others is itself a discipline in patience, and even if genuine equanimity toward everyone feels far out of reach at first — most of us still plainly prefer some people to others — with enough practice and enough years, that preference can genuinely soften into something closer to the Buddha's own impartial care. He adds the practical clarification from the treatise's own sub-commentary: an ordained bodhisattva must still see to basic robes and provisions, and a lay bodhisattva to honest livelihood, and all the hardship that comes with either is simply something a bodhisattva must learn to bear — what this precept forbids is specifically taking on disciples in order to offload that hardship onto them for one's own comfort, not the ordinary, necessary work of training a genuinely new student in the basic duties of attending a teacher, which is itself an act of compassionate instruction rather than craving. This closes the fifth and final precept of the patience-progression cluster — 忍漸次攝 — completing the third of the six perfection-based clusters. The treatise turns next to the fourth cluster: two precepts supporting the perfection of diligence.

31. Milestone: The Diligence-Progression Cluster Opens — Excessive Sleep and the Five Marks of Proper Rest

The fourth cluster within the thirty-two precepts, supporting the perfection of diligence, opens with a precept on sleep: a bodhisattva who, out of laziness, indulges the pleasure of sleep — whether lying flat or reclining against a support — at the wrong time or in excessive measure, commits a tainted violation; the treatise's own commentary lays out, in careful detail, exactly what distinguishes proper, disciplined rest from this kind of indulgence.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，懶惰懈怠，耽睡眠樂、臥樂、倚樂，非時非量，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，若遭疾病；若無氣力；行路疲極，若為斷彼生起樂欲；廣說一切如前應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, out of laziness and indolence, indulge the pleasure of sleep, of lying flat, or of reclining against a support, at the wrong time or beyond proper measure — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who is genuinely ill; who lacks the physical strength to continue; who is exhausted from a long journey; or who genuinely wishes to eliminate this pattern and works diligently toward doing so — all such cases, as described in fuller detail earlier, are without violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two named pleasures precisely: "lying-flat pleasure" (臥樂) is resting on one's back, and "reclining pleasure" (倚樂) is resting propped on one side, head supported by the hand — both genuinely restful postures in themselves, and the precept is careful not to condemn rest as such, only rest indulged carelessly, at the wrong time or beyond what the body actually needs, until dense torpor overtakes the mind so completely that the body can no longer rouse itself promptly at the sound of the wake-up signal. He details what the treatise calls "the yoga of wakefulness" (悎寤瑜伽) — the disciplined framework distinguishing proper rest from mere indulgence — which requires diligent practice of calming and insight meditation through both day and night, permitting only about six hours of nighttime rest, and even that rest governed by five specific marks: maintaining mindfulness and clear awareness even while falling asleep, dwelling in the perception of light rather than pure darkness (light understood here as bodily radiance, the light of the teaching, or the ordinary light of sun, moon, and lamp), lying specifically on the right side, and holding in mind the intention to rise at a specific hour. A bodhisattva who sleeps without any of these five — without mindfulness, without clear awareness, without the perception of light, without the intention to rise, and without lying on the right side — sleeps "at the wrong time" in the sense the precept means, regardless of the literal hour on the clock; and sleeping ten or twelve hours when six to eight would genuinely suffice is what the precept means by "beyond measure."

The no-fault list, he notes, is entirely commonsense: genuine illness, genuine physical exhaustion from travel, or a beginner still working against a not-yet-overcome habit are none of them failures of diligence at all, simply the ordinary limits of an unenlightened body still in training. This closes the precept on excessive sleep; the treatise turns next, and closes the diligence-progression cluster, with idle worldly chatter that wastes a diligent bodhisattva's time.

32. Milestone: Wasting the Day in Idle Worldly Talk — Diligence-Progression Closes

The second and final precept of the diligence-progression cluster: a bodhisattva who, gripped by attached delight in it, engages in idle worldly conversation and lets the day slip away meaninglessly commits a tainted violation; the same waste of time arising merely from a lapse of mindfulness, rather than genuine attachment, is a lesser fault — and the treatise grants real room for briefly, mindfully participating in ordinary conversation when doing so genuinely serves another person.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，懷愛染心，談說世事，虛度時日，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若由忘念，虛度時日，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。……無違犯者，見他談說護彼意故安住正念須臾而聽，若事希奇，或暫問他，或答他問，無所違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, gripped by attached delight in it, engage in idle worldly conversation and let the day slip meaninglessly away — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If the wasted time arises instead merely from a lapse of mindfulness, this too is a violation and a transgression, but it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who, seeing others already engaged in such talk, and wishing to be considerate of their feelings, remains present a brief while — the length of about forty-eight minutes — maintaining mindfulness throughout while listening; nor for briefly discussing a matter that happens to be genuinely unusual or noteworthy; nor for briefly asking or answering a question others raise about worldly affairs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "worldly talk" here with the same catalogue named earlier under idle speech — discussion of rulers, of bandits, of food and drink, and every other kind of conversation carrying no genuine benefit — and notes the plain, familiar observation that this is simply what most people naturally gravitate toward talking about when gathered together, and the entire day can pass this way without anyone quite noticing where the time went. A bodhisattva who has taken up diligent practice cannot afford this particular kind of forgetting, since time itself is the one resource that, once spent, no diligence can ever recover.

What makes this precept genuinely practical rather than merely restrictive, he stresses, is its careful distinction between attachment-driven indulgence, which is tainted, and a bare lapse of mindfulness, which is not — and its explicit permission to remain briefly present in ordinary conversation out of simple consideration for the people involved, provided mindfulness itself is maintained throughout rather than abandoned. A bodhisattva need not become aloof or unsociable to keep this precept; what matters is the underlying state of mind — present and unattached, briefly engaged for another's sake, rather than lost in the pleasure of the talk itself — precisely the same distinction that runs through every precept in this section of the treatise. This closes the second and final precept of the diligence-progression cluster — 精進漸次攝 — completing the fourth of the six perfection-based clusters. The treatise turns next to the fifth cluster: three precepts supporting the perfection of meditative concentration.

33. Milestone: Refusing, Out of Pride, to Seek Meditation Instruction from a Qualified Teacher

The meditation-progression cluster opens: a bodhisattva who wishes to settle and concentrate the mind, but who, gripped by resentment or arrogance, refuses to approach a qualified teacher and request proper instruction, commits a tainted violation — a precept resting on the treatise's own careful distinction between practitioners who genuinely need a teacher's guidance to progress and those whose own settled wisdom already suffices.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，為令心住，欲定其心，心懷嫌恨，憍慢所持，不詣師所求請教授，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。懶惰懈怠而不請者，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若遇疾病；若無氣力；若知其師顛倒教授；若自多聞，自有智力能令心定；若先已得所應教授；而不請者，無所違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, wishing to settle and concentrate the mind, are nonetheless gripped by resentment or held fast by arrogance, and so refuse to approach a qualified teacher and formally request instruction — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. Failing to request instruction merely out of laziness or indolence is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who is genuinely ill; who lacks the physical strength; who knows the available teacher's instruction is actually mistaken; who already possesses sufficient learning and wisdom of their own to settle the mind unaided; or who has already received the necessary instruction previously.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi situates this precept within the treatise's own nine stages of settling the mind, detailed in the Śrāvaka Stage — inward abiding, continuous abiding, repeated abiding, close abiding, taming, calming, thorough calming, single-pointed focus, and finally even placement — the complete progression from a scattered mind to the genuine foundation of meditative absorption; the precept's phrase "settling the mind" spans this entire progression, while "concentrating the mind" specifically names its culmination in even placement, bordering on genuine access concentration.

He explains the crucial distinction between two kinds of practitioner that governs whether a teacher is genuinely necessary: one who "follows through faith" (隨信行) needs to rely on another's guidance and instruction in order to actually realize calming and insight meditation's meaning, as the *Sutra Explaining the Profound Secrets* itself describes; one who "follows through the teaching" (隨法行) can, by contrast, either rely on a teacher or study independently, penetrating the meaning through their own sustained reflection on the dharma without necessarily needing anyone else's guidance. This precept, he notes, addresses specifically the first kind of practitioner — someone who genuinely lacks the capacity to progress alone yet refuses, from pride or resentment, to seek the help they actually need; someone of the second kind who reasonably judges their own study sufficient commits no fault at all in declining to seek a teacher, precisely as the no-fault clause states. This closes the precept on refusing instruction; the treatise turns next to failing to release the five hindrances that block meditative absorption.

34. Milestone: Tolerating the Five Hindrances Rather Than Releasing Them

The second meditation precept, and one of the treatise's most classically systematic: a bodhisattva who allows any of the five hindrances that block genuine meditative absorption — sensual craving, ill will, torpor and drowsiness, restlessness and worry, or doubt — to arise and simply tolerates its presence rather than actively working to release it, commits a tainted violation, unless the resistance is genuine and the hindrance is simply too deeply ingrained to fully overcome yet.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，起貪欲蓋，忍受不捨，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，若為斷彼生起樂欲，發勤精進，煩惱猛利蔽抑心故，時時現行。如貪欲蓋，如是瞋恚、惛沈睡眠、掉舉惡作，及與疑蓋，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts in whom the hindrance of sensual craving arises, and who tolerate and retain it rather than releasing it, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who genuinely wishes to eliminate it and applies diligent effort toward doing so, even where the affliction is so intense that it still overpowers and obscures the mind and recurs from time to time. Exactly as with the hindrance of sensual craving, so too should the hindrances of ill will; of torpor and drowsiness; of restlessness and worry; and of doubt each be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the classical five hindrances precisely and explains why they are called "hindrances" (蓋) at all: they block genuine progress specifically toward and within the meditative absorptions and the higher formless attainments, covering over the true nature of reality the way a lid covers a vessel, which is exactly the image the character itself carries. The five are sensual craving, ill will, torpor and drowsiness (the heavy dullness that makes both body and mind sluggish), restlessness and worry (the agitated counterpart already discussed as a separate precept of ordinary conduct, here specifically as it obstructs concentration), and doubt — a lingering uncertainty about the teaching or the path itself that prevents wholehearted engagement with practice.

The precept treats all five identically: tolerating any of them once arisen, making no genuine effort to release it, is a tainted violation; while a bodhisattva who genuinely applies diligent effort against the hindrance, yet finds it still recurring because the underlying afflictive tendency remains simply too deeply ingrained to fully master yet, commits no violation at all. Master Cháng Bǎi treats this parallel structure as the same basic principle repeated throughout this entire section of the treatise: the bodhisattva precepts consistently distinguish a beginner's honest, ongoing struggle against deeply rooted affliction from actual complacent tolerance of it — the first is simply the condition of anyone still on the path, the second is the fault the precept actually names. This closes the precept on the five hindrances; the treatise turns next, and closes the meditation-progression cluster, with a precept on a subtler danger — becoming genuinely attached to meditative absorption itself.

35. Milestone: The Subtler Danger of Craving Meditative Absorption Itself — Meditation-Progression Closes

The third and final meditation precept, addressing a genuinely subtle danger: a bodhisattva who develops a craving attachment to the pleasurable experience of meditative absorption itself, regarding that very pleasure as the mark of genuine spiritual accomplishment, commits a tainted violation — since absorption practiced without the prior grounding of genuine wisdom, Master Cháng Bǎi warns, is a trap that catches virtually everyone who attempts it.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，貪味靜慮，於味靜慮見為功德，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，若為斷彼生起樂欲，廣說如前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who develop a craving attachment to the flavor of meditative absorption, and regard that very attachment as a genuine spiritual accomplishment, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who genuinely wishes to eliminate this attachment and applies diligent effort toward doing so, as described in fuller detail earlier.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the mechanism the classical commentary describes: someone who has heard a great deal about the wonderful qualities of the meditative absorptions, but has never actually heard the corresponding methods for leaving attachment to those absorptions behind, sees only the impressive qualities and, driven by that one-sided admiration, practices with real vigor until genuinely entering the first absorption or another meditative state — and then, having entered, develops a craving fondness for the experience itself, mistaking the pleasurable state for the path's actual goal, entirely unaware that this very fondness is itself simply another affliction, now arisen and unrecognized as such. A bodhisattva who becomes lost in this pleasure stops doing the actual work of benefiting beings, content simply to remain absorbed day after day — precisely the failure of diligence and compassion together that this precept names.

He closes with a warning attributed to the modern teacher Venerable Miao-Jing, striking in its bluntness: of a hundred people who take up meditation practice without first grounding themselves in genuine right view, every single one of the hundred will become attached to the pleasure of absorption, because the whole-body ease that deep concentration produces is simply that seductive, that difficult to want to leave once tasted. Meditative absorption on its own, however refined, cannot actually uproot the seeds of affliction — only genuine wisdom born from hearing, reflecting on, and directly realizing the teaching can do that — which is exactly why the treatise insists, again and again throughout this whole section, that right view must always come first, with meditative concentration cultivated only afterward, in its proper supporting role, never mistaken for the destination itself. This closes the third and final precept of the meditation-progression cluster — 靜慮漸次攝 — completing the fifth of the six perfection-based clusters. The treatise turns next to the sixth and final cluster: eight precepts supporting the perfection of wisdom.

36. Milestone: The Wisdom-Progression Cluster Opens — Denigrating the Śrāvaka Teachings

The sixth and final cluster within the thirty-two precepts, supporting the perfection of wisdom, opens: a bodhisattva who holds and teaches that bodhisattvas need not listen to, uphold, or study the śrāvaka scriptures at all — reasoning that the Great Vehicle alone should suffice — commits a tainted violation, since a bodhisattva's own vast compassion requires genuine fluency in every vehicle's teaching in order to meet beings wherever their capacity actually stands.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，起如是見，立如是論：菩薩不應聽聲聞乘相應法教，不應受持，不應修學，菩薩何用於聲聞乘相應法教聽聞、受持、精勤修學？是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。何以故？菩薩尚於外道書論精勤研究，況於佛語。……無違犯者，為令一向習小法者捨彼欲故，作如是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who form and propound the view that bodhisattvas need not listen to, uphold, or study the teachings associated with the śrāvaka vehicle — reasoning that a bodhisattva has no use for listening to, upholding, and diligently studying the śrāvaka vehicle's teachings — commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. Why is this so? A bodhisattva diligently researches even non-Buddhist treatises — how much more so the Buddha's own word! ... There is no violation when a bodhisattva says such things specifically to help someone fixed exclusively on the lesser vehicle let go of that narrow aspiration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that a bodhisattva may genuinely feel no personal delight in the śrāvaka teachings, yet must still read, recite, uphold, listen to, and reflect on them, since certain precepts — the restrictive precepts discussed earlier in this fascicle, for instance — are shared in common with the śrāvaka's own code of individual liberation. The deeper reason is compassion itself: a bodhisattva studies the śrāvaka teachings precisely in order to skillfully guide beings of śrāvaka disposition according to their own capacity, ripening their particular wholesome roots on their own terms rather than insisting everyone approach the path identically. He frames the argument from the treatise's own logic: a bodhisattva already diligently studies even non-Buddhist treatises as part of the five classical fields of knowledge (inner wisdom, logic, linguistics, medicine, and the practical arts) — how much more urgently, then, should the Buddha's own śrāvaka teaching itself be studied, since what contains the greater necessarily contains the lesser, but not the reverse.

The no-fault exception, he notes, is aimed specifically at beings of "indeterminate disposition" — among the five classical spiritual dispositions (śrāvaka, solitary-realizer, bodhisattva, indeterminate, and human-or-celestial), those of indeterminate disposition who have fixed themselves exclusively on the lesser vehicle may be told, as genuine skillful means, that the great vehicle is what truly matters — not because the śrāvaka teaching is actually worthless, but purely to loosen their attachment to it and redirect their aspiration toward the vastly broader scope of the bodhisattva path. This closes the first wisdom precept; the treatise turns next to the opposite failure — neglecting the bodhisattva's own canon.

37. Milestone: Neglecting the Bodhisattva Canon Entirely for the Śrāvaka Canon

The mirror-image failure: a bodhisattva who has never seriously studied the bodhisattva canon — the great vehicle's own sutras, discourses, and treatises — and abandons it altogether in favor of exclusively studying the śrāvaka canon commits a violation, though, since the śrāvaka teachings still genuinely serve to uproot affliction and remain squarely within the sphere of the Buddha's own teaching, it is a comparatively lighter, non-tainted one.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於菩薩藏未精研究，於菩薩藏一切棄捨，於聲聞藏一向修學，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who have never diligently studied the bodhisattva canon, who abandon it entirely, and who devote themselves exclusively to studying the śrāvaka canon instead — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, but it is a non-tainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the classical basis for this distinction: of the twelve traditional divisions of the Buddha's teaching, the "extensive teachings" (方廣, vaipulya) division belongs exclusively to the bodhisattva canon, while the remaining eleven divisions contain material shared between both the śrāvaka and bodhisattva canons — meaning a bodhisattva who studies only the śrāvaka teachings is not straying outside the Buddha's word altogether, only neglecting the one division that belongs to the great vehicle specifically, which is precisely why the fault, though genuine, carries no taint: it lacks the actively wrong view and active affliction that mark a tainted violation, being simply an imbalance of emphasis rather than a hostile rejection. This closes this brief precept; the treatise turns next to the reverse imbalance in the other direction — over-investing in non-Buddhist study while neglecting the Buddha's own teaching.

38. Milestone: Studying Non-Buddhist Treatises While Neglecting the Buddha's Own Word — And Xuanzang's Midnight Lesson

A bodhisattva who, while the Buddha's teaching still genuinely flourishes in the world, has never seriously studied it, yet diligently pursues non-Buddhist treatises and rival doctrines instead, commits a tainted violation — though the treatise grants a real exception for the exceptionally capable, whose swift and unshakeable grasp of ultimate reality itself protects them from ever being led astray by what they study, a principle Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates with a memorable episode from Xuanzang's own life in India.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，現有佛教，於佛教中未精研究，於異道論及諸外論精勤修學，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，若上聰敏；若能速受；若經久時能不忘失；若於其義能思能達；若於佛教如理觀察成就俱行無動覺者，於日日中，常以二分修學佛語，一分學外；則無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, while the Buddha's teaching genuinely still exists in the world, have never diligently studied it, yet diligently pursue non-Buddhist and rival treatises instead — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who is exceptionally intelligent; who can grasp material with unusual speed; who, once learned, does not forget it even after long stretches of time; who is able to reflect on and thoroughly penetrate its meaning; and who, through correct contemplation of the Buddha's teaching, has attained an unshakeable realization that accompanies every activity — such a bodhisattva may, each day, devote two parts of their time to studying the Buddha's word and one part to non-Buddhist study, without any violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi grounds the exception in the treatise's own four-fold investigation (四尋思) leading to genuine realized wisdom: examining how names are merely provisionally assigned, how the things they name are likewise provisional, how a thing's own intrinsic nature is provisionally constructed, and how the distinctions drawn between things are provisionally constructed as well — a bodhisattva who has genuinely penetrated all four to the point of unshakeable, direct realization of the emptiness of self and phenomena can no longer be led astray by non-Buddhist doctrine, however deeply studied, since that realization is not itself a matter of words or concepts that a clever rival argument could dislodge. Such a bodhisattva may rightly devote real time to non-Buddhist study, precisely as a tool for engaging and eventually converting non-Buddhist opponents, without the study itself posing any spiritual risk.

He illustrates the underlying principle with a story about Xuanzang himself while traveling in India: challenged to a public debate, Xuanzang first tested his footing against a low-caste practitioner who came to visit, and easily bested him — but realized he was unfamiliar with the specific rival doctrine he would soon have to face in the real debate, and asked the man directly to teach him. The visitor demurred, protesting that Xuanzang's own standing was far too exalted, and that formally taking a low-caste outsider as teacher would embarrass both Xuanzang and the entire monastery watching; instead, he proposed teaching Xuanzang secretly, at midnight, once everyone else was asleep. Xuanzang agreed, studied the rival system through the night, and by morning knew exactly how to construct the logical proofs, reasons, and examples needed to defeat it in open debate. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson directly: to genuinely subdue an opponent, a bodhisattva must first genuinely understand where the opponent actually stands — know yourself and know the other, and victory in a hundred debates follows naturally — which is exactly the spirit behind this precept's own permission for the exceptionally capable to study outside material without fear of being led astray by it. This closes the precept on neglecting the Buddha's teaching; the treatise turns next to the opposite danger — genuine attachment to non-Buddhist doctrine, treated as a treasure rather than the bitter medicine it actually is.

39. Milestone: Treasuring Non-Buddhist Doctrine Instead of Treating It as Bitter Medicine

A subtler failure than simply over-studying non-Buddhist material: a bodhisattva who genuinely comes to cherish, delight in, and grow attached to skill in non-Buddhist treatises — treating the study itself as a genuine pleasure rather than approaching it the way one approaches a necessary but bitter medicine, to be used and then set back down — commits a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，越菩薩法，於異道論及諸外論研求善巧，深心寶翫、愛樂、味著，非如辛藥而習近之，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who overstep the proper bounds of bodhisattva practice, pursuing skill in rival and non-Buddhist treatises with a mind that genuinely treasures, delights in, and grows attached to them — approaching them not as one approaches a necessary but bitter medicine, but as something to be savored for its own sake — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to distinguish this precept from the previous one: the previous precept concerned simply the proportion of time and effort devoted to non-Buddhist study relative to the Buddha's own word, while this precept concerns the underlying attitude with which any such study — however proportioned — is undertaken. Non-Buddhist treatises, however intellectually impressive, produce no wisdom capable of liberating anyone; a bodhisattva who genuinely comes to love them risks absorbing their wrong views along with their content, however diligent and impressive the outward practice otherwise appears, since even sincere effort produces only wrong results when yoked to wrong view. The medicine metaphor he treats as doing real work: bitter medicine is taken with clear purpose — to cure a specific illness, namely an opponent's wrong view, or one's own need to understand it well enough to refute it — and set aside the moment it is no longer needed, never savored for its own taste. A bodhisattva who instead comes to treasure the medicine itself has lost the very purpose that made taking it acceptable in the first place. This closes the precept on treasuring non-Buddhist doctrine; the treatise turns next to one of its richest and most consequential passages — how a bodhisattva should respond to profound teachings they do not yet understand.

40. Milestone: Slandering the Profound Teaching Out of Simple Incomprehension

One of the treatise's doctrinally richest and most consequential passages: a bodhisattva who, encountering the great vehicle's own profound teachings on emptiness and the buddhas' inconceivable spiritual powers, cannot generate genuine faith and understanding, and responds instead with hostility and slander — declaring the teaching without real value, not the Buddha's actual word, and incapable of benefiting anyone — commits a tainted violation carrying consequences as severe as any deed in this entire section; the treatise's own remedy, laid out with real care, is not that a bodhisattva must fully understand everything, but that incomprehension itself must never be allowed to curdle into denial.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，聞菩薩藏，於甚深處最勝甚深真實法義、諸佛菩薩難思神力，不生信解，憎背毀謗：不能引義、不能引法，非如來說，不能利益安樂有情。是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。如是毀謗，或由自內非理作意，或隨順他而作是說。……若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，若聞甚深最甚深處，心不信解，菩薩爾時應彊信受，應無諂曲，應如是學：我為非善，盲無慧目，於如來眼隨所宣說，於諸如來密意語言而生誹謗。菩薩如是自處無知，仰推如來，於諸佛法無不現知等隨觀見。如是正行，無所違犯。雖無信解，然不誹謗。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, hearing the bodhisattva canon's most profound and truest teachings, and the buddhas' and bodhisattvas' inconceivable spiritual powers, cannot generate faith and understanding, and respond with hostility and slander — declaring that such teaching cannot yield genuine value, cannot yield the true dharma, is not what the Tathāgata actually taught, and cannot benefit or bring ease to any being — commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. Such slander arises either from one's own unwise reflection, or simply from repeating what others have said. ... Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, hearing the most profound teachings, find no faith or understanding arising in their own minds, should at that very moment compel themselves to accept the teaching with genuine trust, free of any pretense, training themselves to think: "This lack is my own failing — I am like one blind, lacking the eye of wisdom; toward what the Tathāgata's own eye has proclaimed, and toward the Tathāgata's own hidden intent within these words, I would otherwise give rise to slander." Positioning themselves honestly as simply not yet knowing, and looking up to the Tathāgata instead, whose wisdom leaves nothing among all things whatsoever unseen and fully realized — conducting oneself rightly in this way incurs no violation at all. Though understanding has not yet arisen, no slander follows from it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies exactly what is at stake: the "profound places" named here are the great vehicle sutras themselves, teaching the ultimate truth beyond language, the object of the buddhas' own non-conceptual wisdom alone — beyond both existence and non-existence, hence genuinely "profound" — together with the buddhas' three classical categories of inconceivable spiritual power: miraculous transformation of physical phenomena, mind-reading knowledge of others' thoughts, and the teaching-power that guides beings out of affliction toward the path. A bodhisattva who cannot understand any of this and, rather than admitting the limits of their own current capacity, instead declares the teaching worthless or outright fabricated — asserting the notorious position that "the Great Vehicle is not the Buddha's own word" — commits, he stresses gravely, a fault classed among the karmic equivalents of the five gravest possible deeds, ripening toward the very same uninterrupted hell discussed earlier in this fascicle, precisely because denying the Buddha's own teaching at its very source cuts off the path to liberation for countless other beings who might otherwise have benefited from it.

The remedy the treatise prescribes, however, asks for something genuinely achievable rather than impossible: not full comprehension, which not every bodhisattva has yet reached, but honest humility in its place. A bodhisattva who cannot yet understand should say, in effect, exactly what the text puts in their mouth — "the failing is mine, not the teaching's; I am like someone blind reaching to judge what I cannot see; the Buddha's own all-encompassing wisdom sees clearly into every category of phenomena I cannot yet fathom, so I will trust and look up to that wisdom rather than trust my own present incomprehension." Master Cháng Bǎi calls this response "without any pretense" precisely because it requires no performance of understanding one doesn't have — only genuine acknowledgment of one's own current limits, paired with genuine confidence in the Buddha's complete realization, "equally and thoroughly seeing" every possible category of beneficial, harmful, and neutral phenomena without exception. Held with this attitude, not understanding is simply the honest condition of an unenlightened being still on the path; only turning that honest gap into active denial constitutes the fault. This closes one of the treatise's most consequential precepts; it turns next to the comparatively more familiar territory of self-praise and disparaging others.

41. Milestone: Praising Oneself and Disparaging Others Out of Jealousy for Recognition

A bodhisattva who, driven by craving for others' fame, support, and recognition and by jealous resentment when someone else receives it, praises their own qualities while disparaging another's, commits a tainted violation — though the same words, spoken purely to overcome hostile rivals, protect the Buddha's teaching, or genuinely build another being's faith, are entirely blameless.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於他人所有染愛心、有瞋恚心，自讚毀他，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。……無違犯者，若為摧伏諸惡外道；若為住持如來聖教；若欲方便調彼、伏彼，廣說如前；或欲令其未淨信者發生淨信，已淨信者倍復增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, toward what belongs to another, harbor a craving, attached mind, and a mind of hostile resentment, and praise themselves while disparaging the other person, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. ... There is no violation when done to subdue genuinely harmful non-Buddhist opponents; to uphold and preserve the Tathāgata's own sacred teaching; as a skillful means to guide and subdue a particular being, as described more fully earlier; or to give rise to genuine faith in someone not yet faithful, or to deepen the faith of someone already faithful.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the motive as decisive here, exactly as throughout this whole section of the treatise: craving fame, support, and reverence beyond what one already has, and being unable to feel genuine joy at another's success, are what turn ordinary self-confidence into the fault of self-praise coupled with disparagement. A bodhisattva secure in their own practice has no need to diminish anyone else's standing in order to feel secure in their own.

The no-fault list, once again, turns entirely on whether the same outward words serve a genuinely different, compassionate purpose: refuting a harmful rival teacher in open debate, defending the integrity of the Buddha's own teaching against misrepresentation, skillfully guiding a particular being toward the wholesome, or building and strengthening someone's faith by pointing to genuine, verifiable qualities — none of these arise from jealousy or craving for personal advantage, and so none of them incur any fault, however similar the surface words might sound to genuine self-praise. This closes the precept on self-praise and disparagement; the treatise turns next to a related failure — refusing, out of pride, to attend a dharma talk.

42. Milestone: Refusing Out of Pride to Attend a Dharma Talk — and the Eight Marks of Genuinely Skillful Teaching

A bodhisattva who, gripped by arrogance, resentment, or hostility, refuses to attend a genuine dharma talk or scholarly discussion being given nearby commits a tainted violation — but the treatise grants an unusually long and practical list of legitimate reasons to stay away, including the bodhisattva's own right to judge, by eight specific marks, whether a given teacher's presentation is actually worth attending at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，聞說正法論議決擇，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，而不往聽，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若為懶惰懈怠所蔽，而不往聽，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若不覺知；若有疾病；若無氣力；若知倒說；若為護彼說法者心；若正了知彼所說義，是數所聞、所持、所了；若已多聞，具足聞持，其聞積集；若欲無間於境住心；若勤引發菩薩勝定；若自了知上品愚鈍，其慧鈍濁，於所聞法難受難持，難於所緣攝心令定；不往聽者，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, hearing that the true dharma is being taught, discussed, or carefully analyzed nearby, are gripped by arrogance, resentment, or hostility and so do not go to listen — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If it is mere laziness or indolence that keeps them away, it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation whatsoever: for one who simply does not know a talk is happening; who is genuinely ill; who lacks the physical strength; who knows the teaching being given is actually mistaken; who stays away purely to protect the speaker from the pressure of a more advanced listener's presence; who already correctly understands the very material being discussed, having heard, retained, and mastered it many times before; who is already deeply learned, having fully retained everything heard, with that learning thoroughly consolidated; who wishes, without interruption, to keep the mind settled on a meditation object; who is diligently working to cultivate a bodhisattva's own superior concentration; or who honestly recognizes their own dull and clouded intelligence, finding such teaching genuinely too difficult to receive, retain, or settle the mind upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi compares this precept to the *Brahma Net Sutra*'s own rule that a bodhisattva must travel up to forty li to hear a dharma talk being given nearby — the underlying spirit is the same, prizing genuine hunger for the teaching over pride or indifference. Among the many legitimate exceptions, he draws out the eight specific marks the sub-commentary offers for judging whether a given presentation of the dharma is actually worth attending — marks by which a bodhisattva may rightly conclude a particular speaker's teaching is simply "mistaken" and stay away without fault: whether the terms and phrasing are properly and logically arranged; whether each point genuinely supports and clarifies what follows it; whether the explanation actually unpacks its own structure clearly; whether the delivery itself is clear and luminous rather than muddy; whether it genuinely helps purify the listeners' minds of affliction; whether it is pitched appropriately to the audience's actual level rather than talking past them or beneath them; whether the phrasing draws out the teaching's real meaning rather than obscuring it; and whether it is given thoroughly, consistently, and grounded in genuinely right view, equipping listeners to actually accomplish the path. A speaker lacking these eight qualities, however impressive their reputation or eloquence, is not required listening.

He also draws out the more unusual entries in the list: staying away specifically to spare a less experienced speaker the pressure of teaching in front of a bodhisattva of evidently greater attainment is itself treated as a form of genuine consideration, not mere avoidance; and a bodhisattva actively engaged in intensive meditation retreat, or honestly aware that their own capacity is simply too limited to benefit from a particular talk right now, is likewise excused without any implied criticism of the teaching itself. What unifies the entire list, he stresses, is that none of these reasons involve pride or hostility toward the dharma or the person teaching it — the fault this precept actually targets — but rather genuine, honestly assessed circumstance. This closes the precept on refusing to attend a dharma talk; the treatise turns next, and closes both the wisdom-progression cluster and the entire thirty-two precepts of gathering wholesome dharmas, with the precept against disrespecting the dharma teacher.

43. Milestone: Disrespecting the Dharma Teacher — All Thirty-Two Precepts of Gathering Wholesome Dharmas Complete

The eighth and final precept of the wisdom-progression cluster, and with it the close of all thirty-two precepts supporting the six perfections: a bodhisattva who deliberately belittles a dharma teacher, withholds proper reverence, mocks and ridicules them, and attends only to how elegantly the teaching is phrased rather than to whether the teaching itself is actually correct, commits a tainted violation — the treatise notably granting no exceptions whatsoever to this particular fault, unlike almost every other precept in this long section.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於說法師故思輕毀，不深恭敬，嗤笑調弄，但依於文不依於義，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who deliberately belittle and disrespect a dharma teacher, withhold proper reverence, mock and ridicule them, and attend only to the elegance of the phrasing rather than to the correctness of the meaning — this constitutes a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this is the one precept in this entire cluster carrying no non-tainted grade and no list of exceptions at all — deliberately disrespecting anyone actually teaching the dharma is simply, without qualification, a fault. He explains "attending only to phrasing, not to meaning" precisely: a bodhisattva listens to the dharma in order to genuinely grasp its meaning, never merely to enjoy polished, ornamental language for its own sake — which means that even a plainly, unremarkably delivered talk, so long as its content is genuinely correct, deserves the listener's full and respectful attention, while an eloquent, beautifully phrased talk built on empty or mistaken content deserves none of the reverence its surface polish might otherwise invite. A bodhisattva who judges a teacher by delivery rather than substance has inverted exactly what matters.

With this precept, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, all eight precepts of the wisdom-progression cluster — from denigrating the śrāvaka teachings through disrespecting the dharma teacher — and with them all thirty-two precepts supporting the six perfections within the still broader category of gathering wholesome dharmas, reach their completion: seven precepts of giving-progression, seven of discipline-progression, five of patience-progression, two of diligence-progression, three of meditation-progression, and these final eight of wisdom-progression. Having a place within a gathering of the dharma, listening with genuine respect, is itself already a mark of wisdom in action. The treatise turns next to the third and final major category of the bodhisattva precepts: eleven precepts supporting the precept of benefiting sentient beings, opening with a bodhisattva's basic obligation to lend a helping hand.

44. Milestone: The Benefiting-Beings Cluster Opens — Refusing to Lend a Helping Hand

The third and final major category of the forty-three minor precepts begins: eleven precepts supporting the precept of benefiting sentient beings, opening with the most basic form that benefit takes — simply showing up to help. A bodhisattva who, gripped by resentment or hostility, refuses to lend a hand with another being's genuinely worthwhile undertaking — traveling together on the road, assisting with a dharma-teaching effort, guarding entrusted wealth, mediating a quarrel toward reconciliation, accompanying someone to an auspicious gathering, or joining in an act of merit-making — commits a tainted violation, though the treatise's own no-fault list, unusually long even by this section's standards, gives real weight to the bodhisattva's own limits and judgment.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於諸有情所應作事，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不為助伴。謂於能辦所應作事，或於道路若往若來，或於正說事業加行，或於掌護所有財寶，或於和好乖離諍訟，或於吉會，或於福業，不為助伴，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若為懶惰懈怠所蔽，不為助伴，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若有疹疾；若無氣力；若了知彼自能成辦；若知求者自有依怙；若知所作能引非義、能引非法；若欲方便調彼、伏彼，廣說如前；若先許餘為作助伴；若轉請他有力者助；若於善品正勤修習，不欲暫廢；若性愚鈍，於所聞法難受難持，如前廣說；若為將護多有情意；若護僧制；不為助伴，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, gripped by resentment or hostility, refuse to lend a hand with a being's worthwhile undertaking — traveling with them on the road, assisting a dharma-teaching effort, guarding their entrusted wealth, mediating a rift toward reconciliation, accompanying them to an auspicious gathering, or joining an act of merit-making — commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If mere laziness or indolence keeps them from lending a hand, it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation whatsoever: for one who is ill; who lacks the physical strength; who knows the other can manage the task unaided; who knows the one requesting help already has other support; who knows the undertaking would actually lead toward what is meaningless or unwholesome; who withholds help purely as a skillful means to guide the other, as described more fully earlier; who has already promised to help someone else; who redirects the request to someone else more capable; who is diligently absorbed in wholesome practice and does not wish to interrupt it even briefly; who honestly knows their own dullness makes them unable to receive or retain what is asked of them, as described more fully earlier; who withholds help purely to protect the feelings of the larger community; or who withholds help specifically to uphold a rule the monastic community has properly established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this eleventh-hour category of precepts turns the treatise's whole logic outward, from a bodhisattva's own internal discipline toward the concrete, often unglamorous work of actually being useful to others — starting with something as plain as accompanying someone on a task, guarding their belongings, or helping settle a quarrel. He stresses that what makes refusal a fault here is specifically the presence of resentment or hostility as the motive; simple unavailability, of whatever kind, is not itself a violation at all, only a violation when the underlying reason is ill will toward the person asking.

He walks through the unusually extensive no-fault list as itself a kind of practical teaching on how to say no responsibly: genuine incapacity (illness, exhaustion, lack of relevant skill), a task that turns out not to genuinely need the bodhisattva's help at all, a task that would actually lead toward something unwholesome, an existing prior commitment, an ongoing and important practice not to be casually interrupted, honest recognition of one's own limitations, and — two entries he draws particular attention to — declining specifically to protect the feelings of the wider community, and declining specifically because the monastic community has formally sanctioned the person asking and warned others against assisting them. None of these, he stresses, come from resentment; all of them come from a genuine, considered judgment about what actually serves everyone well, which is exactly the distinction this whole precept turns on. This closes the precept on refusing to help; the treatise turns next to a closely related failure — refusing to visit the genuinely sick.

45. Milestone: Refusing to Visit and Attend to the Seriously Ill — and On Speaking Gently to the Dying

A bodhisattva who, gripped by resentment or hostility, refuses to visit and attend to a being suffering serious illness — or, by direct extension, a being suffering any deep distress at all — commits a tainted violation; Master Cháng Bǎi's own commentary here turns unusually practical, offering real guidance on the delicate art of actually comforting someone who is dying or in genuine anguish.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，見諸有情遭重疾病，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不往供事，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若為懶惰懈怠所蔽，不往供事，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若自有病；若無氣力；若轉請他有力隨順令往供事；若知病者有依有怙；若知病者自有勢力，能自供事；若了知彼長病所觸，堪自支持；若為勤修廣大無上殊勝善品；若欲護持所修善品，令無間闕；若自了知上品愚鈍，其慧鈍濁，於所聞法難受難持，難於所緣攝心令定；若先許餘為作供事。如於病者，於有苦者為作助伴，欲除其苦，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who see beings suffering serious illness, and who, gripped by resentment or hostility, refuse to go and attend to them, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If mere laziness or indolence keeps them away, it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation: for one who is themselves ill; who lacks the physical strength; who redirects a capable other person to go instead; who knows the sick person already has proper support; who knows the sick person can manage on their own; who knows the person is simply managing a long-term chronic condition well enough already; who is diligently cultivating vast, unsurpassed, supremely wholesome practice; who does not wish to interrupt an ongoing practice even briefly; who honestly recognizes their own dull and clouded intelligence makes such visits difficult to conduct wisely; or who has already promised to attend someone else. Exactly as with the physically ill, so too should attending and seeking to relieve anyone in genuine distress be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the ninth no-fault reason — honest recognition of one's own limited wisdom — as worth real emphasis, since visiting the sick or dying badly can do genuine harm. He offers his own direct counsel on end-of-life visits: someone lacking real wisdom and a genuinely gentle heart who walks in and bluntly tells a dying person "just let go" will very often make things worse, not better; similarly, opening with "this illness is simply your karmic obstruction from too much killing in the past" offers no comfort at all and only deepens the person's distress. Genuine care for the dying, he stresses, begins by asking how they actually feel, building real emotional connection before ever attempting to guide them toward any particular spiritual conclusion — since most people facing death still carry real, unresolved attachments that have not yet been worked through, and only after that groundwork of genuine human connection can any skillful guidance toward letting go actually land. A bodhisattva who knows they would likely cause harm by speaking carelessly is entirely right, under this precept, to send someone more capable instead.

He also flags the important closing line extending this entire precept, without qualification, from physical illness to any form of genuine suffering or distress — meaning a bodhisattva's obligation to show up is not limited to hospital visits but extends to anyone genuinely suffering, in whatever form that suffering takes, with the identical structure of violation, non-tainted violation, and no-fault exceptions applying equally. This closes the precept on visiting the sick and suffering; the treatise turns next to a bodhisattva's obligation to teach when someone is genuinely going astray.

46. Milestone: Withholding the Truth from Someone Chasing Happiness the Wrong Way

A bodhisattva who sees a being pursuing happiness in this life or the next through genuinely mistaken methods — corrupt schemes for wealth and status, or meaningless austerities and superstitious rites aimed at rebirth in heaven — and who, out of resentment or hostility, withholds the genuine teaching that would set them straight, commits a tainted violation; though the treatise grants real latitude for declining to teach someone who would only respond with hostility, distortion, or contempt.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，見諸有情為求現法後法事故，廣行非理，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不為宣說如實正理，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若由懶惰懈怠所蔽，不為宣說，非染違犯。……無違犯者，若自無知；若無氣力；若轉請他有力者說；若即彼人自有智力；若彼有餘善友攝受；若欲方便調彼、伏彼，廣說如前；若知為說如實正理，起嫌恨心，若發惡言，若顛倒受，若無愛敬；若復知彼性弊𢤱悷；不為宣說，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who see beings pursuing happiness in this life or the next through genuinely mistaken methods, and who, gripped by resentment or hostility, withhold the true teaching that would set them straight, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If mere laziness or indolence keeps them from teaching, it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation: for one who genuinely does not know the correct teaching themselves; who lacks the strength to teach; who redirects a more capable teacher instead; when the person already has sufficient wisdom of their own; when the person already has another genuine spiritual friend guiding them; when withholding the teaching itself serves as skillful means, as described more fully earlier; when the bodhisattva knows in advance that offering the true teaching would only provoke resentment, harsh speech, willful distortion, or open contempt; or when the bodhisattva knows the person's very nature is genuinely vicious and untamable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the two mistaken pursuits named here precisely. Pursuing "present-life benefit" through wrong methods means chasing wealth, status, and sensory pleasure through corrupt schemes — collusion, fraud, exploitation — pursued with genuine relish rather than reluctance. Pursuing "future-life benefit" through wrong methods means chasing heavenly rebirth or a mistaken notion of liberation through equally mistaken means: fire-worshipping cults that believe burning good and bad things alike pleases heaven and secures fortune, or adherents of the "prior-cause" theory who believe all present suffering stems purely from past-life karma with no bearing on the present, and so try to simply "burn off" that karma through pointless austerity — standing on one leg, eating almost nothing, going naked — mistaking self-punishment for genuine purification, when only actually eliminating affliction, not physically suffering, purifies karma at all. A bodhisattva who sees this kind of confusion and, out of resentment, refuses to offer the correction that would actually help, commits a real fault.

The no-fault list, he notes, turns on a familiar and important principle running through this whole section: offering a teaching nobody is willing to hear helps no one and may do active harm, provoking only greater resentment or entrenchment in wrong view. A bodhisattva who can see in advance, through genuine assessment rather than mere assumption, that a particular person would only respond to correction with hostility, twisted misinterpretation, or contempt is right to hold back — not from resentment of their own, but from the same clear-eyed compassion that runs through every exception in this section. The vivid closing term for an untamable temperament, "fierce and unruly" (𢤱悷), he traces to a colorful classical image from Xuanzang's own travel record: dragons said to change form and mate with mares, producing offspring combining a dragon's ferocious temper with a horse's restless flightiness — a creature genuinely impossible to control, and exactly the kind of person this precept excuses a bodhisattva from attempting to teach. This closes the precept on withholding correction; the treatise turns next to failing to repay a genuine kindness.

47. Milestone: Failing to Repay a Genuine Debt of Gratitude

A bodhisattva who, out of resentment, fails to acknowledge or repay a genuine kindness once done for them — whether through simple courtesy in the moment or through proportionate material return when the opportunity arises — commits a tainted violation; the treatise's exceptions here are notably narrow, since genuine gratitude, unlike many other precepts in this section, admits of very few legitimate reasons to withhold.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於先有恩諸有情所不知恩惠、不了恩惠，懷嫌恨心，不欲現前如應酬報，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。若為懶惰懈怠所蔽，不現酬報，非染違犯。……無違犯者，勤加功用，無力無能不獲酬報；若欲方便調彼、伏彼，廣說如前；若欲報恩而彼不受；皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts who, toward beings who have shown them genuine kindness in the past, fail to recognize or acknowledge that kindness, and, gripped by resentment, are unwilling to repay it as circumstances call for, commit a violation, a transgression, and it is a tainted violation. If mere laziness or indolence keeps them from repaying it, it is a non-tainted violation. ... There is no violation for one who has genuinely applied real effort but simply lacks the capacity to repay the kindness; who withholds repayment purely as a skillful means to guide the other, as described more fully earlier; or who genuinely wishes to repay the kindness but finds the other person unwilling to accept it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this precept directly back to the earlier discussion of how a bodhisattva should treat someone who was once kind to them: rising respectfully to greet them, offering a proper seat, serving tea when they are present, and, when the benefactor's own former material generosity can genuinely be matched or exceeded, doing so rather than repaying generosity with something conspicuously lesser. Failing to even recognize a genuine kindness in the first place — not merely failing to repay it, but not registering that it happened at all — he treats as the deeper failure the precept is really naming, since repayment can hardly follow where recognition never occurred.

He notes how narrow the legitimate exceptions genuinely are here compared to most other precepts in this section: a bodhisattva who has honestly tried but genuinely lacks the means to repay a kindness is not at fault, nor is one who withholds repayment as a specific skillful means to help the benefactor grow, nor one whose sincere attempt at repayment is simply refused by a benefactor unwilling to accept anything in return — itself, he notes, a mark of the purest kind of generosity, giving without any expectation of return. Beyond these few cases, gratitude genuinely owed and genuinely possible to repay should simply be repaid; this precept has no room for resentment or mere reluctance as an acceptable excuse. This closes the precept on repaying kindness; the treatise turns next to relieving another being's grief and sorrow.
