Fascicle 41 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十一 · The Minor Bodhisattva Precepts Begin

The Forty-Three Minor Precepts

Continuing directly from the four gravest root violations already covered, the treatise now turns to discipline's own comprehensive detail: forty-three specific minor precepts, thirty-two supporting the six perfections and eleven supporting the four means of embrace — beginning with six honest ways to check oneself for violation, and the very first precept itself: never letting a single day pass without genuine reverence for the three jewels.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 41 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle Forty-One Opens — The Structure of the Forty-Three Minor Precepts

Directly continuing from the four root violations already covered, the treatise now details the comprehensive body of minor precepts supporting the remaining two storehouses of discipline: thirty-two precepts under gathering wholesome practice, organized around progress through each of the six perfections in turn, and eleven precepts under benefiting beings, organized around the four means of embrace — together with six honest, practical ways a bodhisattva can check whether any given lapse genuinely counts as a violation at all.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於有違犯及無違犯，是染非染、軟中上品，應當了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts should clearly understand, regarding any given matter, whether it constitutes a violation or not, whether it is tainted or untainted by affliction, and whether it falls into a mild, moderate, or severe degree.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the overall structure: the thirty-two precepts under gathering wholesome practice divide into six clusters matching each perfection in turn — seven concerning giving, seven concerning discipline, five concerning patience, two concerning diligence, three concerning meditative concentration, and eight concerning wisdom; while the eleven precepts under benefiting beings divide around the four means of embrace, addressing companionship, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and shared work in turn. He notes traditional enumerations of this same material vary slightly between forty-one, forty-two, and forty-three total precepts depending purely on how certain closely related items are grouped or split, with no actual difference in content.

He then lays out six honest, practical ways a bodhisattva can examine whether a given lapse genuinely constitutes a fault: a contemptuous mind, disrespecting some aspect of the teaching without ever genuinely trying to understand it; a lazy mind, neglecting to do what should be done through simple laziness; a mind covered by affliction, one of the five hindrances or a related affliction currently clouding judgment; a weary mind, genuinely worn out after sustained, vigorous effort; a mind affected by illness, including genuine mental illness; and a mind obstructed by excessive socializing, drawn into idle conversation that serves no one's genuine benefit. In each case, he notes, the treatise's own consistent principle applies: if the underlying state is recognized and not indulged or accepted as acceptable, there is no fault; only continuing to tolerate and indulge the underlying laxity, rather than correcting it, turns an understandable lapse into a genuine violation. This closes the overview of the whole system; the very first of the forty-three precepts, requiring daily reverence for the three jewels, follows next.

2. Milestone: Precept One — Never a Day Without Genuine Reverence for the Three Jewels

The first minor precept requires a bodhisattva to show genuine reverence toward the Buddha, the teaching, and the noble community every single day — through offerings ranging from abundant to modest, but at an absolute minimum through a single bow, a single four-line verse of praise, or, at the very least, a single moment of pure, remembering faith — since letting an entire day pass with no reverence shown at all constitutes a genuine violation of this foundational precept.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於日日中，若於如來、或為如來造制多所；若於正法、或為正法造經卷所；若於僧伽，謂十方界已入大地諸菩薩眾。若不以其或少、或多諸供養具而為供養，下至以身一拜禮敬，下至以語一四句頌讚佛法僧真實功德，下至以心一清淨信隨念三寶真實功德。空度日夜，是名有犯，有所違越。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, every single day, toward the Tathāgata or a monument built for the Tathāgata, toward the true teaching or a scripture recording it, and toward the noble community — the bodhisattvas already established on the noble grounds across the ten directions — should make some offering, whether modest or abundant; at the very least, a single bow of respect, a single four-line verse praising the genuine qualities of the Buddha, the teaching, and the community, or, at the least, a single moment of pure faith calling those genuine qualities to mind. Letting an entire day and night pass with none of this at all constitutes a violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses this precept sets an extraordinarily low floor precisely so that no genuine excuse — busyness, poverty, distance from a temple — can ever justify complete neglect: even a single bow, a single remembered verse, or a single moment of sincere, believing recollection satisfies it entirely. He explains genuine reverence properly extends to the entire community of practitioners on the bodhisattva path, not merely those already fully realized — since every ordinary monastic is, in truth, a future buddha still in training, just as every being without exception carries the same potential, dismissing reverence for the imperfect while reserving it only for the already-accomplished misses the point of genuine devotion entirely.

He cites the classical verse from a foundational treatise as a model worth memorizing: praising the Buddha's own attainment of complete liberation, the teaching's own fearless, undefiled realization now handed down, and the genuine disciples of the Great Vehicle who faithfully transmit it — noting the verse deliberately honors all three jewels together in a single, brief recitation exactly of the kind this precept requires as a bare minimum. He explains that whether this daily lapse counts as a tainted violation, carrying real affliction, or an untainted one depends entirely on the underlying cause: disrespect or laziness taints it with genuine affliction, while a single, honest lapse of mindfulness amid otherwise sincere practice does not. This closes the precept's basic requirement; the specific circumstances under which no fault at all is incurred, including a striking account of how advanced bodhisattvas naturally never lapse in this way, follow next.

3. Milestone: When There Is No Violation — Genuine Illness, and an Advanced Bodhisattva's Natural, Unbroken Reverence

Two circumstances incur no fault at all under this precept: genuine mental illness or derangement, since a mind not functioning normally cannot meaningfully be said to violate anything at all; and, at the opposite extreme, a bodhisattva who has already reached the first ground and beyond, whose settled realization and enduring aspiration mean reverence for the three jewels has become entirely spontaneous and unbroken, requiring no deliberate effort to sustain at all.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂心狂亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one whose mind is genuinely deranged.

Chinese Source

若已證入淨意樂地，常無違犯。由得清淨意樂菩薩，譬如已得證淨苾芻，恆時法爾於佛法僧以勝供具承事供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a bodhisattva have already entered the ground of purified aspiration, there is never any violation at all — a bodhisattva of such purified aspiration, exactly as a monastic who has attained genuine, verified confidence, naturally and spontaneously serves and reveres the Buddha, the teaching, and the community with the finest offerings at every moment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a bodhisattva who has reached the first ground, the ground of great joy, actually beholds buddhas directly through two conditions: a genuinely settled faith, confident enough to trust that buddhas across the ten directions truly exist even without having personally verified it, and a genuine, resolute vow to actually behold one — citing the model of the fourth-century teacher Huiyuan, who, through sustained devotional practice and firm aspiration, reportedly beheld Amitābha directly in meditation on several occasions before his own death, a vivid illustration of how faith and resolve together can bring what might otherwise seem merely aspirational into genuinely lived experience.

He notes such a bodhisattva has attained what the tradition calls the four confirmed certainties regarding the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and discipline itself — a settled confidence so thoroughly rooted that it persists unshaken across every future rebirth without exception, meaning reverence for the three jewels no longer requires deliberate practice at all, having become as natural and spontaneous as breathing. This closes the two circumstances under which this first precept incurs no fault; the second precept, addressing excessive craving for others' offerings, opens next.

4. Milestone: Precept Two Closes — Struggling Against Deep-Rooted Craving Is Not Yet a Fault

The second precept addresses craving others' offerings and never feeling satisfied, incurring no fault at all when a beginning practitioner is genuinely trying to overcome that craving through sustained, sincere effort — even if the same innate, habitual craving, carried over from countless past lives, still occasionally breaks through despite real effort — since the treatise recognizes such deep-rooted tendencies cannot always be immediately mastered, however genuine the underlying wish to overcome them.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂為斷彼，生起樂欲，發勤精進攝彼對治。雖勤遮遏，而為猛利性惑所蔽，數起現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who, wishing to overcome this craving, diligently applies themselves to its antidote — even if, despite this sincere effort, the intense, innate affliction still repeatedly breaks through and manifests.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "innate affliction" as habitual tendency carried forward from countless past lives rather than a fault newly created in this one — some practitioners simply carry a stronger seed of craving or aversion than others, entirely apart from anything done wrong in this present life. He notes this leniency applies specifically to a beginning practitioner still building genuine strength against long-entrenched habit — someone who has practiced for decades and still shows no real progress against the same affliction cannot claim quite the same excuse. This closes the second precept; the third, addressing genuine courtesy toward respected elders and fellow practitioners, follows next.

5. Milestone: Precept Three — Welcoming Elders and Fellow Practitioners With Genuine Courtesy

The third precept requires genuine courtesy toward respected elders and fellow practitioners on the path — rising to greet them, offering the better seat, and engaging warmly in conversation — a fault, and a tainted one, when neglected out of arrogance or resentment, but only an untainted fault when neglected merely through laziness, forgetfulness, or simple unfamiliarity with proper etiquette; a long list of genuine circumstances, from illness to actively protecting a teacher's own concentration or the wider community's peace, incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，見諸耆長有德可敬同法者來，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不起承迎，不推勝座。……是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, seeing a respected elder or genuinely admirable fellow practitioner arrive, who, overcome by arrogance and harboring resentment or irritation, do not rise to greet them or offer them the better seat ... this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

非憍慢制，無嫌恨心、無恚惱心，但由懶惰、懈怠、忘念、無記之心，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should this arise not from arrogance or resentment, but simply from laziness, forgetfulness, or genuine unfamiliarity with what to do, this is an untainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes genuine social awkwardness or an upbringing that simply never taught proper hospitality from actual disrespect — someone who never learned how to welcome a guest is not thereby holding contempt for that guest, and the fault, while real, carries no genuine affliction behind it. He lists the treatise's own extensive no-fault circumstances: genuine illness or mental confusion; being asleep while a visitor mistakenly believes otherwise; being actively engaged in teaching or hearing the dharma oneself; deliberately withholding a greeting in order to protect a teacher's own concentration while listening, or to protect a whole community's shared quiet; using silence itself as a skillful means of correcting someone who needs it; and observing a monastic community's own formal discipline toward someone under sanction. This closes courtesy toward visitors already present; accepting genuine invitations extended by others follows next.

6. Milestone: Precept Four — Accepting Genuine Invitations

The fourth precept requires accepting a sincere invitation to receive support — refusing out of arrogance or resentment denies the host their own chance to build merit and constitutes a tainted violation, while refusing merely from laziness is untainted — with a substantial list of genuine circumstances, from illness and danger on the road to actively protecting one's own meditative practice or shielding others from a host acting in bad faith, incurring no fault whatsoever.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他來延請，或往居家、或往餘寺，奉施飲食及衣服等諸資生具，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不至其所，不受所請，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, invited to a home or another temple to receive food, clothing, or other necessities, who, overcome by arrogance and harboring resentment, refuse to go or accept the invitation — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，或有疾病；或無氣力；或心狂亂；或處懸遠；或道有怖；或欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；或餘先請；或為無間修諸善法，欲護善品令無暫廢……或復知彼懷損惱心，詐來延請；或為護他多嫌恨心；或護僧制；不至其所，不受所請，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who is ill, without strength, mentally deranged, invited somewhere too distant, facing genuine danger on the road, declining in order to skillfully correct the host instead, already committed elsewhere, protecting an unbroken course of meditative practice ... aware the invitation is offered in bad faith, protecting others from resentment, or observing the community's own formal discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes an invitation extended purely to build the host's own merit from one extended specifically to hear teaching in pursuit of genuine liberation — refusing either without real cause denies the host precisely what they sought by inviting a bodhisattva in the first place. He walks through the treatise's own extensive list of legitimate reasons for declining: genuine illness or exhaustion, an unreasonably distant location, real danger along the way, a prior commitment already made, an unbroken retreat or course of study a bodhisattva is unwilling to interrupt, a first direct breakthrough toward genuine realization still being consolidated, sensing the invitation is offered with hidden ill intent, or protecting either an individual or the wider community from an association that would cause more harm than good. This closes accepting genuine invitations; receiving valuable gifts and offerings, where refusal out of resentment carries a striking additional weight, follows next.

7. Milestone: Precept Five — Accepting Valuable Gifts, Where Refusal Out of Resentment "Abandons the Being"

The fifth precept concerns accepting valuable offerings — refusing them out of resentment or irritation constitutes a tainted violation described in unusually strong terms, since it amounts to abandoning the very being making the offering, while refusing merely from laziness remains only an untainted lapse; a substantial list of legitimate reasons — including the risk of one's own attachment, the donor's likely future regret, or the offering's own questionable or already-spoken-for origin — incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他持種種生色可染、末尼、真珠、琉璃等寶，及持種種眾多上妙財利供具殷勤奉施，由嫌恨心、或恚惱心，違拒不受，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯，捨有情故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, offered fine jewels or abundant, excellent material support with genuine sincerity, who refuse it out of resentment or irritation — this constitutes a tainted violation, since it amounts to abandoning the very being making the offering.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，或心狂亂；或觀受已心生染著；或觀後時彼定追悔；或復知彼於施迷亂；或知施主隨捨隨受，由是因緣定當貧匱；或知此物是僧伽物、窣堵波物；或知此物劫盜他得；或知此物由是因緣多生過患，或殺、或縛、或罰、或黜、或嫌、或責；違拒不受，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who is mentally deranged, who foresees genuine attachment would arise from accepting it, who foresees the giver would surely come to regret it, who recognizes the giver is confused about the nature of giving, who knows accepting would leave the giver genuinely impoverished, who recognizes the object belongs to the community or a monument, who knows it was obtained through theft, or who foresees accepting it would lead to real harm — being killed, bound, punished, removed from office, resented, or blamed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the striking phrase "abandoning the being" as underscoring how personal a refusal driven by resentment actually is — declining a gift for practical reasons is one thing, but declining it specifically to spite the giver rejects the person behind the gift, not merely the object itself. He walks through the treatise's own detailed exceptions: recognizing that accepting a particular object would itself feed one's own attachment; foreseeing that the giver, perhaps acting impulsively, would likely regret the gift once given; recognizing the giver does not actually understand what genuine giving even means; knowing acceptance would leave the giver in genuine hardship; recognizing the object already belongs to the wider community or a sacred monument rather than to the giver personally; knowing it was stolen; or foreseeing that accepting it would expose the giver to real danger, whether violence, punishment, or social consequence. This closes accepting valuable gifts; sharing the teaching with genuine seekers, a precept already anticipated at length in the earlier chapter on giving, follows next.

8. Milestone: Precept Six — Sharing the Teaching With Genuine Seekers

The sixth precept requires sharing the teaching itself, whether spoken instruction or written scripture, with anyone genuinely seeking it — refusing out of resentment or jealousy toward a seeker's own future understanding constitutes a tainted violation, while refusing merely from laziness remains untainted — and, echoing the giving chapter's own extensive treatment, a substantial list of genuine circumstances, from a hostile questioner seeking only to find fault to a seeker likely to develop wrong view from teaching too advanced for them, incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他來求法，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，嫉妒變異，不施其法，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, approached by a genuine seeker of the teaching, who, out of resentment, irritation, or jealousy, refuse to give it — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂諸外道伺求過短；或有重病；或心狂亂；或欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；或於是法未善通利；或復見彼不生恭敬，無有羞愧，以惡威儀而來聽受；或復知彼是鈍根性，於廣法教得法究竟深生怖畏，當生邪見，增長邪執，衰損惱壞；或復知彼，法至其手轉布非人；而不施與，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation toward a hostile outsider seeking only to find fault; one who is genuinely ill or mentally deranged; one being deliberately corrected through this withholding; a teaching not yet genuinely mastered oneself; a seeker showing no respect or shame in their manner; one whose limited capacity would only give rise to wrong view and worsening harm from teaching too advanced for them; or one likely to pass the scripture on to unworthy hands.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes jealousy here specifically concerns withholding a written text out of fear the seeker's own resulting wisdom might eclipse one's own standing, while a more general shift in disposition covers simply losing the will to keep teaching consistently once some private irritation has set in — both are genuine, tainted faults, since both put self-interest ahead of a seeker's genuine benefit. He recalls the no-fault list as already familiar from the earlier chapter on giving: an outsider seeking only to win an argument, genuine incapacity to teach a subject not yet mastered, a seeker's own visible disrespect, and, most delicately, a seeker whose limited capacity means teaching that is technically correct could still do them genuine harm by provoking confusion or wrong view rather than insight — withholding in such cases is compassion, not stinginess. This closes sharing the teaching; the remaining precept of this cluster follows in the treatise's own account still ahead.

9. Milestone: Precept Seven Closes — Never Abandoning Even a Violent Wrongdoer

The seventh precept closes this cluster by requiring compassion even toward violently precept-breaking beings — abandoning such a being out of resentment constitutes a tainted violation, since the treatise insists such beings deserve, if anything, even greater compassion than those already practicing well, exactly as a mother's deepest concern falls on whichever child is struggling most rather than the one already thriving.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於諸暴惡犯戒有情，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，由彼暴惡犯戒為緣，方便棄捨，不作饒益，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, harboring resentment or irritation toward a violently precept-breaking being, deliberately abandon them and refuse to help — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

何以故？非諸菩薩於淨持戒、身語意業寂靜現行諸有情所，起憐愍心，欲作饒益；如於暴惡犯戒有情，於諸苦因而現轉者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because a bodhisattva's compassion toward beings of already calm, disciplined conduct is not meant to exceed their compassion toward violently precept-breaking beings actively creating the very causes of their own future suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the treatise's own striking logic here: a being already practicing calmly and well needs relatively little additional concern, while a being actively generating the causes of future suffering through violent, undisciplined conduct needs it most urgently — a bodhisattva who reserves compassion only for those already doing well has the priority exactly backward. He compares this to a mother's own instinctive concern falling hardest on whichever child is struggling or unwell, not because the healthy child matters less, but because urgent need itself calls for urgent compassion. The no-fault exceptions here largely echo those already described — genuine mental illness, deliberately withholding help as a skillful means of correction, protecting a wider community's peace, and observing formal monastic discipline. This closes the entire first cluster of seven giving-related precepts; the second cluster, concerning discipline itself, opens next.

10. Milestone: Discipline-Progression Opens — Observing the Restrictive Precepts Shared With Monastics

The eighth precept opens a new cluster of seven concerning discipline itself, requiring a bodhisattva to observe the same restrictive precepts the Buddha established for monastics — governing proper conduct and etiquette rather than genuinely wrong acts in themselves — precisely because even monastics focused primarily on their own liberation never abandon protecting others' growing faith through visibly disciplined conduct, so a bodhisattva, whose entire priority is benefiting others, should uphold these same restrictive precepts all the more diligently, without exception.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，如薄伽梵於別解脫毗奈耶中，將護他故，建立遮罪，制諸聲聞令不造作。諸有情類，未淨信者令生淨信，已淨信者令倍增長。於中菩薩與諸聲聞應等修學，無有差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts should observe exactly as the Blessed One established within the individually liberating monastic code — restrictive precepts established in order to protect others, so that those without faith come to have it, and those with faith have it deepened further. In this matter, bodhisattvas should train exactly as monastics do, without any difference.

Chinese Source

何以故？以諸聲聞自利為勝，尚不棄捨將護他行，為令有情未信者信、信者增長，學所學處，何況菩薩利他為勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because monastics, who prioritize their own liberation, still do not abandon protecting others through their own visible conduct, training in these precepts so that faith arises and deepens in those who witness them — how much more so, then, should bodhisattvas, who prioritize benefiting others above all, uphold this same practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the six classical qualities that earn a buddha the title "Blessed One" — genuine mastery free of affliction, wisdom blazing like fire that burns away every obscuration, physical majesty adorned with the classical excellent marks, a name and reputation known throughout every world, an auspicious presence everyone delights to approach, and complete, tireless virtue benefiting all beings — and distinguishes restrictive precepts, governing outward conduct and etiquette, from precepts against genuinely wrong acts, noting these restrictive precepts exist purely to inspire confidence in onlookers through visibly disciplined behavior, quite apart from whether the underlying act would otherwise be considered wrong in itself.

He outlines the four components traditionally used to organize the full monastic code — formally receiving ordination itself, then undertaking to observe every specific precept received, then observing proper decorum and appropriate conduct in daily life so as to protect others' confidence, and finally holding even the smallest fault in genuine dread, quick to confess and correct it — and stresses a bodhisattva trains in the third of these, proper decorum protecting others' confidence, in exactly the same way a monastic does, since the underlying logic applies with even greater force to someone whose entire purpose is benefiting beings. This closes the precept requiring shared observance of restrictive conduct; the very next precept, strikingly, marks a point where bodhisattvas and monastics diverge entirely.

11. Milestone: Precept Nine — Why a Bodhisattva May Accumulate What a Monk Must Renounce

Unlike the shared precept just described, this next one marks a genuine, deliberate divergence: while monastics cultivate few possessions, few undertakings, and minimal desire through the classical twelve ascetic practices, a bodhisattva, precisely because their purpose is drawing beings toward the teaching rather than personal renunciation alone, may rightly accumulate abundant clothing, alms bowls, fine silk bedding, and even vast quantities of gold and silver — not for private comfort, but as the very means of generosity that first attracts beings before ever leading them toward wisdom.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，如薄伽梵於別解脫毗奈耶中，為令聲聞少事、少業、少希望住，建立遮罪，制諸聲聞令不造作。於中菩薩與諸聲聞不應等學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, unlike monastics, are not bound by the restrictive precepts the Blessed One established within the monastic code specifically to cultivate few possessions, few undertakings, and minimal desire. In this matter, bodhisattvas should not train exactly as monastics do.

Chinese Source

何以故？以諸聲聞自利為勝，不顧利他……如是菩薩為利他故，從非親里長者、居士、婆羅門等及恣施家，應求百千種種衣服……生色可染百千俱胝，復過是數亦應取積。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because monastics prioritize their own liberation, not the benefit of others ... whereas bodhisattvas, for the sake of benefiting others, may rightly request hundreds and thousands of garments even from unrelated donors and generous households ... and may accumulate even vast quantities of gold and silver beyond that, and rightly gather it all in.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes the classical twelve ascetic practices this precept exempts a bodhisattva from — four concerning food, such as begging door to door without preference and never eating after midday; two concerning clothing, wearing only patchwork robes and never more than the three basic garments; and six concerning dwelling, living in genuine solitude, moving beneath a different tree every few days to avoid even subtle attachment to a favorite spot, and sitting upright without lying down — practices whose entire purpose is uprooting craving for material comfort in a practitioner focused solely on personal liberation.

He explains a bodhisattva's own exemption rests on an entirely different logic: generosity is itself one of the six perfections, and a bodhisattva unable to gather abundant resources cannot actually practice generosity on the scale their vow requires, nor draw beings toward the teaching through the classical principle of first meeting a genuine worldly need before ever introducing something more profound. This closes the precept marking this genuine divergence; the practical consequence of holding such abundance responsibly, distinguished through four classical ways of actually using what is given, follows next.

12. Milestone: Four Ways of Using What Is Given — Thief, Debtor, Heir, or Master

Whether accumulating abundant resources like a bodhisattva or living simply like a monastic, anyone receiving material support falls into one of four classical categories: using it like a thief, having no genuine merit to warrant it at all; using it like a debtor, technically entitled to it yet consumed without the reflection that would make it genuinely wholesome; using it like an heir, the way an advanced practitioner already assured of eventual buddhahood rightly receives what is, in a sense, already their own inheritance; or using it like a true master, the way a fully realized arhat or buddha receives anything at all with complete equanimity, entirely untouched by craving whether the object is fine or humble.

Chinese Source

盜賊受用……欠債受用……繼承者的受用……主人受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Using support like a thief ... using it like a debtor ... using it like an heir ... using it like a true master.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains thief-like use as consuming what generous donors offer while possessing no genuine virtue at all to warrant it — a serious matter, since accepting support given in good faith without any real practice behind it quietly incurs a debt never actually earned. Debtor-like use describes someone genuinely entitled to receive support through their monastic or bodhisattva standing, yet consuming it without the reflection that would make the consumption genuinely wholesome — the treatise recommends two specific contemplations here: first, analyzing whatever is received, whether food, clothing, or shelter, as merely a temporary composite of basic elements and sensory qualities, with no fixed, independent nature of its own; and second, contemplating it as genuinely impure, since even a fine robe becomes something to release attachment toward once its function is properly understood, precisely to prevent craving from quietly attaching itself to the gift.

Heir-like use he describes as belonging to practitioners already advanced enough — a bodhisattva established on the first ground and beyond, or, more broadly, any of the seven kinds of noble disciple already assured of eventual complete liberation — that receiving support is less like being given something new and more like an heir finally receiving what was, in a real sense, always destined to become theirs. And master-like use belongs only to a fully realized arhat or buddha, whose complete freedom from craving means they receive anything at all, fine or humble, with the same untroubled equanimity, genuinely free rather than merely restrained. He notes the treatise's own practical advice for ordinary practitioners in between: formal practices like consecrating money as impure before use, or reflecting on the five contemplations before a meal, exist precisely to help maintain this same mindful awareness even before genuine mastery has been reached. This closes the four ways of using what is given; the treatise's own most philosophically significant teaching on discipline — when genuine compassion may override an ordinarily wrong act entirely — opens next.

13. Milestone: When Compassion Overrides an Ordinary Precept — The Classical Doctrine of Skillful Exception

The treatise now introduces one of its most consequential and carefully qualified teachings: a bodhisattva who, through genuinely skillful judgment and purely out of compassion for beings, occasionally and only in small degree performs what would ordinarily constitute one of the classically wrong deeds, incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts whatsoever and instead generates substantial merit — since what actually completes a genuinely wrong deed requires five factors together, including affliction itself, and a bodhisattva's own act, however similar in outward form, lacks that affliction entirely, motivated purely by love and compassion for the being involved.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，善權方便，為利他故，於諸性罪少分現行，由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, through skillful judgment and purely for the sake of benefiting others, occasionally and only in small degree perform what would ordinarily constitute one of the classically wrong deeds — through this very cause, they incur no violation of the bodhisattva precepts at all, and instead generate substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that any of the classically wrong deeds only fully completes as genuine unwholesome karma when five factors are all present together: a genuine object the deed is directed toward; a matching perception, correctly identifying that object as intended; genuine willing desire to carry out the act, not mere coercion by another; real affliction — craving, aversion, or delusion — driving it; and an actual, completed action carrying the intention through to its conclusion. A bodhisattva's own rare exercise of this exception may well involve a genuine object, a matching perception, and even a deliberate, completed action, yet crucially lacks the fourth factor entirely — no affliction whatsoever drives it, only genuine love and compassion for the being involved — which is precisely why it does not complete as the same kind of unwholesome deed at all, however similar its outward form.

He surveys three classical scholarly positions on exactly who may rightly invoke this exception: one view restricts it strictly to bodhisattvas who have already reached the first ground and directly realized emptiness, since only such settled realization can guarantee genuinely selfless motivation; a second view extends it to any bodhisattva who has genuinely realized the emptiness of a fixed self, even prior to the first ground, since it is precisely the absence of self-interest that makes the exception valid; and a third, more cautious position holds it may extend even to an ordinary, still-unrealized bodhisattva acting from genuine humility and compassion in a truly unavoidable circumstance — but only ever as a response forced by circumstance never actively sought out, approached with real trepidation rather than casual confidence, and never treated as an accomplishment to be proud of. This closes the general principle and its careful qualifications; the treatise's own specific account of this exception applied to killing — illustrated through the classical story of a single life taken to save five hundred — opens next.

14. Milestone: The Exception Applied to Killing — Better That I Go to Hell Than He

The treatise's first and most famous application of the skillful-exception doctrine: a bodhisattva who sees a bandit about to murder many beings, harm a genuinely accomplished practitioner, or commit one of the five gravest possible deeds — the kind that ripens into rebirth in the uninterrupted hell — may, out of pure compassion and after careful reflection, kill that person first rather than let the deed complete, reasoning that a single lifetime of suffering the killer's own karmic consequence is a far smaller harm than the immeasurable suffering that beings' completed crime would bring upon them.

Chinese Source

謂如菩薩見劫盜賊，為貪財故，欲殺多生，或復欲害大德聲聞、獨覺、菩薩，或復欲造多無間業。見是事已，發心思惟：我若斷彼惡眾生命，墮那落迦，如其不斷，無間業成，當受大苦；我寧殺彼墮那落迦，終不令其受無間苦。如是菩薩意樂思惟，於彼眾生或以善心、或無記心，知此事已，為當來故深生慚愧，以憐愍心而斷彼命。由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose a bodhisattva sees a bandit who, driven by greed for wealth, intends to kill many beings, or intends to harm an accomplished monastic, solitary realizer, or bodhisattva, or intends to commit one of the deeds that ripen into the uninterrupted hell. Seeing this, the bodhisattva reflects: "If I take this evil being's life, I myself will fall into hell. But if I do not, his uninterrupted-hell-ripening deed will be completed, and he will suffer greatly for it. I would rather kill him and fall into hell myself than let him suffer the uninterrupted torment." Reflecting with this resolve, and knowing the matter clearly through a wholesome or karmically neutral mind, the bodhisattva — feeling deep and genuine shame at what is to come, and moved purely by compassion — takes that being's life. Through this very cause, the bodhisattva incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and instead generates substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi devotes real care to what counts as "the deeds ripening into the uninterrupted hell": traditionally five direct acts — killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing an arhat, causing a schism in the harmonious monastic community, or drawing a buddha's blood — plus a wider category of deeds "of the same class," such as slandering the true dharma or violating a nun who has attained the fruits of the path, all of which share the same terrible character: suffering with literally no interruption in time, no interruption in space (the hell is always full, however many beings enter), no interruption in the intensity of pain, no distinction of who suffers it, and no interruption across countless cycles of dying and being instantly reborn to suffer again. Weighing the killer's single, bounded lifetime of consequence against a victim's incalculably longer torment is precisely what moves the bodhisattva to act. He also notes the *Yogācārabhūmi Commentary*'s important restriction here: this exception presupposes a bodhisattva who has reached the grounds and possesses genuine mind-reading power — only such a bodhisattva can actually know, with certainty rather than mere suspicion, that the other person's murderous intention is real and otherwise unstoppable. An eighth-ground-or-above bodhisattva like Avalokiteśvara, dwelling constantly in the direct realization of reality without any effortful application, acts only from wholesome mind in such cases; bodhisattvas from the first through seventh grounds, still moving in and out of meditative absorption, may act from either wholesome or karmically neutral mind. An ordinary, pre-ground bodhisattva simply cannot invoke this exception at all, since there is no way to genuinely know another person's murderous intent with the certainty the exception requires.

Vasubandhu's commentary on the *Compendium of the Great Vehicle* frames the same passage slightly differently, and Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the authoritative gloss on what "wholesome mind or karmically neutral mind" actually means in the root text: it is precisely the mind-reading cognition itself, arising either from settled meditative concentration (called wholesome mind) or from supernormal power (called karmically neutral mind) — two descriptions of the same underlying faculty, not two different kinds of motivation. Vasubandhu adds the striking image of the bodhisattva as a skilled physician: inflicting a small, immediate pain — cutting the diseased limb — in order to spare the patient a much greater, much longer suffering. Killed with this beneficial intention, the wrongdoer incurs almost no fault upon the bodhisattva and generates abundant merit instead, precisely because the bodhisattva's own future suffering is willingly accepted on the wrongdoer's behalf; and it is this very willingness, arising from a heart of genuine benefit, that swiftly propels the bodhisattva toward unsurpassed complete awakening. This closes the killing exception; the treatise turns next to a second, related exception involving political power — removing a tyrannical ruler or minister from office.

15. Milestone: Deposing a Tyrannical Ruler or Minister

A second, closely related exception: a bodhisattva who sees a king or a powerful minister ruling with extreme cruelty, utterly without compassion for the people under their power, may — motivated purely by compassion for those being oppressed, and to whatever extent their own standing and ability allow — act to have that ruler or minister deposed or removed from office, without incurring any violation of the bodhisattva precepts at all.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見有增上增上宰官，上品暴惡，於諸有情無有慈愍，專行逼惱。菩薩見已，起憐愍心，發生利益安樂意樂，隨力所能，若廢、若黜增上等位。由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees a supremely powerful king or a supremely powerful minister acting with extreme, top-grade cruelty, utterly without compassion for any being, devoted entirely to oppressing and tormenting others. Seeing this, the bodhisattva gives rise to a heart of compassion, generates the resolve to bring benefit and ease, and, to whatever extent their own strength and ability permit, has that ruler or minister deposed or removed from their position of power. Through this very cause, the bodhisattva incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and instead generates substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the doubled character "supremely powerful, supremely powerful" (增上增上) as naming two distinct offices in one breath: the first "supremely powerful" names the king, whose authority governs every official and every commoner beneath him and so ranks highest of all; the second names a powerful minister — a prime minister or chief counselor — who ranks highest specifically among the other officials. Both share the same essential danger: whoever holds this much concentrated power, if genuinely without compassion, can inflict oppression on an entire population at once, which is precisely why the exception belongs here, directly after the killing exception, rather than being treated as an unrelated matter of politics that the śrāvaka precepts never had occasion to address.

He is careful to note exactly how this differs from ordinary anger-driven harm. Deposing the tyrant certainly resembles committing the karma of hatred — the bodhisattva is, after all, deliberately working to harm this person's position and standing. But it only resembles it: since the bodhisattva's own mind carries no actual aversion, only compassion for the ruler's countless present and future victims, and since acting resembles a violation of the great compassion that grounds the whole of the bodhisattva path without actually being one, the text can truthfully say there is no violation at all, only merit generated. He adds a plain, practical justification: a single ruler or minister governs the fate of an entire territory's worth of beings, so removing one such person from power, however aversive it looks from the outside, serves the welfare of the many — precisely the calculus a bodhisattva, unlike an ordinary person acting from private grievance, is equipped to make correctly. This closes the political exception; a third exception, concerning the forcible reclaiming of stolen property, follows next.

16. Milestone: Forcibly Reclaiming What Belongs to the Three Jewels

A third exception, applied to the precept against taking what is not given: a bodhisattva who sees bandits, or corrupt stewards entrusted with monastic property, treating stolen or misappropriated goods belonging to the monastic community or to a stupa as their own private possession may, purely to spare them the long, meaningless suffering that comes from wrongfully consuming what belongs to the Three Jewels, forcibly take it back — and must then return it precisely to its rightful owner: to the community if it was the community's, to the stupa if it was the stupa's, to the individual being if it belonged to a being.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見劫盜賊奪他財物，若僧伽物、窣堵波物，取多物已執為己有，縱情受用。菩薩見已，起憐愍心，於彼有情發生利益安樂意樂，隨力所能，逼而奪取……由此因緣，所奪財寶，若僧伽物，還復僧伽；窣堵波物，還窣堵波；若有情物，還復有情。……又見眾主、或園林主，取僧伽物、窣堵波物，言是己有，縱情受用。菩薩見已，思擇彼惡，起憐愍心，勿令因此邪受用業，當受長夜無義無利，隨力所能廢其所主。菩薩如是雖不與取，而無違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees a bandit seize another's property — property belonging to the monastic community, or to a stupa — and, having taken a great deal of it, treats it as personal possession, consuming it freely. Seeing this, the bodhisattva gives rise to a heart of compassion, generates the resolve to bring that being benefit and ease, and, to whatever extent their strength permits, forcibly takes it back... Through this cause, the reclaimed wealth is returned precisely to its owner: if it belonged to the community, it is returned to the community; if to a stupa, to the stupa; if to a being, to that being. ...Suppose too the bodhisattva sees a community steward or a groundskeeper of monastic property take goods belonging to the community or a stupa, declare them personal possessions, and consume them freely. Seeing this, the bodhisattva weighs the wrongness of the act, gives rise to a heart of compassion, and — so that this wrongful consuming will not bring the steward long, meaningless suffering — to whatever extent their strength permits, removes that person from their position of trust. Though the bodhisattva has in this way taken what was not given, there is no violation, and substantial merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi points out how strikingly contemporary this exception feels. A monastic passes away without having properly arranged the disposition of monastery-owned property; lay relatives, unaware of or indifferent to its status, inherit it and simply use it as their own. If those relatives cannot be persuaded to return what genuinely belongs to the Three Jewels, a bodhisattva is permitted to reclaim it by force rather than let the family go on wrongfully consuming what was never truly theirs — the same long, meaningless suffering described throughout this section of exceptions is what such wrongful consumption is understood to bring about, and it is purely to spare the family that consequence, not out of any personal claim on the property, that the bodhisattva acts.

He also distinguishes the two kinds of dishonest custodian named in the second scene: a "community steward" (眾主) is specifically one entrusted with property belonging to the monastic community as a whole, while a "groundskeeper" or "garden-master" (園林主) is entrusted specifically with a stupa's property or grounds. Both scenarios share the identical structure and identical justification — reclaim what is misappropriated, restore it to its rightful owner, and, where the wrongdoer holds an ongoing position of trust, remove them from it so the wrong cannot continue. Master Cháng Bǎi notes a genuinely modern parallel: temples where a lay caretaker effectively controls the resident monastic's finances, or even makes demands on temple funds — the same corrective applies. This closes the third exception; a fourth, restricted specifically to lay bodhisattvas, follows next.

17. Milestone: An Exception for Lay Bodhisattvas Only — Sexual Misconduct

A fourth exception, explicitly restricted to a bodhisattva living at home rather than one who has taken ordination: if an unattached woman, belonging to no one else, has become genuinely infatuated with the bodhisattva and seeks intimacy, and the bodhisattva judges that refusing her would provoke deep resentment and much unwholesome karma while accepting would allow her to be skillfully guided toward wholesome roots, the bodhisattva may, motivated purely by compassion rather than desire, engage in the act without incurring any violation — a door the text closes immediately and explicitly for any bodhisattva who has taken ordination.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩處在居家，見有母邑現無繫屬，習婬欲法，繼心菩薩求非梵行。菩薩見已，作意思惟：勿令心恚，多生非福，若隨其欲，便得自在，方便安處令種善根，亦當令其捨不善業。住慈愍心，行非梵行。雖習如是穢染之法，而無所犯，生多功德。……出家菩薩，為護聲聞聖所教誡，令不壞滅，一切不應行非梵行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva living at home sees a woman presently belonging to no one else, given over to habitual sensual craving, who becomes infatuated with the bodhisattva and seeks intimacy. Seeing this, the bodhisattva reflects: "If I do not do as she wishes, resentment will arise in her heart and much that is unwholesome will follow; if I comply with her wish, she will find ease, and I can skillfully settle her in a way that plants wholesome roots, and eventually lead her to abandon the unwholesome altogether." Abiding in a heart of compassion, the bodhisattva engages in the act. Though this involves what is ordinarily an impure and defiling deed, there is no violation, and substantial merit is generated. ... An ordained bodhisattva, however, in order to protect the śrāvaka precepts' own sacred instruction from being undermined, must never engage in such conduct under any circumstance whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is precise about the conditions that must all be present before this exception can apply: the woman must be "presently unattached" — not already married, and not under anyone else's protection or claim — and her feeling must be genuine, habitual infatuation rather than a passing impulse. Her attachment forming specifically toward the bodhisattva, and her explicit seeking of intimacy, together set up the dilemma the bodhisattva must weigh: refusal risks real resentment and the substantial unwholesome karma that resentment toward a bodhisattva generates (just as, he notes, drawing near a bodhisattva in devotion generates immeasurable merit, so pushing a bodhisattva away in resentment generates a correspondingly immeasurable harm to oneself); compliance, by contrast, opens a door through which the bodhisattva can skillfully plant the seeds of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom in her, and in time lead her away from the unwholesome pattern altogether. "Wholesome roots" here means precisely those seeds; "abandon the unwholesome" means she eventually gives up seeking such encounters altogether, having been genuinely helped rather than merely indulged.

The explicit closing line restricting this entirely to lay bodhisattvas is, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, not incidental but essential: an ordained bodhisattva's visible conduct is itself a support for other people's faith in the monastic community as a whole, and the śrāvaka precepts' prohibition on sexual conduct exists specifically to protect that visible integrity — a protection a bodhisattva must never undermine regardless of any private compassionate calculation, however genuine. This closes the fourth exception, and with it the two exceptions concerning bodily conduct proper; the treatise turns next to four parallel exceptions concerning speech, beginning with false speech told to save lives.

18. Milestone: Lying to Save Lives

The first of four speech-related exceptions: a bodhisattva who could, through deliberate false speech, free many beings from mortal danger, imprisonment, mutilation, or similarly brutal punishment will knowingly and deliberately speak falsely to save them — even though, the text stresses, the bodhisattva would never tell such a lie to save their own life — because the bodhisattva's concern throughout is purely for what genuinely benefits others, never anything self-interested.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩為多有情解脫命難、囹圄縛難、刖手足難、劓鼻刵耳剜眼等難，雖諸菩薩為自命難，亦不正知說於妄語，然為救脫彼有情故，知而思擇，故說妄語。以要言之，菩薩唯觀有情義利，非無義利，自無染心，唯為饒益諸有情故，覆想正知而說異語。說是語時，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva, in order to free many beings from mortal danger, imprisonment, or the danger of having their hands and feet cut off, their nose or ears sliced off, or their eyes gouged out, will — though such a bodhisattva would never knowingly speak falsely even to save their own life — for the sake of rescuing those beings, knowingly and deliberately choose to speak falsely. In short, a bodhisattva considers only what genuinely benefits beings, never what lacks benefit, and, the mind itself entirely free of any self-interested motive, purely to bring beings benefit, deliberately sets aside their own true perception and knowingly speaks words other than the truth. In speaking such words, the bodhisattva incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts, and instead generates substantial merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a direct line to a passage later in the treatise, in the discussion of the bodhisattva's "difficult practices": there, it says that even facing sudden mortal danger, a bodhisattva who has taken the precepts will not compromise them in the slightest, let alone violate them outright — which is precisely why this passage insists a bodhisattva would never lie merely to save their own life. The exception exists exclusively for the sake of others, never for the bodhisattva's own benefit, and this asymmetry is the entire point: a bodhisattva who lied to save themselves would be acting from ordinary self-preservation, indistinguishable from anyone else's fear; a bodhisattva who lies to save someone else is acting from the same compassionate calculation found throughout this whole section of exceptions.

He also explains the technical term the text uses for this kind of speech — "different words" (異語) rather than simply "false words." The distinction matters: it names speech that differs from the bodhisattva's own genuine perception and genuine knowledge of the facts, deliberately and knowingly set aside for a specific compassionate purpose, rather than speech born of confusion, self-deception, or any actual departure from truthful character. Master Cháng Bǎi offers a vivid recent-history example: resistance figures during the Japanese occupation who told invading soldiers deliberate falsehoods in order to protect civilians from harm — precisely the kind of "different words" this passage has in mind, and precisely the kind of act a bodhisattva may rightly perform. This closes the exception for false speech; two related exceptions concerning divisive and harsh speech, each aimed at freeing a being from harmful company or open wrongdoing, follow next.

19. Milestone: Divisive Speech to Break a Harmful Friendship, and Harsh Speech to Correct Open Wrongdoing

Two further speech exceptions, closely paired: a bodhisattva who sees beings caught in the grip of a harmful friendship — kept close by someone who will lead them toward great and meaningless future suffering — may deliberately speak words designed to drive the two apart; and a bodhisattva who sees beings behaving in ways that violate ordinary decency and worldly propriety may address them with harsh, forceful reproach, in both cases purely in order to draw the being out of an unwholesome situation and back toward what is wholesome.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見諸有情為惡朋友之所攝受，親愛不捨。菩薩見已，起憐愍心，發生利益安樂意樂，隨能隨力說離間語，令離惡友，捨相親愛……菩薩如是以饒益心說離間語，乖離他愛，無所違犯，生多功德。又如菩薩，見諸有情為行越路，非理而行，出麤惡語，猛利訶擯，方便令其出不善處安立善處。菩薩如是以饒益心，於諸有情出麤惡語，無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees beings taken in by a harmful friend, bound to that friend by affection they will not let go. Seeing this, the bodhisattva gives rise to a heart of compassion, generates the resolve to bring benefit and ease, and, to whatever extent their ability allows, speaks words designed to divide the two, freeing the being from the harmful friend and severing the mutual attachment... Speaking such divisive words with a heart of genuine benefit in this way, and thereby severing another's affection, the bodhisattva incurs no violation, and substantial merit is generated. Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees beings acting outside the bounds of proper conduct, behaving unreasonably, and addresses them with harsh, forceful reproach, skillfully drawing them out of an unwholesome place and settling them in a wholesome one. Speaking such harsh words to beings with a heart of genuine benefit in this way, the bodhisattva incurs no violation, and substantial merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "harmful friend" of the first exception specifically with non-Buddhist teachers who set themselves up as masters over their own followers purely to extract material support, honor, and personal advantage — teachers who, precisely because they care only for the present and never for their followers' future welfare, draw their students toward exactly the kind of long, meaningless suffering this whole section keeps returning to. A bodhisattva who sees a being closely attached to such a figure is permitted to speak words that deliberately create distance between them — an act that would ordinarily count as the karma of divisive speech, since it does produce a rift, but is entirely free of the malice that makes divisive speech a fault, being motivated purely by concern for where the friendship is leading.

The harsh-speech exception he treats as its direct counterpart, aimed not at a bad companion but at the wrongdoer's own conduct: someone visibly violating ordinary worldly decorum and behaving against reason may need forceful, even stinging reproach — precisely the kind of speech a bodhisattva would never otherwise use — to be shaken out of the pattern and redirected toward what is wholesome. Both exceptions share the same underlying logic already established for lying: the outward form resembles a fault (division sown, harshness spoken) but the governing motive is entirely compassionate benefit, with no ill will present at all, which is exactly why neither incurs any actual violation. This closes both exceptions; the eighth and final exception, concerning idle or frivolous talk used to build genuine rapport, follows next.

20. Milestone: Idle Talk as Skillful Rapport — The Eighth Exception Closes the Set

The eighth and final exception, and in some ways the most socially striking: a bodhisattva genuinely skilled in worldly entertainments, songs, and the ordinary gossip people love to discuss — talk of rulers, of thieves, of food, of romance, of whatever the street corner is presently absorbed in — may deliberately engage in exactly this kind of idle talk with beings drawn to it, not for personal enjoyment but purely to win their genuine trust and affection, so that once that bond is formed, the bodhisattva can skillfully guide them out of an unwholesome way of living and into a wholesome one.

Chinese Source

又如菩薩，見諸有情信樂倡妓、吟詠、歌諷，或有信樂王、賊、飲食、婬蕩街衢無義之論，菩薩於中皆悉善巧，於彼有情起憐愍心，發生利益安樂意樂，現前為作綺語相應種種倡妓、吟詠、歌諷、王、賊、飲食、婬衢等論，令彼有情歡喜引攝，自在隨屬，方便獎導出不善處安立善處。菩薩如是現行綺語，無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suppose further that a bodhisattva sees beings who delight in performance, song, and verse, or who delight in talk of rulers, of bandits, of food and drink, of romance, or the meaningless chatter of the street corner. Genuinely skilled in all of it, the bodhisattva, moved by compassion for these beings and the resolve to bring them benefit and ease, engages right there in exactly this kind of idle talk — performance, song, verse, talk of rulers, bandits, food, and romance — so that these beings, delighted, are drawn in and freely attach themselves to the bodhisattva, who then skillfully guides and leads them out of an unwholesome way of living and into a wholesome one. Engaging in idle talk in this way, the bodhisattva incurs no violation, and substantial merit is generated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that classical commentary divides idle talk into exactly two categories, both named in this passage: playful, entertaining speech — song, dance-accompanying verse, musical composition, whatever falls under sheer amusement — and ordinary crowd conversation, the kind of talk that dominates any gathering of people: politics, crime, cuisine, drink, fashion, romance, current events, history, geography, even sailors' tales of the sea's treasures. None of it bears any real connection to the four noble truths or the path to liberation, which is precisely why, in an absolute sense, virtually all worldly conversation qualifies as idle talk — yet the bodhisattva is explicitly permitted, even expected, to be genuinely skilled in all of it.

The purpose he stresses is entirely instrumental and entirely compassionate: beings are drawn toward a bodhisattva who can speak their own language and share their own interests in a way they find genuinely delightful, and it is precisely this delight, this voluntary and easy attachment, that gives the bodhisattva an opening to eventually redirect them toward the wholesome — an opportunity that would never arise if the bodhisattva simply refused, out of misplaced propriety, to ever discuss anything but the dharma directly. This closes the full set of eight exceptions to the classically wrong deeds — four concerning bodily conduct (killing, deposing a tyrant, taking back what is not given, and, for lay bodhisattvas only, sexual misconduct) and four concerning speech (lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle talk) — together constituting one of the most distinctive features separating the bodhisattva precepts from the śrāvaka precepts: where the latter permit no exception whatsoever to these eight classically wrong deeds, the bodhisattva precepts recognize that compassion, entirely free of affliction, can transform the very same outward act into a source of merit. The treatise turns next to an entirely different kind of precept: the prohibition against wrong livelihood.
