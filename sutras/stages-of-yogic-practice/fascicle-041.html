Fascicle 41 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十一 · The Minor Bodhisattva Precepts Begin

The Forty-Three Minor Precepts

Continuing directly from the four gravest root violations already covered, the treatise now turns to discipline's own comprehensive detail: forty-three specific minor precepts, thirty-two supporting the six perfections and eleven supporting the four means of embrace — beginning with six honest ways to check oneself for violation, and the very first precept itself: never letting a single day pass without genuine reverence for the three jewels.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 41 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle Forty-One Opens — The Structure of the Forty-Three Minor Precepts

Directly continuing from the four root violations already covered, the treatise now details the comprehensive body of minor precepts supporting the remaining two storehouses of discipline: thirty-two precepts under gathering wholesome practice, organized around progress through each of the six perfections in turn, and eleven precepts under benefiting beings, organized around the four means of embrace — together with six honest, practical ways a bodhisattva can check whether any given lapse genuinely counts as a violation at all.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於有違犯及無違犯，是染非染、軟中上品，應當了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts should clearly understand, regarding any given matter, whether it constitutes a violation or not, whether it is tainted or untainted by affliction, and whether it falls into a mild, moderate, or severe degree.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the overall structure: the thirty-two precepts under gathering wholesome practice divide into six clusters matching each perfection in turn — seven concerning giving, seven concerning discipline, five concerning patience, two concerning diligence, three concerning meditative concentration, and eight concerning wisdom; while the eleven precepts under benefiting beings divide around the four means of embrace, addressing companionship, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and shared work in turn. He notes traditional enumerations of this same material vary slightly between forty-one, forty-two, and forty-three total precepts depending purely on how certain closely related items are grouped or split, with no actual difference in content.

He then lays out six honest, practical ways a bodhisattva can examine whether a given lapse genuinely constitutes a fault: a contemptuous mind, disrespecting some aspect of the teaching without ever genuinely trying to understand it; a lazy mind, neglecting to do what should be done through simple laziness; a mind covered by affliction, one of the five hindrances or a related affliction currently clouding judgment; a weary mind, genuinely worn out after sustained, vigorous effort; a mind affected by illness, including genuine mental illness; and a mind obstructed by excessive socializing, drawn into idle conversation that serves no one's genuine benefit. In each case, he notes, the treatise's own consistent principle applies: if the underlying state is recognized and not indulged or accepted as acceptable, there is no fault; only continuing to tolerate and indulge the underlying laxity, rather than correcting it, turns an understandable lapse into a genuine violation. This closes the overview of the whole system; the very first of the forty-three precepts, requiring daily reverence for the three jewels, follows next.

2. Milestone: Precept One — Never a Day Without Genuine Reverence for the Three Jewels

The first minor precept requires a bodhisattva to show genuine reverence toward the Buddha, the teaching, and the noble community every single day — through offerings ranging from abundant to modest, but at an absolute minimum through a single bow, a single four-line verse of praise, or, at the very least, a single moment of pure, remembering faith — since letting an entire day pass with no reverence shown at all constitutes a genuine violation of this foundational precept.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，於日日中，若於如來、或為如來造制多所；若於正法、或為正法造經卷所；若於僧伽，謂十方界已入大地諸菩薩眾。若不以其或少、或多諸供養具而為供養，下至以身一拜禮敬，下至以語一四句頌讚佛法僧真實功德，下至以心一清淨信隨念三寶真實功德。空度日夜，是名有犯，有所違越。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas abiding in the bodhisattva precepts, every single day, toward the Tathāgata or a monument built for the Tathāgata, toward the true teaching or a scripture recording it, and toward the noble community — the bodhisattvas already established on the noble grounds across the ten directions — should make some offering, whether modest or abundant; at the very least, a single bow of respect, a single four-line verse praising the genuine qualities of the Buddha, the teaching, and the community, or, at the least, a single moment of pure faith calling those genuine qualities to mind. Letting an entire day and night pass with none of this at all constitutes a violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses this precept sets an extraordinarily low floor precisely so that no genuine excuse — busyness, poverty, distance from a temple — can ever justify complete neglect: even a single bow, a single remembered verse, or a single moment of sincere, believing recollection satisfies it entirely. He explains genuine reverence properly extends to the entire community of practitioners on the bodhisattva path, not merely those already fully realized — since every ordinary monastic is, in truth, a future buddha still in training, just as every being without exception carries the same potential, dismissing reverence for the imperfect while reserving it only for the already-accomplished misses the point of genuine devotion entirely.

He cites the classical verse from a foundational treatise as a model worth memorizing: praising the Buddha's own attainment of complete liberation, the teaching's own fearless, undefiled realization now handed down, and the genuine disciples of the Great Vehicle who faithfully transmit it — noting the verse deliberately honors all three jewels together in a single, brief recitation exactly of the kind this precept requires as a bare minimum. He explains that whether this daily lapse counts as a tainted violation, carrying real affliction, or an untainted one depends entirely on the underlying cause: disrespect or laziness taints it with genuine affliction, while a single, honest lapse of mindfulness amid otherwise sincere practice does not. This closes the precept's basic requirement; the specific circumstances under which no fault at all is incurred, including a striking account of how advanced bodhisattvas naturally never lapse in this way, follow next.

3. Milestone: When There Is No Violation — Genuine Illness, and an Advanced Bodhisattva's Natural, Unbroken Reverence

Two circumstances incur no fault at all under this precept: genuine mental illness or derangement, since a mind not functioning normally cannot meaningfully be said to violate anything at all; and, at the opposite extreme, a bodhisattva who has already reached the first ground and beyond, whose settled realization and enduring aspiration mean reverence for the three jewels has become entirely spontaneous and unbroken, requiring no deliberate effort to sustain at all.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂心狂亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one whose mind is genuinely deranged.

Chinese Source

若已證入淨意樂地，常無違犯。由得清淨意樂菩薩，譬如已得證淨苾芻，恆時法爾於佛法僧以勝供具承事供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a bodhisattva have already entered the ground of purified aspiration, there is never any violation at all — a bodhisattva of such purified aspiration, exactly as a monastic who has attained genuine, verified confidence, naturally and spontaneously serves and reveres the Buddha, the teaching, and the community with the finest offerings at every moment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a bodhisattva who has reached the first ground, the ground of great joy, actually beholds buddhas directly through two conditions: a genuinely settled faith, confident enough to trust that buddhas across the ten directions truly exist even without having personally verified it, and a genuine, resolute vow to actually behold one — citing the model of the fourth-century teacher Huiyuan, who, through sustained devotional practice and firm aspiration, reportedly beheld Amitābha directly in meditation on several occasions before his own death, a vivid illustration of how faith and resolve together can bring what might otherwise seem merely aspirational into genuinely lived experience.

He notes such a bodhisattva has attained what the tradition calls the four confirmed certainties regarding the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and discipline itself — a settled confidence so thoroughly rooted that it persists unshaken across every future rebirth without exception, meaning reverence for the three jewels no longer requires deliberate practice at all, having become as natural and spontaneous as breathing. This closes the two circumstances under which this first precept incurs no fault; the second precept, addressing excessive craving for others' offerings, opens next.

4. Milestone: Precept Two Closes — Struggling Against Deep-Rooted Craving Is Not Yet a Fault

The second precept addresses craving others' offerings and never feeling satisfied, incurring no fault at all when a beginning practitioner is genuinely trying to overcome that craving through sustained, sincere effort — even if the same innate, habitual craving, carried over from countless past lives, still occasionally breaks through despite real effort — since the treatise recognizes such deep-rooted tendencies cannot always be immediately mastered, however genuine the underlying wish to overcome them.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂為斷彼，生起樂欲，發勤精進攝彼對治。雖勤遮遏，而為猛利性惑所蔽，數起現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who, wishing to overcome this craving, diligently applies themselves to its antidote — even if, despite this sincere effort, the intense, innate affliction still repeatedly breaks through and manifests.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "innate affliction" as habitual tendency carried forward from countless past lives rather than a fault newly created in this one — some practitioners simply carry a stronger seed of craving or aversion than others, entirely apart from anything done wrong in this present life. He notes this leniency applies specifically to a beginning practitioner still building genuine strength against long-entrenched habit — someone who has practiced for decades and still shows no real progress against the same affliction cannot claim quite the same excuse. This closes the second precept; the third, addressing genuine courtesy toward respected elders and fellow practitioners, follows next.

5. Milestone: Precept Three — Welcoming Elders and Fellow Practitioners With Genuine Courtesy

The third precept requires genuine courtesy toward respected elders and fellow practitioners on the path — rising to greet them, offering the better seat, and engaging warmly in conversation — a fault, and a tainted one, when neglected out of arrogance or resentment, but only an untainted fault when neglected merely through laziness, forgetfulness, or simple unfamiliarity with proper etiquette; a long list of genuine circumstances, from illness to actively protecting a teacher's own concentration or the wider community's peace, incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，見諸耆長有德可敬同法者來，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不起承迎，不推勝座。……是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, seeing a respected elder or genuinely admirable fellow practitioner arrive, who, overcome by arrogance and harboring resentment or irritation, do not rise to greet them or offer them the better seat ... this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

非憍慢制，無嫌恨心、無恚惱心，但由懶惰、懈怠、忘念、無記之心，是名有犯，有所違越，非染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should this arise not from arrogance or resentment, but simply from laziness, forgetfulness, or genuine unfamiliarity with what to do, this is an untainted violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes genuine social awkwardness or an upbringing that simply never taught proper hospitality from actual disrespect — someone who never learned how to welcome a guest is not thereby holding contempt for that guest, and the fault, while real, carries no genuine affliction behind it. He lists the treatise's own extensive no-fault circumstances: genuine illness or mental confusion; being asleep while a visitor mistakenly believes otherwise; being actively engaged in teaching or hearing the dharma oneself; deliberately withholding a greeting in order to protect a teacher's own concentration while listening, or to protect a whole community's shared quiet; using silence itself as a skillful means of correcting someone who needs it; and observing a monastic community's own formal discipline toward someone under sanction. This closes courtesy toward visitors already present; accepting genuine invitations extended by others follows next.

6. Milestone: Precept Four — Accepting Genuine Invitations

The fourth precept requires accepting a sincere invitation to receive support — refusing out of arrogance or resentment denies the host their own chance to build merit and constitutes a tainted violation, while refusing merely from laziness is untainted — with a substantial list of genuine circumstances, from illness and danger on the road to actively protecting one's own meditative practice or shielding others from a host acting in bad faith, incurring no fault whatsoever.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他來延請，或往居家、或往餘寺，奉施飲食及衣服等諸資生具，憍慢所制，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，不至其所，不受所請，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, invited to a home or another temple to receive food, clothing, or other necessities, who, overcome by arrogance and harboring resentment, refuse to go or accept the invitation — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，或有疾病；或無氣力；或心狂亂；或處懸遠；或道有怖；或欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；或餘先請；或為無間修諸善法，欲護善品令無暫廢……或復知彼懷損惱心，詐來延請；或為護他多嫌恨心；或護僧制；不至其所，不受所請，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who is ill, without strength, mentally deranged, invited somewhere too distant, facing genuine danger on the road, declining in order to skillfully correct the host instead, already committed elsewhere, protecting an unbroken course of meditative practice ... aware the invitation is offered in bad faith, protecting others from resentment, or observing the community's own formal discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes an invitation extended purely to build the host's own merit from one extended specifically to hear teaching in pursuit of genuine liberation — refusing either without real cause denies the host precisely what they sought by inviting a bodhisattva in the first place. He walks through the treatise's own extensive list of legitimate reasons for declining: genuine illness or exhaustion, an unreasonably distant location, real danger along the way, a prior commitment already made, an unbroken retreat or course of study a bodhisattva is unwilling to interrupt, a first direct breakthrough toward genuine realization still being consolidated, sensing the invitation is offered with hidden ill intent, or protecting either an individual or the wider community from an association that would cause more harm than good. This closes accepting genuine invitations; receiving valuable gifts and offerings, where refusal out of resentment carries a striking additional weight, follows next.

7. Milestone: Precept Five — Accepting Valuable Gifts, Where Refusal Out of Resentment "Abandons the Being"

The fifth precept concerns accepting valuable offerings — refusing them out of resentment or irritation constitutes a tainted violation described in unusually strong terms, since it amounts to abandoning the very being making the offering, while refusing merely from laziness remains only an untainted lapse; a substantial list of legitimate reasons — including the risk of one's own attachment, the donor's likely future regret, or the offering's own questionable or already-spoken-for origin — incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他持種種生色可染、末尼、真珠、琉璃等寶，及持種種眾多上妙財利供具殷勤奉施，由嫌恨心、或恚惱心，違拒不受，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯，捨有情故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, offered fine jewels or abundant, excellent material support with genuine sincerity, who refuse it out of resentment or irritation — this constitutes a tainted violation, since it amounts to abandoning the very being making the offering.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，或心狂亂；或觀受已心生染著；或觀後時彼定追悔；或復知彼於施迷亂；或知施主隨捨隨受，由是因緣定當貧匱；或知此物是僧伽物、窣堵波物；或知此物劫盜他得；或知此物由是因緣多生過患，或殺、或縛、或罰、或黜、或嫌、或責；違拒不受，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation for one who is mentally deranged, who foresees genuine attachment would arise from accepting it, who foresees the giver would surely come to regret it, who recognizes the giver is confused about the nature of giving, who knows accepting would leave the giver genuinely impoverished, who recognizes the object belongs to the community or a monument, who knows it was obtained through theft, or who foresees accepting it would lead to real harm — being killed, bound, punished, removed from office, resented, or blamed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the striking phrase "abandoning the being" as underscoring how personal a refusal driven by resentment actually is — declining a gift for practical reasons is one thing, but declining it specifically to spite the giver rejects the person behind the gift, not merely the object itself. He walks through the treatise's own detailed exceptions: recognizing that accepting a particular object would itself feed one's own attachment; foreseeing that the giver, perhaps acting impulsively, would likely regret the gift once given; recognizing the giver does not actually understand what genuine giving even means; knowing acceptance would leave the giver in genuine hardship; recognizing the object already belongs to the wider community or a sacred monument rather than to the giver personally; knowing it was stolen; or foreseeing that accepting it would expose the giver to real danger, whether violence, punishment, or social consequence. This closes accepting valuable gifts; sharing the teaching with genuine seekers, a precept already anticipated at length in the earlier chapter on giving, follows next.

8. Milestone: Precept Six — Sharing the Teaching With Genuine Seekers

The sixth precept requires sharing the teaching itself, whether spoken instruction or written scripture, with anyone genuinely seeking it — refusing out of resentment or jealousy toward a seeker's own future understanding constitutes a tainted violation, while refusing merely from laziness remains untainted — and, echoing the giving chapter's own extensive treatment, a substantial list of genuine circumstances, from a hostile questioner seeking only to find fault to a seeker likely to develop wrong view from teaching too advanced for them, incurs no fault at all.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩安住菩薩淨戒律儀，他來求法，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，嫉妒變異，不施其法，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, approached by a genuine seeker of the teaching, who, out of resentment, irritation, or jealousy, refuse to give it — this constitutes a tainted violation.

Chinese Source

無違犯者，謂諸外道伺求過短；或有重病；或心狂亂；或欲方便調彼、伏彼，出不善處安立善處；或於是法未善通利；或復見彼不生恭敬，無有羞愧，以惡威儀而來聽受；或復知彼是鈍根性，於廣法教得法究竟深生怖畏，當生邪見，增長邪執，衰損惱壞；或復知彼，法至其手轉布非人；而不施與，皆無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no violation toward a hostile outsider seeking only to find fault; one who is genuinely ill or mentally deranged; one being deliberately corrected through this withholding; a teaching not yet genuinely mastered oneself; a seeker showing no respect or shame in their manner; one whose limited capacity would only give rise to wrong view and worsening harm from teaching too advanced for them; or one likely to pass the scripture on to unworthy hands.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes jealousy here specifically concerns withholding a written text out of fear the seeker's own resulting wisdom might eclipse one's own standing, while a more general shift in disposition covers simply losing the will to keep teaching consistently once some private irritation has set in — both are genuine, tainted faults, since both put self-interest ahead of a seeker's genuine benefit. He recalls the no-fault list as already familiar from the earlier chapter on giving: an outsider seeking only to win an argument, genuine incapacity to teach a subject not yet mastered, a seeker's own visible disrespect, and, most delicately, a seeker whose limited capacity means teaching that is technically correct could still do them genuine harm by provoking confusion or wrong view rather than insight — withholding in such cases is compassion, not stinginess. This closes sharing the teaching; the remaining precept of this cluster follows in the treatise's own account still ahead.
