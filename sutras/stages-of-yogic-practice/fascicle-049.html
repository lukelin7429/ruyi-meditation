Fascicle 49 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十九 · The Chapter on the Grounds

Thirteen Abodes Regrouped Into Seven Grounds

The third yoga foundation of ultimate practice comprises five chapters — modes of rebirth, being received into buddhahood, the grounds, conduct, and establishment. This fascicle opens the third of these, the chapter on the grounds, reorganizing the thirteen abodes already explained into seven broader grounds, explaining how a bodhisattva first becomes forever incapable of falling to the lower realms of rebirth, and detailing the ten dharmas that purify an ordinary bodhisattva's own mind even before the lineage-abode and the abode of resolute practice give way to the first ground itself.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 49 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle 49 Opens the Chapter on the Grounds — Seven Grounds Named

Fascicle 49 opens with a note on the treatise's own overall structure: the third yoga foundation of ultimate practice comprises five chapters — modes of rebirth, being received into buddhahood, the grounds, conduct, and establishment. A note on the source material itself: the lecture notes underlying frascicle 48's own translation conclude mid-explanation of the abode of ultimate accomplishment, the tenth and final abode within the chapter on the thirteen abodes, without reaching that chapter's own overall summary or the two intervening chapters on rebirth and being received into buddhahood — chapters the historical treatise does contain at this point, but which do not appear in the specific lecture-transcript source available for this translation. This fascicle's own source material resumes directly with the third of the five chapters just named: the chapter on the grounds, opening with the overall naming of seven grounds. As previously explained across the thirteen abodes, one should understand that seven grounds are established corresponding to them: the first six correspond solely to the bodhisattva stage, while the seventh gathers both bodhisattva and Tathāgata together as a single, mixed ground. The question is posed: what are these seven? The answer lists them: the lineage-ground, the ground of resolute practice, the ground of pure, excellent aspiration, the ground of correct practice in conduct, the ground of certainty, the ground of certain conduct, and the ground of ultimate accomplishment.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the treatise now offers a second organizing scheme for territory already covered across fascicles 47 and 48: the thirteen abodes trace fine-grained, sequential progress, while these seven grounds regroup that same progress under broader headings better suited to certain kinds of doctrinal comparison — a shift in altitude rather than a shift in content. He cites the Sutra of Benevolent Kings' own distinction between the two terms: entering principle through wisdom is called an "abode," since it names the moment of correspondence with ultimate truth itself, while an abode's own generation of further merit is called a "ground," since it names the fertile capacity to produce abundant merit and wisdom once that correspondence is established — abode naming the entering, ground naming what grows from having entered.

He flags candidly, before translation resumes, an important gap in the available source material: fascicle 49's own lecture notes state plainly that the historical treatise's fascicle 48 already covered the chapters on modes of rebirth and being received into buddhahood, along with the abode of ultimate accomplishment's own remaining content and the thirteen-abode chapter's own overall summary — yet none of this appears in the specific lecture-transcript file this translation has been working from, which instead ends mid-explanation of the tenth abode's opening aspect. This is a limitation of the available source material itself, not a deliberate editorial choice, and no attempt will be made to fabricate the missing content; this fascicle instead resumes precisely where the available lecture notes themselves resume, with the chapter on the grounds. Having named all seven grounds, he notes, the treatise turns next to explaining how they correspond to the thirteen abodes already explained in detail.

2. Milestone: Mapping Seven Grounds Onto Thirteen Abodes — the Mixed Ground of Both Bodhisattva and Tathāgata

The treatise now explains, across three parts, exactly how these seven grounds correspond to the thirteen abodes. First, naming the final ground as mixed: among these seven bodhisattva grounds, the last is called the mixed ground. Second, mapping the first six precisely onto the abodes: the prior lineage-abode is called the lineage-ground; the abode of resolute practice is called the ground of resolute practice; the abode of utmost joy is called the ground of pure, excellent aspiration; the abodes of heightened discipline, heightened mind, the three abodes of heightened wisdom, and the abode with applied effort having no further mark are together called the ground of correct practice in conduct; and the abode without applied effort, without effortful application, having no further mark, is called the ground of certainty, since a bodhisattva there falls within the third of three kinds of certainty — the commentary cross-referencing the chapter on the qualities of a bodhisattva's own threefold certainty: certainty through abiding in the lineage, certainty through arousing bodhicitta, and certainty through never practicing in vain, this eighth abode specifically fulfilling the third. Third, naming the remaining ground: the abode of unobstructed understanding is called the ground of certain conduct; the abode of ultimate accomplishment together with the abode of the Tathāgata are together called the ground of ultimate accomplishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the correspondence carefully: two grounds (lineage, resolute practice) map to ordinary, unenlightened bodhisattvas prior to the first ground; four grounds map to noble bodhisattvas already on the ten grounds proper — pure excellent aspiration to the first ground alone, correct practice in conduct sweeping together the second through seventh grounds under a single broad heading, certainty to the eighth ground specifically, and certain conduct to the ninth; and the seventh and final ground, ultimate accomplishment, deliberately gathers both the tenth-ground bodhisattva and the fully awakened Tathāgata into a single "mixed" category, since the tenth ground stands so close to buddhahood that the two are treated, for this classificatory purpose, as belonging together.

He explains the commentary's own cross-reference to the three certainties from the chapter on a bodhisattva's own qualities: certainty through abiding in the lineage (an ordinary being with the bodhisattva lineage, encountering the three jewels, being certain eventually to reach awakening); certainty through arousing bodhicitta (a first-ground bodhisattva's own irreversible ultimate aspiration); and certainty through never practicing in vain (specifically fulfilled by the eighth abode onward, where every effort toward benefiting beings bears fruit without exception) — this eighth ground earning the name "ground of certainty" precisely because it is here that the third of these three certainties is completely fulfilled. Having mapped all seven grounds onto the thirteen abodes and pointed ahead to where the Tathāgata's own ground will be treated in full, he notes, the treatise turns next to resolving a specific doubt about how a bodhisattva first becomes forever free of the lower realms of rebirth.

3. Milestone: How a Bodhisattva First Becomes Forever Free of the Lower Realms — Compassion for a Hundred and Ten Kinds of Suffering

The treatise now takes up a specific doubt: when a bodhisattva, passing from the ground of resolute practice, enters the ground of pure, excellent aspiration, how does such a bodhisattva transcend the lower realms of rebirth and the like? The answer explains the underlying cultivation: such bodhisattvas, relying on worldly, purified meditative absorption, have already, while still at the ground of resolute practice, well accumulated the provisions for awakening; toward the hundred and ten kinds of suffering belonging to sentient beings already explained earlier, such bodhisattvas cultivate compassionate concern, with no further reflection besides — the commentary cross-referencing the earlier chapter on offering, drawing near, and the immeasurables for that same catalogue of a hundred and ten sufferings, observing that all experience, pleasant, painful, or neutral alike, is ultimately suffering, and it is precisely this observation that gives rise to the immeasurable quality of compassion. From this same cultivation, such bodhisattvas' compassionate concern and great compassion toward embodied sentient beings across the desire and form realms arises as a direct result.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "worldly, purified meditative absorption" precisely: the four mundane form-realm absorptions, cultivated without any manifest affliction of craving, wrong view, conceit, or doubt present during the absorption itself, yet not yet having severed the underlying seeds of affliction — genuinely purified in manifest conduct, though not yet purified at the root, which is exactly why the term "worldly" qualifies it.

He recalls the hundred and ten sufferings first catalogued many fascicles earlier in the chapter on offering, drawing near, and the immeasurables, and explains why compassion arises specifically from observing that even pleasant and neutral experience is, at bottom, suffering — a superficial observer sees only obvious pain as worthy of compassion, but a bodhisattva's own deeper observation recognizes that pleasant experience, too, is unstable and ultimately unsatisfactory, and even neutral experience remains caught within the same conditioned round; this comprehensive recognition alone is what produces compassion vast enough to be called immeasurable. Having explained how sustained compassionate cultivation produces genuine compassionate aspiration, he notes, the treatise turns next to the specific vow this aspiration gives rise to.

4. Milestone: Vowing to Dwell in the Lower Realms as One's Own Home — Suffering in Sentient Beings' Place

The treatise now explains the vow this compassionate aspiration gives rise to. By this cause and condition, for the sake of benefiting sentient beings in the lower realms, such a bodhisattva vows to dwell in the lower realms as though dwelling in one's own home, making this vow: even should I, by dwelling in such places alone, be able to realize unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening, I would still be able to bear it. In order to remove every sentient being's own suffering, regarding every karma that leads sentient beings to the lower realms, with pure aspiration, such a bodhisattva vows, in their place, to personally undergo the ripening-fruit of that very suffering — so that, ultimately, every unwholesome karma might never again manifest, and every wholesome karma might constantly manifest instead; this is the correct vow such a mind gives rise to.

Commentary: Regarding "with pure aspiration" — arousing, toward sentient beings, an aspiration for their benefit and ease is wholesome, and is for this reason called pure — not because it is subsumed within undefiled, world-transcending meditative absorption.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi likens this vow directly to Kṣitigarbha's own celebrated pledge — "not until the hells are empty will I myself become a buddha" — and recalls Master Hong Yi's own recorded aspiration to personally undergo suffering on every sentient being's behalf in some future life, precisely the kind of vow this passage describes; an ordinary person already finds it exhausting to bear even a small inconvenience on another's behalf, let alone the ripening suffering of an entire lower rebirth, which is exactly why this vow is presented here as something to aspire toward even before one is genuinely capable of it.

He explains the commentary's own careful qualification of "pure": this aspiration is wholesome and therefore called pure, but it is not the undefiled purity of world-transcending wisdom directly realizing emptiness — it remains a still-conditioned, still-compassionate aspiration operating within the framework of self and others, sentient beings genuinely still appearing as objects of this compassion, precisely because a bodhisattva at this stage has not yet directly realized selflessness. He closes with the classical verse capturing this same spirit — "may all sentient beings be free of suffering; I seek no ease for myself" — noting the sobering corollary the text itself draws: a bodhisattva who genuinely wishes to suffer on others' behalf paradoxically never actually falls into the lower realms, whereas one who fears suffering and hoards ease for oneself alone is, ironically, far more likely to end up there. Having explained this vow in full, he notes, the treatise turns next to how this same cultivation actually eliminates the underlying tendency toward lower rebirth.

5. Milestone: The Underlying Tendency Eliminated — A Transformed Basis, and Three Names for One Attainment

The treatise now explains, across three parts, how this cultivation actually eliminates the tendency toward lower rebirth. First, severing coarse dross: through the power of this worldly, purified meditative absorption together with this same compassionate vow, every coarse residue belonging to every affliction connected with the lower realms is entirely removed from its own underlying basis. Second, attaining a transformed basis: because of this severing, such a bodhisattva, before long, attains a transformed basis — ultimately never again performing any deed leading to the lower realms, and never again, with any certainty, going there at all. Third, concluding with alternate names: a bodhisattva who has reached precisely this point is said to have transcended every lower realm, is also said to have transcended the ground of resolute practice, and is also said to have already entered the ground of pure, excellent aspiration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "transformed basis" as this passage's own technical term for a genuine shift at the level of the store-consciousness itself: not merely suppressing an unwholesome inclination through willpower, but actually removing the underlying seeds that would otherwise ripen into lower rebirth, such that the very basis from which future experience unfolds has itself been altered. He notes that this transformation, while genuine and permanent regarding the lower realms specifically, is not yet the complete transformation of the eighth ground and beyond — a first-ground bodhisattva has decisively closed off rebirth in hell, as a hungry ghost, or as an animal, without yet having severed every subtler root of affliction altogether.

He treats the three alternate names given here as simply three ways of describing one and the same milestone from three angles: negatively, as leaving the lower realms behind; comparatively, as leaving the ordinary, unenlightened ground of resolute practice behind; and positively, as arriving at the very first of the ten true grounds. With this question about transcending the lower realms now fully answered, closing the second of the chapter's own three major sections, he notes, the treatise turns next to its third section: the ten dharmas that purify the mind of an ordinary bodhisattva, still at the lineage-ground and the ground of resolute practice, before even this first transition into the noble grounds occurs.

6. Milestone: The Same Ten Purifying Dharmas, Now Applied to the Ordinary Bodhisattva Before the First Ground

The treatise now opens its third major section: displaying the dharmas that purify. As already explained in the earlier chapter on the abodes, faith and the rest are ten dharmas able to purify every abode; one should understand that, here too, they are equally able to purify these grounds — every one of these ten dharmas' own manner of establishment, what each remedies, what each serves as remedy, and their own brief meaning and sequence, should all be understood. The commentary clarifies precisely how this application differs from its earlier appearance: the same ten dharmas — pure faith, compassion, loving-kindness, generous giving, freedom from weariness, mastering treatises, understanding the world, cultivating conscientiousness, firm resolve, and offering to the Tathāgata — are entirely identical in name to those already explained purifying the abode of utmost joy; but where that earlier passage addressed a bodhisattva already on the noble grounds, this passage addresses specifically the ordinary, unenlightened bodhisattva still at the lineage-ground and the ground of resolute practice, prior to the first ground itself — hence, although the same ten names are used, the meaning carries a genuinely different depth, and everything said below about their establishment, remedy, and sequence applies specifically to the lineage-ground and the ground of resolute practice, not to any of the higher grounds already treated in full.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as a lovely instance of a broader principle running through the entire treatise: the same ten dharmas — faith, compassion, loving-kindness, and the rest — genuinely apply at every stage of the path, from an ordinary being who has not yet even aroused bodhicitta all the way through the tenth ground and beyond, yet what each dharma actually accomplishes deepens enormously at each successive stage. Faith for a first-ground bodhisattva purifies subtle residues of doubt regarding profound emptiness; faith for an ordinary being still at the lineage-ground purifies something far more basic — the very absence, so far, of bodhicitta and of the bodhisattva's own foundational training altogether.

He treats this as genuinely encouraging rather than discouraging for a beginning practitioner: the very same ten dharmas fascicle 47 already explained in such rich detail for a first-ground bodhisattva are not reserved for the spiritually advanced alone — an ordinary person just beginning to cultivate faith, compassion, and generosity is already walking the identical path, at its very earliest stage, that eventually flowers into a buddha's own complete perfection of the same qualities. Having established faith as the first of these ten dharmas, remedying the complete absence of bodhicitta and bodhisattva training, he notes, the treatise turns next to explaining the remaining nine dharmas in the same way, each named for the specific deficiency it remedies in an ordinary being not yet on the noble path.

7. Milestone: Compassion, Loving-Kindness, Giving, and Tirelessness — Remedying an Ordinary Being's Own Harmful Intent, Anger, Attachment, and Self-Interest

The treatise now explains the second through fifth dharmas. Second, compassion: harboring the intent to harm sentient beings — resenting, scolding, or wishing ill upon them whenever they fail to satisfy one's own wishes — is what compassion remedies; remedying this, compassion is established. Third, loving-kindness: harboring anger and hatred toward sentient beings is what loving-kindness remedies; remedying this, loving-kindness is established, since loving-kindness bestows happiness, and a bodhisattva specifically wishes beings to attain the happiness of ultimate truth itself, all worldly and even meditative happiness being, in the end, still a distorted, impermanent state that eventually returns to suffering. Fourth, giving: harboring attachment to one's own body, life, and wealth is what giving remedies; remedying this, giving is established — attachment to the body obstructs diligent practice, and attachment to wealth breeds stinginess, preventing the vast generosity needed to form connections with beings. Fifth, freedom from weariness: hoping for repayment from sentient beings, or, upon seeing beings act wrongly, undertaking effort out of attachment to gain rather than genuine care, is what freedom from weariness remedies; remedying this, freedom from weariness is established, since such self-interested motivation obstructs genuinely benefiting others and prevents that benefit from ever reaching completion.

37. Milestone: When the Marks Are First Obtained, and the Four Kinds of Utter Purity Reserved for Buddhahood Alone

Having completed the full catalogue of thirty-two marks and eighty exemplary signs, the treatise turns to distinguishing when they are acquired. Even at the very first ground — the moment a bodhisattva first enters the ground of pure, superior aspiration — these marks and signs have already ripened as their own karmic result. From that point onward, ground by ground, they grow ever more excellent and pure. The commentary clarifies the sequence still further: while a bodhisattva remains in the stage of mere lineage, these marks and signs exist only as seeds dormant within the body, not yet manifest; upon reaching the stage of resolute practice, the bodhisattva begins for the first time to cultivate the very means — the ten wholesome deeds and other such practices — capable of bringing them forth; only upon reaching the ground of pure, superior aspiration, the first ground itself, are they truly said to be obtained; and from every ground thereafter, all the way to buddhahood, they grow continually more excellent and pure. Only upon sitting at the very seat of awakening and attaining complete buddhahood, however, are all the remaining exclusive buddha-qualities — the four kinds of utter purity, together with the ten powers, four fearlessnesses, three foundations of mindfulness, three things requiring no guarding, great compassion, the dharma of never forgetting, the permanent destruction of habitual tendencies, and wisdom of every kind — themselves brought to complete, excellent purity. The four kinds of utter purity are: purity of every basis (the buddha's threefold body — dharma-body, reward-body, and manifestation-bodies — all perfectly complete); purity of every object (the buddha's own pure land and every circumstance perceived, made pure by the purity of the perceiving mind itself); purity of every mind (all eight consciousnesses transformed into their corresponding wisdoms — mirror-like wisdom, wisdom of equality, wisdom of subtle observation, and wisdom of accomplished action — leaving no trace of self-attachment); and purity of every wisdom (every mental factor, whether universal, particular, or wholesome, perfectly matched to wisdom at all times). And should the marks and signs still be of an inferior grade at some earlier point, the text notes, even that inferior grade was already accomplished back at the stage of a lesser, ordinary-level bodhisattva.

38. Milestone: Far From Awakening, Near to Awakening — and Why the Buddha Teaches the Causes of the Marks at All

The treatise now distinguishes far and near in terms of the marks' ripening, and turns from the fruit of the marks to their cause. "Far" describes anyone who has not yet obtained the ripened result of the marks and exemplary signs at all — one still at the stage of mere lineage or resolute practice, an ordinary being who has only just aroused the aspiration for awakening, still distant from the fruit. "Near" describes one who has just obtained that ripened result for the first time, or who, from the first ground onward, progressively attains ever more excellent purity — both are said to be near to awakening. Having finished discussing the fruit, the treatise turns to the cause: the Blessed One, by the power of his own skill in guiding beings, teaches the great assembly which specific virtuous deeds must be performed to bring about the ripened fruit of the marks and exemplary signs. Asked why he bothers to teach this at all, the answer is given directly: beings not yet trained in the dharma take deep delight in performing all manner of unwholesome deeds out of ignorance; these unwholesome deeds are precisely what must be counteracted, and the various wholesome deeds that bring about the marks and exemplary signs are precisely the counteracting force. Before explaining each power individually, the treatise first clarifies the very meaning of the epithet "Tathāgata," or "Thus Come": whatever the Buddha speaks, teaches, or proclaims — in every word of every sūtra, every precept of the vinaya, every treatise of the abhidharma — is entirely as it truly is, without the slightest falsehood; it is for this reason that he is called "Thus Come," one whose every word matches reality exactly as it is. The treatise then begins explaining the first of the ten powers, the power of wisdom regarding what is possible and impossible, by first distinguishing "possible" from "impossible" in terms of cause and effect. A pure result arising from a pure cause, or an impure result from an impure cause, where cause and effect are never mismatched but instead unfold exactly as they truly are — this rightness of cause and effect is called "possible," also called establishment, also called basis, also called arising. Its opposite — where a pure or impure result would be traced to a cause that does not actually correspond to it, contrary to the rightness just described — is called "impossible": for instance, when non-Buddhist teachers claim that every good or bad result ultimately comes from a great sovereign deity rather than from one's own actions, a mismatch of cause and effect that does not correspond to reality at all.

51. Milestone: What Makes This Wisdom "As It Truly Is," and Why It Is Called "Power" at All

Having distinguished possible from impossible, the treatise explains what makes this the wisdom of things "as they truly are," and why it deserves the name "power." Wisdom that is entirely free of every form of over-estimation — the distorted over-reaching that adds to or subtracts from reality — is what is meant by "as it truly is." This freedom from over-estimation is itself explained through three qualities: possessing all-wisdom, wisdom operating without any obstruction across every realm, every phenomenon, every category, and every time; possessing unimpeded wisdom, wisdom that requires no repeated, laborious reflection but instead comprehends everything at once through a single act of attention; and possessing pure wisdom, wisdom in which every affliction together with its habitual residue has been utterly severed. As for the meaning of "power": this wisdom is unsurpassed, since the Buddha alone can raise this lion's roar of wisdom before the great assembly, with no rival able to exceed it; and it is called "power" also because it possesses the capacity to benefit all beings in every possible way, and because it possesses the great, unshakeable might to utterly subdue every demonic adversary and hostile force that might obstruct that benefit.

52. Milestone: Five Names for the First Power — Great Nirvāṇa, Sage-Lordship, Self-Knowing, Turning the Brahma-Wheel, and the Lion's Roar

The treatise now explains the deeper significance of this same power regarding the possible and impossible through five further names. First, it is called attainment of the supreme nirvāṇa: because the Buddha has, across countless eons, faithfully gathered in the cause for perfectly realizing this wisdom exactly as it truly is, and because he can freely manifest and accomplish whatever he wishes — taking rebirth wherever needed to benefit beings — this is called attaining the supreme nirvāṇa; and because nothing surpasses it, it is called "great." Second, it is called the position of the great sage-lord: because it is attained through the eightfold noble path and brings freedom from every calamity and every fear, it is called the position of the great sage-lord — for although śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas are also called sages, the Buddha alone stands as the very summit among sages, and so is called the "great" sage. Third, it is called self-knowing: because the Buddha, having attained unsurpassed awakening, is able to clearly know for himself exactly what he has realized, without needing to rely on any other's confirmation, it is called self-knowing. Fourth, it is called turning the brahma-wheel: having realized awakening for his own benefit, the Buddha, moved by compassion for suffering beings, broadly and impartially reveals to them the path free of desire; this is called "brahma" because it brings beings to purity free of desire, "tranquil" because it brings freedom from affliction and distraction, and "cooling" because it brings freedom from feverish affliction into cool ease — and because the Buddha is the very first to turn this wheel, with generation after generation of noble disciples, arhats, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas continuing to turn it in succession throughout every assembly of beings ever since, it is called the brahma-wheel.
