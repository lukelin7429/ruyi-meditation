Fascicle 32 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十二 · The Third Yogic Process Concludes

The Five Marks and Near-Attainment Concentration

Fascicle 32 completes the third yogic process of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples. It picks up cultivating mental engagement exactly where fascicle 31 left it — how a teacher guides a beginner to grasp five marks suited to his own affliction — and carries practice forward through near-attainment concentration toward the higher meditative absorptions.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 32 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Five Marks a Teacher Guides a Beginner to Grasp

Fascicle 32 opens exactly where fascicle 31 closed, detailing how a teacher instructs a genuine beginner: five specific marks to be grasped through seeing, hearing, or inward reflection, each serving a distinct role in cultivating the four mental engagements.

Chinese Source

謂善通達修瑜伽師，最初於彼依瑜伽行初修業者，如是教誨：善來賢首！汝等今者應依三種取相因緣，或見、或聞、或心比度增上分別，取五種相。一、厭離相，二、欣樂相，三、過患相，四、光明相，五、了別事相。

Ru-Yi Translation

A teacher well versed in yogic practice, at the very outset, instructs a beginner newly taking up yogic conduct thus: "Welcome, excellent one! You should now, by way of three conditions for grasping a mark — seeing, hearing, or empowered discernment through inward reflection — grasp five marks. First, the mark of revulsion. Second, the mark of delight. Third, the mark of danger. Fourth, the mark of brightness. Fifth, the mark of discerning the object."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the three conditions for grasping a mark onto the practical rhythm of daily practice: seeing and hearing typically happen away from the meditation seat — actually witnessing something loathsome or admirable in the world — while inward reflection through empowered discernment happens seated, deliberately reconstructing what was seen or heard as a vivid mental image. He previews how the five marks distribute across the four mental engagements already defined in fascicle 31: revulsion feeds taming the mind, delight feeds moistening the mind, danger reinforces revulsion's force, brightness supports generating pliancy (since a dull, dark mind cannot settle into genuine stillness), and discerning the object underlies purifying wisdom and insight, since insight requires first clearly identifying what is actually being contemplated. A teacher's first task, in other words, is simply making sure a beginner knows how to find each of these five marks at all — everything that follows depends on getting this foundation right.

2. The Mark of Revulsion: Grasped Through Hearing and Through Seeing

Chinese Source

善來賢首！汝等隨所依止彼彼聚落村邑而住於中，若聞所餘彼彼村邑聚落，或男或女，先受安樂，後遭苦厄。或彼男女自遭重病，命終殞歿。或彼男女所有知識、親戚、眷屬遭如是苦。或彼聚落村邑邊際喪失財寶，或是他來彊敵所作，或火所燒，或水所漂，或由惡作而有喪失，或由不善修營事業而有喪失……

Ru-Yi Translation

"Welcome, excellent one! Wherever village or town you dwell in, if you hear that in some other village or town a man or woman, having first enjoyed ease, later met with suffering and hardship; or that such a man or woman was himself struck by grave illness and died; or that such a man or woman's friends, relatives, or family met with such suffering; or that in the vicinity of some village or town, wealth was lost — whether seized by an invading enemy, consumed by fire, swept away by flood, lost through one's own wrongdoing, or lost through poorly managed affairs …"

Chinese Source

若汝現見，非是傳聞；或即於此村邑聚落，非是所餘村邑聚落；或非是此村邑聚落，亦非他人，即汝自身；先所觸證猛利樂受，後還退失，廣說如前。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Or if you yourself directly witness this, not merely hear it secondhand; or if it occurs in this very village where you dwell, not some other; or if it is not even another person but you yourself — having first experienced intense pleasure, only to have it later decline — extensively explained as before."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this graduated sequence — hearing about a stranger's misfortune, hearing about a neighbor's, witnessing it directly, and finally experiencing it oneself — as deliberately calibrated for increasing emotional force: news from a distant village barely registers, while the identical event witnessed firsthand, or suffered personally, lands with real weight. On the digression into causes of lost wealth, he notes the classical commentary's fourfold "household fire" (afflictive desire that squanders funds, an unworthy heir who dissipates the family fortune, household pests that destroy stored grain, and the Brahmanical ritual fires literally consuming offerings) as illustrating how many different mundane channels ordinary loss can travel through — the point of the whole exercise is not any single cause but the sheer breadth of ways impermanence actually manifests in ordinary life, so that a practitioner has ready examples close at hand rather than only abstract doctrine.

3. Milestone: Deep Revulsion Toward Both Decline and Flourishing Alike

The treatise pushes revulsion further than ordinary grief at misfortune: even flourishing itself, precisely because it is fragile and impermanent, becomes an object of the same deep revulsion — leading to a resolve to delight in cutting off the entire cycle.

Chinese Source

汝既如是聞已、見已，應當生起深心厭患。如是生死甚為重苦，所得自體極大艱辛，而於其中有如是等自他衰損差別可得……諸興盛者，雖現法中住諸安樂，於當來世往諸善趣，而是無常，於彼無常現可證得。若有領受興盛事者，後時衰損定當現前；諸有領受衰損事者，後時興盛難可現前。諸興盛事，皆是難得易失壞法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus heard and seen these things, you should give rise to deep revulsion: this cycle of birth and death is a truly grave suffering, and the very existence one obtains within it is fraught with great hardship — such differentiated declines, both one's own and others', can indeed be found within it … As for flourishing, though in this present life one dwells in ease, and in the life to come proceeds to a fortunate destiny, it is nevertheless impermanent, and this impermanence can be directly verified. Whoever now experiences flourishing will, at some later time, certainly meet with decline; whoever now experiences decline finds flourishing difficult to attain again. Every state of flourishing is a thing hard to gain and easy to lose.

Chinese Source

我今於是生死流轉，未般涅槃，未解脫心，難可保信如是衰損、興盛二法，勿現我前……即由此事增上力故，我當至誠喜樂於斷，修不放逸。又我如是多安住故，當於無義能作邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

"I am now still transmigrating in birth and death, not yet passed into nirvāṇa, my mind not yet liberated — I cannot be assured that these two states, decline and flourishing, will not appear before me … Through the empowering force of this very reflection, I shall wholeheartedly delight in cutting this off, and cultivate non-heedlessness. And by abiding thus, again and again, I shall be able to bring this meaningless cycle to its very limit."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as the treatise's deliberate correction of a common misreading of revulsion practice: it is not simply grief at bad news, but the recognition that flourishing itself offers no real refuge, since whatever is gained is, without exception, "hard to gain and easy to lose." He glosses "meaningless" precisely as birth-and-death's own cycle — not "without any value at all" in a general sense, but specifically unworthy of being clung to as a genuine source of lasting good, since it is inherently unable to deliver on the security it seems to promise. The resolve that follows — delighting in cutting the cycle off entirely, sustained through non-heedlessness — is what actually converts revulsion from passive discouragement into active motivation, the same distinction drawn earlier between timidity's despair and genuine renunciation's clear-eyed resolve.

4. The Mark of Delight Opens: Examining One's Own Precepts

Chinese Source

汝取如是厭離相已，復應精勤取欣樂相。當自觀察所受尸羅，為善清淨？為不清淨？我或失念、或不恭敬、或多煩惱、或由無知，於諸學處有所違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having thus grasped the mark of revulsion, you should further diligently grasp the mark of delight. You should examine for yourself whether the precepts you have undertaken are well purified, or not purified — whether, through a lapse of mindfulness, through irreverence, through abundant affliction, or through ignorance, you have transgressed any of the precepts undertaken."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the "Compendium of Determinations"' own fourfold account of why precepts are broken — ignorance (simply not knowing the rule), heedlessness (knowing but carelessly forgetting), affliction (overwhelmed in the moment by craving or anger), and disrespect (knowing perfectly well and simply not caring) — mapping the treatise's own four causes onto this same scheme. He notes this self-examination is the deliberate first step of cultivating delight, not revulsion: before a practitioner can genuinely rejoice in the Three Jewels and the path, an honest inventory of one's own actual conduct is required, since delight built on self-deception about one's own precept-keeping would collapse the moment that deception became impossible to sustain.
