Fascicle 32 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十二 · The Third Yogic Process Concludes

The Five Marks and Near-Attainment Concentration

Fascicle 32 completes the third yogic process of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples. It picks up cultivating mental engagement exactly where fascicle 31 left it — how a teacher guides a beginner to grasp five marks suited to his own affliction — and carries practice forward through near-attainment concentration toward the higher meditative absorptions.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 32 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Five Marks a Teacher Guides a Beginner to Grasp

Fascicle 32 opens exactly where fascicle 31 closed, detailing how a teacher instructs a genuine beginner: five specific marks to be grasped through seeing, hearing, or inward reflection, each serving a distinct role in cultivating the four mental engagements.

Chinese Source

謂善通達修瑜伽師，最初於彼依瑜伽行初修業者，如是教誨：善來賢首！汝等今者應依三種取相因緣，或見、或聞、或心比度增上分別，取五種相。一、厭離相，二、欣樂相，三、過患相，四、光明相，五、了別事相。

Ru-Yi Translation

A teacher well versed in yogic practice, at the very outset, instructs a beginner newly taking up yogic conduct thus: "Welcome, excellent one! You should now, by way of three conditions for grasping a mark — seeing, hearing, or empowered discernment through inward reflection — grasp five marks. First, the mark of revulsion. Second, the mark of delight. Third, the mark of danger. Fourth, the mark of brightness. Fifth, the mark of discerning the object."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the three conditions for grasping a mark onto the practical rhythm of daily practice: seeing and hearing typically happen away from the meditation seat — actually witnessing something loathsome or admirable in the world — while inward reflection through empowered discernment happens seated, deliberately reconstructing what was seen or heard as a vivid mental image. He previews how the five marks distribute across the four mental engagements already defined in fascicle 31: revulsion feeds taming the mind, delight feeds moistening the mind, danger reinforces revulsion's force, brightness supports generating pliancy (since a dull, dark mind cannot settle into genuine stillness), and discerning the object underlies purifying wisdom and insight, since insight requires first clearly identifying what is actually being contemplated. A teacher's first task, in other words, is simply making sure a beginner knows how to find each of these five marks at all — everything that follows depends on getting this foundation right.

2. The Mark of Revulsion: Grasped Through Hearing and Through Seeing

Chinese Source

善來賢首！汝等隨所依止彼彼聚落村邑而住於中，若聞所餘彼彼村邑聚落，或男或女，先受安樂，後遭苦厄。或彼男女自遭重病，命終殞歿。或彼男女所有知識、親戚、眷屬遭如是苦。或彼聚落村邑邊際喪失財寶，或是他來彊敵所作，或火所燒，或水所漂，或由惡作而有喪失，或由不善修營事業而有喪失……

Ru-Yi Translation

"Welcome, excellent one! Wherever village or town you dwell in, if you hear that in some other village or town a man or woman, having first enjoyed ease, later met with suffering and hardship; or that such a man or woman was himself struck by grave illness and died; or that such a man or woman's friends, relatives, or family met with such suffering; or that in the vicinity of some village or town, wealth was lost — whether seized by an invading enemy, consumed by fire, swept away by flood, lost through one's own wrongdoing, or lost through poorly managed affairs …"

Chinese Source

若汝現見，非是傳聞；或即於此村邑聚落，非是所餘村邑聚落；或非是此村邑聚落，亦非他人，即汝自身；先所觸證猛利樂受，後還退失，廣說如前。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Or if you yourself directly witness this, not merely hear it secondhand; or if it occurs in this very village where you dwell, not some other; or if it is not even another person but you yourself — having first experienced intense pleasure, only to have it later decline — extensively explained as before."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this graduated sequence — hearing about a stranger's misfortune, hearing about a neighbor's, witnessing it directly, and finally experiencing it oneself — as deliberately calibrated for increasing emotional force: news from a distant village barely registers, while the identical event witnessed firsthand, or suffered personally, lands with real weight. On the digression into causes of lost wealth, he notes the classical commentary's fourfold "household fire" (afflictive desire that squanders funds, an unworthy heir who dissipates the family fortune, household pests that destroy stored grain, and the Brahmanical ritual fires literally consuming offerings) as illustrating how many different mundane channels ordinary loss can travel through — the point of the whole exercise is not any single cause but the sheer breadth of ways impermanence actually manifests in ordinary life, so that a practitioner has ready examples close at hand rather than only abstract doctrine.

3. Milestone: Deep Revulsion Toward Both Decline and Flourishing Alike

The treatise pushes revulsion further than ordinary grief at misfortune: even flourishing itself, precisely because it is fragile and impermanent, becomes an object of the same deep revulsion — leading to a resolve to delight in cutting off the entire cycle.

Chinese Source

汝既如是聞已、見已，應當生起深心厭患。如是生死甚為重苦，所得自體極大艱辛，而於其中有如是等自他衰損差別可得……諸興盛者，雖現法中住諸安樂，於當來世往諸善趣，而是無常，於彼無常現可證得。若有領受興盛事者，後時衰損定當現前；諸有領受衰損事者，後時興盛難可現前。諸興盛事，皆是難得易失壞法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus heard and seen these things, you should give rise to deep revulsion: this cycle of birth and death is a truly grave suffering, and the very existence one obtains within it is fraught with great hardship — such differentiated declines, both one's own and others', can indeed be found within it … As for flourishing, though in this present life one dwells in ease, and in the life to come proceeds to a fortunate destiny, it is nevertheless impermanent, and this impermanence can be directly verified. Whoever now experiences flourishing will, at some later time, certainly meet with decline; whoever now experiences decline finds flourishing difficult to attain again. Every state of flourishing is a thing hard to gain and easy to lose.

Chinese Source

我今於是生死流轉，未般涅槃，未解脫心，難可保信如是衰損、興盛二法，勿現我前……即由此事增上力故，我當至誠喜樂於斷，修不放逸。又我如是多安住故，當於無義能作邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

"I am now still transmigrating in birth and death, not yet passed into nirvāṇa, my mind not yet liberated — I cannot be assured that these two states, decline and flourishing, will not appear before me … Through the empowering force of this very reflection, I shall wholeheartedly delight in cutting this off, and cultivate non-heedlessness. And by abiding thus, again and again, I shall be able to bring this meaningless cycle to its very limit."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as the treatise's deliberate correction of a common misreading of revulsion practice: it is not simply grief at bad news, but the recognition that flourishing itself offers no real refuge, since whatever is gained is, without exception, "hard to gain and easy to lose." He glosses "meaningless" precisely as birth-and-death's own cycle — not "without any value at all" in a general sense, but specifically unworthy of being clung to as a genuine source of lasting good, since it is inherently unable to deliver on the security it seems to promise. The resolve that follows — delighting in cutting the cycle off entirely, sustained through non-heedlessness — is what actually converts revulsion from passive discouragement into active motivation, the same distinction drawn earlier between timidity's despair and genuine renunciation's clear-eyed resolve.

4. The Mark of Delight Opens: Examining One's Own Precepts

Chinese Source

汝取如是厭離相已，復應精勤取欣樂相。當自觀察所受尸羅，為善清淨？為不清淨？我或失念、或不恭敬、或多煩惱、或由無知，於諸學處有所違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having thus grasped the mark of revulsion, you should further diligently grasp the mark of delight. You should examine for yourself whether the precepts you have undertaken are well purified, or not purified — whether, through a lapse of mindfulness, through irreverence, through abundant affliction, or through ignorance, you have transgressed any of the precepts undertaken."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the "Compendium of Determinations"' own fourfold account of why precepts are broken — ignorance (simply not knowing the rule), heedlessness (knowing but carelessly forgetting), affliction (overwhelmed in the moment by craving or anger), and disrespect (knowing perfectly well and simply not caring) — mapping the treatise's own four causes onto this same scheme. He notes this self-examination is the deliberate first step of cultivating delight, not revulsion: before a practitioner can genuinely rejoice in the Three Jewels and the path, an honest inventory of one's own actual conduct is required, since delight built on self-deception about one's own precept-keeping would collapse the moment that deception became impossible to sustain.

5. Milestone: Delight from Precept Purity, and Delight from Overcoming Obstructions

Honest self-examination, once completed, yields two distinct kinds of joy in sequence: contentment at precepts kept clean, and a further gladness at having the very capacity to overcome the obstructions and progress toward the noble fruits.

Chinese Source

汝於如是正觀察時，若自了知戒蘊清淨，雖不作思我當發起清淨無悔，然其法爾尸羅淨者，定生如是清淨無悔。若起如是清淨無悔，雖不作思我起歡悅，然其法爾無有悔者，定生歡悅。如是且於一種歡悅所依處所，汝應生起清淨無悔為先歡悅。

Ru-Yi Translation

"When you examine yourself in this way, if you know for yourself that your moral discipline is pure, then even without deliberately intending to arouse a sense of clean, regret-free conscience, purity of precepts naturally, of itself, gives rise to just such a regret-free conscience. And should such a regret-free conscience arise, then even without deliberately intending contentment, freedom from regret naturally, of itself, gives rise to contentment. Thus, from this one basis alone, you should give rise to contentment led by a clean, regret-free conscience."

Chinese Source

復於除障喜悅處所，當生喜悅。謂我今者尸羅清淨，有力有能安住世尊所制學處；於現法中，能得未得、能觸未觸、能證未證。由是處所生喜悅意。若汝獲得前後所證少分差別……於他圓滿所證差別……及自後時所證差別，當生信解，發喜悅意。如是行相諸適悅意，先名歡悅，今名喜悅，總名悅意。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, from the basis of overcoming obstructions, gladness arises: 'My precepts are now pure; I have the strength and capacity to abide by what the World-Honored One has laid down. In this very life, I am able to attain what is not yet attained, to touch what is not yet touched, to realize what is not yet realized.' From this basis, gladness arises. If you have attained some small measure of progress, comparing what you were before to what you are now … then, by that same empowering force, regarding the perfected attainment of others … and your own future attainment as well, confidence and understanding arise, and gladness follows." Such states of ease are, at first, called contentment, and now called gladness — together called ease of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two carefully: contentment (歡悅) arises simply and directly from clean precepts, a quiet freedom from regret needing no further cause; gladness (喜悅), by contrast, arises specifically from recognizing one's own capacity to overcome the five hindrances and progress toward the four noble fruits, plus the confidence that follows from noticing genuine, if modest, improvement over time — comparing hearing-wisdom now to hearing-wisdom a year ago, say, and finding real growth. He notes the treatise is careful to distinguish precept purity that never lapsed from precept purity restored through genuine confession after a lapse — both count equally, since what matters is the present clean state of conscience, not a spotless unbroken record. Together, contentment and gladness constitute what the treatise simply calls "ease of mind" — the concrete, felt content of the mark of delight, moistening the mind exactly as fascicle 31 promised.

6. Milestone: The Mark of Danger — the Ten Marks, Eight Thoughts, and Five Hindrances Revisited

With revulsion and delight both established, the treatise adds a third mark — danger — sharpening the practitioner's vigilance against exactly the distractions already catalogued across fascicles 30 and 31: the ten marks, the eight discursive thoughts, and the five hindrances.

Chinese Source

汝若如是背諸黑品，向諸白品，由調練心、滋潤心故，復應數數取過患相。謂於所有諸相、尋思及隨煩惱，取過患相。言諸相者，謂色等十相。言尋思者，謂欲等八。隨煩惱者，謂貪欲等五。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having thus turned away from the dark category and toward the bright, through taming and moistening the mind, you should further, again and again, grasp the mark of danger — that is, grasp the mark of danger with respect to all marks, discursive thoughts, and secondary afflictions. By 'marks' is meant the ten marks, beginning with form. By 'discursive thoughts' is meant the eight, beginning with desire. By 'secondary afflictions' is meant the five, beginning with craving."

Chinese Source

如是諸相，能令其心作用遽務；如是尋思，能令其心思慕躁擾；如是隨煩惱，能令其心恆不寂靜……由是令心苦惱而住。是故如是諸相、尋思及隨煩惱，是苦非聖，能引無義。令心散動、令心躁擾、令心染汙。

Ru-Yi Translation

These marks cause the mind to become hurried and preoccupied; these discursive thoughts cause the mind to become restless with longing; these secondary afflictions cause the mind to remain forever unsettled … through this, the mind dwells in distress. Therefore, these marks, discursive thoughts, and secondary afflictions are suffering, not the state of the noble ones, and lead to what is without benefit — causing the mind to scatter, causing the mind to become agitated, causing the mind to become defiled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this passage deliberately reprises the exact same ten marks, eight discursive thoughts, and five hindrances detailed at length across fascicles 30 and 31 — the taming and pacifying stages of the nine mental abidings, and the obstruction of being covered over — now reframed specifically as objects for the mark of danger. His gloss on "suffering, not the state of the noble ones" is precise: this condition belongs only to the ordinary, unawakened mind, since a noble one who has genuinely mastered stillness and insight dwells at ease, free of exactly this agitation. He treats the whole passage as continuous with the "perception of danger" already central to taming and pacifying — the same tool, simply named and re-deployed here as one of the five formal marks a teacher instructs a beginner to cultivate.

7. The Mark of Non-Disturbance Opens: Six Qualities of a Settled Mind

Chinese Source

又汝應依心一境性、心安住性、心無亂性，以六種行正取其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, relying on one-pointedness of mind, the mind's abiding nature, and the mind's undisturbed nature, you should rightly grasp its mark through six aspects."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this opens the counterpart to the mark of danger just completed: having learned to recognize disturbance for what it is, the practitioner now learns to positively recognize its absence — the settled, undisturbed quality of a mind actually resting in one-pointedness. He flags that the six specific aspects are about to be enumerated, continuing the same systematic, checklist-like method that has structured the teacher's instruction to the beginner throughout this entire sequence: nothing is left for the student to guess at on his own.

8. The Six Qualities of Non-Disturbance

Chinese Source

何等為六？一、無相想；二、於無相中，無作用想；三、無分別想；四、於無分別中，無所思慕無躁擾想；五、寂靜想；六、於寂靜中，離諸煩惱寂滅樂想。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the six? First, the perception of markless-ness. Second, within marklessness, the perception of no [distracting] function. Third, the perception of non-discrimination. Fourth, within non-discrimination, the perception of freedom from longing and agitation. Fifth, the perception of tranquility. Sixth, within tranquility, the perception of the bliss of cessation, free of every affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps all six directly onto the three sources of disturbance just catalogued: marklessness and its accompanying freedom from distracting function directly counter the ten marks (form, sound, and the rest no longer triggering any reaction); non-discrimination and its accompanying freedom from longing and agitation directly counter the eight discursive thoughts (no rumination, so no restlessness follows); and tranquility, together with the bliss of cessation once affliction has genuinely departed, directly counters the five hindrances. He notes the six form a natural pair-by-pair progression — each disturbance named, then its absence, then the positive fruit that absence yields — giving the beginner not just something to avoid but a concrete, positive state to actually cultivate in its place.

9. Milestone: The Marks of Brightness and of Discerning the Object

The fourth and fifth marks close the teacher's initial instruction: cultivating an inner image of light to counteract meditative darkness, and then actually visiting a charnel ground — or, failing that, a realistic depiction — to grasp the specific object a beginner dominated by craving is meant to contemplate.

Chinese Source

汝取如是過患相已，復應數數取光明相。謂或燈明、或大火明、或日輪明、或月輪明。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having grasped the mark of danger in this way, you should further, again and again, grasp the mark of brightness — that is, the brightness of a lamp, of a great fire, of the sun's disk, or of the moon's disk."

Chinese Source

既取如是光明相已，復詣塚間取青瘀相，廣說乃至取骨鎖相。汝若不能往詣塚間，當取綵畫、木、石所作如是諸相。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having grasped the mark of brightness in this way, you should further go to a charnel ground and grasp the mark of discoloration, and so on at length, up through grasping the mark of the bone-chain. If you are unable to go to a charnel ground, you should instead grasp such marks as rendered in painting, wood, or stone."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the mark of brightness is entirely practical: eyes closed in seated meditation see only darkness, so a mental image of light — the moon, the sun (ideally at dusk, since midday sun would strain the eyes if actually gazed at), a lamp, or a fire — needs to be deliberately cultivated first, simply so that whatever object comes next can actually be seen clearly in the mind's eye rather than groped for in the dark. The mark of discerning the object then supplies contemplation of impurity's actual content — the same eight stages of corpse decay detailed fully in fascicle 30 — with a clear ranking of vividness: direct exposure to an actual charnel ground first, and only failing that, a realistic rendering. Cháng Bǎi treats these two marks as completing the set: revulsion and delight establish the right emotional orientation, danger sharpens vigilance, brightness makes inner vision possible at all, and discerning the object finally supplies something concrete to actually see.

10. Applying the Marks in Seated Practice

Chinese Source

取是相已，還所住處，或阿練若、或林樹下、或空閑室……先洗足已，結加趺坐，端身正願，安住背念，先於一境令心不散，繫念在前。復於其中，依六種想作意思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having grasped these marks, return to your dwelling place — a forest hermitage, beneath a grove of trees, an unoccupied room … having first washed your feet, sit cross-legged, with an erect body and right aspiration, abiding in mindfulness turned away, first fixing the mind unwaveringly on a single object, holding it in mindfulness. And within this, direct your attention according to the six perceptions."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this passage simply directs the beginner back to fascicle 30's own instructions on seclusion and posture — cross-legged sitting, an erect body, right aspiration, mindfulness turned away — now applied specifically to putting the five just-grasped marks to work: settling on the object, then cycling through the six perceptions of non-disturbance to actually stabilize attention on it. Nothing new is introduced here; the passage's role is purely to show the full sequence assembled into one continuous sitting practice, from grasping the marks outside the meditation hall to actually applying them once seated.

11. Milestone: Discerning Disturbed from Undisturbed Mind

The treatise now teaches the beginner to actively monitor his own mental state — carefully, honestly distinguishing when the mind is disturbed from when it is genuinely settled — as the direct path to one-pointedness.

Chinese Source

又於其中，汝當審諦周遍了知亂不亂相，分明現前……又汝於此亂不亂相，如是如是審諦了知，便能安住一所緣境，亦能安住內心寂止，諸心相續、諸心流注，前後一味，無相、無分別，寂靜而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, within this, you should carefully and thoroughly know the disturbed and undisturbed marks, clearly present before you … And to whatever degree you carefully and thoroughly know these disturbed and undisturbed marks, to that degree you become able to abide in a single object, and able to abide in inward stillness — mind continuing upon mind, mind flowing upon mind, one taste from beginning to end, free of marks, free of discursive thought, proceeding in tranquility."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this self-monitoring as the practical hinge connecting everything taught so far: a beginner who has grasped all five marks but never learns to actually notice, in the moment, whether the mind is currently disturbed or settled gains nothing from the preceding instruction. He explains "mind continuing upon mind, mind flowing upon mind" technically: countless individual moments of cognition arise in rapid succession on the same object, yet because none is interrupted by distraction and none lacks continuity with the one before it, this rapid succession is experienced as a single, unbroken, uniform stream — precisely the flowing continuity already defined as part of samādhi itself back in fascicle 30. The whole exercise, in other words, teaches the beginner to recognize the felt difference between scattered moments and a genuine unbroken stream, since only that recognition lets him actually correct course when disturbance arises.

12. Milestone: The Buddha's Parable of the Oil Bowl and the Executioner's Sword

When distraction persists despite every preceding instruction, the treatise turns to non-attentive engagement — and illustrates its life-or-death urgency with a vivid parable the Buddha himself is said to have taught: a man forced to carry a brimming bowl of oil through a festival crowd, an executioner's drawn sword following one step behind.

Chinese Source

世尊依此所緣境相，密意說言：汝等苾芻，當知眾善……假使有一智慧丈夫，從外而來，告一人曰：咄哉！男子！汝於今者，可持如是平滿鉢油，勿令灩溢；經歷如是大眾中過，當避其間所有眾善，及諸最勝歌舞倡妓、大等生等。今有魁膾，露拔利劍隨逐汝行。若汝鉢油一滴墮地，此之魁膾，即以利劍當斬汝首，斷汝命根。

Ru-Yi Translation

The World-Honored One, with reference to this very object, spoke with hidden intent, saying: "Monks, you should know of 'the crowd's pleasures.' By this is meant, within a great assembly gathered together, youthful beauty in its prime; and the very finest of these pleasures — song, dance, and entertainers, amid the great multitude. Suppose a wise man comes from outside and says to another: 'Listen! Take this bowl, brimful of oil, and do not let it spill. You must pass straight through this great assembly, avoiding all its pleasures — the finest song, dance, and entertainers, and the great and lesser folk alike. Behind you, even now, an executioner follows with sword drawn. Should a single drop of your oil fall to the ground, this executioner will strike off your head and sever your life on the spot.'"

Chinese Source

是持鉢人，既見魁膾，露拔利劍隨逐而行，極大怖畏，專作是念：我所持鉢油既彌滿，經是眾中極難將度；脫有一滴當墮地者，定為如是拔劍魁膾當斬我首，斷我命根。是人爾時，於彼眾善……都不作意、思念、觀視，唯於油鉢專心作意而正護持。如是苾芻！我諸弟子，恭敬殷重，專心憶念修四念住，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Seeing the executioner following with sword drawn, this man, in great terror, thinks only: 'My bowl is brimful of oil, and passing through this crowd will be extremely difficult; should even one drop fall, this sword-bearing executioner will surely strike off my head and sever my life.' At that time, this man pays no attention whatsoever to the crowd's pleasures … giving his whole attention only to guarding the oil bowl correctly." "Just so, monks: my disciples, with reverence and earnestness, single-mindedly holding the four foundations of mindfulness in mind, should be understood to be exactly the same."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi supplies the treatise's own point-by-point decoding of the parable: the crowd's pleasures represent whatever fuels the five hindrances (craving foremost among them); the finest entertainment represents the eight discursive thoughts' idle mental chatter; the "great and lesser folk" represent the ten distracting marks of form, sound, and the rest; the wise man giving the instruction represents the teacher; the brimful bowl of oil represents the beginner's own mind settled in calm-abiding, its bodily-mental pliancy the very thing at stake; and the executioner's drawn sword, following one step behind at every moment, represents the danger-mark already grasped from the ten marks, eight thoughts, and five hindrances — a threat immediate and constant, not distant or abstract. Cháng Bǎi stresses that the parable's power lies precisely in the stakes it assigns to ordinary distraction: a single spilled drop means death, which is exactly the degree of seriousness the treatise wants a practitioner to actually feel toward a single moment of carelessness in practice, not calculated worry but the same instinctive, whole-body vigilance a man would show with a blade at his neck.

13. Milestone: Equipoise — Not Even One Moment of Distraction

The parable's lesson is now stated in technical terms: sustained, unbroken guarding of the mind first requires effort, but matures into equipoise, where not even a single momentary thought turns toward any distraction at all.

Chinese Source

由是能令諸心相續、諸心流注；由精進力，無間策發。前後一味，無相、無分別，寂靜而轉；不起一心緣於諸相，或緣尋思及隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through this, mind continues upon mind, mind flows upon mind; through the power of diligence, it is unceasingly roused. One taste from beginning to end, free of marks, free of discursive thought, it proceeds in tranquility — not a single moment of mind arises directed toward any mark, or toward discursive thought, or toward any secondary affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes two stages within this single passage: "mind continuing upon mind, mind flowing upon mind" describes single-pointed continuity, the eighth mental abiding, still requiring deliberate, sustained effort — hence "through the power of diligence, unceasingly roused." "One taste from beginning to end … not a single moment of mind" describes equipoise, the ninth and final abiding, where that same continuity has become entirely spontaneous, requiring no further effort at all. He stresses the phrase "not a single moment" is deliberately precise — not merely infrequent distraction, but the complete absence of even one single instant, out of the countless individual moments making up sustained concentration, turning toward the ten marks, eight thoughts, or five hindrances. This, Cháng Bǎi notes, is what the oil-bowl parable's life-or-death vigilance actually looks like once fully matured: not effortful strain but effortless, total absorption.

14. The Teacher's Further Guidance: When Stillness Brings Joy, and When to Switch to Insight

Chinese Source

賢首！汝若如是精勤修習奢摩他道，如是方便，攝受正念、正知俱行有喜樂心，乃名善修奢摩他道。若復串習諸過失故，不能於中深心喜樂，極大艱辛勵力策發方現前者；還應速疾出無分別所緣境相，於有分別所緣境相繫念在前，如先所取諸不淨相。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Excellent one! If you diligently cultivate the path of calm-abiding in this way, sustaining mindfulness and clear comprehension together with a joyful mind through such means, this is called skillfully cultivating the path of calm-abiding. But if, through habituated faults, you are unable to find deep joy in it, and it appears only through the greatest hardship and forced effort, you should then swiftly abandon the non-discriminating object and instead fix mindfulness on a discriminating object — such as the marks of impurity previously grasped."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "joyful mind" as the actual diagnostic for whether stillness is being cultivated correctly — genuine calm-abiding practice should feel increasingly pleasant, not increasingly effortful, and a practitioner grinding through sitting after sitting with no ease to show for it should read that as a signal to switch strategies rather than simply try harder at the same approach. He notes different temperaments genuinely need different starting points — a naturally reflective person may need to work through insight first (investigating why scattered thought is pointless) before stillness ever comes easily, while a naturally quiet mind may find stillness immediate and only later need insight's investigation — and adds the *Śamatha-vipaśyanā* teaching that even opposite afflictions can sometimes call for an indirect remedy: deliberately provoking a controlled irritation at one's own uncontrolled anger, say, rather than only ever applying loving-kindness head-on. His closing point is practical: ultimate reliance falls on one's own clear-eyed wisdom, cultivated through sustained study, since teachers themselves are impermanent and cannot be followed indefinitely.

15. Milestone: Generating Vivid Understanding — From One Corpse to Boundless Space

When insight resumes, the treatise now teaches the beginner to expand the visualized object systematically — from a single corpse to two, then ten, then thousands, until the entire directions of space are packed without a single gap.

Chinese Source

汝於如是初修觀時，於一青瘀，廣說乃至於一骨鎖，當起勝解。若於其中已串修習，觀道明淨，於所緣相明了勝解相續轉時，復應於二、於三、於四、於五、於十、二十、三十、四十、五十，或百青瘀，或千青瘀，乃至一切諸方諸維所有青瘀，起無量行遍一切處無間勝解，於中乃至無有容受一杖端處。

Ru-Yi Translation

"When you first cultivate insight in this way, you should generate vivid understanding of a single instance of discoloration, and so on at length, up through a single bone-chain. Once you have habitually practiced this, and the contemplation has become clear and pure, with the object's understanding continuing on lucidly, you should then extend to two, three, four, five, ten, twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, a hundred, or a thousand instances of discoloration, up through all instances of discoloration in every direction and quarter — generating boundless, uninterrupted vivid understanding pervading every place, until there is not even room enough for the tip of a walking staff."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells the story of Venerable Miàojìng's own teaching method here: students prone to distraction during chanted liturgy who nonetheless never miss a beat or a syllable — proof, he says, that a habituated mind is genuinely powerful, and that the goal of vivid understanding is to build the identical strength on the object of practice, so strong that the ten marks, eight thoughts, and five hindrances may still arise around the edges but simply fail to actually disrupt the sustained visualization, the way background noise fails to disrupt a well-rehearsed chant. He notes the progression from one corpse to boundless numbers is not decorative excess but a genuine strength-training exercise: a beginner masters a single, modest image first, and only once that foundation is completely stable does the object expand, each stage building directly on the stability of the one before it — jumping straight to the boundless version without first mastering the single image would simply produce a vague, unstable visualization rather than a vivid one.

16. Milestone: True-Reality Engagement — the Mountain of Bones from Countless Past Lives

The treatise now distinguishes confident-understanding engagement (a deliberately constructed mental image) from true-reality engagement — and delivers one of the fascicle's most striking teachings: the number of corpses each being has actually left behind across beginningless rebirth genuinely exceeds anything the imagination could construct.

Chinese Source

汝依如是勝解作意，應當趣入真實作意。於趣入時應作是念：如我今者勝解所作無量青瘀，廣說乃至無量骨鎖，真實青瘀乃至骨鎖，其量過此不可數知。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Relying on this confident-understanding engagement, you should now enter into true-reality engagement. Upon entering, you should reflect thus: 'The boundless instances of discoloration my confident understanding has constructed, and so on up through boundless bone-chains — the true, actual number of instances of discoloration, up through bone-chains, far exceeds even this, beyond all counting.'"

Chinese Source

從前際來，於彼彼有、彼彼趣中，輪迴生死。我所曾經命終夭歿所棄尸骸、所起青瘀，廣說乃至所起骨鎖，無量無邊……假使有能攝聚如是所棄尸骸，令不壞爛，一切大地亦不容受。於一劫中所棄尸骸，乃至骨鎖，假使有能歛在一處，其聚量等廣大脅山。如從前際，後際亦爾，乃至未能作苦邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

"From beginningless time, transmigrating through birth and death across every mode and every destiny of existence — the corpses I myself have left behind, dying again and again, and the instances of discoloration, up through bone-chains, that arose from them, are boundless and without limit … Were it possible to gather all these discarded corpses together, unrotted, the entire earth could not contain them. Were it possible to gather into a single heap just the corpses discarded within a single eon alone, up through their bone-chains, that heap's size would equal a vast mountain range. And as it has been from beginningless time past, so shall it continue into time to come, for as long as the very limit of suffering remains unreached."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two mental engagements precisely: confident-understanding engagement is a meditator's own deliberately constructed image, built up through practice and genuinely useful, but still, in the end, something added by the practitioner's own imagination; true-reality engagement instead reflects on the actual, literal fact — that across beginningless transmigration, the practitioner's own discarded bodies alone, to say nothing of every other being's, already vastly exceed whatever number the visualization constructed. This is offered as sober fact, not embellishment: even confining the count to a single cosmic eon, the resulting pile of bones would rival a mountain range in size. Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as the moment confident-understanding engagement, useful as a training tool, gives way to the deeper realization it was always meant to build toward — that the imagined vastness point toward something genuinely, literally true about the practitioner's own past, present in every direction, at every scale, for as long as liberation remains unattained.

17. The Precise Rhythm of Alternating Stillness and Insight

Chinese Source

又非修習如是青瘀乃至骨鎖毗鉢舍那應頓觀察。纔應於一尸骸青瘀起勝解已，尋復令心於內寂靜。乃至於此所緣境相喜樂明淨，無諸擾惱，不彊勵力。齊爾所時，應於如是尸骸青瘀發起勝解。若纔於此乃至勵力方現在前，爾時於內應修寂靜。如於青瘀，乃至骨鎖，當知亦爾。由此道理，乃至無量，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Nor should this insight into discoloration, up through the bone-chain, be practiced all at once. As soon as vivid understanding of a single corpse's discoloration has arisen, the mind should promptly be settled into inward stillness. When this object appears joyfully, clearly, and purely, free of any disturbance, without needing to be forced — for as long as that lasts, continue generating vivid understanding of the corpse's discoloration. But the moment it can only be made to appear through forced effort, you should then cultivate inward stillness. As with discoloration, so too, up through the bone-chain — and by this same principle, so too up to the boundless version."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as the practical rule governing everything just taught: insight is never sustained in one continuous stretch, but constantly checked against a simple, felt criterion — does the image still arise easily, joyfully, without strain? As long as it does, continue investigating; the instant it starts requiring visible effort, stop and return to stillness instead. He notes this same "ease test" applies at every scale, from a single corpse through the fully boundless version filling all directions — the object may grow vastly larger, but the underlying rhythm of switching to stillness the moment ease gives way to strain remains exactly the same throughout, which is why the treatise says "by this same principle" rather than spelling out separate instructions for every stage.

18. Stabilizing Vivid Understanding into a Settled Quiet

Chinese Source

如是令心內寂靜已，復應發起寂靜勝解。謂從最後無量青瘀，乃至最後無量骨鎖，內略其心，方便除遣，安置眾相不顯現中，不全棄捨有分別相，亦不分別。唯即於此所緣境界安住其心，無相、無分別，寂靜而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the mind has thus been settled into inward stillness, one should further generate the vivid understanding of tranquility itself. That is, from the final, boundless discoloration, up through the final, boundless bone-chain, one gathers the mind inward, skillfully dispelling these many marks so that they no longer appear — without entirely abandoning the discriminated mark, and without further discriminating it. One simply settles the mind on this same object, and it proceeds free of marks, free of discursive thought, in tranquility.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as the culminating return to stillness after the entire expand-then-contract cycle of contemplating impurity: the boundless field of corpses generated through insight is not abandoned outright — the practitioner isn't pretending it never happened — but is also no longer actively analyzed. The mind simply rests on the same object without either discarding it or continuing to dissect it, exactly the "neither grasping nor abandoning" balance the treatise defined precisely back in fascicle 31's account of correct practice. This settled quiet, strong enough now to remain undisturbed by the very ten marks, eight thoughts, and five hindrances that once troubled it, is what the treatise calls the vivid understanding of tranquility — contemplation of impurity's cycle of expansion and stillness now fully mastered.

19. Milestone: Getting the Object Clear From the Very Start — the Mark of Brightness Applied

The treatise now returns to the mark of brightness with a decisive practical warning: whatever clarity — or murkiness — an object has when first visualized will simply persist and compound through every later repetition, no matter how much practice follows.

Chinese Source

若有最初於所緣境多不分明，數習勝解，其相闇昧；由是因緣，後所修習所有勝解亦不分明；雖多串習，而相闇昧。若有最初於所緣境多分分明，數習勝解，其相明了；由是因緣，後所修習轉復分明；雖少串習，而相明了。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, at the very outset, the object is largely unclear, then repeated practice of vivid understanding will leave its mark obscure; because of this, all later cultivated understanding will likewise remain unclear — however extensively practiced, the mark stays obscure. But if, at the very outset, the object is largely clear, then repeated practice of vivid understanding will leave its mark lucid; because of this, later cultivation becomes ever more lucid still — even with little practice, the mark remains clear.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the point with two contrasting practitioners: one who recites a mantra so rapidly, tallying bead after bead without any real correspondence between hand and voice, that ten circuits pass as a thousand recitations and the whole day's practice adds up to tens of thousands — all without the object ever once being clearly present; and, by contrast, Ajahn Mun's method of teaching villagers to anchor "Bud" to the in-breath and "dho" to the out-breath, fusing the recollection directly to something already vivid and continuous, by which many genuinely attained concentration. He extends the same lesson to visualizing a Buddha image, citing the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*'s instruction to first establish a field of brightness in every direction before beginning, then work through the crown, hair, forehead, brow, and eyes one genuinely clear feature at a time — never blurring past an unclear detail just to move on. His summary is blunt: an object entered vaguely stays vague no matter how long practiced, while an object entered clearly compounds into ever-greater clarity even with modest practice — so the decisive moment is always the very first attempt, not the thousandth.

20. Milestone: Entering the Four Foundations of Mindfulness — the Body Contemplated Within, Without, and Both

Having mastered all five marks together, the practitioner now progresses from contemplation of impurity itself into the four foundations of mindfulness proper — beginning with the body, worked through three stages: one's own inner body, another's outer body, and finally both together.

Chinese Source

汝應先於內身所有三十六物，始從髮毛乃至小便，善取其相……如是名為於內身中修循身觀。次應於外諸不淨物，善取其相。汝當發起青瘀勝解，廣說乃至骨鎖勝解……如是名為於外身中修循身觀。後復應於自身內外諸不淨物，善取其相，令心明了。又於他身內外不淨，善取其相，令心明了……如是名為於內外身修循身觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

"You should first, with respect to the thirty-six substances within your own body, beginning with hair and body-hair through to urine, skillfully grasp their marks … This is called cultivating contemplation of the body with respect to the inner body. Next, with respect to the various impure substances outside, you should skillfully grasp their marks. You should generate vivid understanding of discoloration, and so on at length, up through the bone-chain … This is called cultivating contemplation of the body with respect to the outer body. Afterward, you should skillfully grasp the marks of the impure substances both within and outside your own body, making the mind clear. And with respect to the impure substances both within and outside another's body, you should likewise skillfully grasp their marks, making the mind clear … This is called cultivating contemplation of the body with respect to both the inner and outer body."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the deliberate progression: first one's own thirty-six-part inner impurity (hair, nails, organs, and the rest, already catalogued in full in fascicle 30); then an outer corpse's impurity through its full eight stages of decay; then, most demandingly, both together — one's own body and a loved one's body alike, contemplated as equally impure both now, while living, and in their eventual future as a corpse discarded in a charnel ground, discoloring, swelling, devoured, and finally reduced to a scattered bone-chain. He stresses the explicit inclusion of "someone one is attached to" as the passage's real point: contemplating a stranger's corpse is relatively easy, but contemplating the eventual fate of someone genuinely loved is what actually tests — and gradually dissolves — the craving contemplation of impurity was cultivated to counter in the first place. With all three stages complete, this closes the foundation of mindfulness of the body; the treatise next turns to the three formless aggregates — feeling, perception-and-formations, and consciousness — which, lacking any visible form, must be grasped through language and reflection rather than direct sensory resemblance.

21. Milestone: Feeling, Mind, and Phenomena Contemplated — All Four Foundations Complete

The three formless aggregates — feeling, mind, and mental phenomena — are now contemplated through the same calm-abiding, non-distraction, and insight categories, completing all four foundations of mindfulness and closing the arc that began with contemplation of impurity.

Chinese Source

於奢摩他品者，謂若汝心於內略時，起無相、無分別、寂靜想行……於所緣境無亂受等四無色蘊，剎那剎那展轉別異，唯是新新而非故故，相續流轉……如是名為於內受、心、法修循受、心、法觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the calm-abiding type: when your mind gathers inward, giving rise to the perceptions of marklessness, non-discrimination, and tranquility … the undistracted formless aggregates of feeling and the rest, moment by moment, differ one from the next — each ever new, never the old repeated, continuing on in succession … This is called cultivating contemplation of feeling, mind, and phenomena with respect to the inner.

Chinese Source

於無散亂品者，謂汝於先取諸境界……如是諸法，其性皆是誑幻所作，暫時而有，率爾現前，多諸過患，其性無常，不可保信。汝應如是發起勝解。如是名為於外受、心、法修循受、心、法觀。於毗鉢舍那品者，謂汝善取毗鉢舍那相已，住有相、有分別作意……如是名為於內外受、心、法修循受、心、法觀。如是汝由依不淨觀正修加行增上力故，於四念住當得趣入。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the non-distraction type: with respect to the marks, discursive thoughts, and secondary afflictions arising from a prior lapse of mindfulness … all such phenomena are, by nature, entirely the work of deception and illusion — temporary, arising abruptly, fraught with danger, impermanent by nature, and not to be trusted. You should generate vivid understanding in this way. This is called cultivating contemplation of feeling, mind, and phenomena with respect to the outer. As for the insight type: having skillfully grasped the mark for insight, abiding in engagement that is marked and discriminating … This is called cultivating contemplation of feeling, mind, and phenomena with respect to both inner and outer. Thus, through the empowering force of correctly cultivating the practice by way of contemplation of impurity, you shall enter into the four foundations of mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps these three categories onto a slightly different axis than body contemplation's inner/outer/both: the calm-abiding type observes feeling, mind, and phenomena arising freshly moment to moment without any distracting analysis, simply noting their impermanence; the non-distraction type specifically targets the aftermath of a lapse — catching the resulting marks, discursive thoughts, and hindrances and firmly dismissing them as unreliable illusion, exactly as the *Sūtra in Forty-Two Sections* warns that the untrained mind is never to be trusted except in an arhat; and the insight type actively investigates feeling, mind, and phenomena through the full six-things framework, discovering the same fresh, ever-new impermanence through active discrimination rather than passive observation. With feeling, mind, and phenomena all contemplated this way, the fourth foundation now stands complete alongside the body — and, Cháng Bǎi notes, the entire arc from contemplation of impurity's original five marks through vivid and true-reality engagement has finally delivered the beginner into the four foundations of mindfulness proper, the treatise's own stated destination for this whole sequence.

22. Milestone: Progressing Beyond the Cushion — Safety and Vigilance on the Almsround

Practice does not stop when the practitioner rises from the seat. The treatise now extends the same sustained mindfulness into ordinary daily activity, starting with concrete guidance for physical safety and sense-restraint while begging for alms.

Chinese Source

應當善避惡象、惡馬、惡牛、惡狗、惡蛇、惡獸、坑澗、濠塹、株杌、毒刺、泥水、糞穢，及應遠離諸惡威儀、穢坐臥具。汝應如是善護己身。若於如是諸境界相不應策發諸根，汝應於彼不作功用，善守諸根。若於如是諸境界相應當策發諸根，汝應於彼正作功用，善住正念，令諸煩惱不起現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

"You should skillfully avoid vicious elephants, horses, cattle, dogs, snakes, and wild beasts; pits, ravines, moats, tree-stumps, thorns, mud, and filth; and should keep away from improper deportment and unclean seats or bedding. In this way you should skillfully guard your own body. Where the faculties should not be engaged with such objects, you should apply no effort toward them, skillfully guarding the faculties. Where the faculties should be engaged with such objects, you should rightly apply effort toward them, skillfully abiding in mindfulness, so that no affliction is allowed to manifest."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this list as practical common sense given concrete doctrinal weight: a monk on almsround genuinely encountered dangerous animals, uneven terrain, and unsanitary conditions, and the treatise simply instructs sensible caution rather than treating physical safety as beneath a serious practitioner's concern. He illustrates "not engaging the faculties" with a teaching example — encountering a threatening dog, the correct response is simply to step aside, not to engage further by picking up a stick — while "rightly engaging the faculties" covers situations that genuinely do require active attention and response, mindfulness sustained precisely so that whatever affliction the situation might otherwise trigger (fear, anger, aversion) never actually gets the chance to arise. The two rules together capture a single principle: neither careless indifference to real danger nor unnecessary confrontation with what can simply be avoided.

23. Right Speech, and Avoiding Disputes

Chinese Source

又汝應與在家出家說應量語、說應理語、說應時語、說正直語、說寂靜語。一切世間非法言論，皆當遠離。雖復宣說如法言論，不應諍競。何以故？若諸士夫補特伽羅住諍競語，互相難詰，其心便住多戲論中；多戲論故，其心掉舉；心掉舉故，心不寂靜；不寂靜故，便令其心遠三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, with householders and renunciants alike, you should speak words that are measured, reasoned, timely, upright, and tranquil. Every worldly discourse contrary to the Dharma should be entirely avoided. And even in speaking words in accord with the Dharma, you should not engage in contention. Why is this? Because if persons abide in contentious speech, disputing with one another, their minds come to dwell in much idle discourse; through much idle discourse, the mind becomes agitated; through an agitated mind, the mind loses tranquility; and through lost tranquility, the mind is driven far from samādhi."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses each of the five qualities of speech: measured (never contradicting direct perception, inference, or the Buddha's own teaching), reasoned (consistent with the four principles of mutual dependence, function, valid proof, and natural law), timely (matched to the occasion and the listener's actual capacity to benefit), upright (aligned with the noble path's factors, without distortion), and tranquil (genuinely leading the listener toward freedom from affliction, not merely clever). He lists the classical catalogue of topics to avoid — politics, crime, warfare, food, clothing, romantic and worldly gossip — not because such subjects are inherently forbidden, but because they simply have nothing to do with the practice at hand. His sharpest point concerns disputation specifically: even speech that is technically true and Dharma-related becomes destructive the instant it turns competitive, since the very effort to win — however righteous the underlying point — agitates the mind and drives it straight out of samādhi; a single argument, he notes bluntly, is enough to undo an entire session's concentration. The wiser course, when a listener is not yet ready to hear something, is simply to wait for a better occasion rather than force the point.

24. Milestone: The Vow of Unlimited Practice, and the Simile of Kindling Fire — Contemplation of Impurity's Teaching Closes

The teacher's instruction on contemplation of impurity closes with two final pieces: an image of relentless, uninterrupted effort, and a vow so expansive it dissolves any excuse of insufficient time.

Chinese Source

猶如世間鑽火方便，起無間加行及殷重加行，汝應如是恆常修作、畢竟修作。又汝應起如是願心：假使一切贍部洲人，盡贍部洲曾經壽量，今皆總集在我一身，我亦盡此無量壽命，決定於斷瑜伽作意勝奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，精進修習，時無暫捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Just as, in the world, the method of kindling fire by friction requires uninterrupted and earnest effort, so too should you cultivate constantly and to completion. Further, you should give rise to this resolve: 'Suppose the combined lifespans of every person who has ever lived on this continent were all gathered together into this one body of mine — even with such a boundless lifespan, I would resolutely, diligently cultivate the superior mental engagement of calm-abiding and insight that severs affliction, without abandoning it for even a moment.'"

Chinese Source

由正了知如是所修瑜伽加行，有大勝果、大勝利故，何況如是少分壽量、少時存活，雖極遠去不過百年，委悉算計但須臾頃。如是汝應隨所教誨，恆常修作、畢竟修作。汝當最初證得下劣身心輕安，心一境性，後當證得世出世間廣大圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Knowing correctly that such cultivated yogic practice bears great and excellent fruit, great and excellent benefit — how much more so, then, given how brief a lifespan you actually have, living at most a hundred years, which, carefully reckoned, amounts to no more than a fleeting moment. Thus you should, according to this instruction, cultivate constantly and to completion. You shall first attain a modest degree of bodily-mental pliancy and one-pointedness of mind, and later attain the vast, complete fulfillment of both the mundane and supramundane paths."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the fire-kindling image as a corrective against intermittent effort: friction that stops and starts never generates enough heat to actually ignite anything, no matter how many attempts are made — only sustained, uninterrupted friction produces fire, and sustained, uninterrupted practice is what the analogy demands. The vow that follows he reads as a deliberate thought experiment: even granted every lifespan any human being has ever had, combined into one, the resolve would remain exactly the same — undivided practice without a moment's lapse — which makes the ordinary hundred-year human lifespan, once sleep, eating, and daily necessities are subtracted, look almost laughably brief by comparison. Cháng Bǎi's point: the vow isn't really about the impossible scenario it describes, but about exposing how little of an already-short life gets devoted to practice, and how much smaller the excuse of "not enough time" looks once that math is actually done. With this, the teacher's full instruction on contemplation of impurity — from the five marks through vivid understanding, true-reality engagement, the four foundations, and now this closing vow — comes to its complete close.

25. Milestone: The Same Method Applied to Loving-Kindness — Expanding to Boundless Beings

The treatise now shows the identical five-mark methodology applied to the second purifying-conduct object, loving-kindness, beginning with a single loved one, enemy, and neutral person and expanding outward to fill all of space.

Chinese Source

依慈愍觀初修業者，於外親品、怨品及中庸品，善取相已，處如法座。由利益安樂增上意樂俱行定地作意，先於一親、一怨、一中庸所發起勝解。於此三品，由平等利益安樂增上意樂俱行作意，欲與其樂。如是念言：願彼求樂諸有情類，皆當得樂。謂或無罪欲樂，或無罪有喜樂，或無罪無喜樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

"For a beginner cultivating loving-kindness contemplation: having skillfully grasped the marks of an external loved one, an enemy, and a neutral person, seat yourself properly. Through mental engagement fixed in concentration, joined with the heartfelt wish for their benefit and happiness, first generate vivid understanding with respect to one loved one, one enemy, and one neutral person. Toward these three categories, with mental engagement joined equally with the wish for their benefit and happiness, wish to give them happiness. Reflect thus: 'May all these beings who seek happiness attain happiness' — whether blameless sensory pleasure, blameless pleasure with joy, or blameless pleasure free of joy."

Chinese Source

次後或於二親，或於三親，或於四親，或於五親、十親、二十、三十，如前乃至遍諸方維，其中親品充滿無間發起勝解，於中乃至無有容受一杖端處。如於親品，如是於怨及中庸品，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Next, extend to two loved ones, three, four, five, ten, twenty, thirty, and so on as before, up through all directions and quarters, generating uninterrupted vivid understanding filling every place with loved ones, until there is not even room for the tip of a walking staff. As with the category of loved ones, so too, it should be known, with the categories of enemies and neutral persons."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the deliberate order — loved one first, then neutral, then enemy — matches the graduated cultivation already established back in fascicle 30, easiest first so the resulting warmth has enough momentum to eventually extend to the hardest case. The three levels of wished-for happiness map directly onto the three realms: blameless sensory pleasure for a desire-realm level of contentment, blameless joyful pleasure for the first and second dhyānas (both marked by joy), and blameless joy-free pleasure for the third dhyāna, traditionally considered the most refined bliss within saṃsāra — the fourth dhyāna's equanimous non-feeling is left out here since it isn't, properly speaking, "pleasure" at all. The expansion from one being to boundless beings filling every direction follows the identical strength-building logic already established for contemplation of impurity: master the smaller scope first, then expand only once that foundation is genuinely stable.

26. Loving-Kindness Enters the Four Foundations: Corrupted-Object and Body-Only Mindfulness

Chinese Source

又彼不捨慈愍加行，即由修習如是慈愍，於諸念住能正趣入……謂趣入時，應當發起如是勝解：如彼於我謂親、謂怨、謂中庸品，我既欲樂、厭背其苦。如是名為於其內身修循身觀……如我既爾，彼諸有情亦復如是……我當與彼利益安樂。如是名為於內外身修循身觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, without abandoning the practice of loving-kindness, through this very cultivation of loving-kindness, one is able to rightly enter the foundations of mindfulness … Upon entering, one should generate this vivid understanding: 'Just as this person is, to me, a loved one, an enemy, or a neutral person, I myself likewise wish for happiness and turn away from suffering.' This is called cultivating contemplation of the body with respect to the inner body … 'Just as I am thus, so too are all these beings' … 'I shall give them benefit and happiness.' This is called cultivating contemplation of the body with respect to both inner and outer.

Chinese Source

此四念住，總緣諸蘊為境界故，當知說名壞緣念住。若修行者但取色相，謂取顯相、形相、表相，於親品、怨品及中庸品而起勝解，由此建立唯身念住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Since these four foundations of mindfulness take all the aggregates together as their object, this should be known as corrupted-object mindfulness. If the practitioner takes up only the mark of form — that is, color, shape, and outward appearance — generating vivid understanding of the loved one, the enemy, and the neutral person on this basis alone, this establishes body-only mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes loving-kindness contemplation's own three-category structure (loved one, enemy, neutral person) substitutes for contemplation of impurity's own inner/outer/both, but arrives at the identical destination: wishing oneself well, then wishing others well, then recognizing self and other as fundamentally alike in wanting happiness and fearing suffering — the very insight that made loving-kindness toward an enemy possible in the first place, back in fascicle 30's teaching on no-self. He explains "corrupted" (壞) here not as a moral defect but simply as "mixed" or "undifferentiated" — wishing happiness on someone's aggregates as a whole, without separating out body from feeling from mind from mental phenomena, so that one act of loving-kindness toward the whole person functions as all four foundations simultaneously. "Body-only mindfulness," by contrast, is the narrower practice of attending only to a person's visible form and appearance — useful, but covering only the first of the four foundations rather than all four at once.

27. Milestone: True-Reality Engagement Applied to Loving-Kindness — No Relationship Is Fixed

Loving-kindness's confident-understanding practice now gives way to true-reality engagement: recognizing that across the practitioner's own beginningless past, every being has cycled through every possible relationship, making today's fixed categories of "loved one," "enemy," and "neutral" ultimately unreal.

Chinese Source

如是我從先際已來，所有親品、怨品及中庸品落謝過去諸有情類，其數無量，甚過今者勝解所作。如是過去諸有情類，為我親已，復為我怨；為我怨已，復為我親；為怨、親已，復為中庸；為中庸已，復為怨、親。由是義門，一切有情平等平等，無有少分親性、怨性及中庸性，而非真實。由是因緣，遍於三品起平等心，平等應與利益安樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Thus, from beginningless time, the loved ones, enemies, and neutral persons I have known, who have since passed away, number beyond measure — far exceeding what confident understanding alone constructs. And these past beings, having once been my loved ones, later became my enemies; having been my enemies, later became my loved ones; having been either, later became neutral; and having been neutral, later became either again. By this reasoning, all beings are utterly equal — there is not the slightest true reality to the nature of 'loved one,' 'enemy,' or 'neutral.' For this reason, one gives rise to an equal mind toward all three categories, and should equally bestow benefit and happiness upon them."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes an objection the treatise itself raises and answers: if all these past beings have already passed away, what use is loving-kindness directed at them now? The answer given is that the practice's real purpose is purifying one's own mind of its residual impurity — extending goodwill even toward the already-departed, and toward beings not yet born, functions as a kind of confession and universal blessing rather than a transaction requiring a living recipient. Cháng Bǎi then details the fourfold superiority by which true-reality loving-kindness is said to exceed confident-understanding loving-kindness a hundred, a thousand, and countless times over — superior in scope (encompassing the boundless past and future, not just the present), in strength (like a genuinely healthy body outmatching a frail one), in kind (categorically incomparable, the way wealth and poverty admit no real comparison), and in result (yielding uncontaminated merit capable of reaching buddhahood itself, rather than merely a pleasant but still bound rebirth). The practical takeaway, and the reason no relationship in this life should ever be treated as permanently fixed, is Tsongkhapa's own verse, already cited in fascicle 30: friend, foe, and neutral are none of them determined on reflection, so equanimity, not selective attachment, is what merits cultivating.

28. Milestone: Dependent-Origination Contemplation — Grasping the Mark of Delusion's Fourfold Distortion

The third purifying-conduct object now receives the same treatment, opening with what delusion actually consists of: four specific distortions where beings directly perceive one truth yet stubbornly believe its opposite.

Chinese Source

謂諸有情，由有種種無智愚癡，現見無常，妄計為常；現見不淨，妄計為淨；現見其苦，妄計為樂；現見無我，妄計為我。彼諸有情，有如是等種種顛倒；顛倒為因，於現法受及後所生諸自體中，發起貪愛；由貪愛故，造作種種生根本業；此煩惱、業為因緣故，感得當來純大苦蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Beings, through various kinds of ignorance and delusion, directly perceive impermanence yet falsely conceive it as permanent; directly perceive impurity yet falsely conceive it as pure; directly perceive suffering yet falsely conceive it as pleasure; directly perceive the absence of self yet falsely conceive a self. Possessed of such distortions, and with distortion as their cause, they give rise to craving with respect to both their present experience and their future embodiment; through craving, they perform the fundamental karma that generates rebirth; and through this karma and affliction as cause and condition, they bring about the entire mass of suffering to come."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses these are not failures of information but failures of will: beings genuinely see impermanence, impurity, suffering, and no-self in their own direct experience — old age, sickness, and death are not hidden — yet stubbornly insist on the opposite anyway, precisely because craving needs those comforting distortions to keep functioning. He traces the resulting causal chain compactly: distortion generates craving (for present pleasant experience and for continued future existence alike), craving drives the karma that actually produces rebirth, and that karma, fueled by affliction, delivers the entire resulting mass of suffering. Cháng Bǎi notes this passage functions as the object to be grasped for dependent-origination contemplation exactly as the eight stages of corpse decay functioned for contemplation of impurity — the specific content a beginner is meant to hold clearly in mind before any further development of the practice can proceed.

29. Milestone: Dependent Origination Is Entirely True-Reality Engagement — No Confident Understanding Required

Unlike contemplation of impurity and loving-kindness, both of which begin with a constructed image before advancing to reality itself, dependent-origination contemplation is declared to be true-reality engagement from the very start — because the twelve links simply are the literal truth of how life actually operates.

Chinese Source

謂我今此純大苦蘊，亦如是生。又我自體無邊無際，從先際來初不可知，亦如是生。彼諸有情去、來、現在一切自體，苦蘊所攝，亦皆如是已生、當生。如是緣性緣起正觀，一切皆是真實作意，更無所餘勝解作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

"This entire mass of suffering that is my own body and mind has likewise arisen in just this way. And my own existence, boundless and without limit, whose beginning cannot be known from beginningless time past, has likewise arisen in just this way. All the past, future, and present bodies of these other beings, belonging to the mass of suffering, have likewise already arisen, and will arise, in just this way." This correct contemplation of conditioned dependent origination is, in its entirety, true-reality engagement — there remains no further confident-understanding engagement apart from it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why dependent origination alone skips confident-understanding engagement entirely: contemplating a corpse's decay or wishing another being happiness both require a practitioner to first construct a mental image of something not currently present, but the twelve links are already the literal, present structure of the practitioner's own existence — there is nothing to visualize because it is simply what is already the case, observed rather than imagined. He confirms this contemplation covers a phenomenon's particular characteristics (each link's own specific nature, already catalogued in fascicles 9 and 10), its universal characteristics (impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self shared by the whole chain), and its true-suchness (established through the four principles of reasoning) — all three at once, which is precisely what qualifies the whole practice as true-reality engagement through and through. He notes the same pattern applies equally to the practitioner's own body, to the boundless, unknowable extent of the practitioner's own past lives, and to every other being's past, present, and future body alike — dependent origination governs all of it identically, with no exception anywhere.

30. Milestone: Elemental Analysis Opens — Grasping Each of the Six Elements, Outer Then Inner

The fourth purifying-conduct object opens with a straightforward method: for each of the six elements in turn, first grasp its mark in the outer world, then recognize the identical element within one's own body.

Chinese Source

又於界差別觀初修業者，先取其外所有堅相。所謂大地、山林、草木……取彼相已，復於內堅而起勝解。次取其外諸大水相。所謂江、河、眾流……復於內濕而起勝解。次取其外諸大火相……復於內煖而起勝解。次取其外諸大風相……復於內風而起勝解。次取其外諸大空相……於內空界而起勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

"A beginner cultivating elemental-analysis contemplation first grasps the outer marks of solidity — the great earth, mountains, forests, grass, and trees … having grasped these marks, one then generates vivid understanding of the solidity within. Next, one grasps the outer marks of the great waters — rivers, streams, and their many tributaries … generating vivid understanding of the moisture within. Next, one grasps the outer marks of great fire … generating vivid understanding of the warmth within. Next, one grasps the outer marks of great wind … generating vivid understanding of the wind within. Next, one grasps the outer marks of great space … generating vivid understanding of the space within."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells two vivid stories of accomplished meditators here. Master Fǎjìn, absorbed in water-element concentration in his mountain hut, once appeared to a curious shepherd boy as nothing but a pool of water; the boy tossed in a stone and left, and Fǎjìn emerged from concentration with a persistent internal discomfort he couldn't place — until, instructed by the master, the boy returned, found the stone still floating in what looked like a pool of water, and removed it, resolving the trouble at once. Later summoned by force to heal an ailing empress after refusing twice, the meditative master needed no examination at all — his mere gaze alone made her break into a healing sweat and recover, whereupon he declined all reward, asking that it go to a monastery instead. A second story tells of Xūyún's disciple Ā Biàn, illiterate and unable to explain his own practice, who spent years rising earliest and retiring latest, tending the community's most menial labor, and one day announced he was "taking leave" — only to be discovered outside, seated, having consumed himself entirely in the fire of concentrative absorption. Cháng Bǎi offers both as proof that vivid understanding, sufficiently matured, genuinely transforms what a practitioner experiences as real — not mere visualization but a demonstrated capacity reaching into how the element actually manifests.

31. From Coarse to Subtle: Analyzing the Body Down to Its Smallest Particles

Chinese Source

又於如是四大種中，先起支節麤大勝解，後起分析種種細分微細勝解。如是漸次分析，乃至向遊塵量，如是漸漸乃至極微而起勝解。一一支分尚起無量最極微塵積集勝解，何況身中一切支分。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Further, within these four great elements, one first generates vivid understanding of the coarse, large limbs and joints, and afterward generates vivid understanding by analyzing them into ever finer, subtler divisions. Analyzing in this way, step by step, down to the measure of a mote of dust drifting in sunlight, one generates vivid understanding all the way down to the subtlest particle. Even a single limb, thus analyzed, is understood as an accumulation of boundless subtlest particles — how much more so every limb throughout the entire body."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the analysis from the body's largest structural units (head, arms, legs, torso) down through progressively finer divisions (upper arm, elbow, forearm, palm, fingers) to the "mote of dust" visible drifting in a shaft of sunlight, and finally to the subtlest particle — noting the classical debate over whether such a smallest particle is a genuinely existing, indivisible unit (as certain Hīnayāna schools held) or, as the Mahāyāna maintains, merely a conceptual construct arrived at through analysis, since anything with spatial extent could in principle always be divided further, and nothing with a stable, independent nature of its own can actually be found at any scale. Either way, the point of the exercise is the same: dissolving the felt solidity of "my body" into an ever-finer accumulation of parts directly undermines the pride that depends on treating that body as one solid, substantial, superior thing — precisely elemental analysis's purpose as the antidote to pride.
