Fascicle 67 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十七 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation

Sixteen Kinds of Cultivation, from the Śrāvaka's Own Path to the Bodhisattva's

Maitreya completes the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation, briefly naming and explaining sixteen kinds of cultivation practice, then opens the vast decisive discernment of the Śrāvaka Ground itself — five hard questions resolving whether any being is truly without the lineage for liberation, ten kinds of śrāvaka from a pure lineage to an age of decline, and four of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, from the Moon-Simile Sūtra's pure conduct through the four noble truths and the fifteen kinds of harm craving brings, the transcript itself coming to rest mid-explanation of the truth of origin.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 67 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Sixteen Kinds of Cultivation, Briefly Named

Maitreya now closes the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection and opens the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation. Since the Basic Section's own treatment of this ground already explained, in full and careful detail, the four supporting bases and seven branches by which a practitioner actually cultivates the path, the place and cause upon which cultivation rests, the yogic method itself, and the fruit of cultivation attained, together with the seven branches of a perfected basis, a perfected hearing of the true teaching, taking nirvana as one's foremost aim, the ripening of a liberating wisdom capable of ripening others, cultivating the actual antidote, and complete purification both worldly and transcendent, this decisive discernment need only set forth sixteen essential kinds of cultivation practice for a practitioner's own further reflection and application: mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle, mental engagement linked to the great vehicle, cultivation through an image, cultivation through the very limit of a matter, cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand, cultivation as attainment, cultivation as habituation, cultivation as removal, cultivation as antidote, cultivation in part, cultivation that pervades, cultivation in motion, cultivation still requiring effort, cultivation already accomplished, cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation, and cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation. As one earlier treatise asks, why is it called cultivation at all? Because it pervades what is to be cultivated, it is called cultivation; because it is repeatedly practiced, it is called cultivation; because it thoroughly perfumes the mind, it is called cultivation; because it is learned again and again, it is called cultivation; and because it makes the mind bright and pure, it is called cultivation.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection has been explained. The decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation, I shall now explain. One should know there are, briefly, sixteen kinds of cultivation: mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle, mental engagement linked to the great vehicle, cultivation through an image, cultivation through the very limit of a matter, cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand, cultivation as attainment, as habituation, as removal, as antidote, in part, that pervades, in motion, still requiring effort, already accomplished, of a dharma not born of cultivation, and of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the shift from this ground's earlier, exhaustive Basic Section treatment to a bare list of sixteen terms here as a deliberate change of register, the decisive discernment assuming a practitioner already knows the fuller picture and now simply sharpening sixteen precise tools for actually carrying reflection into lived practice. He highlights the five-fold gloss on the word "cultivation" itself, pervading, repeating, perfuming, learning, and brightening, as a genuinely useful reminder that cultivation in this treatise's own sense is never a single event but always a patient, repeated, gradually transformative process.

2. Section: The Śrāvaka's Mental Engagement, Bent on Personal Liberation Alone

What is mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle? It refers to a practitioner of the śrāvaka lineage, abiding in a śrāvaka's own nature, whether not yet entered into the true nature free of ordinary birth or already entered into it, who observes only their own benefit and not the benefit of others, and who, through the gateway of mental engagement upon the conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking as object a limited dharma still bearing conceptual distinction; such a practitioner cultivates mental engagement in order to exhaust craving, by way of the aspects of revulsion, freedom from desire, and liberation. Such a one relies on the four truths as the Buddha himself conceptually established them through language, and when root wisdom directly realizes suchness, does so free of language and characteristics, inwardly observing the non-conceptually established truth of selflessness alone; upon emerging from this root non-conceptual wisdom, in subsequently taking the four truths and their conventional distinctions as object, such a practitioner has not yet penetrated the emptiness of dharmas themselves, and so retains, though without any active turning away from beings' welfare, only a small measure of engagement in saving them, since the true aim remains one's own liberation from birth and death above all else. Revulsion refers to cultivating, again and again, the antidote to the suffering of birth and death in the three realms until affliction no longer manifests at all; freedom from desire refers to a mind so thoroughly imbued with revulsion that craving no longer manifests toward any defiling object, even without deliberately calling the antidote to mind; and liberation refers to repeatedly cultivating this very suppression and severance until the underlying latent tendencies of affliction are permanently uprooted and liberation is actually attained.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle? It refers to one of the śrāvaka lineage who observes only their own benefit, not the benefit of others, and who, through the gateway of the conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking a limited, conceptually distinguished dharma as object, cultivating mental engagement to exhaust craving by way of revulsion, freedom from desire, and liberation. This is called mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the śrāvaka's own careful characterization here, not entirely turning away from other beings' welfare yet still leaving it as only a minor, incidental concern beside the overriding aim of personal liberation, as an unusually fair-minded piece of comparative doctrine, resisting the temptation to caricature the lesser vehicle as wholly indifferent to others. He highlights the threefold progression from revulsion to freedom from desire to liberation as a genuinely practical map of how an antidote actually matures within the mind, from consciously recalling it, to no longer needing to recall it at all, to finally uprooting the affliction it opposes for good.

3. Section: The Bodhisattva's Mental Engagement, Bent on Every Being's Welfare

What is mental engagement linked to the great vehicle? It refers to a practitioner of the bodhisattva lineage, abiding in a bodhisattva's own nature, whether not yet entered into the true nature free of ordinary birth or already entered into it, who observes both their own benefit and the benefit of others, and who, through the gateway of mental engagement upon both the conceptually and the non-conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking as object a boundless dharma free of any conceptual distinction; through the power of great compassion, in order to exhaust the craving of both self and other, such a practitioner cultivates mental engagement by way of skillfully embracing every being's own welfare and by way of steadily advancing toward the unsurpassed path's own footsteps and causes. Such a one, still at the stage of accumulation or of preparatory effort, cultivates both the conceptually established teachings of the four truths and dependent origination and the non-conceptually established fourfold examination and fourfold genuine knowledge, in order to bring about the true and direct seeing of the path to come; upon entering the path of seeing, non-conceptual wisdom directly realizes suchness, personally witnessing the twin emptiness of self and dharmas, and upon emerging into subsequently attained wisdom, takes as object the entire, boundless dharma-realm, free of all conceptual proliferation, making no distinction at all between beings of the past, present, or future, or between friend, enemy, and stranger, wishing only that every single being attain benefit and ease. Through the power of great compassion, arising because the suffering of beings, deep, subtle, and hard to discern, becomes its object; accumulated over an immense span of time; arising through an especially earnest and forceful engagement, willing to give up hundreds of thousands of bodies and lives rather than mere wealth in order to relieve beings' suffering; and reaching its own most complete purity, such a bodhisattva cultivates mental engagement through the six perfections' own skillful means of embracing every being's welfare and through steadily advancing along the path's own stages, from accumulation all the way to the tenth ground and beyond, toward unsurpassed complete awakening itself.

This is called mental engagement linked to the great vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the direct, side-by-side comparison of the śrāvaka's limited, conceptually bound object and the bodhisattva's boundless, conceptually free object as this pair of sections' own clearest statement of what ultimately distinguishes the two vehicles, not merely a difference of motivation but a difference in how vast a scope wisdom itself is finally trained to take in. He highlights the four causes of great compassion, its depth and subtlety, its long accumulation, its forceful earnestness, and its final purity, as together forming this treatise's own fullest working definition of what actually separates ordinary sympathy from the great compassion capable of sustaining a bodhisattva across three incalculable eons of practice.

4. Section: Cultivation through an Image, in Both Insight and Calm-Abiding

What is cultivation through an image? It refers to whatever cultivation takes place either within the conceptually engaged aspect of insight, mentally reflecting upon the image that appears within concentration, itself a counterpart sharing the same basic character as the object originally known before entering concentration, examining that image's own meaning, matter, characteristic, category, timing, and principle through wisdom's own inquiry; or within the non-conceptual aspect of calm-abiding, where the sixth consciousness, without any active analysis or discrimination, simply continues to dwell again and again upon that same counterpart image, itself likewise sharing the same basic character as the object known before entering concentration. Whatever cultivation of this kind takes place, whether through insight's active examination or calm-abiding's steady dwelling, is called cultivation through an image.

Chinese Source

云何影像修？謂或於有分別毗鉢舍那品，三摩地所行影像，所知事同分，作意思惟故；或於無分別奢摩他品，三摩地所行影像，所知事同分，作意思惟故；諸所有修，名影像修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation through an image? It refers to mentally reflecting, whether within the conceptually engaged aspect of insight upon the image that concentration takes as its counterpart to the object originally known, or within the non-conceptual aspect of calm-abiding upon that same counterpart image. Whatever cultivation of this kind takes place is called cultivation through an image.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own careful pairing of insight's active, analytical relationship to the meditative image with calm-abiding's simple, sustained dwelling upon that same image as a precise restatement of this treatise's classic distinction between the two, now applied specifically to the single question of what object a meditator actually holds in mind. He notes that the same underlying image can serve either purpose depending only on how the mind engages it, examining its meaning and characteristics in one mode, or simply resting upon it undisturbed in the other, underscoring that calm-abiding and insight are finally two ways of relating to experience rather than two different experiences altogether.

5. Section: Cultivation through the Very Limit of a Matter, in Both Its Particulars and Its True Nature

What is cultivation through the very limit of a matter? It refers to mentally reflecting upon an object with respect to its past, future, and present aspects, its inner and outer aspects, its coarse and subtle aspects, its inferior and excellent aspects, and its far and near aspects, or else mentally reflecting upon suchness itself in its seven kinds. The first mode, examining an object exhaustively across all eleven of these particular dimensions, its own individual characteristic in every possible respect, yields what is called exhausting the full extent of a thing, from which quantitative wisdom, wisdom that has taken in the whole scope of an object's particulars, is attained; the second mode, examining an object's shared characteristic by way of the seven kinds of suchness common to all things, yields what is called abiding in the true nature of a thing, from which principled wisdom, wisdom that has penetrated a thing's own real nature, is attained. Whatever cultivation proceeds in either of these two ways, exhausting a thing's full extent or abiding in its true nature, is called cultivation through the very limit of a matter, and such cultivation is capable of severing both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known.

Chinese Source

云何事邊際修？謂於過去、未來、現在、內外、麤細、下劣、勝妙、近遠等法作意思惟，或於真如作意思惟。如是或盡所有性故，或如所有性故，諸所有修，名事邊際修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation through the very limit of a matter? It refers to mentally reflecting upon an object's past, future, present, inner, outer, coarse, subtle, inferior, excellent, far, and near aspects, or reflecting upon suchness itself. Whatever cultivation proceeds by exhausting a thing's full extent or by abiding in its true nature is called cultivation through the very limit of a matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of exhaustive-extent wisdom with true-nature wisdom as this treatise's own concise restatement of a wider principle running throughout the entire work, that genuine insight requires knowing both a thing's full particular scope and its shared underlying reality, neither alone being sufficient. He highlights the claim that this single kind of cultivation is capable of severing both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known as a fitting indication of just how much weight this treatise places on the discipline of examining a matter from every possible angle before claiming to understand it.

6. Section: Cultivation That Accomplishes the Task at Hand, in Four Aspects

What is cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand? It refers to whatever cultivation belongs to one who has already entered a fundamental absorption, some further meditative attainment, a worldly concentration, or a transcendent concentration; all such cultivation is called cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand, and it may be understood in four further aspects. The first is accomplishment through mental engagement, referring to a practitioner who cultivates the seven kinds of mental engagement all the way to its culminating result, the mental engagement that is itself the fruit of effort brought to completion. The second is accomplishment through the transformation of one's basis, referring to a practitioner whose body and mind, through the transcendent form of these seven mental engagements brought to full completion, are actually transformed, coarseness and unworkability entirely stilled, culminating in the ultimate transformation of the eight consciousnesses into the four wisdoms. The third is accomplishment through the arising of wisdom, referring to the actual arising of outflow-free direct-perception wisdom once these same transcendent mental engagements have reached completion. And the fourth is accomplishment through meditative absorption, referring to a practitioner's capacity to actually dwell within the very object proper to each attainment once the corresponding worldly form of these seven mental engagements has reached completion, whether the first absorption and onward through the fourth, or the sphere of infinite space and onward through neither-perception-nor-non-perception. In every case, whether the fruit attained is worldly or transcendent, this fourfold accomplishment, of mental engagement, of transformed basis, of arisen wisdom, and of meditative dwelling, is what is meant by cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand.

Chinese Source

云何所作成辦修？謂已證入根本靜慮、或諸等至、或世間定、或出世定，諸所有修，名所作成辦修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand? It refers to whatever cultivation belongs to one who has already entered a fundamental absorption, some further meditative attainment, a worldly concentration, or a transcendent concentration. This is called cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourfold breakdown into mental engagement, transformed basis, arisen wisdom, and meditative dwelling as this treatise's own way of insisting that a completed stage of practice is never a single, isolated event but always shows up simultaneously across the mind's engagement, the body-mind's own underlying condition, the wisdom that results, and the actual meditative object dwelt within. He highlights the culminating transformation of the eight consciousnesses into the four wisdoms, mentioned here only in passing, as a preview of a theme this treatise returns to at far greater length elsewhere, the entire path of cultivation ultimately aiming at nothing less than this complete transformation of consciousness itself.

7. Section: Cultivation as Attainment, Where One Wholesome Idea Strengthens All the Rest

What is cultivation as attainment? It refers to a practitioner who, relying on the first absorption, cultivates any one among a set of wholesome contemplative ideas, the idea of impermanence, of suffering, of non-self, of food's own repulsiveness, of untimely death, and so forth, while all the remaining ideas within this same set stay unmanifest for the time being. These ideas exist to counter three kinds of underlying delusion, delusion regarding time, regarding phase, and regarding intrinsic nature, which in turn give rise to three inverted views, mistaking the impermanent for permanent, mistaking suffering for pleasure, and mistaking what lacks a self for possessing one, along with the further tendency to scheme after gain and to hope for a long life. Whether the idea actually cultivated belongs to this same level, the first absorption itself, or to a lower level, the desire realm, and whether the further merit it induces is worldly, a deepened meditative attainment, or transcendent, a share in the noble path that severs affliction's own seeds, cultivating even this single idea is enough to cause all the other, presently unmanifest ideas and their own respective merits to grow stronger together, becoming purer, freeing the mind further from affliction, and increasing the very ease born of meditative absorption, so that mastery over all of them together is genuinely attained. This is called cultivation as attainment.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，依初靜慮，或修無常想，乃至或修死想，彼所有餘不現前想；或自地攝、或下地攝；及彼所引諸餘功德，或是世間、或出世間；皆能修彼令其增盛清淨樂生，於彼獲得自在成就。是名得修。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to a practitioner who, relying on the first absorption, cultivates the idea of impermanence or of death, while the remaining ideas stay unmanifest; whether belonging to this same level or a lower one, and whether the merit induced is worldly or transcendent, cultivating it causes all such merit to grow stronger and purer, and mastery over it is attained. This is called cultivation as attainment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own central promise, that patiently cultivating even a single wholesome contemplation is enough to strengthen an entire family of related merits one is not currently practicing, as a genuinely encouraging teaching for any practitioner who feels they can only manage to work on one thing at a time. He highlights the three kinds of underlying delusion, regarding time, phase, and intrinsic nature, as this passage's own precise diagnosis of exactly what each of the classic contemplative ideas is designed to counter, impermanence answering delusion about time, the contemplation of suffering answering delusion about phase, and non-self answering delusion about intrinsic nature.

8. Section: Cultivation as Habituation, Keeping the Wholesome Constantly Present

What is cultivation as habituation? It refers to a practitioner who mentally reflects, again and again, upon these very same wholesome ideas, impermanence and the rest, or who, with respect to other wholesome practices such as giving, precepts, and meditative cultivation, keeps them constantly and repeatedly manifest through sheer habituation. This is called cultivation as habituation. The difference between cultivation as habituation and cultivation as attainment is made clear through a series of paired contrasts: habituation is revealed through what is presently manifest, while attainment is revealed through what is yet to come; something is called cultivation because it is habituated now, and something is likewise called cultivation because it will be attained in the future; something is cultivation because it is presently being enjoyed, and something is cultivation because it will be induced in the future; something is cultivation because the task is presently being accomplished, and something is cultivation because a wish will be granted in the future; something is cultivation because it presently resides within the body, and something is cultivation because mastery will be gained over it in the future; and something is cultivation because it is presently manifest, and something is cultivation because it will be accomplished in the future.

Chinese Source

云何習修？謂如有一，即於彼彼無常等諸善想作意思惟，或於善法由習修故，皆現修習，是名習修。現在，習修所顯；未來，得修所顯。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation as habituation? It refers to a practitioner who mentally reflects, again and again, upon these same wholesome ideas, or who keeps other wholesome practices constantly manifest through habituation. This is called cultivation as habituation. What is presently the case is revealed through habituation-cultivation; what is yet to come is revealed through attainment-cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the six paired contrasts distinguishing habituation-cultivation from attainment-cultivation, present versus future, enjoyment versus induction, accomplishing versus granting, residing within versus gaining mastery, and manifest versus accomplished, as together forming a single, consistent axis, habituation always describing what is already here and being repeated, attainment always describing what is not yet here and is being brought about. He notes that this precise distinction matters practically as well as terminologically, since a practitioner genuinely benefits from knowing whether a given moment of practice is meant to deepen something already achieved or to reach toward something still beyond present grasp.

9. Section: Cultivation as Removal, Driving Out One Wedge with Another

What is cultivation as removal? It refers to a practitioner who, through mental engagement upon the characteristics of the counterpart image that appears within concentration, the characteristic of impermanence and the rest, skillfully dispels attachment through a method resembling how one wooden wedge drives out another already lodged in place, thereby abandoning the mind's own attachment to the characteristics it takes its objects, the aggregates and the rest, to intrinsically possess, such as permanence or deathlessness; and once this is accomplished, even the grasping mind's own nature becomes ungraspable, subject and object both falling away as one enters the ultimate truth itself. As a further verse puts it, a bodhisattva abiding in concentration observes that the image before the mind is mind alone; once the object's own apparent characteristic has thereby dissolved, careful examination reveals only one's own thought remaining; abiding thus within the mind itself, one comes to know that what is grasped has no real existence, and next that the grasping mind itself is equally without existence, until afterward one touches what can never actually be grasped at all, the very reality beyond both. And just as one might use a still finer wedge to drive out a coarser one already used to displace the first, a practitioner likewise uses the very bodily ease and pliancy attained through freedom from desire to displace and remove the coarse, unworkable body still burdened by affliction. Whatever cultivation proceeds by this means, dispelling attachment to already-arisen unwholesome states through such skillful displacement, is called cultivation as removal.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，由三摩地所行影像諸相作意故，如楔出楔方便除遣，棄於自性諸相。又如有一，用彼細楔遣於麤楔，如是行者，以輕安身除麤重身。是名除去修。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to a practitioner who, through mental engagement upon the meditative image's own characteristics, dispels attachment to a thing's intrinsic characteristics as one wedge drives out another. And just as a finer wedge drives out a coarser one, such a practitioner uses the ease of a body freed from desire to remove the coarse, burdened body. This is called cultivation as removal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the double wedge image, first one wedge displacing an attachment to characteristics, then a finer wedge displacing an already-coarsened body, as an unusually vivid and memorable illustration of how spiritual progress actually works in stages, each advance not arising from nothing but displacing what came immediately before it. He highlights the verse's own careful sequence, first recognizing the grasped object as mind alone, then recognizing the grasping mind itself as equally without independent existence, and only then touching the ungraspable reality beyond both, as this treatise's own precise map of how a meditator moves from ordinary dualistic experience toward the non-dual realization of suchness itself.

10. Section: Cultivation as Antidote, in Four Stages from Revulsion to Distancing

What is cultivation as antidote? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds by mental engagement upon revulsion-antidote, severing-antidote, sustaining-antidote, or distancing-antidote, this being called cultivation as antidote. Revulsion-antidote refers to every worldly wholesome path, contemplating a lower stage as coarse, afflicted, and confining while a higher stage appears peaceful, subtle, and liberating, thereby attaining freedom from desire and the four absorptions or four formless attainments in turn, excluding only the four immeasurable states of mind and whatever mental engagement induces a playful display of supernatural power, since these are not themselves born of revulsion, the four immeasurables being cultivated instead for the sake of benefiting beings and supernatural display carrying no element of aversion at all. Severing-antidote refers to mental engagement that takes suchness itself, in its seven kinds, as object, this being what actually severs the very seeds of affliction. Sustaining-antidote refers to the subsequently attained worldly and transcendent path that follows upon severing-antidote, together with the path of actual liberation itself, sustaining and upholding what severing-antidote has already accomplished so that the severed affliction never manifests again. And distancing-antidote refers to further and repeated cultivation of the antidote path once affliction has already been severed, or to cultivating the path of some still higher stage, as when a practitioner who has already attained the first absorption goes on to counter that same absorption's own remaining coarse investigation and analysis in order to advance toward the second. A further explanation gives these same four a slightly different sense: the path of hearing, reflection, and cultivation is called revulsion-antidote; the transcendent path itself is called severing-antidote; the transformation of one's own basis that results from it is called sustaining-antidote; and the worldly path of cultivation that follows afterward, by means of subsequently attained wisdom, is called distancing-antidote.

Chinese Source

云何對治修？謂於厭患對治、或斷對治、或持對治、或遠分對治作意思惟，諸所有修，名對治修。斷對治者，謂緣真如為境作意。遠分對治者，謂煩惱斷已，於對治道更多修習，或多修習上地之道。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation as antidote? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds by mental engagement upon revulsion-antidote, severing-antidote, sustaining-antidote, or distancing-antidote. Severing-antidote refers to mental engagement taking suchness itself as object. Distancing-antidote refers to further cultivation of the antidote path, or of a higher stage's path, once affliction has already been severed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the deliberate exclusion of the four immeasurable states and playful supernatural display from revulsion-antidote as a fine but important distinction, since both indeed belong to worldly wholesome cultivation yet arise from compassion and skillful benefit rather than from any aversion toward a lower state, showing that not every wholesome practice fits neatly under a single antidote category. He highlights the concrete example of countering the first absorption's own remaining coarse investigation and analysis in order to advance toward the second as distancing-antidote's clearest illustration, a reminder that even a genuinely peaceful and wholesome attainment can itself become the very thing a more advanced practitioner must next learn to see beyond.

11. Section: Cultivation in Part, the First Stage of Signless Practice

What is cultivation in part? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds through mental engagement upon any single wholesome dharma, such as the idea of impermanence, taken as object, without yet bringing every other wholesome contemplation together at once. This is called cultivation in part. This kind of cultivation corresponds to what is elsewhere called the partial stage within the wider unfolding of signless cultivation, the stage proper to a bodhisattva still short of the first ground, one who has reached the rank of forbearance within the stage of resolute conduct and beyond, who has not yet actually realized signless suchness in direct experience but has already attained a supreme faith and understanding in the teaching, thereby entering into partial accord with a single portion of suchness's own meaning, a first and still incomplete correspondence with reality itself.

Chinese Source

云何少分修？謂於無常想等隨一善法作意思惟，諸所有修，名少分修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation in part? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds by mentally engaging any single wholesome dharma, such as the idea of impermanence, taken as object. This is called cultivation in part.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own brief definition as the first rung of a much larger ladder this treatise elsewhere maps directly onto the bodhisattva path's own ten grounds, cultivation in part corresponding to the sincere but still pre-realization practice of an advanced ordinary bodhisattva who has not yet actually broken through to suchness itself. He highlights the phrase "supreme faith and understanding" as a genuinely important intermediate category in this treatise's own account of spiritual progress, a stage of confidence and comprehension real enough to already partially correspond with truth, even though direct realization still lies ahead.

12. Section: Cultivation That Pervades, Where All Dharmas Share One Suchness

What is cultivation that pervades? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds through mental engagement upon the single, uniform suchness shared equally by every dharma, this being called cultivation that pervades. Since every dharma's own real nature is empty in exactly the same way, suchness itself being the one shared characteristic running through all things, unchanging and never other than itself, taking this single suchness as object in mental engagement, rather than any one dharma among many, is what earns this kind of cultivation its name. This corresponds to what is elsewhere called the pervading stage, the stage proper to a bodhisattva of purified, superior aspiration, namely one who has reached the first ground, capable of directly realizing every dharma's true nature through non-conceptual wisdom and thereby observing suchness as a single, uniform reality running through all things at once, no longer confined to any one dharma's own partial characteristic.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation that pervades? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds through mental engagement upon the single, uniform suchness shared by all dharmas. This is called cultivation that pervades.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the leap from cultivation in part to cultivation that pervades as marking this treatise's own decisive line between an advanced but still unrealized ordinary bodhisattva and a genuinely noble one, the first ground's own non-conceptual realization of suchness transforming what was previously only partial correspondence into direct, unified contact with reality's single shared nature. He notes that the very word "pervades" here is doing real philosophical work, insisting that suchness is not one truth among the many truths belonging to individual dharmas but the single truth equally present within every single one of them.

13. Section: Cultivation in Motion, Still Interrupted by What Bears a Sign

What is cultivation in motion? It refers to a practitioner who, while still engaged in the preliminary methods leading toward signless meditation, such as first contemplating a fire's own characteristic and then contemplating that same fire's extinguishing in order to arrive at an experiential sense of signlessness, from time to time loses that signless state and must fall back upon a sign-bearing preliminary method once again in order to re-enter it, the practice thereby proceeding through an alternation of signless moments interrupted by sign-bearing ones. This is called cultivation in motion, distinguished from a uniform cultivation in which no such interruption occurs at all, the mind entering directly into correspondence with the signless the moment it is approached, with only the signless itself ever remaining as object. This corresponds to what is elsewhere called the stage of motion, the stage belonging to noble bodhisattvas across the first six grounds, who, even while directly realizing suchness through mental engagement upon signless truth, still find that practice interrupted from time to time by what bears a sign, still stirred, as it were, by the functioning power of habitual linguistic seeds, and for this reason called the stage of motion, or equally the conduct still bearing a sign.

Chinese Source

云何動轉修？謂於無相修方便修時，時時有相間隔而修，名動轉修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation in motion? It refers to a practitioner who, while practicing the preliminary methods toward signless meditation, from time to time falls back into an interruption bearing a sign. This is called cultivation in motion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fire-and-extinguishing example as a genuinely helpful, concrete illustration of what "sign-bearing" and "signless" practice actually feel like from the inside, first holding a definite characteristic in mind and then letting even that characteristic's own cessation lead the mind toward signlessness itself. He highlights the honest acknowledgment that even noble bodhisattvas across the first six grounds, who have already directly realized suchness, still experience interruption and lapse back into sign-bearing states, as a fittingly realistic corrective to any assumption that realization alone immediately grants unbroken, effortless stability.

14. Section: Cultivation Still Requiring Effort

What is cultivation still requiring effort? It refers to a practitioner who, during that same preliminary practice toward signless meditation, is not yet able to proceed spontaneously and effortlessly, but must instead apply deliberate exertion at intervals in order to sustain it. This is called cultivation still requiring effort, corresponding to what is elsewhere called the stage requiring effort, the stage proper to a bodhisattva of the seventh ground, who is indeed already able to directly realize suchness but still needs to apply deliberate exertion in order to do so, not yet having attained the spontaneous, effortless engagement that later grounds bring.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fine distinction between cultivation in motion and cultivation still requiring effort as worth pausing over, since both involve interruption of a kind, yet the seventh ground's own effort is a conscious, deliberate exertion rather than the more passive lapsing back into a sign-bearing state described in the stage of motion, marking real progress even though neither stage has yet reached spontaneity. He notes that naming an entire ground of advanced bodhisattva practice simply "requiring effort" is itself a humbling reminder of how much sustained exertion this path still demands even at so exalted a stage.

15. Section: Cultivation Already Accomplished, Where Every Vehicle Completes Its Own Task

What is cultivation already accomplished? It refers to a practitioner of the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, or the great vehicle who has already attained the complete transformation of their own basis, body and mind entirely transformed, the ālaya-consciousness itself transformed into the ripening consciousness or, in its fullest sense, into the stainless consciousness, and who has attained complete mastery over all dharmas, entering, dwelling in, and emerging from every supernatural power and meditative attainment entirely at will. Whatever cultivation belongs to such a practitioner is called cultivation already accomplished. In brief, a śrāvaka practitioner attaining arhatship or a pratyekabuddha practitioner attaining the fruit of solitary awakening has thereby severed the craving and views afflicting the three realms; a great vehicle practitioner attaining unsurpassed complete awakening has severed both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known, gaining complete freedom of function over every dharma; each of the three vehicles thus brings its own wholesome qualities to genuine and final completion, and this completion itself is called cultivation already accomplished. This corresponds, in the wider unfolding of signless cultivation across the ten grounds, to the eighth ground's own stage free of both effort and exertion, where suchness is realized entirely spontaneously, unfolding onward through the ninth and tenth grounds across the third incalculable eon until buddhahood itself is reached, this entire final stretch together constituting the stage of fulfillment.

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to a practitioner of the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, or the great vehicle who has already attained the complete transformation of their own basis and complete mastery over all dharmas. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five kinds of cultivation together, in part, that pervades, in motion, still requiring effort, and already accomplished, as forming a single, continuous arc this treatise elsewhere maps directly onto the bodhisattva path's own ten grounds, from an advanced ordinary being's first partial correspondence with suchness, through the first ground's unified realization, the first six grounds' own remaining interruption, the seventh ground's deliberate exertion, and finally the eighth ground onward into full spontaneity and, at last, buddhahood itself. 16. Section: Cultivation Not Born of Concentration, Cultivation Born of It, and the Ground's Own Close

What is cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation? It refers to whatever wholesome practice, giving, precepts, patience, mindfulness of the Buddha, mantra recitation, seated meditation, and the like, is undertaken with a scattered mind that has not yet settled into any actual level of concentration, since a practice carried out this way, however wholesome, does not yet count as belonging to the wisdom born of cultivation proper, real concentration requiring at the very least the preliminary access concentration that first makes possible the suppression of affliction. This is called cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation. What is cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation? It refers to whatever wholesome practice is instead undertaken while genuinely settled within some level of concentration, whether contemplating the four noble truths through insight in order to sever affliction, or cultivating the eight forms of liberation, the eight bases of mastery, the ten bases of totality, and other such wholesome practices proper to concentration itself, resting on at least this same preliminary access concentration as its foundation. This is called cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation, and with this pair the full list of sixteen kinds of cultivation belonging to the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation comes to a close. And with this same close, the third and final section of this passage follows, briefly declining to say more: within this ground's own decisive discernment, no further text of decisive discernment will appear again. Since the Basic Section's own earlier treatment of this ground, by way of its four supporting bases and seven branches, had already explained cultivation's own place, cause, method, and fruit in complete and careful detail, this decisive discernment needed only set out these sixteen essential kinds of cultivation practice, and having done so, its work here is genuinely finished.

He highlights this ground's own unusually brief decisive discernment, a mere sixteen terms briskly explained where the earlier grounds ran to entire fascicles, as itself following directly from the Basic Section's own already exhaustive treatment, this treatise consistently declining to repeat at length what has already been said in full elsewhere.

17. Section: Opening the Śrāvaka Ground's Decisive Discernment, in Three Parts across Five Fascicles

As has now been explained, the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation is complete. The decisive discernment of the Śrāvaka Ground, Maitreya now says, I shall next explain. The Basic Section's own treatment of the Śrāvaka Ground already unfolds across four yogic processes: the first, spanning several fascicles, covers the ground of lineage, the ground of entry, and the ground of renunciation, the last of these explaining in detail the thirteen kinds of worldly and transcendent provisions for the path; the second sets forth the establishment of categories, seventeen topics covering the various kinds of practitioner, how a practitioner is established, the object, instruction, training, and the yoga master's own work; the third sets forth the establishment of yogic method itself, explaining the sequence and skillful means by which a teacher guides a student through calm-abiding and insight; and the fourth broadly explains the very character of cultivation, covering both the worldly path's four absorptions and eight attainments and the transcendent path's own detailed progress from the first fruit all the way to arhatship. Since the Basic Section has already treated all of this so fully, this decisive discernment, spanning five further fascicles, confines itself to three deeper and more hidden matters not yet fully addressed there: the śrāvaka's own lineage, resolving five difficult questions that might be raised against it; the śrāvaka's own varieties, ten distinct kinds distinguished by circumstance and attainment; and the śrāvaka canon itself, thirteen topics drawn from key sūtras and verses within the Āgama scriptures. The Śrāvaka Ground stands as one of this treatise's own most foundational sections, laying out with exceptional clarity everything from the initial accumulation of provisions, through the actual methods of practice, to the precise character of the fruit finally attained upon reaching arhatship itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation has been explained. The decisive discernment of the Śrāvaka Ground, I shall now explain.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own careful preview, three deeper matters, lineage, varieties, and canon, spanning five entire fascicles, as fair warning that the Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discernment will prove one of this treatise's most substantial undertakings, fitting given how central the Śrāvaka Ground itself already is to the whole work's account of the path. He highlights the deliberate decision to leave aside anything the Basic Section already covered in full and to focus instead only on what remained genuinely difficult or hidden there, as this treatise's own consistent editorial principle now stated once again in a fresh context.

18. Section: Are Some Beings Truly without the Lineage for Liberation?

The Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discernment opens with the first of its five difficult questions concerning lineage, resolving the difficulty raised against the very notion of a being with no lineage at all. As the Basic Section had already stated, a being abiding in no lineage whatsoever, lacking the seed proper to the śrāvaka, the solitary-awakened, or the bodhisattva vehicle alike, is said to be one for whom complete nirvana is ultimately impossible. At this a doubt may naturally arise in the mind: how could there possibly be such a being, permanently barred from nirvana no matter what? To press this difficulty further, one should turn the question back upon the questioner: what, then, is your own view? Do the many different kinds of sentient being possess distinct elemental natures at all, and among these natures, are there truly boundless, immeasurable varieties, some inferior and some excellent? If the answer given is that such various elemental natures do indeed exist, immeasurable in their variety, ranging genuinely from inferior to excellent, then it stands to reason that a being permanently barred from nirvana ought likewise to exist among these boundless varieties, and to deny this while granting the premise would itself be unreasonable. As one commentary explains, each being's own continuous mindstream already contains all eighteen elements, itself called elemental nature in its basic kind, yet within each single element countless further distinctions of variety exist, since different beings of different karmic destinies accumulate that same element quite differently, so that a single named kind of element in fact conceals boundless further variety beneath it; and these elemental natures are themselves gathered and accumulated through the power of a being's own past resolute conviction, an inferior nature accumulated through past unwholesome conviction, an excellent nature accumulated through past wholesome conviction.

One should ask in turn: do sentient beings possess various, immeasurable, inferior and excellent elemental natures, or not? If such natures do exist, then denying a being permanently barred from nirvana would be unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening dilemma's own method, granting the questioner's premise about boundless elemental variety and then showing that a being without the lineage for liberation follows as a direct consequence of that very premise, as a genuinely elegant piece of argumentation, turning what looked like an objection into its own best evidence. He notes that a fuller resolution of just how troubling the notion of a being permanently barred from nirvana actually is, and whether this truly forecloses that being's future without exception, still lies ahead in the second horn of this same dilemma, cautioning against drawing a final conclusion from only the first half of the argument.

19. Section: The Second Horn Closes the First Difficulty, and Rootless Beings Cannot Rescue the Second

The dilemma posed against the difficulty of a being with no lineage at all now turns to its second horn. If, instead, the answer given is that sentient beings do not in fact possess such various, boundless elemental natures, this too fails, for scripture itself plainly teaches that sentient beings possess various elemental natures, ranging genuinely from inferior to excellent, so that denying this outright would contradict the Buddha's own established word. Either horn of the dilemma thus fails, and with this the first of the five difficulties, the difficulty of a being with no lineage at all, is fully resolved. A second difficulty then arises: granted that elemental natures vary boundlessly, there is still no such thing as a being genuinely without any faculties at all, since whatever lacks eyes, ears, and the rest of the six faculties is thereby not a sentient being to begin with, merely an insentient thing like a mountain or a tree, and so, by this same reasoning, why should one grant that a being without any lineage for liberation at all could genuinely exist? One should turn this question back as well: what do you take a rootless thing itself to be, a sentient being, or not a sentient being? If it is granted to be a sentient being, then insentient things outside the body altogether, mountains, rivers, tables, and chairs, would all have to count as sentient beings too, which is plainly unreasonable. And if it is granted not to be a sentient being, then the very premise of the objection collapses, since something already agreed not to be a sentient being can offer no analogy at all to the question of whether a genuine sentient being might lack the lineage for liberation; asking why one does not therefore accept the existence of rootless beings, once rootless things have already been conceded to be no beings at all, is itself unreasonable. With this the second difficulty, too, is resolved.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this second difficulty's own hidden confusion, quietly smuggling an insentient thing into the category of sentient being in order to construct its analogy, as the real target of the reply, the counter-question exposing that the objection never actually compared two genuine cases of the same kind at all. He notes that this same dialectical pattern, granting a premise only provisionally and then showing that either possible answer to a follow-up question leads the objection back into contradiction, will recur across all five of these difficulties, and is worth watching for as this treatise's own consistent method of debate throughout the section.

20. Section: Can a Being Cross Castes the Way It Might Cross into a Different Lineage?

Having resolved this, a third difficulty is raised: just as a being born a kshatriya in one life may, in another, be born a brahmin, a vaishya, or a shudra, and just as one born a shudra may later be born a kshatriya, and just as a being who has fallen into hell may later be born among the gods, and one born among the gods may later fall back into hell, why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not likewise, at some future time, come to possess it? One should again turn the question back: do you take it that a single being's own mindstream contains, all at once, the seeds for every one of these castes and destinies together, or only the seed for one exclusively? If it is granted that all such seeds are present together, then the analogy itself fails to hold, since it is not a genuine likeness at all, for no single being's mindstream can contain, at one and the same time, both the seed proper to one who will ultimately attain nirvana and the seed proper to one who genuinely never will, the very presence of the one excluding the other in a way that caste and destiny, which may indeed coexist as seeds within a single stream, do not. And if it is instead granted that only one such seed is exclusively present at a time, this too is unreasonable, since it plainly contradicts what is actually observed, a being once a kshatriya being later, in fact, reborn a shudra. Either way the analogy fails, and the third difficulty is likewise resolved.

He highlights the treatise's own willingness to appeal directly to observed fact, a kshatriya plainly being reborn a shudra in practice, as a useful reminder that even highly abstract Abhidharma argument in this work still ultimately answers to how things are actually seen to occur.

21. Section: If All Castes' Seeds Coexist, Must Every Lineage Coexist Too?

A fourth difficulty then follows directly from this same admission: since a kshatriya's own mindstream has just been granted to contain the seeds of every caste together, why, by that same reasoning, should a being without the lineage for liberation not likewise contain the element proper to one who does possess it? One should turn the question back once more: do you take the element of no lineage for liberation and the element of possessing that lineage to be mutually exclusive of one another, or not mutually exclusive? If they are granted to be mutually exclusive, then the very question of why a being lacking the one does not also possess the other collapses, since mutual exclusion was just conceded, meaning their coexistence was never actually intended in the first place. And if they are instead granted not to be mutually exclusive, then it would follow that one and the same being could be, at once, both without the lineage for liberation and possessing it, which is plainly unreasonable, since, as one commentary explains, sentient beings divide, in the end, into exactly two kinds, those genuinely capable of ultimately severing both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known, who thereby possess the very nature that makes nirvana possible, and those genuinely incapable of ever severing them, who are precisely those without the lineage for liberation, the two admitting of no overlap between them at all. With this the fourth difficulty, too, is resolved.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the cited twofold division of all sentient beings, those capable of ultimately severing both obstructions and those genuinely incapable of ever doing so, as this passage's own clearest positive statement of what the entire, sometimes dense dialectic of these five difficulties is actually protecting, a real and irreducible distinction between two fundamentally different possibilities open to different beings, rather than a distinction this treatise is merely reluctant to give up on technical grounds alone. He notes that this fourth difficulty, in exposing the internal contradiction of holding two mutually exclusive elements at once, brings the debate to what is in effect its logical center, the remaining fifth difficulty largely testing this same conclusion against one further kind of analogy.

22. Section: Gold and Salt Appear and Vanish — Could a Lineage Do the Same?

A fifth and final difficulty is then raised, drawing on the observed behavior of the natural world itself: just as a given locality may, at one time, be found to contain no vein of gold at all and yet, at another time, be found to contain one, and just as the same may be observed of pearls, lapis lazuli, salt, and other such natural resources, appearing where they were once entirely absent, why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not, at some point, likewise come to possess it? One should ask in reply: do you mean to say that lineage itself is indeterminate in just this way, so that a being of the fixed śrāvaka lineage might later cease to be so, a being of the fixed great-vehicle lineage might likewise later cease to be so, or a being without any fixed lineage at all might later come to acquire one? If this indeterminacy is indeed what is meant, then two serious faults immediately follow. The first is that the very wholesome roots conducive to liberation would be rendered empty and fruitless, since a being possessing them now could simply lose them later, leaving no reliable basis on which the path to liberation could ever actually be completed. The second is that positing any fixed lineage at all, whether śrāvaka, solitary-awakened, or great-vehicle, would itself become unreasonable, since the very notion of a fixed lineage presupposes precisely the stability that indeterminacy denies. And if, to avoid these faults, one instead insists that lineage is not indeterminate after all, then the original analogy collapses on its own terms, since a being with the lineage for liberation, once granted to possess it fixedly, could then, by that same mineral-like logic, later lose it again, which is equally unreasonable and was never actually intended by anyone advancing this analogy in the first place.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not, at some point, come to possess it, just as gold or salt may appear in a place once devoid of it? One should ask whether this means lineage itself is indeterminate. If so, the wholesome roots conducive to liberation would be rendered fruitless, and positing any fixed lineage at all would become unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two faults exposed here, wholesome roots rendered fruitless and fixed lineage itself becoming incoherent, as together showing exactly what is actually at stake in this entire five-part debate, not an abstract point of classification but the very reliability of the path itself, since a practitioner needs genuine confidence that sincere effort toward liberation will not simply evaporate on its own. He notes that this fifth difficulty's own resolution is not yet complete at this point in the text, the dialogue continuing further still, and treats the patience with which this treatise works through even a natural, intuitively appealing analogy like minerals appearing and vanishing as a model of how carefully a difficult doctrinal claim ought to be tested before being accepted.

23. Section: This Life or the Next? The Fifth Difficulty Closes, and Ten Kinds of Śrāvaka Open

The fifth difficulty is pressed once further, from yet another angle: if a being without the lineage for liberation, dwelling within the desire realm's own inferior element, is ever to become one possessing that lineage, does this transformation occur within this very same life, or only in some future life? If it is said to occur within this very life, then, once such a being encounters the three jewels, does this present life actually give rise to the wholesome roots conducive to liberation, or not? If it does, then a being who has just been granted to give rise to these very wholesome roots upon encountering the three jewels can hardly still be called one without the lineage for liberation at all, since only a being already possessing the seed for it could cultivate such roots in the first place, so this answer is unreasonable. And if it does not, then claiming such a being nonetheless becomes one possessing the lineage is equally unreasonable, since no such transformation has actually been shown to occur. If instead it is said that this transformation occurs only in some future life, one must ask further whether such a being had already accumulated these same wholesome roots in some even earlier life or had not. If it had already accumulated them, then this very present life, upon encountering the three jewels, should already give rise to the wholesome roots conducive to liberation, so postponing the transformation to some future life is unreasonable, and the very cause already planted would be rendered fruitless besides. And if it had not accumulated them at all, then present life and future life stand exactly alike, both equally lacking any wholesome roots already cultivated, so there remains no reason whatsoever why the future life alone should succeed where this present life could not. Every possible answer thus fails, and with this the fifth and final difficulty concerning lineage is fully resolved, closing all five difficulties raised against the very notion of a being without the lineage for liberation. A further topic then opens: the second of this ground's three deeper matters, the ten kinds of śrāvaka. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing move of this fifth difficulty, forcing the objector to specify exactly when the supposed transformation occurs and then showing that neither this life nor any future life can coherently house it, as a fitting capstone to the whole five-part debate, the argument's cumulative weight finally closing off every avenue by which a being without the lineage for liberation could be imagined to acquire it after all. He highlights the shift in register that follows, from tightly argued dialectic to a simple, numbered list of ten kinds of śrāvaka, as this treatise's own way of moving from defending a difficult doctrine to the more descriptive, almost pastoral task of actually mapping the many different circumstances in which a genuine śrāvaka practitioner may be found.

24. Section: From a Pure Lineage to a World Clouded by Five Kinds of Turbidity

Each of the ten kinds of śrāvaka is now explained in turn. Whoever abides inwardly in the śrāvaka lineage at all is the first kind, the śrāvaka of a pure element, so called simply for possessing this lineage in the first place, whether born into a pure land or into an age when the world itself flourishes, as when Maitreya himself will appear in the world to come. Whoever has already entered the Dharma, already possessing the lineage and having actually encountered and entered the Buddha's teaching, is the second kind, the śrāvaka who has already met the right conditions. Whoever is born into a world where a śrāvaka's own arising encounters abundant suffering, admitting of the five kinds of turbidity, turbidity of lifespan, of sentient beings, of affliction, of view, and of the eon itself, is the third kind, the śrāvaka born into a defiled world. Turbidity of lifespan refers to a shortening of the human lifespan itself, from an age when people once lived eighty-four thousand years down to a present age in which reaching even a hundred is rare, human unwholesome karma having steadily increased. Turbidity of sentient beings refers to an age in which beings largely fail to recognize what is honorable, neglecting filial devotion, disrespecting genuine spiritual teachers, feeling no dread at wrongdoing in this life or the next, and abandoning giving, merit-making, and the upholding of precepts altogether. Turbidity of affliction refers to an age in which unwholesome craving, hatred, and delusion burn especially fiercely, given to violence, deceit, and false speech in overwhelming measure. Turbidity of view refers to an age in which wrong views proliferate wildly, actively working to destroy or extinguish the true teaching and setting up counterfeit teachings that only resemble it. And turbidity of the eon refers to a declining age in which, as the human lifespan falls to thirty years, famine arises; as it falls to twenty, plague arises; and as it falls to ten, warfare arises, leaving scarcely a being in the world untouched by calamity. Whatever stands opposite to all this, one should know, is the fourth kind, the śrāvaka born into a pure world, born instead into a pure land or into a flourishing age when the human lifespan and stature are both immense and the ten wholesome courses of action are universally practiced.

Chinese Source

Whatever stands opposite to this is the śrāvaka born into a pure world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five turbidities, of lifespan, of sentient beings, of affliction, of view, and of the eon, as this treatise's own remarkably concrete and almost sociological account of what a genuinely degenerate age actually looks like, moving well beyond abstract doctrine into observable shifts in human lifespan, filial devotion, violence, wrong view, and even climate and famine. He highlights the pairing of a defiled-world śrāvaka against a pure-world śrāvaka as a reminder that this same lineage may unfold in radically different outward circumstances, the inward seed for liberation remaining identical even where the surrounding world differs as greatly as a five-turbidity age differs from an age as fortunate as the one in which Maitreya himself is said to appear.

25. Section: The Śrāvaka Born in the Age of the Dharma's Decline

A further pair of kinds is then explained, the śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline and the śrāvaka born in a wholesome and fortunate age. What, one may ask, are the characteristic marks of a śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline? Briefly, such śrāvakas, born into a future age when the true teaching has already faded into obscurity following the Buddha's own passing, are largely given over to a fondness for material gain and for others' esteem, turning their backs on the subtle Dharma itself, so that craving, hatred, delusion, and wrong, improper teachings all flourish together in overwhelming measure among them.

Chinese Source

末法時生諸聲聞相，云何可知？謂諸聲聞，於當來世法末時生，多分愛重利養恭敬，違背妙法，諸貪恚癡及不正法并皆增盛。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the characteristic marks of a śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline? Such śrāvakas, born in a future age when the true teaching has faded, are largely given over to fondness for gain and esteem, turning their backs on the subtle Dharma, craving, hatred, delusion, and wrong teachings all flourishing among them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this unflinching portrait of decline, monastics themselves turning toward fame and material gain and away from the very teaching they claim to follow, as this treatise's own sober acknowledgment that even the monastic order is not immune to the same five turbidities already described, its own internal integrity capable of eroding right alongside the wider world's own decline. He notes that this description is only the opening statement of a fuller characterization still to come, cautioning against treating it as the complete picture before the passage's own further elaboration has been read.

26. Section: Stinginess, Flattery, Craving, Delusion, and Hatred in a Declining Age

The characteristic marks of a śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline are now explained in fuller detail. Such practitioners are bound and disturbed in mind by stinginess, envy, and other secondary afflictions, fully possessing every kind of stinginess there is: stinginess over one's own dwelling place, unwilling to share it with others; stinginess over the household that supports oneself, unwilling to let others share in its offerings; stinginess over gain itself, unwilling to share wealth or benefit; stinginess over reverence, unwilling to praise or honor fellow practitioners; stinginess over reputation, unwilling to spread another's good name; and stinginess even over the Dharma itself, unwilling to freely share the teaching. Flattery, deceit, feigned dignity, and outright fraud constantly show themselves as well, such practitioners going forth from home, in the end, merely to secure a livelihood rather than out of any genuine wish for nirvana. Their afflictions flourish further still, marked by restlessness, arrogance, harsh and boastful speech, laziness that lets no wholesome quality already gained increase, constant loss of mindfulness, an unsettled mind, frequent confusion, and dull, benighted faculties, affliction manifesting without interruption. Their own craving shows itself in never growing weary of the very suffering craving brings, delighting instead in crowded company, abandoning the humble bedding proper to a forest hermitage in favor of the comfortable lodging found near a village, and rejoicing in fine talk, noisy gatherings, and the indiscriminate mixing of monastic and lay life, indulgent and unrestrained, unable to properly cultivate the body, precepts, mind, and wisdom together. Their delusion shows itself first in abandoning the Buddha's own profound teachings on emptiness and dependent origination in favor of eloquent, ornately composed worldly writings, taking far greater delight in fine literature than in scripture, and second in mistaking counterfeit teachings that merely resemble the true Dharma for the genuine article while regarding the genuine Dharma itself with suspicion, going on to actively promote such counterfeit teachings and even slander the true Dharma and its discipline outright. And their hatred shows itself in regarding whoever actually teaches the true Dharma and its discipline as an enemy to be resented rather than a teacher to be honored.

Chinese Source

為慳嫉等諸隨煩惱纏擾其心；處慳、家慳、利慳、敬慳、譽慳、法慳，無不具足；諂、誑、矯、詐恆現在前；廣說乃至為活命故而求出家，非為涅槃。於似正法非正法中妄生法想，於正法中起非法想。於說正法及毗奈耶補特伽羅生怨家想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bound by stinginess and other secondary afflictions, fully possessing every kind of stinginess, flattery and deceit constantly showing themselves, such practitioners go forth from home merely to secure a livelihood rather than for nirvana. They wrongly take a counterfeit teaching for true and the true Dharma for false, and regard whoever teaches the true Dharma and discipline as an enemy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this exceptionally detailed inventory of six kinds of stinginess as a fittingly precise diagnostic tool, naming not just a general fault but its exact points of application, one's dwelling, household, gain, reverence, reputation, and even the Dharma itself, so that a practitioner examining their own conduct has real, specific categories to check against rather than a single vague accusation. He highlights the pairing of mistaking counterfeit teaching for genuine and genuine teaching for counterfeit as this passage's own sharpest warning, a confusion that does not merely weaken practice but actively inverts it, and notes that this same inversion, more than any single moral failing, is what this treatise seems most concerned to guard against in describing a declining age.

27. Section: Wrong Conduct Flourishes, Opening with the Gravest Broken Precepts

A further topic then opens, describing how wrong conduct flourishes among śrāvakas born in this same declining age, unfolding across six further categories, the first of which concerns broken precepts. Such practitioners violate the moral precepts extensively, cultivating every manner of unwholesome deed, inwardly rotten through and through while outwardly displaying an appearance of virtue and worthiness, extending even to practitioners who are in truth anything but chaste while nonetheless calling themselves chaste. Among the five categories into which all monastic offenses may be gathered, the unpardonable offense that permanently severs one's standing as a monastic and the pardonable offenses of the second, more serious rank alike, such practitioners deliberately and knowingly commit even these gravest transgressions, to say nothing of the lesser and lightest categories of offense, which they violate all the more freely still.

Chinese Source

多犯尸羅，習諸惡法，內實腐敗，外現賢善；廣說乃至實非梵行，自稱梵行。無餘、有餘二篇重罪，尚起故思現行毀犯，何況中輕。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such practitioners violate the moral precepts extensively, cultivating every unwholesome deed, inwardly rotten while outwardly displaying virtue, even claiming a chastity they do not possess. They deliberately commit even the gravest, unpardonable and pardonable offenses alike, to say nothing of the lesser categories.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold classification of monastic offenses, from the single unpardonable category that ends one's standing as a monastic entirely down to the lightest misdeeds confessable directly before the Buddha, as evidence of just how carefully and compassionately the Vinaya itself is constructed, offering real paths back to purity for every offense except the very gravest, precisely because expectations for a monastic's own conduct are set so high in the first place. He notes that the deliberate, knowing violation of even the gravest offenses described here marks the clearest possible contrast with the earlier picture of a pure-lineage śrāvaka, showing exactly how far conduct can fall even within a lineage genuinely capable of liberation when a declining age's own conditions are allowed to take hold.

28. Section: From Wrong Livelihood to Hell, Closing the Portrait of a Declining Age

The catalogue of flourishing wrong conduct continues through its remaining categories. Having broken the precepts, such practitioners for the most part fail to properly confess and address the offense at all, or confess only once found out by others rather than from any genuine wish to be honest before the community. A second category concerns wrong livelihood: outwardly ordained yet inwardly still bound to worldly pleasures, such practitioners eagerly cultivate connections with relatives, friends, and generous donor households, delighting in unworthy lay affairs, showing far greater affection and reverence toward wealthy lay patrons than toward genuine fellow practitioners sharing the same precepts, and resorting to feigned spiritual attainment, deceptive speech, or manufactured hardship in order to extract donations not rightly earned, while eagerly hoarding household wealth and possessions as though quantity of possession were itself a mark of spiritual excellence. A third category concerns the governing of disciples: such a teacher ordains students chiefly to be used and served rather than out of genuine compassion, however loudly compassion is proclaimed, favoring lazy, indulgent disciples who flatter and serve him while neglecting genuinely diligent ones who do not. A fourth category concerns favoritism in personal regard: honoring monastics with wealthy connections capable of generous support while looking down on monastics who, however genuinely accomplished in contentment and wisdom, lack such connections. A fifth category concerns communal misconduct: consuming communal or others' resources without any remorse, favoring precept-breakers, forming factions with the like-minded, and confessing either too little, from sheer shamelessness, or too much, needlessly confessing what was never actually an offense at all. And a sixth category concerns hearing the Dharma itself only for the sake of reputation and praise or for further material gain, never in order to actually tame one's own body and mind. All such afflicted and corrupted qualities together, one should know, are fully possessed by a śrāvaka born in this declining age, called the age of the Dharma's decline precisely because it names the period after the Great Teacher's own final nirvana when the noble teaching itself has faded from the world; and such a declining age's own śrāvaka disciples, upon the body's dissolution at death, for the most part fall into a miserable destiny, being born instead into the hells.

Ru-Yi Translation

All such afflicted and corrupted qualities are fully possessed by such a śrāvaka. The age of the Dharma's decline names the period after the Great Teacher's own final nirvana when the noble teaching has faded. Such an age's own śrāvaka disciples, upon death, for the most part fall into a miserable destiny, born into the hells.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sixfold catalogue of wrong conduct, precept-breaking, wrong livelihood, self-serving discipleship, favoritism toward the wealthy, communal misconduct, and hearing the Dharma only for reputation, as together forming an unusually candid institutional self-examination, this treatise willing to name precisely how a monastic community itself can decay from within rather than attributing decline only to outside forces. He highlights the blunt closing verdict, that such practitioners for the most part fall into the hells, as a deliberately severe warning rather than mere description, this treatise clearly intending the whole preceding portrait to serve as an examination of conscience for any reader who recognizes even a little of themselves within it.

29. Section: The Fortunate Age's Śrāvaka, the Direct Reverse of Decline

Having described the śrāvaka born in a declining age at such length, the sixth kind now follows directly as its own deliberate mirror image. Whoever instead actually possesses the qualities standing opposite to every one of these corrupted qualities just described, free of broken precepts and wrong livelihood, governing disciples with genuine fairness and valuing the six points of communal harmony, welcoming any earnest, diligently practicing monastic regardless of their outward connections, dwelling among the community with genuine conscience, and hearing the Dharma only in order to tame body and mind, should be known as the śrāvaka born in a wholesome and fortunate age.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever possesses the qualities opposite to these corrupted ones just described should be known as the śrāvaka born in a wholesome and fortunate age.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own remarkable economy, defining an entire sixth kind of śrāvaka in a single sentence simply as the mirror reverse of the exhaustively detailed fifth kind, as a deliberate rhetorical choice, the sheer length already spent cataloguing decline making any comparably detailed positive portrait unnecessary, since a careful reader can simply invert every fault already named to arrive at the corresponding virtue. He notes that a fuller account of exactly what age and circumstances actually produce such a fortunate-age śrāvaka still remains to be explained in what follows, this section offering only the defining contrast rather than the complete picture.

30. Section: An Age without Broken Vows, and the Eye Still Wanting or Already Attained

The fortunate age itself is now explained more precisely. It refers either to the very time when the Tathāgata first appeared in the world, before the corrupting growth that broken precepts and broken views represent had yet arisen, the earliest disciples still entirely free of either fault, or else to a time after the Great Teacher's own final nirvana in which a particular lineage of practitioners nonetheless remains free of broken precepts and broken views, upright in conduct and pure in body and mind alike. Such fortunate-age practitioners, for the most part, upon the body's dissolution at death, return instead to a favorable destiny, reborn among the joyful worlds of the heavens above. A further pair of kinds then follows, concerning what Buddhist teaching calls the eye, a name for genuine wisdom itself. Whatever ordinary, unawakened śrāvaka has not yet attained this wisdom is called one not yet possessing the eye, since without having truly realized the real nature of things, such a practitioner remains, in effect, sighted yet blind, still obstructed by ignorance from genuinely seeing reality as it is. And the stream-enterer, the once-returner, the non-returner, together with the intermediate ranks approaching each of these fruits, are called those already possessing the eye, the Buddha himself declaring that such noble ones have already attained the very eye of wisdom, from the first glimpse of the four truths at the very threshold of the path of seeing, through the stream-enterer whose remaining rebirths are bound by an absolute ceiling of no more than seven further round-trips through the human and heavenly realms, through further intermediate ranks distinguished by exactly how many of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction have already been severed, to the very approach of arhatship itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

The fortunate age refers to when the Tathāgata first appeared, before corruption had arisen, or to a later time free of it still. Such practitioners mostly return to a favorable destiny at death. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the phrase "sighted yet blind" as an unusually vivid way of naming the ordinary condition this whole treatise is written to address, physical sight and even considerable learning proving entirely compatible with a genuine blindness to reality's own true nature until wisdom itself, called here the eye, actually opens. He highlights the stream-enterer's own guaranteed ceiling of at most seven further rebirths as one of this treatise's most concretely reassuring teachings, a definite and calculable limit placed on how much further wandering even this first, still incomplete taste of the eye of wisdom already secures.

31. Section: The Purest Eye of All, and the Ten Kinds' Own Final Closing Pair

The remaining pair of kinds then closes the full list of ten. The arhat liberated through wisdom alone, one who has cultivated the four noble truths at least as far as access concentration and thereby severed ignorance and attained liberation from affliction through wisdom's own power without necessarily attaining the deepest meditative absorptions, is called one possessing the pure eye. And whoever possesses the threefold knowledge, of past lives, of the heavenly eye that sees future rebirths, and of the exhaustion of outflows, together with liberation in both respects, from affliction and from the obstruction of concentration itself, having mastered the nine successive absorptions and thereby also attained the very cessation of feeling and perception, is called one possessing the utterly pure eye. With this the full list of ten kinds of śrāvaka, from the śrāvaka of a pure element through to this utterly pure-eyed arhat, comes to its close. A further and simpler twofold classification then follows. One kind of śrāvaka, however genuinely valiant and diligent the actual effort applied to calm-abiding and insight, still fails to realize any state surpassing the ordinary within this present life. And another kind of śrāvaka, though genuinely possessing, within this very life, the actual capacity to attain such a surpassing state and the very fruit of a genuine renunciant, nonetheless fails to attain it, simply on account of heedlessness.

The arhat liberated through wisdom alone is called one possessing the pure eye. Whoever possesses the threefold knowledge and liberation in both respects is called one possessing the utterly pure eye. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp distinction drawn in this closing pair, between a diligent practitioner who simply has not yet succeeded and a genuinely capable practitioner undone purely by heedlessness, as this section's own quiet but pointed warning, capacity alone guaranteeing nothing at all without the sustained effort to actually use it. He highlights the arhat possessing the threefold knowledge and both forms of liberation as this list's own natural high point, an attainment combining not merely the severing of affliction but a complete and lucid comprehension of one's own causal history across past, present, and future alike, fittingly named the utterly pure eye.

32. Section: Opening the Śrāvaka Canon with the Moon-Simile Sūtra's Four Pure Qualities

With the ten kinds of śrāvaka and their supplementary twofold division now fully explained, the second of this ground's three deeper matters comes to a close, and the third opens: the śrāvaka canon itself, thirteen topics drawn from key sūtras and verses within the Āgama scriptures. The first of these thirteen topics explains the various distinct phrases found within the Moon-Simile Sūtra, precept-complete, virtue-complete, gentle and harmonious, and possessing wholesome qualities, a discourse that likens the ideal conduct of a mendicant approaching a household for alms to the moon's own manner of shining. Briefly, the śrāvaka's own practice comprises four pure and wondrous qualities, such that any being who merely witnesses them, through body, speech, or mind, thereby generates boundless merit. The first of these four is dwelling in complete precepts, upholding the code of individual liberation, one's own bearing and conduct in every respect fully accomplished, regarding even the smallest transgression with genuine dread, and diligently training in every precept one has undertaken to observe, exactly as the fuller sūtra itself explains at length.

Chinese Source

復次，當釋月喻經中具戒、具德、柔和、善法諸句差別。謂聲聞中略有四種淨妙之法，令諸有情若得見者，由身語意生無量福。何等為四？謂住具足尸羅，守別解脫律儀，廣說如經，是名初法。

Ru-Yi Translation

We shall next explain the various phrases in the Moon-Simile Sūtra: precept-complete, virtue-complete, gentle and harmonious, and wholesome qualities. Briefly, the śrāvaka's practice comprises four pure and wondrous qualities, such that any being who witnesses them generates boundless merit. The first is dwelling in complete precepts, upholding the code of individual liberation, exactly as the sūtra explains at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the framing device of this entire thirteen-part canon section, working outward from individual named sūtras and verses within the Āgama scriptures rather than from abstract categories alone, as a fitting way to close this ground's deeper matters, grounding even its most careful philosophical distinctions in the Buddha's own actual recorded teaching. He highlights the striking claim that a being need only witness these four qualities, without necessarily understanding the doctrine behind them, to generate boundless merit, as a reminder that genuine virtue in this treatise's own view carries its own visible, almost luminous power, moon-like precisely in how naturally its light reaches whoever simply happens to see it.

33. Section: Complete Precepts in Full, and the Virtue of Wanting Little

The first of the Moon-Simile Sūtra's four pure qualities, complete precepts, is now explained at greater length. Proper deportment means following, without deviation, the established discipline governing every aspect of a monastic's own bearing across walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, and every task proper to communal monastic life, entering the hall, practicing in one's assigned place, studying the teaching, or attending upon one's teacher, all conducted strictly within the bounds this discipline sets. Proper conduct means avoiding five kinds of place a monastic should never enter at all, the household of one who summons others to disreputable gatherings, a brothel, a place selling liquor, a royal palace, and the household of an outcast executioner or dog-butcher, going instead, at fitting times, only to blameless places, and this passage's own unflinching inclusion of caste-based outcast households among these forbidden places reflects the deeply stratified society this teaching first addressed, a stark reminder of how such prejudice was simply assumed rather than challenged at the time. Regarding even the smallest transgression with genuine dread means holding real fear at heart toward any lapse, however minor and however easily confessed and purified, precisely because even a small fault left unexamined threatens one's own capacity for realizing the noble path, risks an unfavorable rebirth, and stains one's reputation everywhere. And training in every precept one has undertaken means skillfully studying whatever precepts have been received from one's own preceptor or from any other genuine teacher, so thoroughly that transgression becomes rare, and any transgression that does occur is immediately and properly confessed. Such complete deportment, conduct, dread of small faults, and precept training together constitute the sūtra's own first pure quality in full. A second pure quality, virtue itself, is then described: contentment with little, wanting nothing more than whatever robe, food, or lodging one happens to have, and, crucially, never wishing others to know of one's own contentment or of any other genuine accomplishment, since publicizing such virtue only invites fame and material gain, the very temptations most likely to lead a practitioner astray.

Chinese Source

軌則、所行皆悉圓滿，於微小罪見大怖畏，受學學處，是名廣說應知。復有少欲喜足，廣說乃至諸漏永盡作證讚美，是第二法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Complete deportment and conduct, regarding even the smallest transgression with genuine dread, and training in every precept, together constitute the first quality in full. A second quality is contentment with little, wanting nothing more than what one has, and never wishing others to know of this or any other virtue one possesses.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the deliberate concealment of one's own contentment as this second quality's own most demanding requirement, harder in practice than contentment itself, since it asks a practitioner to forgo even the quiet satisfaction of being recognized as virtuous. He notes, without softening the historical record, that the sūtra's list of forbidden places includes households marked by caste-based exclusion current in its own time and place, treating this as a reminder to read such passages with an awareness of the society that first produced them rather than importing their social assumptions unexamined into the present.

34. Section: Gentle and Easy to Live With, Finding Fault Nowhere but in Oneself

A third pure quality is then described: being gentle and harmonious, genuinely easy to live alongside, never provoking or troubling wise fellow practitioners sharing the same discipline, so that other monastics take real joy in dwelling together with such a person, and possessing, besides, the four kinds of wholly pure faith, in the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, and the precepts themselves. Living harmoniously in community means learning to appreciate what might be called the beauty of imperfection, since no companion, and no self either, is ever without fault, and dwelling on another's shortcomings only breeds one's own affliction rather than resolving anything. When another's flaw provokes irritation, the wiser response is to examine the irritation itself rather than the flaw, since the very moment of anger, however apparently justified, is already an unwholesome karma in its own right; turning inward in this way, recognizing that no fixed self stands ready to be offended and no fixed other stands ready to be blamed, is what actually allows practitioners to dwell together in genuine and lasting harmony.

Chinese Source

復有柔和易可共住，不惱有智同梵行者，令諸苾芻喜樂同處，又具成就四種證淨，是第三法。

Ru-Yi Translation

A third quality is being gentle and harmonious, genuinely easy to live alongside, never provoking wise fellow practitioners, so that other monastics take joy in dwelling together with such a person, and possessing the four kinds of wholly pure faith in the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, and the precepts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the teaching that even righteous anger toward a genuine fault still counts as an unwholesome karma in its own right as this quality's own sharpest and most practically demanding point, since it denies a practitioner even the usual refuge of feeling justified in irritation. He offers the classic image of a mirror here, that a companion's apparent flaw functions only as a mirror reflecting back one's own mind, and that wiping at the mirror itself accomplishes nothing when the actual smudge belongs to one's own face, as a vivid summary of why genuine communal harmony always begins with turning attention inward rather than outward.

35. Section: Wholesome Qualities Unpublicized, and Three Afflictions to Sever before Almsround

A fourth and final pure quality is then described: never craving material gain, never attached to others' reverence, genuinely compassionate toward others, concealing one's own wholesome qualities rather than displaying them, openly confessing one's own faults rather than hiding them, and, even where real accomplishment in calm-abiding, insight, or any other virtue genuinely exists, never wishing others to know of it or to be moved toward reverence and offering on that account. With these four qualities, complete precepts, contentment, gentle harmony, and unpublicized virtue, the Moon-Simile Sūtra's own core teaching is complete. A further teaching then follows concerning a monastic's actual visit to a donor's household for alms: before going, three secondary afflictions must first be severed. The first is the affliction of cultivating overly familiar ties with a donor household purely to secure ever more reverence and support; the second is the affliction of household stinginess, wishing to be the sole or foremost visitor received by a given household, begrudging any other monastic equal access; and the third is the affliction of teaching the Dharma with a mind already contaminated by the wish for fame or gain. Regarding the first of these three, a monastic should sever it by six distinguishing marks before ever approaching a household at all. The first mark is possessing a genuine sense of shame, visiting a household only at fitting and appropriately spaced times rather than too frequently, and feeling immediate shame the moment even a fleeting attachment arises toward some pleasing object encountered there. The second mark is freedom from arrogance, never resorting to harsh, boastful speech or haughty, improper conduct in pursuit of robes, food, or other requisites, and never falsely embellishing one's own reputation or leaning on the prestige of one's family lineage in order to win greater regard from others.

Chinese Source

又有不耽利養，不著恭敬，憐愍於他，覆藏己善，發露己惡，雖復實有種種功德，而不欲求令他知有，是第四法。復次，若有聲聞欲往他家，應先斷除三隨煩惱，然後當往。一、結親友家隨煩惱，二、家慳隨煩惱，三、以有染心而行法施隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

A fourth quality is never craving gain, never attached to reverence, genuinely compassionate, concealing one's own virtues, openly confessing one's own faults, and never wishing others to know of real accomplishment. A monastic visiting a donor's household should first sever three afflictions: cultivating overly familiar ties, household stinginess, and teaching the Dharma with a mind contaminated by the wish for fame or gain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of concealing one's own virtues with openly confessing one's own faults as this fourth quality's own elegant symmetry, a monastic's public image meant to run, if anything, in the opposite direction from an ordinary person's natural instinct to display strengths and hide weaknesses. He highlights household stinginess, the wish to be a donor's sole or favorite recipient, as a fault easy to overlook precisely because it can masquerade as simple gratitude or closeness, making the six marks that follow for severing even the milder affliction of overly familiar household ties a genuinely useful, concrete checklist for self-examination.

36. Section: A Mind Cleansed like a Familiar, Clear Pool

The remaining marks for severing the affliction of overly familiar household ties are now explained, together forming what is called cleansing body and mind through the Dharma. The third mark is guarding oneself well, keeping the body properly covered and composed, never touching what should not be touched, sitting where one should not sit, eating what should not be eaten, drinking what should not be drunk, especially any intoxicant, or accepting what should not be accepted, thereby protecting the very substance of one's own precepts. The fourth mark is careful reflection, maintaining a mind inclined toward renunciation, moving steadily toward it, and drawing ever nearer to it, so as to reflect firmly and mindfully on wholesome qualities such as precepts, concentration, and wisdom throughout the visit. The fifth mark is inspiring faith where it does not yet exist, approaching even a household without pure faith in the three jewels the way one might climb a genuinely difficult mountain, undertaking real effort out of compassion in order to bring such faith into being. And the sixth mark is doubling faith where it already exists, treating a household already possessing pure faith the way one treats a clear, familiar pool once visited before, returning there precisely in order to deepen that same faith still further. A monastic who accomplishes all six of these marks has thereby severed the first of the three secondary afflictions, the affliction of overly familiar household ties, and may rightly proceed to a donor's household to receive alms or to teach.

Chinese Source

善自守護，善覆其身，不以身觸所不應觸，亦不坐於所不應坐，終不食於所不應食，亦終不飲所不應飲，又不應受所不應受。猶如山岳難往趣處，非淨信家能令淨信。如舊所履清淨泉池，諸淨信家倍令增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Guarding oneself well, keeping the body properly covered, never touching, sitting, eating, drinking, or accepting what should not be. Like a difficult mountain, one brings faith to a household without it. Like a familiar, clear pool, one deepens the faith of a household that already possesses it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the paired images closing this set of six marks, a difficult mountain climbed for the sake of a household still without faith and a familiar clear pool revisited for the sake of one that already has it, as a fitting summary of a genuine teacher's twofold task, neither neglecting the harder work of first planting faith nor growing complacent about faith already present, since both equally deserve real and sustained effort. He highlights the third mark's own concrete, almost monastic-etiquette-manual specificity, exactly what may be touched, sat upon, eaten, or drunk, as this treatise's own reminder that lofty spiritual aims are always meant to be lived out through entirely ordinary, minute bodily choices.

37. Section: Equanimity toward Gain, Whether One's Own or Another's

A further six marks then follow, by which the second secondary affliction, household stinginess, is severed before ever approaching a donor's household. The first three marks concern remaining free of attachment and defilement toward interpersonal matters at a donor's household, toward the very gain received there, and, in just the same way, toward the reverence shown there. The next two marks concern feeling no sorrow or distress whatever should no gain be forthcoming, and, in just the same way, feeling no sorrow or distress whatever should no reverence be shown. And the sixth and final mark concerns holding an entirely equal mind toward whatever gain and reverence is received, whether by oneself or by another practitioner, never praising oneself for what one has personally received and never disparaging another for what they have received in turn. A monastic who accomplishes all six of these marks has thereby severed household stinginess as well.

Chinese Source

謂往他家，於有情事不染不著。如有情事，於利養事、於恭敬事，當知亦爾。又於無利不生憂苦。如於無利，於不恭敬，當知亦爾。又於自他諸利養中其心平等，於己所得利養恭敬不自讚美，於他所得利養恭敬終不毀訾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Remaining free of attachment toward interpersonal matters, gain, and reverence at a donor's household; feeling no sorrow at receiving no gain or reverence; and holding an equal mind toward gain received by oneself or another, neither praising oneself nor disparaging another. By these six marks, household stinginess is severed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the final mark's own even-handed treatment of self-praise and disparaging others as two faces of exactly the same underlying fault, a mind still keeping score of gain and reverence rather than having genuinely let go of the whole comparison. He notes that these six marks progress in a clear order, from non-attachment, through equanimity when gain fails to appear, to equanimity even when comparing one's own outcome against another's, each stage removing a slightly subtler layer of the same basic clinging to recognition.

38. Section: Teaching the Dharma for the Donor's Sake, Not One's Own

A third and final set of six marks then follows, by which the affliction of teaching the Dharma with a mind already contaminated by the wish for fame or gain is severed before ever approaching a donor's household to teach. The first mark is not wishing the donor's pure faith to settle upon oneself personally at all, but only upon the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, and the precepts themselves. The second mark is genuinely and thoroughly understanding, in one's own direct experience, the very path of renunciation one intends to teach, since only a teacher who has truly grasped how liberation and unsurpassed awakening are actually accomplished can genuinely benefit a donor at all. A further three marks then follow, concerning three kinds of wholly wholesome intention a monastic should hold toward the donor: the intention to bring about genuine ease, the intention to remove suffering, and the intention that the donor, through reverently hearing and then actually practicing the teaching received, might attain the very highest benefit it offers. Removing suffering, more precisely, means freeing a donor already afflicted by birth, aging, sickness, death, and every other form of suffering from that very affliction, by leading them, through the Dharma actually taught, toward genuine practice of the noble path.

Chinese Source

謂不希望他於己淨信。又於出離法如實了知。又於他所應起三種純善意樂。何等為三？謂引發樂故，除遣苦故，恭敬聽受法隨法行得勝利故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not wishing a donor's pure faith to settle upon oneself, and genuinely understanding the path of renunciation oneself. A monastic should further hold three wholly wholesome intentions toward the donor: bringing about ease, removing suffering, and enabling the highest benefit through reverently hearing and practicing the teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the very first mark here, redirecting a donor's faith away from the teacher personally and toward the three jewels themselves, as this whole section's own quiet safeguard against exactly the kind of charismatic self-promotion the earlier portrait of a declining age warned against at such length. He highlights the requirement that a teacher must genuinely understand the path of renunciation in their own direct experience, not merely repeat received formulas, as this treatise's own consistent insistence that authentic teaching can never be separated from authentic practice.

39. Section: Teaching Even the Already Liberated, and the Three Afflictions' Own Final Result

The sixth and final mark for severing a contaminated mind in teaching the Dharma is then explained: even toward a donor who has already attained liberation, the subtle and wholly wholesome nature of every dharma, gathered within the conventionally established true teaching found across the discourses, the discipline, and the systematic treatises, should still be taught, precisely so that such a donor continues to uphold, recite, and thereby help the true teaching endure long into the future, since even the already liberated benefit from strengthening what keeps the Dharma itself alive in the world. With this the full set of eighteen marks, six for each of the three secondary afflictions to be severed before ever approaching a donor's household, is complete, and the ultimate result of severing each is now revealed in turn. One should know that once the first affliction, overly familiar household ties, is severed, its own final result is no longer wishing to dwell indiscriminately mixed among both monastic and lay company. Once the second affliction, household stinginess, is severed, its own final result is rightly making use of material support, precisely what constitutes right livelihood. And once the third affliction, contaminated Dharma-teaching, is severed, its own final result is rightly making use of the Dharma itself, free of any distortion. With this the Moon-Simile Sūtra's own full teaching, the first of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, comes to its close.

Chinese Source

當知初隨煩惱斷故，無猥雜住以為究竟。第二隨煩惱斷故，正受用財以為究竟。第三隨煩惱斷故，正受用法以為究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that once the first affliction is severed, its final result is no longer dwelling indiscriminately mixed among company. Once the second is severed, its final result is rightly making use of material support. Once the third is severed, its final result is rightly making use of the Dharma itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing summary of three final results, no indiscriminate mixing, right livelihood, and right use of the Dharma, as an unusually clean and memorable way to close eighteen individually detailed marks, distilling everything just explained into three outcomes any practitioner can hold in mind as a simple, ongoing check on their own conduct. He highlights the instruction to keep teaching even an already liberated donor, purely for the sake of the true teaching's own long survival, as a fitting final note on how seriously this treatise takes the preservation of the Dharma itself, treating it as a responsibility that continues even after personal liberation has already been achieved.

40. Section: Opening the Vṛji Discourse — One Body, Two Very Different Uses

A further topic then opens: the second of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, explaining the Vṛji Discourse. If a śrāvaka wishes to become genuinely skilled in the very means by which defilement and purity each come about, three matters, briefly, should be thoroughly understood: the shared basis underlying both defiled and pure action, defilement itself, and purity itself. The shared basis refers to this very body, coarse and burdensome, composed of the coarse four elements, sustained only through food, requiring constant covering, bathing, and care, and destined, in the end, to break apart, decay, and dissolve, exactly as the discourse itself explains at length. This same coarse body serves, on the one hand, as the basis on which a foolish being lacking wisdom and inclined toward ignorance carries out defiled action, and, on the other hand, as the very same basis on which a wise being of great intelligence inclined toward genuine understanding carries out pure action instead, one and the same vessel capable, like water itself, of either bearing a boat safely along or capsizing it entirely, depending only on how it is used.

Chinese Source

復次，當釋伐地迦經。若有聲聞欲於染淨方便善巧，略於三處應遍了知。謂於雜染、清淨所依中⋯此是愚夫無有智慧趣無明者雜染所依，亦是聰明有大智慧趣於明者清淨所依。

Ru-Yi Translation

We shall next explain the Vṛji Discourse. If a śrāvaka wishes to become skilled in the means of defilement and purity, three matters should be understood: the shared basis of both, defilement itself, and purity itself. This coarse body is both the foolish being's basis for defiled action and the wise being's basis for pure action.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the water-and-boat image, one and the same body capable of either bearing a practitioner safely toward liberation or capsizing them into ever deeper defilement, as this discourse's own memorable rebuttal to any temptation toward simple body-denial, since the very same coarse physical basis that enables affliction is also the indispensable vehicle for genuine practice. He highlights the discourse's own opening insistence on thoroughly understanding all three matters together, shared basis, defilement, and purity, rather than any one alone, as a reminder that this treatise consistently prefers a complete diagnostic picture over a partial one before recommending any actual remedy.

41. Section: How Karma Shapes Rebirth, and the Two Trainings That Purify

Defilement itself is then explained: it refers to how a foolish, ordinary being, wishing to carry out either a pure or an impure deed, first deliberates mentally before then actually carrying out, through body and speech, whatever pure or impure deed results, and, through the causal power of these very deeds, comes to acquire, across the five destinies of birth and death, whether human, heavenly, hellish, hungry-ghost, or animal, a resulting body either pleasing and untroubled or displeasing and greatly afflicted, exactly as the particular deeds performed determine. Purity is then explained in turn: among the trainings that produce genuine purity, two prove supreme above all others. The first of these two is the training in higher mind, which serves as the very basis on which the wisdom directly realizing suchness itself is able to accomplish its work; and the second is the training in higher wisdom, through which every affliction is finally and completely freed from its own binding power. It is precisely these two trainings, cultivating concentration and cultivating insight together, that most effectively purify a practitioner's own mind of affliction, and are therefore rightly called the supreme means to genuine purity.

Chinese Source

云何雜染？謂諸愚夫，為欲造作淨不淨業，先尋思已，後以身語造作所有淨不淨業；由此因緣，於五趣生死中，感愛非愛有惱無惱自體令生。云何清淨？謂二種學清淨品中最為殊勝。第一學者，謂真如智以為依止能有所作；第二學者，謂為煩惱皆得離繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is defilement? An ordinary being, wishing to act, deliberates and then carries out a pure or impure deed through body and speech, acquiring through this a pleasing or displeasing body across the five destinies. What is purity? Among the trainings, two are supreme: the training in higher mind, basis for the wisdom of suchness, and the training in higher wisdom, which frees one from affliction's binding power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise sequence given for how an ordinary deed actually unfolds, mental deliberation first, bodily and verbal action only afterward, as this treatise's own consistent insistence that karma begins in intention long before it ever shows up in visible conduct, making the mind itself the real point of practice rather than outward behavior alone. He highlights the pairing of higher-mind training as the basis enabling wisdom's own work and higher-wisdom training as what actually severs affliction's binding power as a precise division of labor between concentration and insight, concentration providing the stable ground and insight doing the actual work of liberation upon it.

42. Section: Five Causes That Bring Purity to Light

Purity itself, having been briefly introduced through its two supreme trainings, is now explained in fuller detail, by way of five distinct causes through which it comes to be revealed. The first cause is the one who correctly teaches, namely the Tathāgata himself, since even a being of real capacity can accomplish nothing at all toward purity without first encountering someone able to correctly point the way, exactly as approaching a genuine teacher stands as the very first and most fundamental of the four conditions for entering the stream of noble practice. The second cause is the one who correctly practices, namely a śrāvaka whose faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom have already ripened, also called a genuinely wise person, one whose every bodily, verbal, and mental action already accords with what is wholesome, since without such ripened faculties no amount of effort within a single lifetime can bring the noble path to realization. And the third cause is correct practice itself, namely the very wisdom belonging to the four noble truths, since this wisdom alone, actually severing the seeds of affliction rather than merely suppressing their manifestation the way concentration by itself can only do, stands as the truly unsurpassed antidote to affliction.

Chinese Source

復次，即此清淨，略由五因之所顯示。何等為五？一、正說者，二、正行者，三、正行⋯此中如來是正說者。根熟聲聞是正行者，亦名聰慧者。諦智所攝名為正行。據能斷煩惱義，是無上對治故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Purity itself is revealed, briefly, by five causes. The Tathāgata is the one who correctly teaches. A śrāvaka of ripened faculties is the one who correctly practices, also called a genuinely wise person. The wisdom of the four noble truths is correct practice itself, since it alone, actually severing affliction's seeds, is the truly unsurpassed antidote.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp distinction drawn here between wisdom, which actually severs affliction's own seeds, and concentration alone, which can only suppress affliction's manifestation without uprooting it, as this passage's own precise statement of why insight rather than tranquility alone must always remain the path's ultimate aim, however genuinely valuable calm concentration is along the way. He highlights the naming of encountering a genuine teacher as the very first of the four conditions for entering the noble stream as a fitting reminder that even the most capable practitioner accomplishes nothing at all in complete isolation, the Dharma always first arriving through another's genuine transmission.

43. Section: Five Afflictions the Training in Higher Mind Overcomes

The fourth cause, the severing of secondary afflictions through the trainings in higher mind and higher wisdom, is then explained, beginning with what the training in higher mind specifically overcomes: briefly, five such afflictions. The first is whatever hindrance troubles one dwelling in solitude, the five familiar hindrances of sensory craving, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt, which concentration first suppresses in their coarser form to attain access concentration and then, in their subtler form, to attain the first absorption itself. The second is whatever anger or vexation arises toward a teacher's own instruction or admonition when a practitioner proves unable to bear correction, whether concerning meditation and wisdom or concerning the precepts themselves. The third is whatever stinginess or envy arises from deep attachment to gain and reverence, unwilling to share what one has received or resentful of what another has received instead. The fourth is whatever wrong recollection arises toward objects, whether sights, sounds, or other sensory experience, once enjoyed or received in the past, dwelling with distorted longing upon former pleasures. And the fifth is whatever discriminative craving arises toward such objects strongly enough to lead a practitioner to abandon the very precepts once undertaken, simply in order to indulge that craving more freely. It is the training in higher mind, cultivated deeply enough, that overcomes each of these five afflictions in turn, since a mind genuinely absorbed in meditative joy grows correspondingly less inclined toward worldly craving in the first place.

Chinese Source

略有五法，名增上心學所治隨煩惱。一、居遠離者，所有諸蓋；二、於教授教誡不堪忍者，所有忿、惱；三、於利養恭敬深貪著者，所有慳、嫉；四、於先所用、所受境界發起邪念；五、順捨所學分別貪愛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly, five afflictions are overcome by the training in higher mind: the hindrances troubling one dwelling in solitude; anger and vexation toward instruction one cannot bear; stinginess and envy over gain and reverence; wrong recollection toward past sensory objects; and discriminative craving strong enough to lead one to abandon the precepts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifth affliction, discriminative craving strong enough to lead a practitioner to actually abandon precepts already undertaken, as this list's own most serious warning, showing that unchecked craving can eventually unravel not merely a single moment of practice but an entire commitment to the path itself. He notes that genuine, deep meditative absorption naturally starves worldly craving of its usual appeal, offering a real taste of ease that renders ordinary sensory pleasure comparatively unappealing, which is precisely why sustained concentration practice proves so effective against exactly this kind of affliction.

44. Section: Arhatship as Utmost Purity, Closing the Five Causes

The training in higher wisdom, for its part, overcomes three further afflictions. The first is whatever ignorance remains toward the conventional teaching set forth through language, failing to genuinely comprehend its own meaning. The second is whatever lingering doubt and hesitation remains, even in an ordinary being who has already come to understand that meaning intellectually, regarding the four noble truths themselves, an affliction the training in higher wisdom overcomes precisely at the path of seeing, thereby enabling the direct realization of the truths. And the third is whatever innate conceit still remains, even in a noble learner who has already glimpsed the very footprint of the noble truths, an affliction the training in higher wisdom, as it belongs to the path of cultivation, continues to overcome even after that first direct realization. Once these afflictions belonging to both trainings are permanently and completely extinguished, one should know that the fruit of arhatship, complete with the most excellent and thoroughly purified higher-mind training and higher-wisdom training alike, has genuinely been attained, and that such an arhat's own mind should be known as utmost purity itself, abiding at every moment in the highest equanimity. With this the detailed explanation of purity by way of its five causes, the correct teacher, the correctly practicing śrāvaka, correct practice, and the afflictions overcome by each of the two supreme trainings, comes to its close.

Chinese Source

初，於世俗理門不了法義者，所有無明；次，已了法義諸異生者，於諸諦中所有猶豫疑惑未斷；後，已見諦跡諸有學者，修道所攝慧所對治所有我慢。由於如是諸隨煩惱永斷滅故，當知證得最善清淨增上心學、增上慧學阿羅漢果，此阿羅漢當知是名最極清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, ignorance of the conventional teaching's own meaning; next, lingering doubt regarding the truths even once that meaning is understood; and last, innate conceit remaining even in a learner who has seen the truths. Once these are permanently extinguished, the fruit of arhatship, complete with utmost purity in both trainings, is attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third and final affliction, an innate conceit persisting even after a noble learner has already directly witnessed the truths, as this list's own sobering reminder that direct realization itself does not instantly complete the path, subtle traces of self-regard still requiring the path of cultivation's own continued, patient work even after the breakthrough of seeing has already occurred. He highlights the closing image of an arhat abiding at every moment in the highest equanimity as this whole five-cause explanation's own fitting summit, purity here culminating not in some dramatic final event but in an entirely settled, undisturbed ease sustained continuously through every ordinary moment.

45. Section: What Should Be Thoroughly Known about This Shared Bodily Basis

A further topic then opens: what a śrāvaka should thoroughly understand regarding this same coarse body, shared basis of both defilement and purity alike. Briefly, three marks should be understood: the body's own intrinsic nature, the causal conditions that bring it about, and the faults it carries. Regarding intrinsic nature, the human body proper to the desire realm is revealed through three further aspects. By possessing form at all, it shares the same basic character common to every other body whatsoever, since all are alike composed of the four elements. By being coarse and burdensome, the varying degree of affliction's own coarse residue carried within each individual mindstream, it reveals each being's own distinct, individual character, no two bodies burdened in quite the same way. And by being impure, given over to ordinary food and to sexual desire, it reveals a character altogether different in kind from the bodies of beings born into the heavens of the desire realm and beyond, whose own nature is comparatively refined.

Chinese Source

復次，於此雜染、清淨所依諸聲聞眾，略由三相應遍了知。一、由自性故；二、由因緣故；三、由過患故。由有色故，顯餘一切身之共相；由麤重故，顯其各別身之自相；由不清淨故，顯與天身不同分相。

Ru-Yi Translation

A śrāvaka should thoroughly understand this shared basis by three marks: its intrinsic nature, its causal conditions, and its faults. By possessing form, it shares the common character of every body; by being coarse, it reveals each being's own individual character; by being impure, it reveals a character unlike that of heavenly bodies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold framework of intrinsic nature, causal conditions, and fault as this treatise's own consistent method for examining absolutely anything with real thoroughness, a pattern already seen applied to dharmas of every kind now turned specifically upon the practitioner's own body. He highlights the distinction between the body's shared, common character as mere form and its own distinctly individual character as coarse residue, varying precisely according to each being's own accumulated affliction, as a reminder that even something as basic as physical embodiment already carries the unmistakable fingerprint of one's own particular karmic history.

46. Section: Conceived through Union, Sustained through Food, Undone by Cold and Blade Alike

The causal conditions bringing this body about are then explained, briefly three in number. The first is the single cause shared by every body whatsoever, namely the four elements' own respective, particular characteristics. The remaining two are specific to the arising of a human body in particular: the cause that first brings an as-yet-unborn body into being, namely the impure union of father and mother, and the cause that sustains an already-born body once it exists, namely the nourishment provided by food. The faults such a body carries are then explained, briefly two in number: its nature as suffering, and its nature as impermanent. Regarding its nature as suffering, when cold weather brings its own suffering, covering and warm clothing must be sought as a remedy; when heat brings its own suffering, bathing must be sought as a remedy; and when the fatigue of travel or labor brings its own suffering, massage and similar relief must be sought as a remedy, this same pattern of suffering met only by seeking some further remedy, on and on without end, being what is meant by the body's own nature as suffering. Regarding its nature as impermanent, a body struck by hand, stone, or staff breaks under the blow, since it is, by nature, a thing capable of being broken; a body touched by a blade is cut and wounded, since it is, by nature, a thing capable of being severed; and once life itself has ended, that same body is buried in the earth, or consumed by fire, or devoured by animals and insects, or left to be dried and worn away by wind and sun, every one of these fates simply making plain what was already true all along, that the body's very nature is to scatter, decay, and finally dissolve away entirely.

Chinese Source

A human body's own causes are two further: the union of father and mother that first brings an unborn body into being, and the nourishment of food that sustains an already-born body. Its faults are two: its nature as impermanent, and its nature as suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the deliberately unflinching catalogue of what ultimately happens to every body, struck, cut, buried, burned, devoured, or simply weathered away, as this treatise's own classic contemplation of impermanence pressed to its full and honest conclusion, offered not to induce despair but to loosen the ordinary, unexamined attachment a practitioner otherwise carries toward their own physical form. He highlights the suffering caused merely by cold, heat, and fatigue as this passage's own reminder that even the most ordinary, unremarkable discomforts of daily embodied life already qualify as genuine instances of the body's own inherent suffering nature, no dramatic crisis required to make the point.

47. Section: Scattering, Decay, and Final Dissolution, Closing the Vṛji Discourse

The very meaning of scattering, decay, and dissolution is then clarified, closing the explanation of impermanence and, with it, the Vṛji Discourse's own full teaching, the second of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics. What once, in life, remained gathered together as a single functioning whole later falls apart entirely once life itself ends, the earth element returning to earth, water to water, fire to fire, and wind to wind, and this very falling-apart is called scattering; what has scattered continues, over time, to change and decay, until at last nothing whatsoever remains, and this final vanishing is called dissolution. A further topic then opens: the third of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, returning once more to the verse on name already introduced earlier in this treatise's own decisive discernment, repeated here because of its own exceptional importance for dispelling ignorance, severing affliction, and purifying the mind. As this verse itself says, name eclipses everything, nothing surpasses name; by this one thing, name, everything follows self-mastery's course. Briefly, this verse carries four distinct meanings: observing the cause of purity, observing name's own characteristic, observing the cause of defilement, and revealing the twin emptiness of self and of every dharma.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What was once gathered later falls apart, called scattering; what has scattered continues to decay until nothing remains, called dissolution. A further topic returns to the verse on name: name eclipses everything, nothing surpasses name; by this one thing, name, everything follows self-mastery's course. This carries four meanings: the cause of purity, name's own characteristic, the cause of defilement, and the twin emptiness of self and dharmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this deliberate repetition of the name verse, already explained once earlier in this treatise's decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection, as itself a teaching device, the text's own way of signaling that this particular insight is important enough to bear returning to a second time from a fresh angle rather than being mentioned only once and left behind. He notes that readers already familiar with the earlier explanation, self-grasping and dharma-grasping both ultimately resting on nothing more than name, will find this section deepening rather than merely restating that same core insight.

48. Section: Name Eclipses Everything, Closing the Verse and Opening the Four Truths

Each line of this verse is then explained in turn. Name eclipses everything, first, because thoroughly comprehending that a person is without any real self, that the four formless aggregates themselves are merely a name, allows a practitioner to sever every form of self-grasping projected onto one's own experience; and second, because thoroughly comprehending that every dharma is likewise without any real self, that language's own conventionally imagined nature carries no genuine independent reality, allows a practitioner to remove every attachment to whatever such language merely designates. Nothing surpasses name, next, because no notion of a real self is ever found to exceed these same four formless aggregates once they are properly understood as mere name, and no notion of a real dharma-nature is ever found to exceed conventional language once it too is properly understood as mere name. And the final two lines, observing the cause of defilement, explain that failing to genuinely comprehend either of these two kinds of name is precisely what allows every defilement bound up with one's own sense of self and with one's attachment to things as genuinely real to keep on unfolding, every such defilement arising through the compounding power of exactly these two namings; the World-Honored One himself, in reference to this same hidden meaning, spoke a further verse, that because of grasping at a dharma's own nature one grasps at a self and continues on, that realizing the former brings realization of the latter, and that through this realization cessation itself follows and returns. With this the verse on name's own full meaning, the third of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, comes to its close. A further topic then opens: the fourth topic, explaining the four noble truths, the very object that purifies affliction, as already introduced earlier in the Śrāvaka Ground itself.

Chinese Source

若遍了知補特伽羅無我四無色蘊，能斷一切自境界相，名映一切。若遍了知法無我遍計所執自性世俗言名，能除一切彼所依相，名映一切。依此密意，薄伽梵說：執法自性故，執我性而轉，覺此故覺彼，由覺故還滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Comprehending that a person lacks a real self, that the four formless aggregates are mere name, severs all self-grasping. Comprehending that every dharma lacks a real self, that conventional language too is mere name, removes attachment to what it designates. The World-Honored One accordingly said: grasping a dharma's nature leads to grasping a self; realizing the former brings realizing the latter, and cessation follows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing citation, that dharma-grasping actually precedes and gives rise to self-grasping rather than the reverse, as this verse's own most important corrective to the common intuition that clinging to a self is somehow the more basic error, revealing instead that the deeper root lies in treating any dharma at all, not merely the self, as possessing some independent reality of its own. He notes the Sixth Patriarch's own famous line, that fundamentally there is not a single thing, so where could dust ever settle, as an apt echo of this same insight from within the Chan tradition, both traditions converging on the same recognition that affliction ultimately rests on nothing more solid than an unexamined name.

49. Section: Do False Views Still Count among the Noble Truths?

The fourth of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics now opens in full: an explanation of the four noble truths, which the World-Honored One taught to śrāvakas as the very object that purifies affliction, namely the truth of suffering and the rest, exactly as already explained earlier in the Śrāvaka Ground itself, suffering encompassing the eight forms of suffering that pervade ordinary existence, origin explaining how affliction and karma draw forth suffering's own future fruit across the three realms, cessation naming the very extinguishing of both affliction and its resulting suffering that constitutes nirvana, and the path naming the actual practice by which affliction is removed and nirvana approached. A difficult question is then raised concerning this very teaching: if the defining mark of a truth is precisely its being genuinely real and free of distortion, how can heretical views, mistaken convictions, and wrongheaded theories, none of them genuinely real and all of them thoroughly distorted, still be gathered under the truth of suffering or the truth of origin at all? And if they are not properly gathered there, should it not follow that such wrong views fail to draw forth any future rebirth and thus fail to count as a genuine cause of suffering in the first place, a conclusion that plainly contradicts this treatise's own repeated teaching elsewhere that wrong view is itself among the most potent causes of continued suffering?

Chinese Source

復次，有四聖諦，世尊為諸聲聞說是淨煩惱所緣境界，謂苦諦等，如前已說。問：若真實無顛倒相是諦相者，諸外道見、諸邪勝解、諸邪論等非真非實，并是顛倒，云何諦攝？若不攝者，彼應不感當來後有，應非苦因？

Ru-Yi Translation

The four noble truths, which the World-Honored One taught as the object purifying affliction, are as already explained. A question is raised: if a truth must be genuinely real and undistorted, how can heretical views and wrong theories, themselves distorted, still be gathered under these truths? If not, should they not fail to draw forth future rebirth as a cause of suffering?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening difficulty as a genuinely sharp piece of internal criticism, correctly noticing an apparent tension in calling something a truth while also gathering under it views that are themselves false, and thereby setting up a resolution that promises to clarify exactly what kind of reality the truth of suffering and the truth of origin actually claim to describe. He notes that the answer to this difficulty still lies ahead in what follows, cautioning against assuming the question exposes a genuine flaw before the treatise's own reply has actually been read.

50. Section: False in Content Yet Real as Suffering's Own Cause

The difficulty concerning heretical views is now resolved: although such views, convictions, and theories are indeed distorted and not genuinely real in their own content, the very fact of their being distorted in just this way is itself something real and undistorted, since they genuinely do function as the nature of suffering and as a genuine cause of suffering, drawing forth continued wandering through birth and death precisely because they obstruct liberation. It is on this basis, their real causal power rather than their false content, that they are rightly gathered under the truth of suffering and the truth of origin after all. The four aspects proper to the truth of suffering, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, are then explained one by one, each admitting of both a great-vehicle and a lesser-vehicle sense. Impermanence is shown through two such senses: in the great vehicle's own sense, it means simple non-existence, since the imagined nature that ordinary beings project onto conventional names and language was never real to begin with in any time or place at all; and in the lesser vehicle's own sense, it means destruction, since every conditioned dharma among the five aggregates arises and perishes moment by moment, subject to real and observable dissolution. The lesser vehicle's teaching addresses only this second, coarser sense, while the great vehicle's own teaching addresses both senses together, entering the path chiefly through the gate of impermanence in the śrāvaka's own case and chiefly through the gate of emptiness in the bodhisattva's.

Chinese Source

答：雖非真實，亦是顛倒，然說苦、集二諦所攝。所以者何？彼雖皆是邪性所攝，然即此邪性相是真是實，皆不顛倒，是苦性故、是苦因故。復次，由二相故顯無常義。一、依大乘道理相，二、依聲聞乘道理相。謂非有義及其相滅壞義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: though not genuinely real and indeed distorted, they are still gathered under the truths of suffering and origin, since this very distorted nature is itself real and undistorted, being the nature of suffering and a cause of suffering. Impermanence is shown by two senses: the great vehicle's sense of simple non-existence, and the lesser vehicle's sense of destruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the resolution offered here, that a view's distorted content and its real causal power are two entirely separate questions, as this treatise's own elegant way of having it both ways without contradiction, wrong view remains genuinely false as a claim about reality while still counting as genuinely real in its capacity to produce suffering. He highlights the pairing of a great-vehicle and lesser-vehicle sense running through every one of these four aspects as a structure worth watching closely across the sections that follow, since it consistently reveals the great vehicle's own teaching as the more comprehensive of the two, addressing both a dharma's illusory imagined nature and its real, conditioned arising together rather than only the latter.

51. Section: Suffering and Emptiness, Each in Two Registers

Suffering is likewise shown through two senses. In the great vehicle's own sense, it means the coarse residue left by habitual tendencies toward the imagined nature, since such deeply conditioned habituation continues to generate attachment to both a real self and a real dharma-nature, leaving a being unable to move freely and at ease among the very conditioned processes that make up experience. And in the lesser vehicle's own sense, it means the eight familiar forms of suffering, birth, aging, sickness, death, parting from what is loved, meeting what is hated, failing to obtain what is sought, and the very flourishing of the five aggregates themselves, all arising from the three kinds of feeling, painful, pleasant, and neutral, as these unfold across a being's experience. Emptiness, too, is shown through two senses: the lesser vehicle's own sense, the emptiness of any real self within a person, and the great vehicle's own further sense, the emptiness of any real self-nature within language's own conventionally designated dharmas. Both traditions recognize personal emptiness, but only the great vehicle presses this same insight through to the emptiness of dharmas themselves, refusing to let go of self-grasping while still leaving dharma-grasping quietly intact.

Chinese Source

由二種相顯示苦義。謂非有執習氣麤重義，及三受所隨生等八種苦相轉義。由二種相顯示空義。謂補特伽羅自性遠離相義，及諸法自性遠離相義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Suffering is shown by two senses: the coarse residue of habitual tendencies toward the imagined nature, and the eight forms of suffering arising from the three kinds of feeling. Emptiness is shown by two senses: the emptiness of a real self within a person, and the emptiness of a real self-nature within dharmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the great vehicle's own sense of suffering, a coarse residue of habitual tendency rather than any single painful experience, as pointing to something subtler and more pervasive than the eight familiar sufferings already well known from the lesser vehicle's own teaching, a background unfreedom present even amid ordinary comfort rather than only amid obvious pain. He highlights the emptiness of dharmas as the great vehicle's own genuinely distinguishing contribution here, since a practitioner who has thoroughly uprooted self-grasping alone, without also addressing dharma-grasping, still remains bound to a subtler form of the very same underlying confusion.

52. Section: Selflessness, and the Beginningless Realm That Grounds Every Dharma

Selflessness, the fourth and final aspect of the truth of suffering, is shown in just the same twofold way: the lesser vehicle's own emphasis falling upon a person's own selflessness, free of any real, constant, autonomous self, and the great vehicle's own further emphasis falling upon every dharma's own selflessness as well, since language's own dharmas were never in fact real to begin with. A further pair of teachings is then introduced: the teaching of impermanence and the teaching of the beginningless. The teaching of impermanence belongs to the lesser vehicle's own approach, addressing arising and perishing moment by moment. The teaching of the beginningless belongs to the great vehicle's own approach instead, explaining how every dharma arises through the ālaya-consciousness as its underlying cause, as one verse itself declares: the realm existing since beginningless time serves as the equal basis of every dharma; by this, all destinies exist, and the attainment of nirvana too. Rather than positing some absolute first beginning for conditioned existence, a move that would fall either into imagining a fixed, independent self-nature or into denying causality altogether, the great vehicle's own teaching holds that every dharma's seed has always already been stored within the ālaya-consciousness since beginningless time, this same consciousness serving as the shared support underlying both continued wandering through the three realms and the eventual attainment of nirvana alike.

Ru-Yi Translation

Selflessness is shown by two senses: a person's own selflessness, and every dharma's own selflessness. A further two teachings follow: the teaching of impermanence, and the teaching of the beginningless. The realm existing since beginningless time serves as the equal basis of every dharma; by this, all destinies exist, and the attainment of nirvana too.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the beginningless-realm verse's own careful refusal to posit any absolute first moment for conditioned existence, avoiding equally the trap of a fixed, self-existing origin and the trap of denying causation altogether, as one of Yogācāra thought's own most distinctive philosophical moves, replacing the question of when things began with the very different question of what continuously supports their ongoing arising. He highlights the striking claim that this same ālaya-consciousness serves as the shared basis for both samsaric wandering and nirvana's own attainment as this treatise's own reminder that liberation is never a matter of escaping to some entirely different foundation, but of transforming how the very same underlying basis already at work is finally put to use.

53. Section: Why Impermanence Itself Already Means Suffering

A further pair of penetrations is then introduced, closing this section's account of the two teachings: penetration into the conditioned realm, belonging to the lesser vehicle's own approach, realizing that no self can be found either within the five aggregates or apart from them, and penetration into the unconditioned realm, belonging to the great vehicle's own further approach, directly realizing suchness itself, the twin emptiness of both self and dharmas together. A further teaching then explains, by five distinct causes, exactly why impermanence itself already amounts to suffering. The first cause is inclusion, since every impermanent conditioned process is already bound up with the coarse residue of afflictive seeds. The second is its very nature, since arising, aging, sickness, death, and every other familiar form of suffering already belong to the intrinsic nature of whatever is impermanent. The third is constant accompaniment, since the three kinds of suffering, the suffering of pain itself, the suffering of change, and the pervasive suffering built into conditioned existence as such, continuously follow every impermanent process wherever it goes. The fourth is causal power, since impermanence itself is precisely what causes affliction to keep growing, craving increasing during pleasant feeling, aversion increasing during painful feeling, and delusion increasing during neutral feeling. And the fifth is serving as the very basis for a fundamental distortion, since it is precisely the impermanent five aggregates that an unawakened being mistakenly clings to as permanent, blissful, and pure, a clinging inevitably met with disappointment precisely because impermanence was the truth all along.

Ru-Yi Translation

A further two penetrations follow: into the conditioned realm, and into the unconditioned realm. By five causes, impermanence itself already means suffering: inclusion within afflictive seeds, its own suffering-laden nature, constant accompaniment by the three kinds of suffering, its causal power in growing affliction, and its serving as the basis for the distorted clinging to permanence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fivefold explanation as this treatise's own thorough answer to a question every beginning practitioner eventually asks, namely why impermanence itself should count as suffering rather than as simple, neutral fact, the five causes together showing that impermanence is never merely a passive description but an active, causal force generating both affliction's growth and the very clinging that then meets impermanence with resistance and pain. He highlights the fifth cause, serving as the basis for the mistaken clinging to permanence, as this list's own most psychologically astute point, locating suffering not in impermanence as such but in the gap between impermanence's own reality and an unawakened mind's stubborn insistence on something else entirely.

54. Section: Why Suffering Itself Already Means Selflessness

A further teaching then explains, by five further causes, why suffering itself already amounts to selflessness. The first cause is that no self whatsoever can be found apart from these very suffering-laden conditioned processes themselves. The second is that these same suffering-laden processes arise only in dependence on manifold conditions, and whatever depends on conditions in this way can never be the constant, unitary, autonomous self a real self would have to be. The third is that these processes carry no genuine controlling function at all, the eye, for instance, never truly seeing form by its own independent power, as one verse itself puts it, exercising no real autonomous action of any kind before simply ceasing again on its own once conditions change. The fourth is that these processes arise moment after moment in constant, uninterrupted succession, each moment genuinely new rather than any single, continuous thing enduring unchanged, so that no persisting self could ever be located within their ceaseless flow. And the fifth is that these processes only ever arise through mutual dependence, consciousness conditioning name-and-form and name-and-form in turn conditioning consciousness, each arising only in reliance upon the other rather than through any single, independent, self-sufficient cause. Through this same reasoning, the chain running from impermanence to suffering to selflessness is now complete: because conditioned existence is impermanent, it is suffering, and because it is suffering, it is without any real self at all.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

By five causes, suffering itself already means selflessness: no self is found apart from these suffering processes; they depend on manifold conditions; they carry no genuine controlling function; they arise in constant momentary succession; and they arise only through mutual dependence, one upon another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third cause's own vivid illustration, that the eye never truly sees by any independent power of its own but simply arises and ceases according to conditions, as an unusually direct challenge to the ordinary, unexamined sense of agency running through everyday experience, suggesting that even so basic an act as seeing already lacks the independent controlling self it seems, on the surface, to require. He highlights the completed chain from impermanence through suffering to selflessness as this whole section's own hard-won destination, three apparently separate teachings now shown to follow from one another with genuine logical necessity rather than standing as three merely juxtaposed observations.

55. Section: Even Pleasant Feeling Is Gathered under Suffering

A further question then probes exactly what the truth of suffering actually subsumes: if something is gathered under the truth of suffering, must it always be undesirable, unpleasant, painful, harmful, and injurious? And conversely, if something is undesirable, unpleasant, painful, harmful, and injurious, must it always be gathered under the truth of suffering? The second question is answered directly: whatever is undesirable, unpleasant, painful, harmful, and injurious should indeed always be known as gathered under the truth of suffering. But the first question requires a further distinction: some things are gathered under the truth of suffering without being undesirable or unpleasant at all, namely every conditioned process belonging to the suffering of change or the pervasive suffering built into conditioned existence as such, setting aside only the suffering of pain itself. Pleasant feeling, after all, does not feel painful in the moment it is experienced, yet because it is impermanent and bound eventually to change and pass away, it still counts as the suffering of change; and neutral feeling likewise does not feel painful, yet because the aggregates themselves flow on ceaselessly, moment after moment, without any real rest, it still counts as the pervasive suffering inherent in conditioned existence itself. The traditional eight forms of suffering are then sorted among these same three kinds: the first five, birth, aging, sickness, death, and encountering what is hated, reveal the suffering of pain; the middle two, parting from what is loved and failing to obtain what is sought, reveal the suffering of change, the former arising when something already possessed and cherished is lost, the latter when something still merely hoped for slips away instead; and the last, the very flourishing of the five aggregates of clinging themselves, reveals the pervasive suffering running through conditioned existence as a whole.

Chinese Source

答：若不可愛、不可樂，是苦、是損惱、是違害，當知皆是苦諦所攝。或有是苦諦攝，非不可愛，乃至廣說。謂除苦苦所攝諸行，所餘壞苦、行苦所攝諸行。前五顯苦苦性。中二顯壞苦性⋯後一總略五取蘊苦，顯行苦性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever is undesirable and painful is indeed gathered under the truth of suffering. But some things are gathered under it without being undesirable at all, namely conditioned processes belonging to the suffering of change or pervasive suffering. Among the eight sufferings, the first five reveal the suffering of pain, the middle two the suffering of change, and the last, the flourishing of the five aggregates, pervasive suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own careful demonstration, that even genuinely pleasant feeling still counts as suffering in the deeper sense of change and impermanence, as this treatise's own most counterintuitive yet philosophically essential point about the truth of suffering, since it prevents a practitioner from mistakenly assuming the path only concerns itself with obvious pain while leaving ordinary pleasure untouched. He highlights the pervasive suffering revealed by the mere flourishing of the five aggregates as the subtlest and most far-reaching of the three, present in every single moment of conditioned experience regardless of whether that moment happens to feel pleasant, painful, or neutral.

56. Section: The One Impermanent Thing That Is Never Suffering

A further question then distinguishes impermanence from suffering more precisely: is whatever is impermanent thereby suffering, and is whatever is suffering thereby impermanent? Every genuine suffering, it is answered, is indeed impermanent, yet there exists one thing that, while genuinely impermanent, is never suffering at all: the truth of the path itself. Why is this so? The path is not gathered under the suffering of pain, since it is not itself a form of painful feeling. Nor does the path undergo the suffering of change, since it never functions as an object of craving whose eventual loss could bring about such suffering, unlike an ordinary pleasure a being might cling to and then lose. And the path is not gathered under pervasive suffering either, since it actually liberates a practitioner from the very coarse residue of every kind of afflictive seed and directly opposes the continued unfolding of birth and death itself, functioning as precisely the opposite of what pervasive suffering names. The path, though momentary and impermanent exactly like every other conditioned process, including even a noble one's own non-conceptual wisdom arising and perishing moment by moment, is thus the sole impermanent thing that never counts as suffering at all, standing entirely apart from the truth of suffering rather than belonging to it.

Chinese Source

答：諸苦皆無常。有無常非苦，謂道諦。所以者何？道非苦受等所攝，故非苦苦。道非變壞，何有變時當生壞苦。道能解脫一切雜染品麤重故，能違一切生相續故，是故亦非行苦所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every suffering is impermanent. But there is one impermanent thing that is never suffering: the truth of the path. Why so? The path is not painful feeling, so it is not the suffering of pain. The path is never an object of craving whose loss brings suffering, so it is not the suffering of change. And the path liberates from affliction's own coarse seeds and opposes continued rebirth, so it is not pervasive suffering either.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the path's own unique status here, genuinely impermanent yet never suffering, as this treatise's own careful safeguard against a tempting but mistaken shortcut, the assumption that impermanence and suffering are simply identical rather than related in the more precise way this whole section has just taken such care to establish. He highlights the third reason especially, that the path actively opposes the very continuity of birth and death rather than merely passing through it like every other conditioned process, as this passage's own clearest statement of what makes the path genuinely different in kind from everything it is meant to lead a practitioner beyond.

57. Section: Nirvana Alone Is Empty Yet Neither Impermanent Nor Suffering

A further question then asks whether whatever is impermanent and suffering is thereby also empty and selfless, and whether whatever is empty and selfless is thereby also impermanent and suffering. Everything impermanent and suffering, it is answered, is indeed empty and selfless, yet some things are empty and selfless without being either impermanent or suffering at all. Regarding the four truths specifically: the truth of suffering and the truth of origin both carry all four marks together, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness; the truth of the path carries three of these four, impermanence, emptiness, and selflessness, but never suffering, exactly as already explained; and the truth of cessation carries only two, being neither impermanent nor suffering at all, since nirvana, as an unconditioned reality free of arising and perishing altogether, remains solely and simply empty and selfless. A further question then asks whether whatever is impermanent and suffering is always suffering specifically because it is impermanent. Suffering arises, it is answered, for two distinct reasons. Some suffering arises precisely because of impermanence, as when the suffering of change occurs simply because a pleasant condition is impermanent and bound to pass away. And some suffering arises instead from its own intrinsic nature, as with the suffering of pain, inherently injurious in its very nature, and the pervasive suffering of conditioned existence itself, which carries both of the other two kinds mixed within it, comparable to a body already covered by a festering, burning sore, where a pleasant sensation is like ice briefly touching that same sore, offering only momentary relief without healing it, a painful sensation is like hot ash making the sore still worse, and a neutral sensation is simply the moment when the sore's own underlying, ever-present suffering becomes most plainly apparent, no longer masked by either hot or cold contact at all.

Chinese Source

答：諸無常、苦，皆空、無我；有空、無我，非無常、苦。謂於此中，苦、集諦具四種；道諦有三；滅諦有二，非無常、非苦。答：或無常故，或自性故。謂所有行壞苦故苦，彼無常故是苦。若苦苦故苦、行苦故苦，彼自性故是苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Everything impermanent and suffering is empty and selfless, but not everything empty and selfless is impermanent and suffering. The truths of suffering and origin carry all four marks; the path carries three, not suffering; cessation carries only two, neither impermanent nor suffering. Suffering arises either because of impermanence, as with the suffering of change, or from its own intrinsic nature, as with the suffering of pain and pervasive suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats nirvana's own unique status here, empty and selfless yet neither impermanent nor suffering at all, as this treatise's own precise way of distinguishing cessation from every other reality discussed throughout this whole section, genuinely unconditioned rather than merely another impermanent process like the path itself remains. He highlights the festering-sore image as an unusually visceral way of making pervasive suffering vividly felt rather than merely conceptually understood, pleasant and painful sensation both shown as simply different ways of touching the same underlying wound, with neutral sensation finally revealing that wound's own suffering nature stripped of any temporary disguise.

58. Section: An Arhat's Own Remaining Dharmas Are Suffering but Not Its Cause

A final question then closes this section's examination of the suffering-truth: is whatever belongs to the truth of suffering also thereby belonging to the truth of origin, and does the reverse hold true as well? Whatever belongs to the truth of origin, it is answered, does indeed always belong to the truth of suffering as well, since origin, essentially craving itself together with the karma it produces, invariably draws forth suffering as its result; but some things belong to the truth of suffering without belonging to the truth of origin at all. Three such cases are given. The first is whatever wholesome or neutral worldly dharma remains within an arhat's own purified mindstream, since an arhat has already permanently severed every affliction of the three realms and no longer creates any karma capable of drawing forth a future retributive result, such dharmas therefore counting as suffering without themselves being any further cause of it. The second is whatever neutral dharma, belonging to the very ripened result of past karma, is found within an ordinary, unawakened being's own body, since a ripened result is itself simply the fruit of earlier karma rather than a fresh cause capable of drawing forth any further result of its own. And the third is whatever neutral bodily, verbal, or mental conduct presently accompanies pleasant, painful, or neutral feeling in the course of ordinary activity, walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, since such neutral conduct likewise draws forth no future retributive result at all. A further topic then opens: what belongs to the truth of origin, beginning with an explanation of conduct that generates genuine revulsion.

Chinese Source

答：諸是集諦者，皆是苦諦；或是苦諦，而非集諦。謂一切阿羅漢清淨相續中，所有若善、若無記一切世間法，一切異生身中所有果異熟攝無記諸法，一切現在士用所生若苦、若樂、若不苦不樂俱行無記諸行，當知唯是苦諦所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever belongs to the truth of origin always belongs to the truth of suffering, but some things belong to suffering without belonging to origin: an arhat's own remaining wholesome or neutral worldly dharmas, an ordinary being's own neutral ripened-result dharmas, and present neutral conduct accompanying feeling — all these should be known as belonging to suffering alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats an arhat's own remaining wholesome dharmas, genuinely suffering in the sense of still being conditioned and impermanent yet no longer any cause of further suffering at all, as this section's own clearest illustration of what liberation actually changes, not the mere presence of conditioned experience, which continues until the body itself finally ends, but its causal power to generate anything further. He highlights the parallel treatment of a ripened result and present neutral conduct as together reinforcing the same basic principle, that a genuine cause of continued suffering requires more than mere conditioned existence, requiring specifically the karma-generating power that only affliction-driven action actually carries.

59. Section: Why Craving Alone Names the Truth of Origin

A question is then raised: if a practitioner genuinely feels revulsion toward continued existence, actively turning away from it, and thereby draws forth, through hearing, reflection, and cultivation, the very worldly wholesome dharmas that lead toward the transcendent path, by what reasoning should such wholesome dharmas still be gathered under the truth of origin at all? Although such a practitioner's own underlying wish is indeed to turn away from continued existence, it is answered, the actual worldly, outflow-laden bodily, verbal, and mental conduct produced along the way still fails to sever affliction's own seeds, and so still accords with and supports continued existence regardless of its own well-intentioned motivation, and for this reason remains gathered under the truth of origin nonetheless. A further question then asks why, if every karma and affliction capable of drawing forth continued existence should equally count as origin, the World-Honored One chose to designate craving alone as this truth's own defining name. Craving is answered to hold four distinctive powers that set it apart: it gives rise to grasping, the very next link that actually secures a future rebirth; it sets karma itself into motion; it pervades every matter whatsoever, both what lies within the body and what lies in the surrounding world; and the moment it arises, it universally gives rise to fifteen kinds of conduct entirely without benefit for the path. Among every cause capable of drawing forth continued existence, craving alone proves supreme in exactly this fourfold way, and it is for this reason that the Buddha named craving, and craving alone, as the truth of origin.

Chinese Source

答：雖彼自性厭背後有，然能隨順後有身語意妙行，是故亦是集諦所攝。答：此愛能起取故、能發業故、遍諸事故，此愛生時，普能發起十五種無義利故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though such conduct's own underlying wish is to turn away from continued existence, it still accords with and supports it, and so remains gathered under origin. Craving alone is named because it gives rise to grasping, sets karma in motion, pervades every matter, and gives rise to fifteen kinds of unbeneficial conduct the moment it arises.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first answer here, that even sincerely revulsion-motivated worldly practice still counts as origin so long as affliction's own seeds remain unsevered, as a genuinely sobering reminder that good intention alone never substitutes for the actual work of uprooting affliction at its root. He highlights craving's fourfold supremacy among every possible cause of continued existence as this treatise's own clear justification for singling out one link within the twelvefold chain of dependent origination as deserving its own dedicated name, craving proving not merely one cause among equals but the pivotal one on which grasping, karma, and every further unbeneficial consequence directly depend.

60. Section: Three Faces of Craving, and the One Craving That Seeks Liberation Itself

Craving's own pervasiveness across every matter is then explained through three distinct kinds. The first is craving for continued existence itself, longing for a future rebirth to occur. The second is craving conjoined with delight, clinging to whatever pleasing object has already been obtained or is presently being enjoyed, wishing it to remain, unseparated, for as long as possible. And the third is craving for this or that further delight, reaching toward some desired object not yet obtained, wishing to unite with it, to never be parted from it once gained, or simply to increase it further still, craving for continued existence pervading what lies within the body while the latter two pervade whatever lies in the surrounding world, together spanning every matter whatsoever. A further question then asks whether craving in general always amounts to one of these same three specific kinds. The answer proceeds through four distinct cases. In the first case, something counts as craving without being any of the three, namely the wholly wholesome longing to actually realize the supreme liberation of nirvana itself, a craving, if it may even be called that, oriented not toward continued existence at all but toward its very opposite. The second case then begins to unfold: something may count as craving for continued existence, delight in what is already possessed, or delight in what is not yet possessed, without actually being craving itself, namely whatever feeling, perception, volition, or ignorance arises conjoined with these same three cravings and with other afflictions besides, sharing in their unfolding without constituting the craving itself.

Chinese Source

遍諸事者，謂如此愛名順後有愛，若喜貪俱行愛、若彼彼喜樂愛名遍諸事，當知亦爾。問：若是愛者，亦是順後有、喜貪俱行、彼彼喜樂耶？答：應作四句。或有是愛，非順後有、非喜貪俱行、非彼彼喜樂。謂於上解脫希求欲證。

Ru-Yi Translation

Craving pervades every matter through three kinds: craving for continued existence, craving conjoined with delight in what is possessed, and craving for delight in what is not yet possessed. A further question asks whether craving always amounts to one of these three. The answer proceeds through four cases, the first being the wholesome longing to realize liberation itself, which is craving yet none of the three.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the identification of a genuinely wholesome longing for liberation as itself a kind of craving, sharing the same underlying word yet oriented in the very opposite direction from the other three, as a fitting reminder that this treatise consistently refuses to let terminology alone settle a question, the same word capable of naming both the very chain that binds a being to continued existence and the aspiration capable of breaking it. He notes that this fourfold analysis remains unfinished at this point, only its first case fully worked out so far, and treats this simply as where the read has currently reached rather than any incompleteness in the argument itself.

61. Section: Sorrow-Laden Longing for a Better Rebirth, Craving Yet Not Delight

The fourfold analysis relating craving in general to its three specific kinds is now completed. The third case is simply these same three cravings themselves, for continued existence, for delight in what is possessed, and for delight in what is not yet possessed. And the fourth case gathers whatever remains once these three prior cases have been set aside. A further comparison then follows, testing whether craving for continued existence and delight-craving for what is already possessed always coincide. Every instance of delight-craving for what is already possessed, it is answered, does indeed count as craving for continued existence as well, yet some craving for continued existence is not delight-craving for what is already possessed at all. Such craving includes a defiled longing conjoined with sorrow, as when someone, dissatisfied with their present life, anxiously hopes for a better one to come without any confidence that this hope will actually be fulfilled; it also includes the craving to be separated from some already-possessed but genuinely undesirable object, and the craving never to be united at all with some undesirable object not yet encountered. Each of these three remains oriented toward continued existence itself, yet none involves the delight in an already-pleasing object that defines the second, narrower kind of craving.

Chinese Source

即此三愛，是第三句。除上爾所相，是第四句。答：諸喜貪俱行愛，亦是順後有愛；或有順後有愛，非喜貪俱行愛。謂染汙憂俱行愛，或別離愛，或不和合愛。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third case is these same three cravings themselves; the fourth gathers what remains. Every delight-craving for what is already possessed also counts as craving for continued existence, but some craving for continued existence, including sorrow-laden longing, craving to be separated from what is undesirable, and craving never to encounter it, is not delight-craving at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sorrow-laden longing for a better rebirth as this analysis's own quietly poignant addition to the usual picture of craving as simple, confident desire, showing that craving for continued existence can just as easily wear the face of anxious hope tinged with real doubt as the face of eager anticipation. He highlights the inclusion of craving to avoid an undesirable object as a reminder that aversion and craving, though often treated as opposites, actually share the same underlying root, wanting the world to be other than it presently is.

62. Section: Already Possessed, Not Yet Possessed, or Certain to Be Possessed

A further comparison then tests craving for continued existence against craving for delight in what is not yet possessed. Every such delight-craving for what is not yet possessed, it is answered, does indeed count as craving for continued existence as well, yet some craving for continued existence is not this kind of delight-craving at all, namely the craving that takes the very object of a future rebirth itself, rather than any presently pleasing object, as its focus, together with the delight-craving already described for objects already possessed or currently being enjoyed. A final comparison then opens between delight-craving for what is already possessed and delight-craving for what is not yet possessed, proceeding through four distinct cases. The first case is whatever craving concerns an object already obtained and pleasing, or presently being enjoyed, belonging to delight-craving for what is possessed alone. The second case is whatever craving concerns a pleasing object still awaited in the future, not yet certain to be obtained, belonging instead to delight-craving for what is not yet possessed alone. And the third case is whatever craving concerns an object that, though not yet actually obtained, is nonetheless already certain to be obtained, belonging to both kinds of delight-craving together.

Chinese Source

答：若彼彼喜樂愛，亦是順後有愛；或有順後有愛，非彼彼喜樂愛。謂緣後有境愛，及喜貪俱行愛。謂於已得可愛境界，或於正受用中所有愛，是初句。即於可愛未來當得未決定中所有愛，是第二句。即於此當得決定中所有愛，是第三句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every delight-craving for what is not yet possessed also counts as craving for continued existence, but craving for the future rebirth-object itself and delight-craving for what is already possessed do not. Comparing the two delight-cravings: craving for what is already possessed alone is the first case; craving for what is not yet certainly obtained alone is the second; craving for what is not yet obtained but already certain is the third, belonging to both.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third case here, an object not yet obtained but already certain to be, as this analysis's own subtle but important middle ground between simple present enjoyment and mere uncertain hope, the psychological experience of craving toward a sure future outcome resembling, in this treatise's own careful account, the craving for something already in hand more closely than the craving for something merely wished for. He notes that a fourth and final case, gathering whatever remains once these first three are set aside, still remains to be read, this fourfold comparison not yet brought to its own close.

63. Section: Bound to Desire, Bound to Form, or Bound to Neither at All

The fourfold comparison between delight-craving for what is possessed and delight-craving for what is not yet possessed is now completed: a craving toward an object still awaited yet already certain to be obtained counts as both kinds together, while whatever remains once every prior case has been set aside belongs to neither kind at all. A further set of comparisons then opens, this time examining craving by way of feeling itself, painful, pleasant, and neutral. The first such comparison asks whether whoever possesses feelings bound to the desire realm also possesses feelings bound to the form realm, and whether the reverse holds true as well, proceeding through four cases. The first case is possessing feelings bound to the desire realm alone, true of a being born into the desire realm who has not yet attained the form-realm concentration capable of counteracting it. The second is possessing feelings bound to the form realm alone, true of a being actually born into the form realm itself. The third is possessing both together, true of a being born into the desire realm who has nonetheless already attained that same counteracting concentration through freedom from desire. And the fourth is possessing neither at all, true both of a being born into the formless realm and of an arhat, born into any of the three realms, who has already accomplished the task at hand and now abides in the transcendent path or in the very attainment of cessation itself. This same fourfold pattern, the text notes, should be applied in just the same way to every other possible pairing, desire-realm-bound feelings against formless-realm-bound feelings, against the unconditioned path itself free of all binding, and so on through every remaining combination.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Four cases: possessing desire-realm feelings alone, born there without the counteracting concentration; possessing form-realm feelings alone, born there directly; possessing both, born in the desire realm but already free of it; and possessing neither, born formless, or an arhat abiding in the transcendent path or the attainment of cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the inclusion of an arhat dwelling in the attainment of cessation alongside a formless-realm being as this fourfold pattern's own most striking case, two beings arriving at the very same classification, possessing neither realm-bound feeling, by entirely different roads, one through rebirth's own natural limitation and the other through the deliberate, hard-won stilling of the six consciousnesses altogether. He highlights the instruction to extend this same fourfold method across every remaining pairing as a fitting demonstration of how thoroughly this treatise prefers to work out a genuinely complete map of possibilities rather than leaving any single comparison to stand as a merely isolated example.

64. Section: Refined Pleasures without Attachment Are Not the Faulted Desires

A further comparison then opens, examining craving by way of sensory pleasure itself: are refined sensory pleasures always the very desires bound up with fault and harm the Buddha so often warned against, likened elsewhere to dry bones, a morsel of soft meat, a flaming torch of grass, a great pit of fire, a venomous serpent, a mere dream, borrowed finery, or fruit hanging from a branch's furthest tip, desires the Buddha named as offering scant satisfaction yet endless craving, condemned by every buddha and every genuine practitioner, only ever deepening the very fetters that bind, and the root from which no unwholesome deed fails to grow? And does the reverse hold true as well? The answer proceeds through four cases. The first case is a refined sensory pleasure, whether of sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch, that is not among the desires bound up with fault and harm at all, namely whenever such pleasant objects are encountered without any defiled attachment arising, requiring no special effort to resist them, allowing a practitioner to dwell at ease and free of wrongdoing while still upholding the pure conduct proper to the path, exactly as one already free of craving for such objects experiences them without any of the harm that craving alone actually produces.

Chinese Source

問：諸妙欲，亦是過患過失相應欲耶？設是過患過失相應欲，是妙欲耶？答：應作四句。或有妙欲，非過患過失相應欲。謂若色聲香味觸，能不染汙現行，若於彼不作功力，無罪安樂住，能攝受梵行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Are refined sensory pleasures always the desires bound up with fault and harm? The answer proceeds through four cases. The first is a refined pleasure that is not among the faulted desires at all, namely whenever such an object is encountered without defiled attachment arising, allowing ease and pure conduct without any special effort to resist it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own opening case, a genuinely pleasant sensory object encountered without any craving arising at all, as this treatise's own important safeguard against a common misreading of its many vivid warnings about desire, since the danger these images describe was always craving itself rather than sensory experience as such. He highlights the eightfold catalogue of desire's own faults, from offering scant satisfaction to serving as the ground of every unwholesome deed, as among this treatise's most quotable and immediately graspable teachings, its images, dry bones, a flaming torch, a venomous serpent, chosen precisely for how vividly they linger in memory long after the more abstract doctrinal points have faded.

65. Section: True Desire Is the Distorted Craving, Not the World Itself

The fourfold analysis of refined pleasure against faulted desire is now completed. The second case is a desire bound up with fault and harm that is not a refined pleasure at all, namely whatever sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch is uniformly unpleasant, joyless, and displeasing, together with the very affliction of craving itself. The third case is a pleasure that is both refined and faulted together, namely whenever a genuinely fine sensory object appears and defiled attachment inevitably arises in response, uncontrolled delight and clinging taking hold the moment such an object is encountered. And the fourth case is whatever belongs to neither category, namely the neutral afflictions and objects bound to the form and formless realms, realms already free of the desire realm's own coarser cravings. Drawing on both refined pleasure and faulted desire together, the World-Honored One accordingly declared that it is distorted, discriminating craving itself, not any sensory object as such, that truly constitutes desire for an ordinary being, exactly as one verse elsewhere puts it, that the world's own fine appearances are never the true desire, true desire being only a person's own discriminating craving, since such fine appearances remain exactly as they always were in the world while the wise have already removed all desire from among them; and as another verse says, addressing desire directly, I have always known your root lies in thought and imagination alone, and were I never to imagine you at all, you yourself would cease to exist.

A pleasure both refined and faulted is a fine object met with uncontrolled attachment. What is neither belongs to the form and formless realms. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing citation, that the world's fine appearances remain exactly as they always were while only the wise have removed desire from among them, as this whole analysis's own carefully earned conclusion, the object of desire was never really the problem at all, only the mind's own distorted, imagining relationship to it. He highlights the companion verse addressing desire directly, as though craving itself were a person capable of being spoken to, as an unusually intimate and memorable way of driving home the same point, that desire's entire existence depends on being thought into being in the first place.

66. Section: Craving in Two, Three, and by the Views It Grounds

This same craving is then briefly gathered into further categories. In its simplest division, it comprises two kinds: craving for continued existence itself, and craving for actual enjoyment, this second kind further dividing according to whether the object enjoyed has already been obtained or is merely hoped for still. By realm, craving instead divides into three kinds. Desire-realm craving belongs to a being born in the desire realm who longs for continued existence there, delighting in whatever pleasing object has already been obtained and hoping for whatever pleasing object has not yet been obtained. Form-realm craving belongs to a being, born in the desire realm or the form realm, who has already grown free of the desire realm's own craving and now longs instead for continued existence in the form realm, delighting in whatever meditative attainment has already been reached and hoping for whatever still more excellent attainment remains ahead. And formless-realm craving unfolds in just the same way, belonging to a being already free of both the desire and form realms' craving, oriented instead toward the formless realm's own attainments. This same craving for continued existence, depending on which underlying view it rests upon, further grounds two opposing convictions: resting on the view of eternal continuation, it becomes the craving for existence itself; resting on the view of utter annihilation at death, it becomes instead the craving for non-existence. And because this craving, in all its forms, pervades the desire, form, and formless realms alike, and pervades every matter both within the body and in the surrounding world, it is for this very reason called the craving that pervades all matters.

Chinese Source

復次，此愛略有二種。初是有愛，後是受用愛。又即此愛界差別故，復有三種。謂欲愛、色愛、無色愛。即此後有愛，常見、斷見為依止故，建立有愛及無有愛。是故此愛名遍諸事。

Ru-Yi Translation

This craving is briefly two kinds: craving for existence, and craving for enjoyment. By realm it is three: desire-craving, form-craving, and formless-craving. This same craving for continued existence, resting on the view of eternal continuation or of utter annihilation, grounds the craving for existence and the craving for non-existence in turn. It is therefore called the craving that pervades all matters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the grounding of both eternalism and annihilationism in this same underlying craving as this section's own sharp diagnosis of what actually drives two views ordinarily treated as simple opposites, showing that wanting existence to continue forever and wanting it to end absolutely both spring from the identical refusal to accept conditioned existence exactly as it actually is. He highlights the threefold realm-based division as a natural and expected extension of the desire-realm pattern already explained at length, the same underlying structure of existence-craving and enjoyment-craving simply repeating itself at each successively subtler level of rebirth.

67. Section: Fifteen Kinds of Harm, Ending Where the Lecture Itself Stops

Why, finally, does this craving, the moment it arises, universally give rise to fifteen kinds of conduct entirely without benefit? Briefly, these fifteen are as follows. It hardens the underlying latent tendencies toward affliction. Through its own entanglement, it defiles every mind and mental factor it touches. It causes the mind to keep turning, again and again, toward a distorted view of its object as permanent, pleasant, possessing a real self, and pure. It gives rise to grasping and to whatever further affliction grasping itself carries along with it. It perpetuates its own kind, one craving simply continuing to breed further craving of the very same sort. It brings about unwholesome states not yet arisen. It causes unwholesome states already arisen to grow further still. It obstructs wholesome states not yet arisen from ever arising at all. It obstructs wholesome states already arisen from remaining stable, from persisting without being forgotten, and from growing broader and stronger still. It leads to unwholesome conduct that accumulates the very suffering of the miserable destinies. Through its own longing for continued existence, it accumulates the suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death. It causes a being to actually fear nirvana itself, clinging so tightly to continued existence that liberation itself comes to seem a genuine threat. It causes a being to love saṃsāra instead, wrongly imagining continued existence to possess some real merit or advantage. Just as with existence itself, so too with whatever object is desired, the very same wrongful clinging to imagined merit taking hold there as well. And it causes a being to contemplate harming both self and other alike, exactly as the discourses explain at length, extending all the way to the many forms of sorrow and distress that craving itself, in the end, brings the mind to bear. With this the truths of suffering and of origin have now been discussed as far as this transcript itself extends, the lecture's own record coming to rest at precisely this point.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far the truths of suffering and origin have been discussed, and here this transcript itself comes to rest.

He notes that the lecture transcript underlying this fascicle's own translation ends at precisely this point, midway through the four noble truths' own fuller treatment, well before the truths of cessation and the path have been addressed and long before the śrāvaka canon's remaining nine topics could even be reached, and treats this simply as where Master Cháng Bǎi's own transmitted teaching happens to stop for now rather than any sign of an argument left genuinely incomplete, the patient, methodical work already accomplished across this fascicle, from the sixteen kinds of cultivation through the Śrāvaka Ground's own lineage, varieties, and four canonical topics, standing as a substantial and coherent teaching in its own right.
