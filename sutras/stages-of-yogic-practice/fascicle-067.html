Fascicle 67 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十七 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation

Sixteen Kinds of Cultivation, from the Śrāvaka's Own Path to the Bodhisattva's

Maitreya completes the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation, briefly naming and explaining sixteen kinds of cultivation practice — from the śrāvaka's and bodhisattva's own distinct mental engagement through a five-stage arc of signless cultivation mapped onto the bodhisattva grounds — before opening the vast decisive discernment of the Śrāvaka Ground itself with its first difficult question: whether some beings, however boundless the varieties of elemental nature among sentient beings, are truly and permanently without the lineage for liberation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 67 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Sixteen Kinds of Cultivation, Briefly Named

Maitreya now closes the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection and opens the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation. Since the Basic Section's own treatment of this ground already explained, in full and careful detail, the four supporting bases and seven branches by which a practitioner actually cultivates the path, the place and cause upon which cultivation rests, the yogic method itself, and the fruit of cultivation attained, together with the seven branches of a perfected basis, a perfected hearing of the true teaching, taking nirvana as one's foremost aim, the ripening of a liberating wisdom capable of ripening others, cultivating the actual antidote, and complete purification both worldly and transcendent, this decisive discernment need only set forth sixteen essential kinds of cultivation practice for a practitioner's own further reflection and application: mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle, mental engagement linked to the great vehicle, cultivation through an image, cultivation through the very limit of a matter, cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand, cultivation as attainment, cultivation as habituation, cultivation as removal, cultivation as antidote, cultivation in part, cultivation that pervades, cultivation in motion, cultivation still requiring effort, cultivation already accomplished, cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation, and cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation. As one earlier treatise asks, why is it called cultivation at all? Because it pervades what is to be cultivated, it is called cultivation; because it is repeatedly practiced, it is called cultivation; because it thoroughly perfumes the mind, it is called cultivation; because it is learned again and again, it is called cultivation; and because it makes the mind bright and pure, it is called cultivation.

Chinese Source

如是已說思所成慧地決擇。修所成慧地決擇，我今當說。當知略有十六種修。謂聲聞乘相應作意修、大乘相應作意修、影像修、事邊際修、所作成辦修、得修、習修、除去修、對治修、少分修、遍行修、動轉修、有加行修、已成辦修、非修所成法修、修所成法修。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection has been explained. The decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation, I shall now explain. One should know there are, briefly, sixteen kinds of cultivation: mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle, mental engagement linked to the great vehicle, cultivation through an image, cultivation through the very limit of a matter, cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand, cultivation as attainment, as habituation, as removal, as antidote, in part, that pervades, in motion, still requiring effort, already accomplished, of a dharma not born of cultivation, and of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the shift from this ground's earlier, exhaustive Basic Section treatment to a bare list of sixteen terms here as a deliberate change of register, the decisive discernment assuming a practitioner already knows the fuller picture and now simply sharpening sixteen precise tools for actually carrying reflection into lived practice. He highlights the five-fold gloss on the word "cultivation" itself, pervading, repeating, perfuming, learning, and brightening, as a genuinely useful reminder that cultivation in this treatise's own sense is never a single event but always a patient, repeated, gradually transformative process.

2. Section: The Śrāvaka's Mental Engagement, Bent on Personal Liberation Alone

What is mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle? It refers to a practitioner of the śrāvaka lineage, abiding in a śrāvaka's own nature, whether not yet entered into the true nature free of ordinary birth or already entered into it, who observes only their own benefit and not the benefit of others, and who, through the gateway of mental engagement upon the conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking as object a limited dharma still bearing conceptual distinction; such a practitioner cultivates mental engagement in order to exhaust craving, by way of the aspects of revulsion, freedom from desire, and liberation. Such a one relies on the four truths as the Buddha himself conceptually established them through language, and when root wisdom directly realizes suchness, does so free of language and characteristics, inwardly observing the non-conceptually established truth of selflessness alone; upon emerging from this root non-conceptual wisdom, in subsequently taking the four truths and their conventional distinctions as object, such a practitioner has not yet penetrated the emptiness of dharmas themselves, and so retains, though without any active turning away from beings' welfare, only a small measure of engagement in saving them, since the true aim remains one's own liberation from birth and death above all else. Revulsion refers to cultivating, again and again, the antidote to the suffering of birth and death in the three realms until affliction no longer manifests at all; freedom from desire refers to a mind so thoroughly imbued with revulsion that craving no longer manifests toward any defiling object, even without deliberately calling the antidote to mind; and liberation refers to repeatedly cultivating this very suppression and severance until the underlying latent tendencies of affliction are permanently uprooted and liberation is actually attained.

Chinese Source

云何聲聞乘相應作意修？謂如有一，是聲聞，住聲聞法性；或未證入正性離生，或已證入正性離生；不觀他利益事，唯觀自利益事；由安立諦作意門，內觀真如，緣有量有分別法為境；為盡貪愛，由厭、離欲、解脫行相，修習作意。是名聲聞乘相應作意修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle? It refers to one of the śrāvaka lineage who observes only their own benefit, not the benefit of others, and who, through the gateway of the conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking a limited, conceptually distinguished dharma as object, cultivating mental engagement to exhaust craving by way of revulsion, freedom from desire, and liberation. This is called mental engagement linked to the śrāvaka vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the śrāvaka's own careful characterization here, not entirely turning away from other beings' welfare yet still leaving it as only a minor, incidental concern beside the overriding aim of personal liberation, as an unusually fair-minded piece of comparative doctrine, resisting the temptation to caricature the lesser vehicle as wholly indifferent to others. He highlights the threefold progression from revulsion to freedom from desire to liberation as a genuinely practical map of how an antidote actually matures within the mind, from consciously recalling it, to no longer needing to recall it at all, to finally uprooting the affliction it opposes for good.

3. Section: The Bodhisattva's Mental Engagement, Bent on Every Being's Welfare

What is mental engagement linked to the great vehicle? It refers to a practitioner of the bodhisattva lineage, abiding in a bodhisattva's own nature, whether not yet entered into the true nature free of ordinary birth or already entered into it, who observes both their own benefit and the benefit of others, and who, through the gateway of mental engagement upon both the conceptually and the non-conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking as object a boundless dharma free of any conceptual distinction; through the power of great compassion, in order to exhaust the craving of both self and other, such a practitioner cultivates mental engagement by way of skillfully embracing every being's own welfare and by way of steadily advancing toward the unsurpassed path's own footsteps and causes. Such a one, still at the stage of accumulation or of preparatory effort, cultivates both the conceptually established teachings of the four truths and dependent origination and the non-conceptually established fourfold examination and fourfold genuine knowledge, in order to bring about the true and direct seeing of the path to come; upon entering the path of seeing, non-conceptual wisdom directly realizes suchness, personally witnessing the twin emptiness of self and dharmas, and upon emerging into subsequently attained wisdom, takes as object the entire, boundless dharma-realm, free of all conceptual proliferation, making no distinction at all between beings of the past, present, or future, or between friend, enemy, and stranger, wishing only that every single being attain benefit and ease. Through the power of great compassion, arising because the suffering of beings, deep, subtle, and hard to discern, becomes its object; accumulated over an immense span of time; arising through an especially earnest and forceful engagement, willing to give up hundreds of thousands of bodies and lives rather than mere wealth in order to relieve beings' suffering; and reaching its own most complete purity, such a bodhisattva cultivates mental engagement through the six perfections' own skillful means of embracing every being's welfare and through steadily advancing along the path's own stages, from accumulation all the way to the tenth ground and beyond, toward unsurpassed complete awakening itself.

Chinese Source

云何大乘相應作意修？謂如有一，是菩薩，住菩薩法性；觀自、觀他諸利益事；由安立、非安立諦作意門，內觀真如，緣無量無分別法為境；大悲增上力故，為盡自他所有貪愛，由攝受有情諸利益事方便行相，及由趣向無上足跡因緣行相，修習作意。是名大乘相應作意修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is mental engagement linked to the great vehicle? It refers to one of the bodhisattva lineage who observes both their own and others' benefit, who, through the gateway of both the conceptually and non-conceptually established truths, inwardly observes suchness, taking a boundless, non-conceptual dharma as object, and who, through the power of great compassion, cultivates mental engagement by skillfully embracing every being's welfare and by advancing toward the unsurpassed path. This is called mental engagement linked to the great vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the direct, side-by-side comparison of the śrāvaka's limited, conceptually bound object and the bodhisattva's boundless, conceptually free object as this pair of sections' own clearest statement of what ultimately distinguishes the two vehicles, not merely a difference of motivation but a difference in how vast a scope wisdom itself is finally trained to take in. He highlights the four causes of great compassion, its depth and subtlety, its long accumulation, its forceful earnestness, and its final purity, as together forming this treatise's own fullest working definition of what actually separates ordinary sympathy from the great compassion capable of sustaining a bodhisattva across three incalculable eons of practice.

4. Section: Cultivation through an Image, in Both Insight and Calm-Abiding

What is cultivation through an image? It refers to whatever cultivation takes place either within the conceptually engaged aspect of insight, mentally reflecting upon the image that appears within concentration, itself a counterpart sharing the same basic character as the object originally known before entering concentration, examining that image's own meaning, matter, characteristic, category, timing, and principle through wisdom's own inquiry; or within the non-conceptual aspect of calm-abiding, where the sixth consciousness, without any active analysis or discrimination, simply continues to dwell again and again upon that same counterpart image, itself likewise sharing the same basic character as the object known before entering concentration. Whatever cultivation of this kind takes place, whether through insight's active examination or calm-abiding's steady dwelling, is called cultivation through an image.

Chinese Source

云何影像修？謂或於有分別毗鉢舍那品，三摩地所行影像，所知事同分，作意思惟故；或於無分別奢摩他品，三摩地所行影像，所知事同分，作意思惟故；諸所有修，名影像修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation through an image? It refers to mentally reflecting, whether within the conceptually engaged aspect of insight upon the image that concentration takes as its counterpart to the object originally known, or within the non-conceptual aspect of calm-abiding upon that same counterpart image. Whatever cultivation of this kind takes place is called cultivation through an image.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own careful pairing of insight's active, analytical relationship to the meditative image with calm-abiding's simple, sustained dwelling upon that same image as a precise restatement of this treatise's classic distinction between the two, now applied specifically to the single question of what object a meditator actually holds in mind. He notes that the same underlying image can serve either purpose depending only on how the mind engages it, examining its meaning and characteristics in one mode, or simply resting upon it undisturbed in the other, underscoring that calm-abiding and insight are finally two ways of relating to experience rather than two different experiences altogether.

5. Section: Cultivation through the Very Limit of a Matter, in Both Its Particulars and Its True Nature

What is cultivation through the very limit of a matter? It refers to mentally reflecting upon an object with respect to its past, future, and present aspects, its inner and outer aspects, its coarse and subtle aspects, its inferior and excellent aspects, and its far and near aspects, or else mentally reflecting upon suchness itself in its seven kinds. The first mode, examining an object exhaustively across all eleven of these particular dimensions, its own individual characteristic in every possible respect, yields what is called exhausting the full extent of a thing, from which quantitative wisdom, wisdom that has taken in the whole scope of an object's particulars, is attained; the second mode, examining an object's shared characteristic by way of the seven kinds of suchness common to all things, yields what is called abiding in the true nature of a thing, from which principled wisdom, wisdom that has penetrated a thing's own real nature, is attained. Whatever cultivation proceeds in either of these two ways, exhausting a thing's full extent or abiding in its true nature, is called cultivation through the very limit of a matter, and such cultivation is capable of severing both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known.

Chinese Source

云何事邊際修？謂於過去、未來、現在、內外、麤細、下劣、勝妙、近遠等法作意思惟，或於真如作意思惟。如是或盡所有性故，或如所有性故，諸所有修，名事邊際修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation through the very limit of a matter? It refers to mentally reflecting upon an object's past, future, present, inner, outer, coarse, subtle, inferior, excellent, far, and near aspects, or reflecting upon suchness itself. Whatever cultivation proceeds by exhausting a thing's full extent or by abiding in its true nature is called cultivation through the very limit of a matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of exhaustive-extent wisdom with true-nature wisdom as this treatise's own concise restatement of a wider principle running throughout the entire work, that genuine insight requires knowing both a thing's full particular scope and its shared underlying reality, neither alone being sufficient. He highlights the claim that this single kind of cultivation is capable of severing both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known as a fitting indication of just how much weight this treatise places on the discipline of examining a matter from every possible angle before claiming to understand it.

6. Section: Cultivation That Accomplishes the Task at Hand, in Four Aspects

What is cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand? It refers to whatever cultivation belongs to one who has already entered a fundamental absorption, some further meditative attainment, a worldly concentration, or a transcendent concentration; all such cultivation is called cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand, and it may be understood in four further aspects. The first is accomplishment through mental engagement, referring to a practitioner who cultivates the seven kinds of mental engagement all the way to its culminating result, the mental engagement that is itself the fruit of effort brought to completion. The second is accomplishment through the transformation of one's basis, referring to a practitioner whose body and mind, through the transcendent form of these seven mental engagements brought to full completion, are actually transformed, coarseness and unworkability entirely stilled, culminating in the ultimate transformation of the eight consciousnesses into the four wisdoms. The third is accomplishment through the arising of wisdom, referring to the actual arising of outflow-free direct-perception wisdom once these same transcendent mental engagements have reached completion. And the fourth is accomplishment through meditative absorption, referring to a practitioner's capacity to actually dwell within the very object proper to each attainment once the corresponding worldly form of these seven mental engagements has reached completion, whether the first absorption and onward through the fourth, or the sphere of infinite space and onward through neither-perception-nor-non-perception. In every case, whether the fruit attained is worldly or transcendent, this fourfold accomplishment, of mental engagement, of transformed basis, of arisen wisdom, and of meditative dwelling, is what is meant by cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand.

Chinese Source

云何所作成辦修？謂已證入根本靜慮、或諸等至、或世間定、或出世定，諸所有修，名所作成辦修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand? It refers to whatever cultivation belongs to one who has already entered a fundamental absorption, some further meditative attainment, a worldly concentration, or a transcendent concentration. This is called cultivation that accomplishes the task at hand.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourfold breakdown into mental engagement, transformed basis, arisen wisdom, and meditative dwelling as this treatise's own way of insisting that a completed stage of practice is never a single, isolated event but always shows up simultaneously across the mind's engagement, the body-mind's own underlying condition, the wisdom that results, and the actual meditative object dwelt within. He highlights the culminating transformation of the eight consciousnesses into the four wisdoms, mentioned here only in passing, as a preview of a theme this treatise returns to at far greater length elsewhere, the entire path of cultivation ultimately aiming at nothing less than this complete transformation of consciousness itself.

7. Section: Cultivation as Attainment, Where One Wholesome Idea Strengthens All the Rest

What is cultivation as attainment? It refers to a practitioner who, relying on the first absorption, cultivates any one among a set of wholesome contemplative ideas, the idea of impermanence, of suffering, of non-self, of food's own repulsiveness, of untimely death, and so forth, while all the remaining ideas within this same set stay unmanifest for the time being. These ideas exist to counter three kinds of underlying delusion, delusion regarding time, regarding phase, and regarding intrinsic nature, which in turn give rise to three inverted views, mistaking the impermanent for permanent, mistaking suffering for pleasure, and mistaking what lacks a self for possessing one, along with the further tendency to scheme after gain and to hope for a long life. Whether the idea actually cultivated belongs to this same level, the first absorption itself, or to a lower level, the desire realm, and whether the further merit it induces is worldly, a deepened meditative attainment, or transcendent, a share in the noble path that severs affliction's own seeds, cultivating even this single idea is enough to cause all the other, presently unmanifest ideas and their own respective merits to grow stronger together, becoming purer, freeing the mind further from affliction, and increasing the very ease born of meditative absorption, so that mastery over all of them together is genuinely attained. This is called cultivation as attainment.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，依初靜慮，或修無常想，乃至或修死想，彼所有餘不現前想；或自地攝、或下地攝；及彼所引諸餘功德，或是世間、或出世間；皆能修彼令其增盛清淨樂生，於彼獲得自在成就。是名得修。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to a practitioner who, relying on the first absorption, cultivates the idea of impermanence or of death, while the remaining ideas stay unmanifest; whether belonging to this same level or a lower one, and whether the merit induced is worldly or transcendent, cultivating it causes all such merit to grow stronger and purer, and mastery over it is attained. This is called cultivation as attainment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own central promise, that patiently cultivating even a single wholesome contemplation is enough to strengthen an entire family of related merits one is not currently practicing, as a genuinely encouraging teaching for any practitioner who feels they can only manage to work on one thing at a time. He highlights the three kinds of underlying delusion, regarding time, phase, and intrinsic nature, as this passage's own precise diagnosis of exactly what each of the classic contemplative ideas is designed to counter, impermanence answering delusion about time, the contemplation of suffering answering delusion about phase, and non-self answering delusion about intrinsic nature.

8. Section: Cultivation as Habituation, Keeping the Wholesome Constantly Present

What is cultivation as habituation? It refers to a practitioner who mentally reflects, again and again, upon these very same wholesome ideas, impermanence and the rest, or who, with respect to other wholesome practices such as giving, precepts, and meditative cultivation, keeps them constantly and repeatedly manifest through sheer habituation. This is called cultivation as habituation. The difference between cultivation as habituation and cultivation as attainment is made clear through a series of paired contrasts: habituation is revealed through what is presently manifest, while attainment is revealed through what is yet to come; something is called cultivation because it is habituated now, and something is likewise called cultivation because it will be attained in the future; something is cultivation because it is presently being enjoyed, and something is cultivation because it will be induced in the future; something is cultivation because the task is presently being accomplished, and something is cultivation because a wish will be granted in the future; something is cultivation because it presently resides within the body, and something is cultivation because mastery will be gained over it in the future; and something is cultivation because it is presently manifest, and something is cultivation because it will be accomplished in the future.

Chinese Source

云何習修？謂如有一，即於彼彼無常等諸善想作意思惟，或於善法由習修故，皆現修習，是名習修。現在，習修所顯；未來，得修所顯。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation as habituation? It refers to a practitioner who mentally reflects, again and again, upon these same wholesome ideas, or who keeps other wholesome practices constantly manifest through habituation. This is called cultivation as habituation. What is presently the case is revealed through habituation-cultivation; what is yet to come is revealed through attainment-cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the six paired contrasts distinguishing habituation-cultivation from attainment-cultivation, present versus future, enjoyment versus induction, accomplishing versus granting, residing within versus gaining mastery, and manifest versus accomplished, as together forming a single, consistent axis, habituation always describing what is already here and being repeated, attainment always describing what is not yet here and is being brought about. He notes that this precise distinction matters practically as well as terminologically, since a practitioner genuinely benefits from knowing whether a given moment of practice is meant to deepen something already achieved or to reach toward something still beyond present grasp.

9. Section: Cultivation as Removal, Driving Out One Wedge with Another

What is cultivation as removal? It refers to a practitioner who, through mental engagement upon the characteristics of the counterpart image that appears within concentration, the characteristic of impermanence and the rest, skillfully dispels attachment through a method resembling how one wooden wedge drives out another already lodged in place, thereby abandoning the mind's own attachment to the characteristics it takes its objects, the aggregates and the rest, to intrinsically possess, such as permanence or deathlessness; and once this is accomplished, even the grasping mind's own nature becomes ungraspable, subject and object both falling away as one enters the ultimate truth itself. As a further verse puts it, a bodhisattva abiding in concentration observes that the image before the mind is mind alone; once the object's own apparent characteristic has thereby dissolved, careful examination reveals only one's own thought remaining; abiding thus within the mind itself, one comes to know that what is grasped has no real existence, and next that the grasping mind itself is equally without existence, until afterward one touches what can never actually be grasped at all, the very reality beyond both. And just as one might use a still finer wedge to drive out a coarser one already used to displace the first, a practitioner likewise uses the very bodily ease and pliancy attained through freedom from desire to displace and remove the coarse, unworkable body still burdened by affliction. Whatever cultivation proceeds by this means, dispelling attachment to already-arisen unwholesome states through such skillful displacement, is called cultivation as removal.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，由三摩地所行影像諸相作意故，如楔出楔方便除遣，棄於自性諸相。又如有一，用彼細楔遣於麤楔，如是行者，以輕安身除麤重身。是名除去修。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to a practitioner who, through mental engagement upon the meditative image's own characteristics, dispels attachment to a thing's intrinsic characteristics as one wedge drives out another. And just as a finer wedge drives out a coarser one, such a practitioner uses the ease of a body freed from desire to remove the coarse, burdened body. This is called cultivation as removal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the double wedge image, first one wedge displacing an attachment to characteristics, then a finer wedge displacing an already-coarsened body, as an unusually vivid and memorable illustration of how spiritual progress actually works in stages, each advance not arising from nothing but displacing what came immediately before it. He highlights the verse's own careful sequence, first recognizing the grasped object as mind alone, then recognizing the grasping mind itself as equally without independent existence, and only then touching the ungraspable reality beyond both, as this treatise's own precise map of how a meditator moves from ordinary dualistic experience toward the non-dual realization of suchness itself.

10. Section: Cultivation as Antidote, in Four Stages from Revulsion to Distancing

What is cultivation as antidote? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds by mental engagement upon revulsion-antidote, severing-antidote, sustaining-antidote, or distancing-antidote, this being called cultivation as antidote. Revulsion-antidote refers to every worldly wholesome path, contemplating a lower stage as coarse, afflicted, and confining while a higher stage appears peaceful, subtle, and liberating, thereby attaining freedom from desire and the four absorptions or four formless attainments in turn, excluding only the four immeasurable states of mind and whatever mental engagement induces a playful display of supernatural power, since these are not themselves born of revulsion, the four immeasurables being cultivated instead for the sake of benefiting beings and supernatural display carrying no element of aversion at all. Severing-antidote refers to mental engagement that takes suchness itself, in its seven kinds, as object, this being what actually severs the very seeds of affliction. Sustaining-antidote refers to the subsequently attained worldly and transcendent path that follows upon severing-antidote, together with the path of actual liberation itself, sustaining and upholding what severing-antidote has already accomplished so that the severed affliction never manifests again. And distancing-antidote refers to further and repeated cultivation of the antidote path once affliction has already been severed, or to cultivating the path of some still higher stage, as when a practitioner who has already attained the first absorption goes on to counter that same absorption's own remaining coarse investigation and analysis in order to advance toward the second. A further explanation gives these same four a slightly different sense: the path of hearing, reflection, and cultivation is called revulsion-antidote; the transcendent path itself is called severing-antidote; the transformation of one's own basis that results from it is called sustaining-antidote; and the worldly path of cultivation that follows afterward, by means of subsequently attained wisdom, is called distancing-antidote.

Chinese Source

云何對治修？謂於厭患對治、或斷對治、或持對治、或遠分對治作意思惟，諸所有修，名對治修。斷對治者，謂緣真如為境作意。遠分對治者，謂煩惱斷已，於對治道更多修習，或多修習上地之道。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation as antidote? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds by mental engagement upon revulsion-antidote, severing-antidote, sustaining-antidote, or distancing-antidote. Severing-antidote refers to mental engagement taking suchness itself as object. Distancing-antidote refers to further cultivation of the antidote path, or of a higher stage's path, once affliction has already been severed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the deliberate exclusion of the four immeasurable states and playful supernatural display from revulsion-antidote as a fine but important distinction, since both indeed belong to worldly wholesome cultivation yet arise from compassion and skillful benefit rather than from any aversion toward a lower state, showing that not every wholesome practice fits neatly under a single antidote category. He highlights the concrete example of countering the first absorption's own remaining coarse investigation and analysis in order to advance toward the second as distancing-antidote's clearest illustration, a reminder that even a genuinely peaceful and wholesome attainment can itself become the very thing a more advanced practitioner must next learn to see beyond.

11. Section: Cultivation in Part, the First Stage of Signless Practice

What is cultivation in part? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds through mental engagement upon any single wholesome dharma, such as the idea of impermanence, taken as object, without yet bringing every other wholesome contemplation together at once. This is called cultivation in part. This kind of cultivation corresponds to what is elsewhere called the partial stage within the wider unfolding of signless cultivation, the stage proper to a bodhisattva still short of the first ground, one who has reached the rank of forbearance within the stage of resolute conduct and beyond, who has not yet actually realized signless suchness in direct experience but has already attained a supreme faith and understanding in the teaching, thereby entering into partial accord with a single portion of suchness's own meaning, a first and still incomplete correspondence with reality itself.

Chinese Source

云何少分修？謂於無常想等隨一善法作意思惟，諸所有修，名少分修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation in part? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds by mentally engaging any single wholesome dharma, such as the idea of impermanence, taken as object. This is called cultivation in part.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own brief definition as the first rung of a much larger ladder this treatise elsewhere maps directly onto the bodhisattva path's own ten grounds, cultivation in part corresponding to the sincere but still pre-realization practice of an advanced ordinary bodhisattva who has not yet actually broken through to suchness itself. He highlights the phrase "supreme faith and understanding" as a genuinely important intermediate category in this treatise's own account of spiritual progress, a stage of confidence and comprehension real enough to already partially correspond with truth, even though direct realization still lies ahead.

12. Section: Cultivation That Pervades, Where All Dharmas Share One Suchness

What is cultivation that pervades? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds through mental engagement upon the single, uniform suchness shared equally by every dharma, this being called cultivation that pervades. Since every dharma's own real nature is empty in exactly the same way, suchness itself being the one shared characteristic running through all things, unchanging and never other than itself, taking this single suchness as object in mental engagement, rather than any one dharma among many, is what earns this kind of cultivation its name. This corresponds to what is elsewhere called the pervading stage, the stage proper to a bodhisattva of purified, superior aspiration, namely one who has reached the first ground, capable of directly realizing every dharma's true nature through non-conceptual wisdom and thereby observing suchness as a single, uniform reality running through all things at once, no longer confined to any one dharma's own partial characteristic.

Chinese Source

云何遍行修？謂於諸法一味真如作意思惟，諸所有修，名遍行修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation that pervades? It refers to whatever cultivation proceeds through mental engagement upon the single, uniform suchness shared by all dharmas. This is called cultivation that pervades.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the leap from cultivation in part to cultivation that pervades as marking this treatise's own decisive line between an advanced but still unrealized ordinary bodhisattva and a genuinely noble one, the first ground's own non-conceptual realization of suchness transforming what was previously only partial correspondence into direct, unified contact with reality's single shared nature. He notes that the very word "pervades" here is doing real philosophical work, insisting that suchness is not one truth among the many truths belonging to individual dharmas but the single truth equally present within every single one of them.

13. Section: Cultivation in Motion, Still Interrupted by What Bears a Sign

What is cultivation in motion? It refers to a practitioner who, while still engaged in the preliminary methods leading toward signless meditation, such as first contemplating a fire's own characteristic and then contemplating that same fire's extinguishing in order to arrive at an experiential sense of signlessness, from time to time loses that signless state and must fall back upon a sign-bearing preliminary method once again in order to re-enter it, the practice thereby proceeding through an alternation of signless moments interrupted by sign-bearing ones. This is called cultivation in motion, distinguished from a uniform cultivation in which no such interruption occurs at all, the mind entering directly into correspondence with the signless the moment it is approached, with only the signless itself ever remaining as object. This corresponds to what is elsewhere called the stage of motion, the stage belonging to noble bodhisattvas across the first six grounds, who, even while directly realizing suchness through mental engagement upon signless truth, still find that practice interrupted from time to time by what bears a sign, still stirred, as it were, by the functioning power of habitual linguistic seeds, and for this reason called the stage of motion, or equally the conduct still bearing a sign.

Chinese Source

云何動轉修？謂於無相修方便修時，時時有相間隔而修，名動轉修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation in motion? It refers to a practitioner who, while practicing the preliminary methods toward signless meditation, from time to time falls back into an interruption bearing a sign. This is called cultivation in motion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fire-and-extinguishing example as a genuinely helpful, concrete illustration of what "sign-bearing" and "signless" practice actually feel like from the inside, first holding a definite characteristic in mind and then letting even that characteristic's own cessation lead the mind toward signlessness itself. He highlights the honest acknowledgment that even noble bodhisattvas across the first six grounds, who have already directly realized suchness, still experience interruption and lapse back into sign-bearing states, as a fittingly realistic corrective to any assumption that realization alone immediately grants unbroken, effortless stability.

14. Section: Cultivation Still Requiring Effort

What is cultivation still requiring effort? It refers to a practitioner who, during that same preliminary practice toward signless meditation, is not yet able to proceed spontaneously and effortlessly, but must instead apply deliberate exertion at intervals in order to sustain it. This is called cultivation still requiring effort, corresponding to what is elsewhere called the stage requiring effort, the stage proper to a bodhisattva of the seventh ground, who is indeed already able to directly realize suchness but still needs to apply deliberate exertion in order to do so, not yet having attained the spontaneous, effortless engagement that later grounds bring.

Chinese Source

云何有加行修？謂即於彼方便修時，由有加行相間隔而修，名有加行修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation still requiring effort? It refers to a practitioner who, during that same preliminary practice, must apply deliberate exertion at intervals in order to sustain it. This is called cultivation still requiring effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fine distinction between cultivation in motion and cultivation still requiring effort as worth pausing over, since both involve interruption of a kind, yet the seventh ground's own effort is a conscious, deliberate exertion rather than the more passive lapsing back into a sign-bearing state described in the stage of motion, marking real progress even though neither stage has yet reached spontaneity. He notes that naming an entire ground of advanced bodhisattva practice simply "requiring effort" is itself a humbling reminder of how much sustained exertion this path still demands even at so exalted a stage.

15. Section: Cultivation Already Accomplished, Where Every Vehicle Completes Its Own Task

What is cultivation already accomplished? It refers to a practitioner of the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, or the great vehicle who has already attained the complete transformation of their own basis, body and mind entirely transformed, the ālaya-consciousness itself transformed into the ripening consciousness or, in its fullest sense, into the stainless consciousness, and who has attained complete mastery over all dharmas, entering, dwelling in, and emerging from every supernatural power and meditative attainment entirely at will. Whatever cultivation belongs to such a practitioner is called cultivation already accomplished. In brief, a śrāvaka practitioner attaining arhatship or a pratyekabuddha practitioner attaining the fruit of solitary awakening has thereby severed the craving and views afflicting the three realms; a great vehicle practitioner attaining unsurpassed complete awakening has severed both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known, gaining complete freedom of function over every dharma; each of the three vehicles thus brings its own wholesome qualities to genuine and final completion, and this completion itself is called cultivation already accomplished. This corresponds, in the wider unfolding of signless cultivation across the ten grounds, to the eighth ground's own stage free of both effort and exertion, where suchness is realized entirely spontaneously, unfolding onward through the ninth and tenth grounds across the third incalculable eon until buddhahood itself is reached, this entire final stretch together constituting the stage of fulfillment.

Chinese Source

謂或聲聞乘、或獨覺乘、或復大乘，已得一切所有轉依，及得一切諸法自在，此所有修名成辦修。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to a practitioner of the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, or the great vehicle who has already attained the complete transformation of their own basis and complete mastery over all dharmas. Whatever cultivation belongs to such a practitioner is called cultivation already accomplished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five kinds of cultivation together, in part, that pervades, in motion, still requiring effort, and already accomplished, as forming a single, continuous arc this treatise elsewhere maps directly onto the bodhisattva path's own ten grounds, from an advanced ordinary being's first partial correspondence with suchness, through the first ground's unified realization, the first six grounds' own remaining interruption, the seventh ground's deliberate exertion, and finally the eighth ground onward into full spontaneity and, at last, buddhahood itself. He highlights the closing observation that all three vehicles, śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva, each reach their own genuine completion by this same basic pattern of transformed basis and mastery over dharmas, differing chiefly in how much is severed, affliction alone or affliction together with the obstruction to what can be known, as a fitting reminder that this treatise honors real attainment wherever it is found even while reserving its fullest scope for the great vehicle's own path.

16. Section: Cultivation Not Born of Concentration, Cultivation Born of It, and the Ground's Own Close

What is cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation? It refers to whatever wholesome practice, giving, precepts, patience, mindfulness of the Buddha, mantra recitation, seated meditation, and the like, is undertaken with a scattered mind that has not yet settled into any actual level of concentration, since a practice carried out this way, however wholesome, does not yet count as belonging to the wisdom born of cultivation proper, real concentration requiring at the very least the preliminary access concentration that first makes possible the suppression of affliction. This is called cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation. What is cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation? It refers to whatever wholesome practice is instead undertaken while genuinely settled within some level of concentration, whether contemplating the four noble truths through insight in order to sever affliction, or cultivating the eight forms of liberation, the eight bases of mastery, the ten bases of totality, and other such wholesome practices proper to concentration itself, resting on at least this same preliminary access concentration as its foundation. This is called cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation, and with this pair the full list of sixteen kinds of cultivation belonging to the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation comes to a close. And with this same close, the third and final section of this passage follows, briefly declining to say more: within this ground's own decisive discernment, no further text of decisive discernment will appear again. Since the Basic Section's own earlier treatment of this ground, by way of its four supporting bases and seven branches, had already explained cultivation's own place, cause, method, and fruit in complete and careful detail, this decisive discernment needed only set out these sixteen essential kinds of cultivation practice, and having done so, its work here is genuinely finished.

Chinese Source

謂不定地諸施、戒等所有善法修，名非修所成法修。云何修所成法修？謂定地諸善法修，名修所成法修。於此地中餘決擇文，更不復現。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever wholesome practice, giving, precepts, and the like, undertaken with a scattered mind not settled in concentration, is called cultivation of a dharma not itself born of cultivation. What is cultivation of a dharma that is itself born of cultivation? It refers to wholesome practice undertaken while settled in concentration. Within this ground's own decisive discernment, no further text of decisive discernment will appear again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp line drawn here between concentrated and merely scattered wholesome practice as a genuinely important corrective for any practitioner tempted to assume that giving, precepts, or devotional recitation alone already constitute the wisdom born of cultivation, when in this treatise's own careful terminology they remain, however genuinely wholesome, still short of it without at least access concentration as their foundation. He highlights this ground's own unusually brief decisive discernment, a mere sixteen terms briskly explained where the earlier grounds ran to entire fascicles, as itself following directly from the Basic Section's own already exhaustive treatment, this treatise consistently declining to repeat at length what has already been said in full elsewhere.

17. Section: Opening the Śrāvaka Ground's Decisive Discernment, in Three Parts across Five Fascicles

As has now been explained, the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation is complete. The decisive discernment of the Śrāvaka Ground, Maitreya now says, I shall next explain. The Basic Section's own treatment of the Śrāvaka Ground already unfolds across four yogic processes: the first, spanning several fascicles, covers the ground of lineage, the ground of entry, and the ground of renunciation, the last of these explaining in detail the thirteen kinds of worldly and transcendent provisions for the path; the second sets forth the establishment of categories, seventeen topics covering the various kinds of practitioner, how a practitioner is established, the object, instruction, training, and the yoga master's own work; the third sets forth the establishment of yogic method itself, explaining the sequence and skillful means by which a teacher guides a student through calm-abiding and insight; and the fourth broadly explains the very character of cultivation, covering both the worldly path's four absorptions and eight attainments and the transcendent path's own detailed progress from the first fruit all the way to arhatship. Since the Basic Section has already treated all of this so fully, this decisive discernment, spanning five further fascicles, confines itself to three deeper and more hidden matters not yet fully addressed there: the śrāvaka's own lineage, resolving five difficult questions that might be raised against it; the śrāvaka's own varieties, ten distinct kinds distinguished by circumstance and attainment; and the śrāvaka canon itself, thirteen topics drawn from key sūtras and verses within the Āgama scriptures. The Śrāvaka Ground stands as one of this treatise's own most foundational sections, laying out with exceptional clarity everything from the initial accumulation of provisions, through the actual methods of practice, to the precise character of the fruit finally attained upon reaching arhatship itself.

Chinese Source

如是已說修所成慧地決擇。聲聞地決擇，我今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Cultivation has been explained. The decisive discernment of the Śrāvaka Ground, I shall now explain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own careful preview, three deeper matters, lineage, varieties, and canon, spanning five entire fascicles, as fair warning that the Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discernment will prove one of this treatise's most substantial undertakings, fitting given how central the Śrāvaka Ground itself already is to the whole work's account of the path. He highlights the deliberate decision to leave aside anything the Basic Section already covered in full and to focus instead only on what remained genuinely difficult or hidden there, as this treatise's own consistent editorial principle now stated once again in a fresh context.

18. Section: Are Some Beings Truly without the Lineage for Liberation?

The Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discernment opens with the first of its five difficult questions concerning lineage, resolving the difficulty raised against the very notion of a being with no lineage at all. As the Basic Section had already stated, a being abiding in no lineage whatsoever, lacking the seed proper to the śrāvaka, the solitary-awakened, or the bodhisattva vehicle alike, is said to be one for whom complete nirvana is ultimately impossible. At this a doubt may naturally arise in the mind: how could there possibly be such a being, permanently barred from nirvana no matter what? To press this difficulty further, one should turn the question back upon the questioner: what, then, is your own view? Do the many different kinds of sentient being possess distinct elemental natures at all, and among these natures, are there truly boundless, immeasurable varieties, some inferior and some excellent? If the answer given is that such various elemental natures do indeed exist, immeasurable in their variety, ranging genuinely from inferior to excellent, then it stands to reason that a being permanently barred from nirvana ought likewise to exist among these boundless varieties, and to deny this while granting the premise would itself be unreasonable. As one commentary explains, each being's own continuous mindstream already contains all eighteen elements, itself called elemental nature in its basic kind, yet within each single element countless further distinctions of variety exist, since different beings of different karmic destinies accumulate that same element quite differently, so that a single named kind of element in fact conceals boundless further variety beneath it; and these elemental natures are themselves gathered and accumulated through the power of a being's own past resolute conviction, an inferior nature accumulated through past unwholesome conviction, an excellent nature accumulated through past wholesome conviction.

Chinese Source

如本地分說：住無種性補特伽羅，是名畢竟無般涅槃法。此中或有心生疑惑，云何而有畢竟無般涅槃法耶？應詰彼言：汝何所欲？諸有情類種種界性、無量界性、下劣界性、勝妙界性，為有耶？為無耶？若言有者，無有畢竟無般涅槃法補特伽羅，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Basic Section states, a being abiding in no lineage at all is said to be one for whom complete nirvana is ultimately impossible. A doubt may arise: how could such a being exist? One should ask in turn: do sentient beings possess various, immeasurable, inferior and excellent elemental natures, or not? If such natures do exist, then denying a being permanently barred from nirvana would be unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening dilemma's own method, granting the questioner's premise about boundless elemental variety and then showing that a being without the lineage for liberation follows as a direct consequence of that very premise, as a genuinely elegant piece of argumentation, turning what looked like an objection into its own best evidence. He notes that a fuller resolution of just how troubling the notion of a being permanently barred from nirvana actually is, and whether this truly forecloses that being's future without exception, still lies ahead in the second horn of this same dilemma, cautioning against drawing a final conclusion from only the first half of the argument.

19. Section: The Second Horn Closes the First Difficulty, and Rootless Beings Cannot Rescue the Second

The dilemma posed against the difficulty of a being with no lineage at all now turns to its second horn. If, instead, the answer given is that sentient beings do not in fact possess such various, boundless elemental natures, this too fails, for scripture itself plainly teaches that sentient beings possess various elemental natures, ranging genuinely from inferior to excellent, so that denying this outright would contradict the Buddha's own established word. Either horn of the dilemma thus fails, and with this the first of the five difficulties, the difficulty of a being with no lineage at all, is fully resolved. A second difficulty then arises: granted that elemental natures vary boundlessly, there is still no such thing as a being genuinely without any faculties at all, since whatever lacks eyes, ears, and the rest of the six faculties is thereby not a sentient being to begin with, merely an insentient thing like a mountain or a tree, and so, by this same reasoning, why should one grant that a being without any lineage for liberation at all could genuinely exist? One should turn this question back as well: what do you take a rootless thing itself to be, a sentient being, or not a sentient being? If it is granted to be a sentient being, then insentient things outside the body altogether, mountains, rivers, tables, and chairs, would all have to count as sentient beings too, which is plainly unreasonable. And if it is granted not to be a sentient being, then the very premise of the objection collapses, since something already agreed not to be a sentient being can offer no analogy at all to the question of whether a genuine sentient being might lack the lineage for liberation; asking why one does not therefore accept the existence of rootless beings, once rootless things have already been conceded to be no beings at all, is itself unreasonable. With this the second difficulty, too, is resolved.

Chinese Source

若言無者，經言：諸有情類有種種界性，乃至勝妙界性。不應道理。應詰彼言：汝何所欲？諸無根者為是有情，為非有情？若是有情，外無根物應是有情，然不應道理。若非有情，而言何不許有無根有情者，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

If elemental natures are said not to exist, scripture itself contradicts this. One should ask: is a rootless thing a sentient being, or not? If it is a sentient being, external insentient things like mountains would have to be sentient beings too, which is unreasonable. If it is not a sentient being, then asking why rootless beings are not accepted to exist is likewise unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this second difficulty's own hidden confusion, quietly smuggling an insentient thing into the category of sentient being in order to construct its analogy, as the real target of the reply, the counter-question exposing that the objection never actually compared two genuine cases of the same kind at all. He notes that this same dialectical pattern, granting a premise only provisionally and then showing that either possible answer to a follow-up question leads the objection back into contradiction, will recur across all five of these difficulties, and is worth watching for as this treatise's own consistent method of debate throughout the section.

20. Section: Can a Being Cross Castes the Way It Might Cross into a Different Lineage?

Having resolved this, a third difficulty is raised: just as a being born a kshatriya in one life may, in another, be born a brahmin, a vaishya, or a shudra, and just as one born a shudra may later be born a kshatriya, and just as a being who has fallen into hell may later be born among the gods, and one born among the gods may later fall back into hell, why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not likewise, at some future time, come to possess it? One should again turn the question back: do you take it that a single being's own mindstream contains, all at once, the seeds for every one of these castes and destinies together, or only the seed for one exclusively? If it is granted that all such seeds are present together, then the analogy itself fails to hold, since it is not a genuine likeness at all, for no single being's mindstream can contain, at one and the same time, both the seed proper to one who will ultimately attain nirvana and the seed proper to one who genuinely never will, the very presence of the one excluding the other in a way that caste and destiny, which may indeed coexist as seeds within a single stream, do not. And if it is instead granted that only one such seed is exclusively present at a time, this too is unreasonable, since it plainly contradicts what is actually observed, a being once a kshatriya being later, in fact, reborn a shudra. Either way the analogy fails, and the third difficulty is likewise resolved.

Chinese Source

如是何故不作無般涅槃法已，或時復作有般涅槃法耶？應詰彼言：汝何所欲？諸剎帝利乃至戍陀羅，及那落迦乃至諸天，為有一切界耶？為獨有一界耶？若有一切界者，喻不相似，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not likewise later come to possess it, just as a kshatriya may be reborn a shudra? One should ask: does a single being's mindstream contain the seeds for every caste and destiny at once, or only one exclusively? If all such seeds coexist, the analogy does not genuinely hold, and is unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise point of disanalogy identified here, that caste and destiny seeds may indeed coexist within a single mindstream while the seed for eventual nirvana and the seed for its permanent impossibility genuinely cannot, as the philosophical heart of this entire difficulty, everything else in the exchange simply working out the logical consequences of this one asymmetry. He highlights the treatise's own willingness to appeal directly to observed fact, a kshatriya plainly being reborn a shudra in practice, as a useful reminder that even highly abstract Abhidharma argument in this work still ultimately answers to how things are actually seen to occur.

21. Section: If All Castes' Seeds Coexist, Must Every Lineage Coexist Too?

A fourth difficulty then follows directly from this same admission: since a kshatriya's own mindstream has just been granted to contain the seeds of every caste together, why, by that same reasoning, should a being without the lineage for liberation not likewise contain the element proper to one who does possess it? One should turn the question back once more: do you take the element of no lineage for liberation and the element of possessing that lineage to be mutually exclusive of one another, or not mutually exclusive? If they are granted to be mutually exclusive, then the very question of why a being lacking the one does not also possess the other collapses, since mutual exclusion was just conceded, meaning their coexistence was never actually intended in the first place. And if they are instead granted not to be mutually exclusive, then it would follow that one and the same being could be, at once, both without the lineage for liberation and possessing it, which is plainly unreasonable, since, as one commentary explains, sentient beings divide, in the end, into exactly two kinds, those genuinely capable of ultimately severing both the obstruction of affliction and the obstruction to what can be known, who thereby possess the very nature that makes nirvana possible, and those genuinely incapable of ever severing them, who are precisely those without the lineage for liberation, the two admitting of no overlap between them at all. With this the fourth difficulty, too, is resolved.

Chinese Source

如是無般涅槃法，何故不有般涅槃法界耶？應詰彼言：汝何所欲？諸無般涅槃法界、諸有般涅槃法界，此二界為互相違耶？為不相違耶？若不相違，即此補特伽羅是無般涅槃法，亦是有般涅槃法者，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why should a being without the lineage for liberation not also possess the element of one who does? One should ask: are the element of no lineage and the element of possessing lineage mutually exclusive, or not? If not mutually exclusive, then the same being would be both without the lineage and possessing it at once, which is unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the cited twofold division of all sentient beings, those capable of ultimately severing both obstructions and those genuinely incapable of ever doing so, as this passage's own clearest positive statement of what the entire, sometimes dense dialectic of these five difficulties is actually protecting, a real and irreducible distinction between two fundamentally different possibilities open to different beings, rather than a distinction this treatise is merely reluctant to give up on technical grounds alone. He notes that this fourth difficulty, in exposing the internal contradiction of holding two mutually exclusive elements at once, brings the debate to what is in effect its logical center, the remaining fifth difficulty largely testing this same conclusion against one further kind of analogy.

22. Section: Gold and Salt Appear and Vanish — Could a Lineage Do the Same?

A fifth and final difficulty is then raised, drawing on the observed behavior of the natural world itself: just as a given locality may, at one time, be found to contain no vein of gold at all and yet, at another time, be found to contain one, and just as the same may be observed of pearls, lapis lazuli, salt, and other such natural resources, appearing where they were once entirely absent, why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not, at some point, likewise come to possess it? One should ask in reply: do you mean to say that lineage itself is indeterminate in just this way, so that a being of the fixed śrāvaka lineage might later cease to be so, a being of the fixed great-vehicle lineage might likewise later cease to be so, or a being without any fixed lineage at all might later come to acquire one? If this indeterminacy is indeed what is meant, then two serious faults immediately follow. The first is that the very wholesome roots conducive to liberation would be rendered empty and fruitless, since a being possessing them now could simply lose them later, leaving no reliable basis on which the path to liberation could ever actually be completed. The second is that positing any fixed lineage at all, whether śrāvaka, solitary-awakened, or great-vehicle, would itself become unreasonable, since the very notion of a fixed lineage presupposes precisely the stability that indeterminacy denies. And if, to avoid these faults, one instead insists that lineage is not indeterminate after all, then the original analogy collapses on its own terms, since a being with the lineage for liberation, once granted to possess it fixedly, could then, by that same mineral-like logic, later lose it again, which is equally unreasonable and was never actually intended by anyone advancing this analogy in the first place.

Chinese Source

如是先是無般涅槃法種性，何故不於一時或有般涅槃法種性耶？應詰彼言：汝何所欲？⋯若言爾者，順解脫分善根，應空無果。又若爾者，立定種性，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why should a being once without the lineage for liberation not, at some point, come to possess it, just as gold or salt may appear in a place once devoid of it? One should ask whether this means lineage itself is indeterminate. If so, the wholesome roots conducive to liberation would be rendered fruitless, and positing any fixed lineage at all would become unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two faults exposed here, wholesome roots rendered fruitless and fixed lineage itself becoming incoherent, as together showing exactly what is actually at stake in this entire five-part debate, not an abstract point of classification but the very reliability of the path itself, since a practitioner needs genuine confidence that sincere effort toward liberation will not simply evaporate on its own. He notes that this fifth difficulty's own resolution is not yet complete at this point in the text, the dialogue continuing further still, and treats the patience with which this treatise works through even a natural, intuitively appealing analogy like minerals appearing and vanishing as a model of how carefully a difficult doctrinal claim ought to be tested before being accepted.

23. Section: This Life or the Next? The Fifth Difficulty Closes, and Ten Kinds of Śrāvaka Open

The fifth difficulty is pressed once further, from yet another angle: if a being without the lineage for liberation, dwelling within the desire realm's own inferior element, is ever to become one possessing that lineage, does this transformation occur within this very same life, or only in some future life? If it is said to occur within this very life, then, once such a being encounters the three jewels, does this present life actually give rise to the wholesome roots conducive to liberation, or not? If it does, then a being who has just been granted to give rise to these very wholesome roots upon encountering the three jewels can hardly still be called one without the lineage for liberation at all, since only a being already possessing the seed for it could cultivate such roots in the first place, so this answer is unreasonable. And if it does not, then claiming such a being nonetheless becomes one possessing the lineage is equally unreasonable, since no such transformation has actually been shown to occur. If instead it is said that this transformation occurs only in some future life, one must ask further whether such a being had already accumulated these same wholesome roots in some even earlier life or had not. If it had already accumulated them, then this very present life, upon encountering the three jewels, should already give rise to the wholesome roots conducive to liberation, so postponing the transformation to some future life is unreasonable, and the very cause already planted would be rendered fruitless besides. And if it had not accumulated them at all, then present life and future life stand exactly alike, both equally lacking any wholesome roots already cultivated, so there remains no reason whatsoever why the future life alone should succeed where this present life could not. Every possible answer thus fails, and with this the fifth and final difficulty concerning lineage is fully resolved, closing all five difficulties raised against the very notion of a being without the lineage for liberation. A further topic then opens: the second of this ground's three deeper matters, the ten kinds of śrāvaka. Briefly, there are ten: the śrāvaka of a pure element, the śrāvaka who has already met the right conditions, the śrāvaka born into a defiled world, the śrāvaka born into a pure world, the śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline, the śrāvaka born in a wholesome and fortunate age, the śrāvaka not yet possessing the eye, the śrāvaka already possessing the eye, the śrāvaka possessing the pure eye, and the śrāvaka possessing the utterly pure eye.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing move of this fifth difficulty, forcing the objector to specify exactly when the supposed transformation occurs and then showing that neither this life nor any future life can coherently house it, as a fitting capstone to the whole five-part debate, the argument's cumulative weight finally closing off every avenue by which a being without the lineage for liberation could be imagined to acquire it after all. He highlights the shift in register that follows, from tightly argued dialectic to a simple, numbered list of ten kinds of śrāvaka, as this treatise's own way of moving from defending a difficult doctrine to the more descriptive, almost pastoral task of actually mapping the many different circumstances in which a genuine śrāvaka practitioner may be found.

24. Section: From a Pure Lineage to a World Clouded by Five Kinds of Turbidity

Each of the ten kinds of śrāvaka is now explained in turn. Whoever abides inwardly in the śrāvaka lineage at all is the first kind, the śrāvaka of a pure element, so called simply for possessing this lineage in the first place, whether born into a pure land or into an age when the world itself flourishes, as when Maitreya himself will appear in the world to come. Whoever has already entered the Dharma, already possessing the lineage and having actually encountered and entered the Buddha's teaching, is the second kind, the śrāvaka who has already met the right conditions. Whoever is born into a world where a śrāvaka's own arising encounters abundant suffering, admitting of the five kinds of turbidity, turbidity of lifespan, of sentient beings, of affliction, of view, and of the eon itself, is the third kind, the śrāvaka born into a defiled world. Turbidity of lifespan refers to a shortening of the human lifespan itself, from an age when people once lived eighty-four thousand years down to a present age in which reaching even a hundred is rare, human unwholesome karma having steadily increased. Turbidity of sentient beings refers to an age in which beings largely fail to recognize what is honorable, neglecting filial devotion, disrespecting genuine spiritual teachers, feeling no dread at wrongdoing in this life or the next, and abandoning giving, merit-making, and the upholding of precepts altogether. Turbidity of affliction refers to an age in which unwholesome craving, hatred, and delusion burn especially fiercely, given to violence, deceit, and false speech in overwhelming measure. Turbidity of view refers to an age in which wrong views proliferate wildly, actively working to destroy or extinguish the true teaching and setting up counterfeit teachings that only resemble it. And turbidity of the eon refers to a declining age in which, as the human lifespan falls to thirty years, famine arises; as it falls to twenty, plague arises; and as it falls to ten, warfare arises, leaving scarcely a being in the world untouched by calamity. Whatever stands opposite to all this, one should know, is the fourth kind, the śrāvaka born into a pure world, born instead into a pure land or into a flourishing age when the human lifespan and stature are both immense and the ten wholesome courses of action are universally practiced.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five turbidities, of lifespan, of sentient beings, of affliction, of view, and of the eon, as this treatise's own remarkably concrete and almost sociological account of what a genuinely degenerate age actually looks like, moving well beyond abstract doctrine into observable shifts in human lifespan, filial devotion, violence, wrong view, and even climate and famine. He highlights the pairing of a defiled-world śrāvaka against a pure-world śrāvaka as a reminder that this same lineage may unfold in radically different outward circumstances, the inward seed for liberation remaining identical even where the surrounding world differs as greatly as a five-turbidity age differs from an age as fortunate as the one in which Maitreya himself is said to appear.

25. Section: The Śrāvaka Born in the Age of the Dharma's Decline

A further pair of kinds is then explained, the śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline and the śrāvaka born in a wholesome and fortunate age. What, one may ask, are the characteristic marks of a śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline? Briefly, such śrāvakas, born into a future age when the true teaching has already faded into obscurity following the Buddha's own passing, are largely given over to a fondness for material gain and for others' esteem, turning their backs on the subtle Dharma itself, so that craving, hatred, delusion, and wrong, improper teachings all flourish together in overwhelming measure among them.

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the characteristic marks of a śrāvaka born in the age of the Dharma's decline? Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this unflinching portrait of decline, monastics themselves turning toward fame and material gain and away from the very teaching they claim to follow, as this treatise's own sober acknowledgment that even the monastic order is not immune to the same five turbidities already described, its own internal integrity capable of eroding right alongside the wider world's own decline. He notes that this description is only the opening statement of a fuller characterization still to come, cautioning against treating it as the complete picture before the passage's own further elaboration has been read.
