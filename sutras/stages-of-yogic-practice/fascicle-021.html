Fascicle 21 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十一 · A New Ground Opens

The Ground of the Hearer-Disciples Begins

Fascicle 21 opens exactly where fascicle 20 left off: the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation (修所成地) formally closes, and the thirteenth of the treatise's seventeen grounds — the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples (聲聞地) — begins. Where the three prior grounds (hearing, reflection, cultivation) traced practice common to all three vehicles, this long ground turns to the śrāvaka path specifically, opening with the Ground of Spiritual Lineage: the dormant seed, present or absent in a given being, that makes liberation possible at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 21 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Ground of the Hearer-Disciples Opens

The treatise marks the transition plainly: the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation has now been fully explained, and a new ground begins. Master Cháng Bǎi first orients the reader to where this ground sits within the treatise's larger architecture, before the root text itself asks its opening question.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第十三初瑜伽處種性地品第一……如是已說修所成地。云何聲聞地？

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the thirteenth ground, the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples: the first yogic process, the chapter on the Ground of Spiritual Lineage. …Thus the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation has already been explained. What is the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by distinguishing two kinds of practice the treatise covers: practice common to all three vehicles ("general conduct"), meaning hearing, reflecting, and cultivating — exactly the three grounds fascicles 15 through 20 have just finished tracing, since a hearer-disciple, a solitary realizer, and a bodhisattva must all pass through the same three stages of learning before any of them can practice their own path — and practice specific to each vehicle ("distinct conduct"), meaning the hearer-path, the solitary-realizer path, and the bodhisattva path treated separately. This fascicle opens the first of these three: the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, spanning fascicles 21 through the first half of 34, structured around four "yogic processes." The first (fascicles 21–25) covers three grounds — spiritual lineage, entry onto the path, and setting out toward liberation — and gives, in great detail, the fourteen kinds of provisions, mundane and supramundane, that a hearer-disciple needs to gather. The second (fascicles 26–29) classifies seventeen categories of practitioner-related topics. The third (fascicles 30–32) lays out the actual sequence and skillful means by which a teacher guides a student through calm-abiding and clear-seeing. The fourth (fascicles 33–34) gives the detailed marks of practice itself, both mundane and supramundane — in effect, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, an extended, practitioner-focused unpacking of exactly the mundane and supramundane purity that fascicle 20 already outlined in brief. Fascicle 21 begins this entire arc with its first ground: the Ground of Spiritual Lineage, asking what it actually means for a being to already carry the capacity for awakening, whether or not that capacity has yet been activated.

2. The Ground of Spiritual Lineage Opens: What Is "Lineage"?

Before unpacking the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples as a whole, the treatise gives its own summary verse, then turns immediately to its first ground: the Ground of Spiritual Lineage, structured around four questions — what lineage intrinsically is, how it is established, what marks reveal it, and which twenty-three types of persons possess it. The treatise opens with the first of these.

Chinese Source

云何種性地？……謂若種性自性、若種性安立、若住種性者所有諸相、若住種性補特伽羅，如是一切總略為一，名種性地。云何種性？謂住種性補特伽羅，有種子法。由現有故，安住種性補特伽羅，若遇勝緣，便有堪任、便有勢力，於其涅槃能得能證。……問：此種性名有何差別？答：或名種子，或名為界，或名為性，是名差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the Ground of Spiritual Lineage? …Whether it is the intrinsic nature of spiritual lineage, or the establishment of spiritual lineage, or the marks possessed by one who abides in a lineage, or the persons who abide in a lineage — all of this together, in brief, is called the Ground of Spiritual Lineage. What is spiritual lineage? A person who abides in a lineage possesses the dharma of a seed. Because this seed presently exists, a person abiding in this lineage, upon meeting favorable conditions, thereby has the capacity, thereby has the power, to attain and to realize nirvāṇa. …Question: What distinctions exist among the names for this lineage? Answer: It is called, variously, a seed, a realm, or a nature — these are the distinctions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first walks through the ground's own outline verse: lineage can be approached through its intrinsic nature, its manner of establishment (coarse or subtle, singular or manifold, and how favorable or unfavorable conditions affect it), the marks that reveal a person actually possesses it, and finally the twenty-three types of beings who possess it, described individually. Turning to intrinsic nature itself, he explains that a person with the hearer's spiritual lineage already carries, right now, a seed capable of yielding liberation — not a seed that will exist someday, but one genuinely present already, dormant until favorable conditions activate it, at which point that person gains the capacity and strength actually to attain and realize nirvāṇa. Two kinds of condition matter here: the "superior condition" of encountering the true Dharma and applying correct attention to it, and the "lesser condition" of gathering the fourteen kinds of provisions, mundane and supramundane, that this whole first yogic process will go on to detail. The three names for this capacity — "seed," evoking a seed's power to eventually flower and bear fruit; "realm" (dhātu, meaning cause or category — this class of being carries the specific cause that will one day ripen into nirvāṇa's fruit); and "nature" (the intrinsic character that will eventually manifest its own distinctive marks and functions) — all point to the same underlying reality, simply named from different angles. Master Cháng Bǎi notes in passing that the same logic, applied to the other two vehicles, yields the solitary-realizer's lineage (contemplating the aggregates' dependent origination and practicing detachment) and the buddha-lineage that bodhisattvas cultivate (realizing the intrinsic emptiness and quiescence of all phenomena) — all three are, in this sense, "noble" lineages, though this ground concerns the hearer's lineage specifically.

3. The Essence of Lineage: A Seed Dormant in the Mind-Root Since Beginningless Time

Having named lineage and its synonyms, the treatise asks a sharper question: what, exactly, is this seed's essence, and where does it reside? The answer carries real doctrinal weight — this treatise holds that spiritual lineage is innate, not something acquired through habituation, a position it explicitly distinguishes from the view found in the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*.

Chinese Source

問：今此種性以何為體？答：附在所依，有如是相，六處所攝。從無始世展轉傳來，法爾所得。於此立有差別之名，所謂種性、種子、界、性。是名種性。……問：如是種性，當言墮一相續？墮多相續？答：當言墮一相續。……如是種子，非於六處有別異相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: What is the essence of this spiritual lineage? Answer: It is attached to its underlying basis, possessing marks such as these, comprised within the six sense-fields. It has come down, transmitted from one moment to the next, since beginningless time — it is obtained simply as things naturally are. Various distinguishing names are established for it: spiritual lineage, seed, realm, nature. This is called spiritual lineage. …Question: Should this lineage be said to belong to a single continuum, or to belong to many continua? Answer: It should be said to belong to a single continuum. …This seed does not possess distinct, differentiated marks across the six sense-fields.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as staking out a genuine doctrinal position. The seed's "underlying basis" is the ālaya-vijñāna, the storehouse consciousness — using Mahāyāna vocabulary here even though this is nominally hearer-vehicle material, since he takes the Yogācāra framework to apply throughout. He is careful to flag a real disagreement with the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, which holds that this liberating seed arises freshly through habituation — specifically, through correctly hearing the Dharma and letting that hearing imprint the storehouse consciousness, "mixing with it like water mixing with milk." This treatise takes the opposite position: the seed is innate, present since beginningless time, not manufactured by later habituation — if it could simply be acquired through hearing, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, there would be no reason to distinguish beings who possess this lineage from beings who lack it at all, yet the treatise insists that distinction is real, since experienced teachers can see plainly that some students are capable of awakening and others, at least for now, are not.

He then works through where, precisely, this seed resides. Among the six sense-faculties, it cannot be lodged in the five sense-based faculties (eye through body), since not every realm of rebirth possesses all five — some heavens retain only a functioning mind-faculty, and the formless realms have no physical faculties at all. Only the mind-faculty (manas) is guaranteed to accompany a being through every possible rebirth, in every realm, without exception — hell, animal, hungry ghost, human, or god. The seed, therefore, "belongs to a single continuum": it is not scattered variously across the six faculties the way an ordinary mental event is, but lodged singly in the mind-root itself, carried forward, unbroken, through every rebirth a being with this lineage undergoes, however long that chain of lives may run, and however different one rebirth's circumstances are from the next.

4. Establishing Lineage: Subtle Before the Fruit, Coarse Once It Ripens

Turning from lineage's essence to how it is "established," the treatise's first question is deceptively simple: is this seed subtle or coarse? The answer tracks a single distinction — whether the seed has already ripened into its fruit or not — that the treatise elsewhere ties to a broader, two-part account of how any spiritual lineage comes to be.

Chinese Source

云何種性安立？謂應問言：今此種性，為當言細？為當言麤？應答言：細。何以故？由此種子未能與果、未習成果，故名為細。若已與果、已習成果，爾時種性若種、若果，俱說名麤。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the establishment of spiritual lineage? One should ask: should this lineage be called subtle, or should it be called coarse? One should answer: subtle. Why is this? Because this seed has not yet yielded its fruit, has not yet been cultivated to fruition, it is called subtle. If it has already yielded its fruit, has already been cultivated to fruition, then at that time both the lineage-as-seed and the lineage-as-fruit are called coarse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this directly to a distinction the treatise draws more fully in the Bodhisattva Ground: lineage comes in two forms, an "innately abiding lineage" (本性住種性) — the seed simply present from the start, prior to any cultivation, exactly what this ground has been describing — and an "acquired lineage" (習所成種性), built up through sustained practice, repeatedly cultivating renunciation and walking the path in earnest. Before the fruit has ripened — while a practitioner is still gathering the provisions for the path, the innately-abiding seed not yet activated — that seed remains genuinely hard to detect, subtle in the precise sense that its presence cannot yet be confirmed by observation. Once it has ripened, whether partially or all the way to arhatship, both the seed that made this possible and the fruit it produced become plainly evident, and so both are called coarse. Subtlety and coarseness, in other words, track nothing more mysterious than whether the path's outcome has become visible yet.

5. A Seed in Name Only: Not Found by Dissection, Known by Its Fruit

Before moving on, the treatise's commentarial tradition pauses to guard against a natural misunderstanding: calling this capacity a "seed" does not mean some literal object could be located anywhere in the body if searched for closely enough. The name is functional, assigned from effect back to cause, the way any capacity is recognized — by what it produces, not by dissection.

Chinese Source

即於如是種類、分位、六處殊勝，從無始世展轉傳來，法爾所得，有如是想及以言說，謂為種性、種子、界、性，是故當言墮一相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is precisely with regard to this kind of being, this stage of abiding in lineage, this excellence belonging to the six sense-fields — transmitted from one moment to the next since beginningless time, obtained simply as things naturally are — that such a notion and such language arise: calling it spiritual lineage, seed, realm, or nature. It should therefore be said to belong to a single continuum.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* here to make an important qualification: the "seed" is not something that could be found by analyzing the six sense-faculties down to some discoverable object — that would wrongly identify the seed with the faculties themselves — nor is it something existing somewhere entirely apart from them, off in some other location — that would wrongly separate the seed from the faculties altogether. Rather, the name "seed" is simply assigned to the faculties themselves, precisely insofar as they display this particular capacity, arise this particular way, and are arranged this particular way in the storehouse consciousness. He offers an everyday illustration: if someone plays music beautifully, people say this person has a "gift for music" — but cutting the body open would never locate any physical object called a musical gift; the name is inferred entirely from the visible capacity itself. The same logic applies, he notes, to karma and rebirth generally — citing the well-known story behind the Emperor Liang Repentance Liturgy, in which Empress Chi's jealousy and cruelty toward the palace's other consorts were said to have led to her rebirth as a giant python, a fate later relieved through ten eminent monks' repentance rites performed on her behalf. In every such case, the "seed" is named backward from its fruit, never verified by dissection — and the same holds for spiritual lineage: a person is recognized as possessing it not by any inspection of the body, but by the visible fact that, given the Three Jewels and correct effort, the fruit of stream-entry through arhatship actually follows.

6. Four Obstacles That Keep a Genuine Seed From Bearing Fruit

Having established that the seed for liberation is real and innate, the treatise turns to an uncomfortable question: if it is genuinely present, why do so many beings who carry it remain endlessly cycling through birth and death instead of attaining nirvāṇa? Four obstructing conditions answer this, the first of which — being born where there is simply no opportunity to practice at all — receives the most extended treatment.

Chinese Source

問：若住種性補特伽羅有涅槃法，此住種性有涅槃法補特伽羅，何因緣故有涅槃法，而前際來長時流轉，不般涅槃？答：四因緣故，不般涅槃。何等為四？一、生無暇故；二、放逸過故；三、邪解行故；四、有障過故。云何生無暇？謂如有一，生於邊國及以達須、蔑戾車中，四眾、賢良、正至、善士不往遊涉。是名生無暇。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: If a person abiding in lineage possesses the dharma of nirvāṇa, why is it that this person, possessing the dharma of nirvāṇa, has nonetheless been cycling through a long span of time since the beginningless past, and has not yet attained nirvāṇa? Answer: Because of four causes and conditions, nirvāṇa is not attained. What are the four? Birth without leisure; the fault of heedlessness; wrongly-oriented conduct; and the fault of being obstructed. What is birth without leisure? It is like one who is born in a border region, or among the Daśyu or Mleccha peoples — places where the fourfold assembly, the virtuous, those who have arrived at the truth, and good persons do not travel or pass through. This is called birth without leisure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the underlying puzzle sharply: many people who hear the Mahāyāna teachings may well carry the seed for supreme awakening, or the seed of a hearer or solitary-realizer, and yet remain ordinary beings cycling through birth and death — precisely because one or more of these four obstacles is operating. "Birth without leisure" — the first and most basic — means being born into circumstances where practice simply is not available: a border land or a people (the Daśyu and Mleccha, roughly meaning debased or barbarian castes in the treatise's own idiom) that no monk, nun, layman, laywoman, virtuous practitioner, noble one, or morally upright teacher ever visits, so that the Dharma never reaches that person at all, however capable they might otherwise be.

He connects this to the well-known Tibetan formulation of the "eight unleisured states and ten endowments" that make a human rebirth genuinely precious for practice. The eight states lacking leisure are: the hells, tormented by unceasing suffering; the hungry ghosts, consumed by hunger and thirst; animals, too confused to grasp the Dharma's meaning; long-lived gods absorbed in meditative states with no interest in the Dharma; border regions where people are too absorbed in pleasure to be taught; those trapped in wrong views who reject the Three Jewels and the reality of cause and effect; those born when no buddha has appeared, or after a buddha's teaching has already vanished; and those born with such severe karmic obstruction that even encountering a buddha, their faculties are too impaired — mute, deaf, or otherwise incapacitated — to receive the teaching. The ten endowments divide into five a practitioner must supply personally (human rebirth, birth where the Dharma flourishes, intact faculties, no history of the five gravest transgressions, genuine faith in the Three Jewels) and five that must be supplied by circumstance (a buddha having appeared, having turned the wheel of the Dharma, that teaching still surviving in the world, a path genuinely open to practice regardless of class or background, and the presence of a qualified teacher). Master Cháng Bǎi notes these ten map closely onto the treatise's own account of the "ten kinds of self- and other-perfection" that make practice possible — precious conditions this lineage-bearing person, for now, simply lacks.

7. Two More Obstacles: Heedless Comfort, and Wrong Views That Block Every Teacher

The second and third obstacles show that lacking access to the Dharma is not the only way a genuine seed can lie dormant. A person can have every outward opportunity to practice and still be stopped — by the numbing comfort of wealth, or by wrong views so entrenched they make a genuine teacher impossible to find.

Chinese Source

云何放逸過？謂如有一，雖生中國……而生貴家，財寶具足，於諸妙欲耽著受用，不見過患，不知出離。是名放逸過。云何邪解行？謂如有一，雖生中國，乃至廣說；而有外道種種惡見。謂起如是見，立如是論：無有施與，廣說乃至我自了知無諸後有。復由如是外道見故，不值諸佛出現世間，無諸善友說正法者。是名邪解行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the fault of heedlessness? It is like one who, though born in a central land [where the Dharma is present] …is born into a wealthy family, possessing an abundance of riches, indulging attachment to fine sensory pleasures, seeing no fault in them, and knowing nothing of renouncing them. This is called the fault of heedlessness. What is wrongly-oriented conduct? It is like one who, though born in a central land, and so on as stated above, holds various wrong views of the non-Buddhist teachers. Such a one gives rise to views and establishes teachings such as: there is no such thing as giving — and so on at length, down to — I myself know that there is no further existence. Further, because of holding such non-Buddhist views, this person never encounters a buddha appearing in the world, nor any good friend who teaches the true Dharma. This is called wrongly-oriented conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second obstacle as a warning about wealth itself: a person born where the Dharma is fully present, surrounded by monks, nuns, and accomplished teachers, can still fail to practice simply because ease and abundance never force the question. Immersed in fine food, entertainment, and sensory comfort, such a person never sees anything wrong with that immersion and never feels any pull toward renunciation — the old saying "it is hard to seek the path from within wealth and rank" applies exactly here: when good fortune outpaces wisdom, fortune itself becomes the obstacle.

The third obstacle, wrongly-oriented conduct, is entrenched wrong view — the same nihilistic teachings the treatise catalogued at length several fascicles earlier under the sixteen non-Buddhist doctrines: denying that giving yields any merit, denying reverence toward parents or ancestors accomplishes anything, denying that virtuous and unwholesome deeds ripen into any result at all, holding that death is simply extinction and nothing survives it. Master Cháng Bǎi's sharper point is the compounding consequence: someone this entrenched in wrong view will not merely fail to seek out a genuine teacher — that person will not even recognize a buddha or a genuine teacher for what they are, should one actually appear. He lists the eight qualities the treatise elsewhere requires of a genuinely qualified teacher — upholding moral discipline, extensive learning, having actually realized what is taught, deep compassion, tireless patience with repeated instruction, genuine forbearance, freedom from fear when teaching before others, and complete, well-proportioned speech — and notes how rare it is, in any era, for a suitable student and a teacher meeting all eight to actually find one another; the surest way to increase the odds, he adds, is simply to focus on what is admirable in whoever one already keeps company with, since everyone's strengths and blind spots differ and mutual learning runs in both directions.

8. The Fourth Obstacle: Three Kinds of Genuine Obstruction, and the Monk Who Could Not Be Taught

The fourth and final obstacle covers cases where none of the first three apply — favorable birth, no heedless indulgence, no entrenched wrong view — and yet the path still cannot open, because of a deeper obstruction in one's very constitution, one's past karma, or the sheer force of present affliction.

Chinese Source

云何有障過？謂如有一，雖生中國……而性愚鈍，頑騃無知，又復瘖瘂，以手代言，無力能了善說、惡說所有法義。或復造作諸無間業。或復長時起諸煩惱。是名有障過。如是名為四種因緣。由此因緣故，雖有般涅槃法，而不般涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the fault of being obstructed? It is like one who, though born in a central land …is by nature dull-witted, stupid and ignorant, or further, mute — communicating only by gesture — lacking the capacity to understand the meaning of any teaching, well-spoken or ill-spoken. Or again, one who has committed one of the acts of immediate retribution. Or again, one who has, for a long time, given rise to the afflictions. This is called the fault of being obstructed. These, then, are the four causes and conditions. Because of these causes and conditions, even though the dharma for attaining nirvāṇa is present, nirvāṇa is not attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three distinct sub-obstacles here. The "ripening obstruction" covers both being born where the Dharma cannot reach at all (the very first obstacle, seen from a different angle) and being born where the Dharma is present but one's own faculties are impaired — deafness, muteness, blindness, or intellectual disability severe enough to prevent distinguishing genuine teaching from its counterfeit. The "karmic obstruction" covers the five acts of immediate retribution — killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, wounding a buddha, and causing schism in the monastic community — together with a range of comparably grave acts the treatise treats as their equivalents: violating a nun who has attained arhatship or committing incest with one's mother (equivalent to killing an arhat or killing one's mother); harming a bodhisattva in their final life before buddhahood (equivalent to wounding a buddha); publicly promoting animal sacrifice as an enduring practice, or betraying those who have entrusted themselves to one's care, or first rescuing the destitute only to later turn and harm them (all treated as equivalent to killing one's father); and seizing monastic property or destroying a shrine that would otherwise lead beings toward liberation (equivalent to causing schism, or to wounding a buddha). Of the five, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, causing schism in the community is considered gravest of all, since it deprives every being who might otherwise have taken refuge there of a functioning spiritual home, blocks the entire community's teaching function, and can even disturb the unseen beings — gods and nāgas among them — who are said to listen invisibly whenever the Dharma is taught. Whoever commits any of these acts, or their equivalents, cannot attain the noble path in this very life, whatever practice they might otherwise attempt — Master Cháng Bǎi tells of a monk guilty of matricide who, despite genuine effort and outwardly impeccable discipline, was turned away by the arhat Upagupta, renowned for guiding others to realization, the moment Upagupta's meditative insight revealed this obstruction. The "affliction obstruction," finally, is simply affliction so intense and so persistent that even sustained contemplative practice cannot purify it — a person genuinely present in the Dharma, yet leaving, as the saying goes, a mountain of treasure empty-handed.

With all four obstacles now named — birth without leisure, heedless comfort, wrong view, and genuine obstruction — the treatise closes this sub-topic plainly: a being can carry the authentic seed for liberation and still remain, for now, an ordinary being cycling through birth and death, precisely because one or more of these four conditions stands in the way.

9. When the Obstacles Lift — and the Beings for Whom They Never Will

Having explained why a lineage-bearer might remain unliberated, the treatise now states the positive case plainly, then turns to a much starker class of being: those who carry no seed for liberation at all, for whom no combination of favorable conditions, however complete, will ever be enough.

Chinese Source

彼若值遇諸佛出世，聽聞正法，獲得隨順教授教誡，無彼因緣，爾時方能善根成熟，漸次乃至得般涅槃。……無涅槃法補特伽羅，住決定聚。彼若遇緣、若不遇緣，遍一切種，畢竟不能得般涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

If that person encounters a buddha appearing in the world, hears the true Dharma, and receives instruction and admonition in accordance with it — free of those obstructing causes and conditions — then, at that time, the roots of goodness are able to ripen, and one gradually proceeds, in time, to the attainment of nirvāṇa. …A person who lacks the dharma of nirvāṇa abides in the fixed group. Whether or not such a person encounters favorable conditions, in every possible circumstance, that person is ultimately unable to attain nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the positive case as simply the mirror image of the four obstacles already covered: given genuine access to a buddha or a genuine teacher, correct hearing, and freedom from the four obstructions, a lineage-bearer's faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom gradually ripen and, in time, nirvāṇa is reached.

The treatise then turns to a harder case: beings who possess no lineage for liberation whatsoever — the icchantika, as later tradition calls them — who abide, as the text puts it, in a "fixed group," meaning their trajectory is fixed toward continued rebirth no matter what. Whether or not such a being ever encounters a buddha, hears true teaching, or receives instruction makes no ultimate difference; at most, favorable conditions might help such a being avoid the lower realms and enjoy human or heavenly rebirth for a time, but genuine liberation remains permanently out of reach. Master Cháng Bǎi flags, without resolving in this passage, the obvious tension this raises against Mahāyāna sūtras that teach all beings without exception possess buddha-nature — a tension this treatise's own tradition addresses at length elsewhere (in the tathāgatagarbha-versus-lineage literature), but which this fascicle simply leaves standing as its own honest acknowledgment of two different doctrinal strands within the wider canon.

10. Favorable Conditions Begin: Superior and Lesser, and the First of Twelve

Having finished the violating conditions, the treatise turns at last to the favorable conditions themselves — first naming two broad categories, superior and lesser, then opening the twelve-fold list of lesser conditions with the first: simply being born human, with the body actually needed to practice at all.

Chinese Source

何等名為涅槃法緣……答：有二種緣：一、勝，二、劣。云何勝緣？謂正法增上他音，及內如理作意。云何劣緣？謂此劣緣乃有多種，謂若自圓滿、若他圓滿……云何自圓滿？謂善得人身，生於聖處，諸根無闕，勝處淨信，離諸業障。云何名為善得人身？謂如有一，生人同分，得丈夫身，男根成就，或得女身。如是名為善得人身。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the condition for the dharma of nirvāṇa? …Answer: There are two kinds of condition: superior, and lesser. What is the superior condition? It is the excellent sound of the true Dharma from another, together with correct attention arising within. What is the lesser condition? This lesser condition is, in fact, of many kinds — namely, one's own perfection, the perfection of others, and so on. …What is one's own perfection? It is: a good human birth, birth in a noble place, unimpaired faculties, pure faith regarding what is excellent, and freedom from karmic obstructions. What is called a good human birth? It is like one who is born within the human category, obtaining a man's body with the male faculty complete, or obtaining a woman's body. This is called a good human birth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi likens the superior condition to a meal's staple food — hearing the true Dharma from a qualified teacher, and turning that hearing into genuine, correctly-oriented inward reflection — while the twelve lesser conditions are like the accompanying dishes: individually secondary, but together indispensable for a balanced, workable practice. The twelve are: one's own perfection, others' perfection, a genuine wish for the good, proper renunciation, moral discipline, guarding the senses, moderation in eating, diligent wakefulness through the first and last watches of the night, clear awareness in all activity, delight in solitude, purifying the five hindrances, and resting on concentration — twelve conditions the treatise will now unpack one at a time.

The first, one's own perfection, itself has five parts, the first of which is simply obtaining a human body at all — full stop, whether male or female, since both are equally capable of walking the path. Master Cháng Bǎi explains why human birth specifically is singled out over any of the six realms: the desire-realm heavens are too pleasurable to motivate practice, the form and formless heavens' deep absorption breeds a subtle contempt for the coarser desire-realm and offers only pleasant feeling with no admixture of pain to spur renunciation, while the human realm's mixture of pleasure and pain makes genuine practice possible at all. He cites Venerable Yin Shun's observation that human beings possess three distinctive excellences unmatched by any other realm: superior memory, capable of retaining what is learned in real depth; superior courage, capable of sustained diligence once the resolve to practice actually arises; and superior moral capacity, the self-restraint needed to renounce desire — three qualities that, taken together, make the human realm the one place in all of saṃsāra where the path can genuinely be walked.

11. Self-Perfection Completes: Sound Faculties, Genuine Faith, and Freedom From the Gravest Karma

The remaining four marks of self-perfection round out the picture: a body and mind actually capable of practice, a settled faith in the Three Jewels specifically rather than in any lesser object, and freedom from the five gravest possible transgressions — closing this first of the twelve favorable conditions in full.

Chinese Source

云何名為諸根無闕？謂如有一，性不愚鈍，亦不頑騃，又不瘖瘂，乃至廣說支節無減……云何名為勝處淨信？謂如有一，於諸如來正覺所說法毗奈耶，得淨信心，如是名為勝處淨信……云何名為離諸業障？謂能遠離五無間業，所謂於彼害母、害父、害阿羅漢、破和合僧、於如來所惡心出血……唯由如是五種支分，自體圓滿，是故說此名自圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called unimpaired faculties? It is like one who is by nature neither dull-witted, nor stupid, nor mute — and so on at length, down to being free of any missing limb. …What is called pure faith regarding what is excellent? It is like one who attains a pure faith in the Dharma and discipline taught by the fully awakened tathāgatas. This is called pure faith regarding what is excellent. …What is called freedom from karmic obstructions? It is the ability to avoid the five acts of immediate retribution — namely, killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing an arhat, causing schism in the harmonious community, and, with malicious intent, drawing a tathāgata's blood. …It is solely by means of these five components that one's own being is perfected — this, therefore, is called one's own perfection.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats unimpaired faculties as covering both mind and body: a mind capable of telling correct teaching from wrong teaching, and a body — ears especially, since hearing the Dharma comes first, but eyes and limbs as well — capable of actually receiving, reading, and practicing it. Pure faith regarding what is excellent narrows specifically to the Three Jewels: not faith in local deities or spirits, however sincerely held, but a settled, reasoned confidence in the Buddha's teaching and discipline specifically, since only that teaching reliably produces the wholesome qualities, mundane and supramundane, capable of purifying the mind's confusion.

To illustrate how thoroughly such faith and practice can transform even a resistant mind, Master Cháng Bǎi tells an extended story from the Thai Forest tradition: a Western monk ordained under Ajahn Chah found himself endlessly irritated by one uncomfortable assignment after another — an unwelcoming monastery, hours of sweeping leaves, sleeping exposed in the forest, humbling chores like washing a disliked senior monk's feet. Ajahn Chah's teaching throughout was the same: comparing the monk to a mangy dog convinced each new garden it moved to was somehow dirtier than the last, when the flies were following the dog's own sore, not the garden. Only in his fifth year of training did the monk recognize that his discomfort had never actually come from the places or people around him, but from his own unexamined mind — a recognition Master Cháng Bǎi holds up as exactly what "pure faith" is meant to produce: not blind acceptance, but a settled trust sturdy enough to let genuine practice actually work on the mind, rather than blaming whatever circumstance happens to be at hand.

Freedom from karmic obstructions returns to the five acts of immediate retribution already named earlier as an obstacle — killing one's father or mother, killing an arhat, causing schism in the monastic community, or maliciously wounding a buddha — restated here from the positive side: a practitioner seeking liberation must never have committed any of these acts, nor their equivalents, in this very life. Master Cháng Bǎi adds a cautionary anecdote of his own, of a woman near the turn of the twentieth century who habitually slandered those who gave generously to monastics, and who, after a fish's tail brushed her eye while she was preparing to kill it, suffered an infection that spread and ultimately took her life — offered as a reminder that even lesser transgressions against the Three Jewels can ripen with real speed. With unimpaired faculties, genuine faith, and freedom from these gravest transgressions now added to a good human birth and birth in a place where the Dharma is present, the treatise declares the first of the twelve favorable conditions — one's own perfection — complete in all five of its parts.

12. The Second Favorable Condition Opens: What Others Must Supply

Self-perfection alone is not enough — a practitioner also needs the world itself to cooperate. The second of the twelve favorable conditions, perfection supplied by others, names five things a practitioner cannot provide alone: a buddha must have appeared, must have taught, and that teaching must still be genuinely available. The treatise opens with the first of these — what it actually means for a buddha to appear in the world.

Chinese Source

云何他圓滿？謂諸佛出世，說正法教，法教久住，法住隨轉，他所哀愍。云何名為諸佛出世？謂如有一，普於一切諸有情類，起善利益增上意樂，修習多千難行苦行，經三大劫阿僧企耶，積集廣大福德、智慧二種資糧，獲得最後上妙之身，安坐無上勝菩提座，斷除五蓋，於四念住善住其心，修三十七菩提分法，現證無上正等菩提。如是名為諸佛出世。過去、未來、現在諸佛，皆由如是名為出世。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is perfection supplied by others? It is: buddhas appearing in the world; the teaching of the true Dharma; the true teaching abiding long in the world; the true teaching continuing to be transmitted; and being shown compassion by others. What is called a buddha appearing in the world? It is like one who, giving rise to a supremely powerful intention to benefit all beings without exception, cultivates many thousands of difficult practices and austerities, and, over three great incalculable eons, gathers the vast twin provisions of merit and wisdom, thereby attaining the final, most excellent body, sitting upon the unsurpassed seat of awakening, cutting off the five hindrances, settling the mind well in the four applications of mindfulness, cultivating the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, and directly realizing unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. This is called a buddha appearing in the world. All buddhas — past, future, and present — are called "appearing in the world" for this same reason.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats a buddha's appearance as the single most important thing others can supply, since without it there is no Dharma to encounter at all: a bodhisattva rooted in bodhicitta, guided by great compassion, undertakes countless difficult practices across three vast incalculable eons, gathers the twin provisions of merit and wisdom to completion, attains the final body a buddha will ever take, and, seated at the seat of awakening, cuts through the five hindrances and directly realizes unsurpassed, complete awakening — a pattern identical, he notes, for every buddha of the past, present, or future, whether the historical Śākyamuni or Maitreya still to come. Having a living buddha in the world is the best possible form of this condition, but even without one physically present, the same purpose is served so long as the Dharma he taught still survives and someone remains able to teach it.

13. Teaching the True Dharma: The Twelvefold Body of Scripture

A buddha's appearance alone accomplishes little if nothing is actually taught. The treatise's second condition covers exactly this — the Buddha, moved by compassion for his disciples, teaching the four noble truths and, more broadly, every form the recorded teaching eventually took, traditionally organized into twelve categories.

Chinese Source

云何名為說正法教？謂即如是諸佛世尊出現於世，哀愍一切諸聲聞故，依四聖諦，宣說真實苦集滅道無量法教。所謂契經、應頌、記別、諷誦、自說、因緣、譬喻、本事、本生、方廣、希法、論議。……諸佛世尊及聖弟子，一切正士皆乘此法而得出離，然後為他宣說稱讚，是故說此名為正法。宣說此故，名正法教。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the teaching of the true Dharma? It is precisely that these buddha-bhagavats, having appeared in the world, out of compassion for all their hearer-disciples, teach — resting on the four noble truths — the true and boundless doctrine of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path. Namely: discourses, verses that restate what has been said, predictions of future attainment, spontaneous verses, unprompted teachings, teachings prompted by an occasion, parables, accounts of disciples' past lives, accounts of the Buddha's own past lives, teachings of vast scope, accounts of wondrous and rare qualities, and structured treatises. …The buddha-bhagavats, their noble disciples, and all persons of genuine integrity, all attain liberation by relying on this very Dharma, and then, for the sake of others, teach and praise it — it is for this reason that it is called the true Dharma. Teaching it is what is meant by "the teaching of the true Dharma."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the twelve traditional categories into which the recorded teaching is organized, later gathered into the three-part canon of discourse, discipline, and treatise. Discourses (sūtra) are teachings suited to both the underlying truth and the listener's capacity; verses that restate prose already given, or clarify a point poetry expresses better than prose, form a second category, while verses standing entirely on their own, with no accompanying prose, form a distinct third. Predictions cover the Buddha foretelling a disciple's future buddhahood — as when Śāriputra or Maitreya were told exactly when and how they would attain full awakening. Unprompted teachings are given with no one having asked — the *Amitābha Sūtra* being the classic example — while teachings prompted by an occasion arise from a specific question, as in the *Diamond Sūtra*, or, in another reading of this same category, cover the disciplinary rules themselves, each formulated only once a specific situation called for it. Parables use story to illustrate a point — Master Cháng Bǎi cites the well-known tale of a man who, tasting a well-seasoned dish and learning salt was the secret, went home and ate a fistful of plain salt expecting the same delight, mistaking the means for the end. Accounts of disciples' own past lives and accounts of the Buddha's own past lives (the jātaka tales — famous animal-rebirth stories included) form two more categories, followed by teachings of vast scope reserved for the bodhisattva path, such as the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s account of the ten bodhisattva grounds; accounts of wondrous, rarely-seen qualities displayed by the Buddha or his fourfold assembly of disciples; and finally structured treatises, which organize the Dharma's principles systematically, often in question-and-answer form. Having appeared, and having taught in these many forms, the Buddha's disciples then carry that same teaching forward to others — which is precisely what earns it the name "true Dharma": not merely correct in the abstract, but a teaching that has actually, demonstrably, led real people to liberation.

14. The Teaching Enduring Long: Genuine Attainment vs. Its Many Counterfeits

A teaching's mere existence is not enough — it must actually keep producing genuine attainment for as long as it endures. The treatise's third condition, the teaching abiding long in the world, turns on exactly this: whether people are still, in fact, reaching the fruits the teaching promises, or whether only a counterfeit resemblance of practice remains.

Chinese Source

云何名為法教久住？謂說正法已，轉法輪已，乃至世尊壽量久住，及涅槃後。經爾所時，正行未滅、正法未隱，如是名為正法久住。……如是久住，當知說彼勝義正法作證道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the teaching abiding long in the world? It is that, once the true Dharma has been taught and the wheel of the Dharma has been turned — whether during the Bhagavat's own lifetime, or after his final nirvāṇa — for however long a span of time, genuine practice has not yet ceased, and the true teaching has not yet vanished: this is called the true Dharma abiding long. …This "abiding long" should be understood as referring to the principle of directly realizing the ultimate true Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "abiding long" as meaning something quite specific: not simply that scriptures still physically exist, but that people are still genuinely attaining the fruits those scriptures describe — stream-entry and beyond for the hearer-path, the corresponding stages for the solitary-realizer and bodhisattva paths. He devotes considerable space to a striking catalogue, drawn from elsewhere in the treatise, of what counterfeit Dharma looks like once genuine attainment starts to fade: on the teaching side, fabricated scriptures falsely attributed to the Buddha, doctrines that quietly inflate or deflate core teachings like impermanence and no-self, mistaken insistence on a real, substantial self, treating provisionally-designated things as ultimately real, and idle speculation about nirvāṇa's nature that the Buddha himself refused to engage in. On the practice side, the counterfeits run even more varied: refusing to see or hear anything at all in a mistaken literalism about "guarding the senses"; praising and correcting no one out of a misapplied evenhandedness; falling silent altogether under a distorted notion of restraint; starving or exposing the body under a misreading of "few desires"; treating consciousness as strictly singular; imagining sense-pleasure poses no obstacle to practice; assuming an arhat simply ceases to exist after death; clinging to bare intellectual faith in the two truths without ever moving into disciplined practice; treating "no thought at all" as equivalent to genuine non-conceptual wisdom; losing oneself in communal busywork instead of practice; treating generosity alone as sufficient for the entire path; treating hearing the Dharma alone as sufficient; discarding moral discipline as unnecessary "adornment"; drifting into worldly pursuits under the banner of "benefiting beings"; mistreating a fellow practitioner who has broken a precept rather than guiding them back; feigning dignified conduct without any real inner cultivation (illustrated with a Tibetan teaching story of a student caught tidying his usually-dusty altar room to impress his visiting teacher, who deliberately re-dirtied it rather than let a moment of vanity pass for genuine devotion); soliciting donations through manipulative display; performing outwardly virtuous acts that conceal real unwholesome intent; and offering teachings that sound profound but carry no real benefit. Whether the true teaching has faded into any of these counterfeits, or genuine attainment still continues, is exactly what determines whether the teaching can be said to be truly "abiding long" — mere survival of the texts is not enough.

He then surveys, more briefly, the treatise's own six-part hermeneutical framework for approaching the entire canon: the gateway of ultimate reality (six varieties, from what is commonly agreed true by ordinary convention up through the non-conceptual suchness realized only in meditation); the gateway of actual attainment (karmic ripening generally, and the distinct attainments of the hearer, solitary-realizer, and bodhisattva paths); the gateway of pedagogical method (how the canon itself is organized — scriptural basket, the treatise's own seventeen-ground framework, and ten further categories of instruction); the gateway of avoiding extremes (exaggeration versus denial, eternalism versus annihilationism, indulgence versus self-mortification); the gateway of the inconceivable (six topics the Buddha declined to speculate about in the abstract — the self, other beings, the nature of the world, the full depth of karmic ripening, the actual experience of deep meditation, and a buddha's own realization — matters to be directly known through practice, never settled by argument); and finally the gateway of underlying intention, sorted into sixteen distinct purposes a given teaching might serve — showing reality plainly, turning students away from craving, urging moral discipline, encouraging effort, celebrating a disciple's success, drawing the uncommitted in, ripening those already committed, pointing toward liberation, resolving doubt, teaching concentration, carrying a deeper meaning beneath the surface statement, producing blameless joy in those who attain, cultivating respect between teacher and student, keeping the Dharma-eye continuously open through new attainment, encouraging wholesome practice broadly, and finally cutting through attachment to any fixed mark or characteristic altogether. Understanding a passage's actual intention, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, matters more than reciting its words — the whole point of studying scripture is to be moved by what it is actually pointing toward.

15. Passing the Torch, and the Compassion of Those Who Provide

Even a teaching that endures needs living hands to carry it forward, one generation to the next. The fourth favorable condition covers exactly this transmission; the fifth, opening here, turns outward — to the compassion of lay supporters, without whose material generosity monastics could not practice at all.

Chinese Source

云何名為法住隨轉？謂即如是證正法者，了知有力能證如是正法眾生，即如所證，隨轉隨順教授教誡。如是名為法住隨轉。云何名為他所哀愍？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the true teaching continuing to be transmitted? It is that those who have thus realized the true Dharma, recognizing beings who have the capacity to likewise realize this true Dharma, transmit it — exactly as they themselves realized it — through instruction and admonition offered in accordance with it. This is called the true teaching continuing to be transmitted. What is called being shown compassion by others?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourth condition as the living link between one generation's attainment and the next: a teacher who has genuinely realized the Dharma must also recognize which students actually have the capacity to realize it too, and pass on exactly what was realized — not a diluted or distorted version — through the same instruction and admonition received in turn. Without this ongoing transmission, even a teaching that has not yet formally vanished would, in practice, stop reaching anyone at all. The fifth condition, being shown compassion by others, now opens: since monastics own no independent means of livelihood, their capacity to practice depends entirely on lay supporters willing to provide food, robes, shelter, and medicine out of genuine compassion — a condition entirely outside a practitioner's own control, yet just as essential as any of the others.

16. Perfection Supplied by Others Completes, and the Third Favorable Condition Opens: A Genuine Wish for the Good

With lay generosity now named, all five marks of the second favorable condition — perfection supplied by others — are complete. The treatise moves to the third of the twelve: a genuine wish for what is wholesome, born from hearing the Dharma and settling into real, reasoned confidence rather than any fleeting emotional impulse.

Chinese Source

他謂施主。彼於行者起哀愍心，惠施隨順淨命資具，所謂如法衣服、飲食、諸坐臥具、病緣醫藥。如是名為他所哀愍。云何善法欲？謂如有一，或從佛所、或弟子所，聞正法已，獲得淨信。得淨信已，應如是學：在家煩擾，若居塵宇，出家閑曠，猶處虛空，是故我今應捨一切妻子眷屬、財穀珍寶，於善說法毗奈耶中，正捨家法，趣於非家。既出家已，勤修正行，令得圓滿。於善法中生如是欲，名善法欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Others" means benefactors. Giving rise to compassion for the practitioner, they bestow the requisites that support a pure livelihood — namely, robes, food, seat and bedding, and medicine for illness, all rightly obtained. This is called being shown compassion by others. What is a genuine wish for the good? It is like one who, having heard the true Dharma from the Buddha or from his disciples, attains pure faith. Having attained this pure faith, one should train thus: household life is troubled and vexing, like dwelling in a dust-filled dwelling; going forth is open and spacious, like abiding in open space. Therefore I should now relinquish my spouse, children, kin, wealth, grain, and treasures, and, within this well-taught Dharma and discipline, rightly relinquish the householder's way and go forth into homelessness. Having thus gone forth, one diligently cultivates correct practice, bringing it to completion. Giving rise to such a wish within what is wholesome is called a genuine wish for the good.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With lay compassion now named, Master Cháng Bǎi tallies "perfection supplied by others" complete in all five parts: a buddha appearing, the true Dharma taught, that teaching abiding through continued genuine attainment, its ongoing transmission from teacher to student, and the material support lay benefactors provide — the full external half of what a practitioner needs, mirroring the five internal marks of self-perfection covered earlier.

He then turns to the third favorable condition, stressing that a genuine wish for renunciation is never a blind emotional reaction or a way of running from difficulty — it arises specifically from hearing true teaching and letting it settle into real, considered confidence. Only then does household life start to look, in the vivid image the text itself uses, like living inside a dust-choked room, cluttered with the entangling cares of spouse, children, and possessions, while renunciation opens out like empty space, unconfined in every direction — "no home, yet everywhere a home." A wish this well-founded, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, does not bend before ordinary discouragement, unlike a resolve merely urged on someone else's persuasion, which typically collapses the moment real hardship appears; a genuine wish for the good, arising from one's own settled understanding, is what can actually carry a person through the difficulties renunciation itself will inevitably bring.

17. Formal Ordination, and the Six Components of Moral Discipline

A genuine wish for renunciation, sustained by real vigor, leads naturally into formal ordination — and ordination in turn calls for moral discipline in the fullest sense: not merely avoiding transgression, but a comprehensive discipline covering conduct, protocol, and even the fear appropriate to the smallest fault.

Chinese Source

云何正出家？謂即由此勝善法欲增上力故，白四羯磨，受具足戒；或受勞策所學尸羅。是名正出家。云何戒律儀？謂彼如是正出家已，安住具戒，堅牢防護別解律儀，軌則、所行皆得圓滿，於微小罪見大怖畏，受學一切所有學處。是名戒律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is proper going-forth? It is that, by the power of this excellent wish for the good, one undergoes the formal act of ordination and receives the full precepts; or receives the training precepts of a novice. This is called proper going-forth. What is moral discipline in its proper restraint? It is that, having thus properly gone forth, one abides in complete discipline, firmly guarding the prātimokṣa restraint, perfecting both protocol and proper conduct, seeing great danger in even the slightest fault, and training in every one of the points of training. This is called moral discipline in its proper restraint.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains proper going-forth simply as the wish for the good actually put into action: undergoing formal ordination — the traditional "one proposal, three confirmations" procedure — to receive full monastic precepts, or, for a novice, the ten training precepts appropriate to that stage.

Moral discipline itself, he explains, unpacks into six components. Abiding in complete discipline means sustained, continuous adherence, with no lapse in either bodily or verbal conduct, to every precept received. Firmly guarding the prātimokṣa restraint means steadfastly protecting the specific vows appropriate to one's own status among the sevenfold assembly — monk, nun, novice, or lay follower — each precept freeing the mind from its own corresponding affliction. Perfected protocol covers the proper form of the four postures (walking, standing, sitting, lying down) and the customary activities of monastic life — communal assemblies, attending on teachers, formal study — each governed by its own established etiquette, followed without either falling short of or exceeding what is proper. Perfected conduct means avoiding five kinds of place a monastic should never visit — the homes of dancers or musicians, brothels, wine-sellers, royal courts, and outcaste households — while still knowing when it is appropriate to visit any other place freely. Seeing great danger in even the slightest fault means genuine dread at the prospect of any transgression, however minor, recognizing that even a small lapse can compound into an inability to attain the noble fruits, rebirth in an unfortunate realm, or lasting damage to one's reputation — precisely the disposition that lets a practitioner hold both major and minor precepts with equal seriousness, since faithfully guarding the small protects the great. And training in every point of training means studying the precepts continuously under a preceptor's guidance, reviewing them at the fortnightly recitation, and discussing them regularly with fellow practitioners, for as long as one lives.

18. Guarding the Senses: Neither Grasping the Whole Nor Its Alluring Details

Moral discipline alone is not sufficient protection — the senses themselves need active guarding in the moment of contact. The treatise illustrates this with the eye, then extends the same principle to every other sense-faculty in turn.

Chinese Source

云何根律儀？謂即依此尸羅律儀，守護正念，修常委念，以念防心，行平等位。眼見色已，而不取相，不取隨好。恐依是處，由不修習眼根律儀防護而住，其心漏泄所有貪憂惡不善法。故即於彼修律儀行，防護眼根，依於眼根修律儀行。如是行者，耳聞聲已、鼻齅香已、舌嘗味已、身覺觸已、意了法已，而不取相，不取隨好……故即於彼修律儀行，防護意根，依於意根修律儀行。是名根律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is restraint of the faculties? It is that, resting on this discipline and restraint, one guards correct mindfulness, cultivates constant and thoroughgoing mindfulness, protects the mind by means of mindfulness, and dwells in equanimity. Having seen a form with the eye, one does not grasp its general mark, nor its alluring details — fearing that, by not cultivating restraint and protection of the eye faculty in this respect, the mind might leak out craving, distress, and other unwholesome states. One therefore cultivates restraint precisely with regard to this, guarding the eye faculty, and practicing restraint in reliance on the eye faculty. In just the same way, having heard a sound with the ear, smelled a scent with the nose, tasted a flavor with the tongue, felt a touch with the body, or cognized a mental object with the mind, one does not grasp its general mark, nor its alluring details. …One therefore cultivates restraint precisely with regard to this, guarding the mind faculty, and practicing restraint in reliance on the mind faculty. This is called restraint of the faculties.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes two levels at which an object can be grasped, both of which sense-restraint guards against: its "general mark" — the coarse overall impression, "this is beautiful," "this is desirable" — and its "alluring details," the finer particulars that draw the mind in even further once the general impression has already taken hold. Genuine restraint means sustained, thoroughgoing mindfulness applied at the very moment of contact, protecting the mind so that it settles into equanimity rather than sliding into either attraction or aversion. He offers a vivid image for what happens without this restraint: an affliction left unguarded is like an untreated sore — the moment the senses touch an alluring object without restraint, craving, distress, or some other unwholesome state seeps out exactly the way infection seeps from an open wound, drawing further trouble the way flies are drawn to a sore, rather than to whatever place the sufferer happens to be standing. Though the eye receives the fullest illustration, Master Cháng Bǎi notes the same restraint applies equally across all six sense-faculties, the mind-faculty above all, since ultimately it is the sixth consciousness that must be kept in check regardless of which of the five physical senses first makes contact.

19. Moderation in Eating, and Wakefulness Through the Watches of the Night

The seventh and eighth favorable conditions turn to two of the most ordinary facts of monastic life — eating and sleeping — and show how even these can either support or undermine practice, depending on the intention and awareness brought to them.

Chinese Source

云何於食知量？謂彼如是守諸根已，以正思擇食於所食。不為倡蕩、不為憍逸、不為飾好、不為端嚴食於所食。然食所食，為身安住、為暫支持、為除饑渴、為攝梵行、為斷故受、為令新受當不更生、為當存養力樂無罪安隱而住。是名於食知量。云何初夜後夜常勤修習悎寤瑜伽？謂彼如是食知量已，於晝日分，經行、宴坐二種威儀，從順障法淨修其心；……過此分已，出住處外，洗濯其足，右脅而臥，重累其足，住光明想、正念、正知、思惟起想。於夜後分，速疾悎寤，經行、宴坐二種威儀，從順障法淨修其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is moderation in eating? It is that, having thus guarded the faculties, one eats with correct discernment. One does not eat for the sake of wanton pleasure, nor for pride and indulgence, nor to enhance one's beauty, nor for physical elegance. Rather, one eats so that the body may be sustained, so as to provide temporary support, so as to remove hunger and thirst, so as to uphold the pure conduct of one's practice, so as to end old feelings [of discomfort], so that new feelings [of discomfort] will not arise, and so as to preserve strength, ease, and a blameless, peaceful abiding. This is called moderation in eating. What is diligently cultivating wakeful yogic practice through the first and last watches of the night? It is that, having thus practiced moderation in eating, during the day, through the two postures of walking meditation and sitting meditation, one purifies the mind of whatever obstructs it; …once this period ends, one goes outside one's dwelling, washes the feet, lies down on the right side, one foot resting upon the other, dwelling in the perception of light, with correct mindfulness, correct awareness, and the intention to rise. In the last watch of the night, one wakes swiftly, and through walking meditation and sitting meditation, purifies the mind of whatever obstructs it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames moderation in eating as avoiding two extremes at once: eating is never for indulgence, pride, or physical vanity, yet it is also never refused out of excessive austerity, since the body genuinely needs sustaining. Eight specific, sober reasons justify each meal: sustaining the body, providing temporary support, removing hunger and thirst, upholding celibate practice, ending existing discomfort, preventing new discomfort from arising, and preserving the strength and ease needed to continue practicing at all — reflections traditionally condensed into the "five contemplations before eating" many monastics still recite.

Wakeful practice through the night divides the twenty-four hours into three watches: the first (roughly six to nine in the evening) and the last (three to six in the morning) are both given to walking and sitting meditation, purifying the mind of the five hindrances, while the middle watch allows genuine rest — lying on the right side, one foot resting on the other in the Buddha's own traditional posture, the mind dwelling in a perception of light (visualizing sun, moon, starlight, or reciting a mantra or a buddha's name as a kind of inner light), sustained mindfulness and clear awareness held even into sleep, and — notably — a deliberate intention set before sleeping to wake at a specific hour, which Master Cháng Bǎi notes the storehouse consciousness itself reliably registers and acts on, waking the practitioner close to the intended time even without an external signal.

20. Clear Awareness in Every Activity, and the Delight of Genuine Solitude

The ninth favorable condition extends mindful awareness across literally every ordinary action of a monastic's day, down to the smallest gesture; the tenth turns outward, to the physical solitude — genuinely austere dwelling places — that supports uninterrupted practice.

Chinese Source

云何正知而住？謂彼如是常勤修習悎寤瑜伽已，若往、若來正知而住，若覩、若瞻正知而住，若屈、若伸正知而住，持僧伽胝及以衣鉢正知而住，若食、若飲、若噉、若嘗正知而住，若行、若住、若坐、若臥正知而住，於悎寤時正知而住，若語、若默正知而住，於解勞睡時正知而住。是名正知而住。云何樂遠離？謂由如是所修善法，無倒修治初業地已，遠離一切臥具貪著，住阿練若、樹下、空室、山谷、峰穴、草𧂐、迥露、塚間、林藪、虛曠平野、邊際臥具。是名樂遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is clear awareness in activity? It is that, having thus diligently practiced wakeful yogic discipline, one maintains clear awareness whether going or coming, whether looking afar or looking near, whether bending or extending a limb, while carrying the great robe, bowl, and other robes, while eating, drinking, chewing, or tasting, whether walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, while awake, whether speaking or silent, and even while resting from fatigue. This is called clear awareness in activity. What is delight in solitude? It is that, by means of the wholesome states thus cultivated, having rightly trained the foundational stage of practice without error, one relinquishes all attachment to comfortable bedding, and dwells in a forest retreat, beneath a tree, in an empty room, a mountain valley, a mountain cave, a heap of grass, an open clearing, a charnel ground, a thicket, or a vast open plain, using only the barest bedding. This is called delight in solitude.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats clear awareness as extending the same discipline already cultivated in sense-restraint into every remaining detail of ordinary life — walking, glancing near or far, bending a limb, wearing the robes correctly, eating, drinking, chewing, moving through the four postures, speaking or staying silent, even resting. He tells a compact, self-deprecating story from a Western monk's memoir: craving sugar during a strict single-meal-a-day practice, the monk secretly arranged for a large shipment of palm sugar, was caught out gently by his own teacher, shared most of it with the community as instructed, but kept back enough for himself that he made himself sick eating it — and spent that whole night going door to door in the middle of the night, giving away the rest so he wouldn't be tempted again. Master Cháng Bǎi offers the story as a plain illustration of what happens the moment clear awareness lapses even around something as ordinary as a snack.

Delight in solitude, the tenth condition, means genuinely renouncing comfortable lodging in favor of the barest possible dwelling — forest, tree-root, empty room, mountain cave, charnel ground, open plain — since a mind not yet stabilized in practice benefits from removing every unnecessary comfort that might otherwise distract it. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this training is harder than it sounds, and tells of Ajahn Chah being sent, in his own training years, to sit through the night in a charnel ground, terrified by every sound in the dark, imagining ghosts approaching with each footstep — until, forcing himself to open his eyes at what he was certain was an approaching threat, he found only a stray dog, and realized the entire fear had been his own mind's fabrication; he chose to remain and sit through until dawn. The point, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is not that austere dwelling is inherently sacred, but that stripping away comfort and company exposes exactly the kind of unexamined fear and craving that ordinary, comfortable life lets a practitioner avoid confronting.

21. Purifying the Five Hindrances: The Eleventh Favorable Condition

With bare, solitary dwelling now established, the treatise turns to the actual work that solitude makes possible: purifying the mind of the five hindrances that block concentration, using the mind's own most persistent hindrance — craving, especially sexual craving — as the primary target.

Chinese Source

云何清淨諸蓋？謂彼如是住阿練若，或復樹下，或空室等，於五種蓋淨修其心。所謂貪欲、瞋恚、惛沈睡眠、掉舉惡作，及以疑蓋。從彼諸蓋淨修心已，心離諸蓋，安住賢善勝三摩地。如是名為清淨諸蓋。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purifying the hindrances? It is that, dwelling thus in a forest retreat, beneath a tree, in an empty room, or the like, one purifies the mind of the five hindrances — namely, sensual craving, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt. Having purified the mind of these hindrances, the mind, free of the hindrances, abides in an excellent and wholesome concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the standard five hindrances — craving, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and regret, and doubt about the value of practice itself — as the specific obstacles that prevent access concentration from ever forming. The classic remedy the treatise names here is meditation on the body's unattractiveness (visualizing a corpse's stages of decay: discoloration, swelling, decomposition, scattered bones), traditionally called "excellent" precisely because sensual craving, and above all sexual craving, is considered the single strongest of all afflictions, and this practice targets it directly. Master Cháng Bǎi cautions, though, that this particular method is not universally suitable — practiced to excess, it can tip an already melancholic temperament into genuine despair, even suicidal feelings, which is exactly why the Buddha also taught breath-meditation as a gentler alternative for practitioners prone to that kind of discouragement. Whichever method fits the individual, purifying even the subtlest trace of these five hindrances is what finally opens the door to the first absorption itself.
