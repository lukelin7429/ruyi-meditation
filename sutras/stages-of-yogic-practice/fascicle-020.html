Fascicle 20 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十 · A New Ground Opens

The Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation Begins

Fascicle 20 opens with the moment this treatise has been building toward since fascicle 17: the Ground of What Is Attained Through Reflection (思所成地) formally closes, and the twelfth of the seventeen grounds — the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation (修所成地) — begins, laying out the four-part, seven-branch framework that structures the whole of a practitioner's path from a genuinely favorable rebirth to complete liberation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 20 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. A Ground Transition at Last: The Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation Opens

After three full fascicles spent inside the Ground of What Is Attained Through Reflection (思所成地) — the essential-body-verse cycle that ran from fascicle 17 through the whole of fascicle 19 — the treatise finally turns the page. Fascicle 20 opens with the single formulaic question that has marked every ground-transition across this entire translation project, and lays out, in a compact four-part and sevenfold-branch framework, the complete shape of the path this new, twelfth ground will spend the rest of the treatise unpacking.

Chinese Source

本地分中修所成地第十二
已說思所成地。云何修所成地？

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the twelfth ground: the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation.
The Ground of What Is Attained Through Reflection has now been explained. What, then, is the Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this twelfth ground as the treatise's own synthesis of everything the much longer "Ground of the Hearer" (聲聞地), several fascicles away, will later spell out in exhaustive detail — a compressed map of the entire journey from an ordinary, still-unawakened life to full liberation, useful to any of the three vehicles, since even a bodhisattva aiming at buddhahood must still pass through genuine hearing, reflection, and cultivation, and must still become thoroughly familiar with the path a hearer-disciple walks. He introduces the ground's own governing structure immediately: four broad areas — the basis for cultivation (修處所, the practitioner's own body and the external conditions needed to practice at all), the causes and conditions for cultivation (修因緣, the specific means by which mundane and supramundane paths actually get established), the actual practice of yogic cultivation (修瑜伽, calm-abiding and insight themselves, and how affliction is countered along the way), and the fruit of cultivation (修果, the mundane and supramundane accomplishments that result) — which expand into seven more specific branches: a genuinely favorable rebirth-basis; genuinely complete access to hearing the true teaching; nirvāṇa held as the governing aim; the ripening of the wisdom that matures into liberation; the actual cultivation of remedies against affliction; complete purity of every kind achieved within mundane practice; and complete purity of every kind achieved beyond it.

He maps the four onto the seven directly: the basis for cultivation corresponds to the first branch alone (a favorable rebirth); the causes and conditions for cultivation expand into the next three branches (hearing the teaching fully, holding nirvāṇa as the governing aim, and the ripening of liberating wisdom); the actual practice of cultivation corresponds to the fifth branch (cultivating remedies against affliction); and the fruit of cultivation expands into the final two branches — mundane purity (the full ladder of meditative absorption, form and formless alike) and supramundane purity (for a hearer-disciple, the four fruits culminating in arhatship; for a bodhisattva, the ten grounds culminating in buddhahood). He closes this opening framework by noting, almost as an aside, that these four areas and seven branches between them contain the entire content of the Buddhist teaching, distilled into ten still-broader categories he traces back to the Ground of What Is Attained Through Hearing — what can be cultivated, what should be cultivated, what carries genuine danger, what is defiled, what obstructs the path, what supports it, what belongs to ultimate reality itself, what belongs to special attainments beyond ordinary liberation, what conforms to worldly life without contradiction, and what marks the path's actual completion — a reminder, before the detailed unpacking even begins, of just how much territory this single ground is about to cover.

2. The First Branch Opens: What Makes a Rebirth Genuinely Favorable

The ground's first branch, "a favorable rebirth-basis," asks what conditions — some belonging to the practitioner's own body and mind, some belonging to their surrounding circumstances — actually make a given life capable of supporting real spiritual practice, rather than merely being a life at all.

Chinese Source

云何生圓滿？當知略有十種。謂依內有五，依外有五，總依內外合有十種。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a favorable rebirth-basis? It should be known that, in brief, there are ten kinds: five belonging to one's own person, and five belonging to external circumstance — ten altogether, internal and external combined.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this opening branch as a deliberately practical starting point: before any discussion of meditation technique or philosophical insight, the treatise first asks whether the very conditions of a given life even permit genuine practice to occur — since not every rebirth, however pleasant or however painful, actually offers a real opportunity. The five internal conditions concern the practitioner's own person directly, and the five external conditions concern the surrounding circumstances that support or obstruct practice regardless of personal merit — together forming a checklist the treatise will now work through one item at a time, the first tool in the ground's larger project of mapping, with real precision, exactly what a life capable of reaching liberation actually requires.

3. The Five Internal Conditions of a Favorable Rebirth

The treatise now works through the five conditions belonging to the practitioner's own body and mind: the right kind of birth, the right place, an intact and capable body, freedom from any truly disqualifying past deed, and freedom from any prior attachment to a mistaken teaching.

Chinese Source

謂眾同分圓滿、處所圓滿、依止圓滿、無業障圓滿、無信解障圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely: a favorable class of birth, a favorable place, a favorable bodily basis, freedom from karmic obstruction, and freedom from obstruction by wrong conviction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why human birth specifically heads this list: hell-beings suffer too constantly to practice at all, desire-realm gods are too pleasurably distracted, and form- and formless-realm gods, however refined, are too prone to spiritual pride and too remote from where buddhas actually teach — only the human realm's balance of pleasure and pain leaves genuine room for practice, ideally (though not exclusively, since a healthy woman's body works equally well) with unimpaired faculties. The second condition, a favorable place, means being born somewhere the fourfold Buddhist community — monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen — is actually present and teaching; he glosses this generously as anywhere with a living Buddhist community, not merely classical India, so that by this definition Taiwan itself counts as such a place, while regions with no such presence at all — however comfortable materially — do not. The third, a favorable bodily basis, means having all six sense faculties intact and a mind capable of actually understanding and evaluating what is taught, distinguishing sound teaching from unsound; he notes that even blindness or deafness needn't be an absolute barrier given modern accommodations like braille, though muteness historically posed a much harder obstacle to real dialogue with a teacher.

The fourth, freedom from karmic obstruction, means never having committed — or induced someone else to commit — any of the five acts of immediate retribution (killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, wounding a buddha, or causing schism in the monastic community); committing even one of these, he stresses, closes off any possibility of reaching even the preparatory stages of the path in that same lifetime, since these five uniquely damage the very capacity to receive the teaching rather than being ordinary karma that sincere repentance can resolve. The fifth, freedom from obstruction by wrong conviction, means never having placed genuine faith in a false deity or a non-Buddhist teaching; Master Cháng Bǎi explains this present freedom as itself the fruit of past causes — sustained, repeated confidence in the Three Jewels cultivated across previous lives, the same insight the Diamond Sūtra points to in saying that anyone able to trust this teaching even briefly has already planted roots of goodness across countless past buddhas, not merely stumbled onto it by chance.

4. The Five External Conditions of a Favorable Rebirth

The remaining five conditions belong not to the practitioner but to their surrounding circumstances: encountering a genuine teacher, having the teaching actually articulated, having living realization of it still being transmitted, having it survive even after the teacher's own passing, and having genuine material support to practice at all.

Chinese Source

謂大師圓滿、世俗正法施設圓滿、勝義正法隨轉圓滿、正行不滅圓滿、隨順資緣圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely: a perfected teacher, the conventional true teaching being fully articulated, the ultimate true teaching still being transmitted through living realization, right practice not yet having died out, and favorable material support.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains a "perfected teacher" as encountering an actual buddha — one whose every word is free of falsehood, worthy of offerings, and whose awakening is genuinely unsurpassed, with unobstructed knowledge and vision reaching every realm, every phenomenon, every era without exception — the single most valuable circumstance available to any being. The "conventional true teaching being fully articulated" means that buddha then actually teaches, in ordinary language, both what should be practiced and what should be avoided, using — as the tradition catalogues it — twelve distinct literary forms across the Buddhist canon: direct discourses, verses that restate a preceding passage, predictions of a disciple's future attainment, verses that stand alone, teachings given unprompted, texts explaining the origin-story behind a monastic rule, teachings built around analogy (he notes the famous example of the Buddha telling Ānanda that splitting a single hair into ten thousand strands and hitting one from a hundred paces is still easier than fully grasping dependent origination), accounts of a disciple's past lives, accounts of the Buddha's own past lives as a bodhisattva, the expansive Mahāyāna discourses, accounts of miraculous or rare events, and doctrinal treatises structured as questions and answers — twelve forms that reduce, in the end, to the three baskets of discourse, discipline, and doctrine.

The third external condition, living transmission of the ultimate teaching, means that beyond merely being taught the words, disciples also find teachers who guide them into actually cultivating the thirty-seven factors of awakening and reaching real attainment — the fruits of the path passed hand to hand, mind to mind, rather than surviving only as text. The fourth, right practice not yet having died out, extends this same transmission past the teacher's own death: even after a buddha's final nirvāṇa, so long as the teaching continues to be accurately preserved and taught without distortion, and so long as at least some practitioners continue to realize it directly and pass that realization on in turn, a genuine opportunity remains open to later generations. And the fifth, favorable material support, means having generous patrons of genuine faith provide the four requisites — robes, food, bedding, and medicine — freely enough that a renunciant can devote themselves to practice without the constant anxiety of material want undermining their resolve; Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that this quiet, unglamorous support is no less essential to the whole structure than any of the other nine conditions, since even the most favorable inner circumstances collapse without it.

5. The First Branch Closes: What "Sagely" Actually Means Here

With all ten conditions named, the treatise closes the ground's first branch by clarifying a term it has been using loosely: "sagely dharma" comes in two grades, one still incomplete and one fully realized — and specifies that this entire ground is built around the second, the arhat's complete and final freedom, not the partial freedom still available to a trainee.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that all ten conditions just described — five internal, five external — together constitute what the treatise calls the "basis for cultivation," the necessary ground on which any of the Buddha's own disciples has ever actually reached realization. He then draws a careful distinction: "sagely dharma" has two grades, the trainee's (anyone from stream-enterer through the approach to arhatship) and the one beyond training (the arhat alone), and specifies that wherever this ground speaks simply of "sagely dharma" without qualification, it means the second — full arhatship, complete with its own tenfold path (right view through right concentration, plus right liberation and right knowledge) — precisely because even an accomplished trainee still carries some trace of ordinary affliction alongside their genuine realization, not yet fully purified.

He illustrates how real this gap remains even at an advanced stage with the well-known story of an arhat wrongly executed after a farmer's grief-distorted mind mistook him, dyeing his robes by a riverbank, for a cattle-thief who had butchered his missing ox — the king who ordered the execution acting on the false accusation without investigation, and the arhat's own community only discovering his fate once his prolonged absence became impossible to ignore. When they finally secured his release and asked why he hadn't simply used his spiritual powers to prove his innocence, he explained that the ripening of a specific past karmic debt had temporarily blocked those powers until its own natural conclusion — after which he freely demonstrated them before entering final nirvāṇa on the spot. Among the group who witnessed this, one who was only a trainee rather than a full arhat felt a flash of indignation at the king's carelessness — a fleeting thought, nothing more, yet still enough of a residual imperfection that it drew a response from protective spirits displeased on the arhat's behalf. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it is precise: even a mind this advanced, this close to full realization, can still be moved in a way a genuine arhat's mind simply cannot be — which is exactly why this ground's own "sagely dharma" points specifically to the far shore, not the crossing still underway.

6. The Second Branch: Genuine Teaching Requires Both a Good Teacher and a Good Listener

The second of the seven branches — "complete access to hearing the true teaching" — turns from the conditions of a favorable life to the conditions of a single teaching encounter itself, insisting that genuine transmission requires integrity on both sides: correct teaching, and correct listening.

Chinese Source

云何聞正法圓滿？謂若正說法，若正聞法，二種總名聞正法圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is complete access to hearing the true teaching? Both correct teaching and correct listening together are called complete access to hearing the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names two qualities that make teaching itself genuine: it must accord with the Buddha's own teaching rather than personal opinion, and it must come from a mind free of any self-interested motive — never withheld out of personal grudge, never given for reputation or gain. He notes the treatise develops this into twenty concrete marks elsewhere (proper timing and manner, responsiveness to the audience, and five further marks of a genuinely undefiled motive, among others), but treats the two-part summary given here as sufficient for this ground's purposes.

He then names four corresponding faults a listener must avoid to receive teaching well: arrogance, which keeps genuinely valuable teaching from penetrating a mind already convinced it knows better; contempt for the teacher, which poisons the encounter regardless of how sound the teaching itself is; timidity, the opposite error of assuming the teaching is simply beyond one's own capacity — a discouragement Master Cháng Bǎi rejects outright, noting that the Buddha himself began as an ordinary being and reached full awakening only through sustained effort, so no one is disqualified in principle; and distraction, receiving teaching with a scattered rather than genuinely attentive mind. Free of these four faults, listening becomes genuine listening rather than merely being present while words are spoken — and only when both sides of the exchange meet this standard, he stresses, does the encounter actually count as "complete," a reminder that transmission is never a one-way event but always something built jointly between teacher and listener.

7. The Third Branch: Ten Stages From First Hearing to Final Nirvāṇa

The third branch, "nirvāṇa held as the governing aim," answers a simple but easily lost question: what should a practitioner actually be listening for? For a hearer-disciple, the treatise answers, every act of listening should be oriented toward nirvāṇa alone — and it then lays out, in exact sequence, the ten stages that carry a listener from that first orientation all the way to complete liberation.

Chinese Source

云何涅槃為上首？謂如來弟子依生圓滿轉時，如先所說相而聽聞正法，唯以涅槃而為上首。唯求涅槃、唯緣涅槃而聽聞法，不為引他令信於己，不為利養恭敬稱譽。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is nirvāṇa held as the governing aim? It means that when the Tathāgata's disciples, resting on a favorable rebirth-basis, listen to the true teaching in the manner already described, they hold nirvāṇa alone as the governing aim — seeking nirvāṇa alone, listening with nirvāṇa alone in mind, never to win others' trust in themselves, and never for material gain, honor, or praise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the nine stages that carry a practitioner from first hearing to nirvāṇa with remainder still living. First, hearing the teaching produces three kinds of confident conviction regarding the truths of the path and its result: conviction that the path and cessation genuinely exist as described; conviction that cultivating them carries real benefit — release from suffering, not merely a comforting idea; and conviction that one is personally capable of both the practice and its result. Second, that conviction, pursued through withdrawal from noise and distraction and freedom from the five hindrances and unwholesome trains of thought, ripens into the wisdom born of reflection — genuine, well-reasoned understanding rather than secondhand belief. Third, this wisdom then supports two kinds of sustained effort: unbroken diligence, practicing at every moment without gaps, and thorough diligence, practicing completely rather than carelessly or partially. Fourth, this sustained effort in turn yields the wisdom born of cultivation itself — direct insight arising from meditation, not merely reasoning about it — from which the practitioner clearly sees saṃsāra's real dangers and nirvāṇa's real value, no longer as doctrine but as felt conviction. Fifth and sixth, that seeing, repeated and deepened, first yields the path of seeing (stream-entry, severing every affliction rooted in conceptual wrong view) and then, through continued practice on the path of cultivation, yields full arhatship — freedom complete, though the body and mind inherited from past karma still remain, which is what "nirvāṇa with remainder" means.

He then explains the tenth and final stage as this same freedom, once fully ripened, become the basis for the ultimate aim: nirvāṇa without any remainder at all, reached only at the arhat's death, when the last trace of that inherited mind-body complex is finally relinquished and no future rebirth follows. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in setting out all ten stages this precisely, rather than simply saying "practice diligently and you'll get there eventually," is that a listener who genuinely holds nirvāṇa as their aim can locate their own progress on this exact sequence at any given moment — knowing concretely what stage comes next rather than practicing toward a vague, undefined future, which is itself part of what "holding nirvāṇa as the governing aim" is meant to provide.

8. The Third Branch Closes: Five Benefits of Keeping Nirvāṇa in View

Holding nirvāṇa as the aim while listening and while practicing yields five concrete benefits — benefiting the teacher, benefiting oneself in listening, benefiting the Buddha through genuine practice, benefiting oneself in practicing, and, finally, reaching the actual limit of suffering itself.

Chinese Source

謂聽聞法時，饒益自他；修正行時，饒益自他；及能證得眾苦邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely: benefiting both self and other while listening to the teaching; benefiting both self and other while cultivating right practice; and being able to reach the very limit of suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the first pair: when a teacher speaks with genuine liberation as the aim and a listener listens with that same aim, the teacher's effort is never wasted on an audience with mismatched motives — a real risk the tradition elsewhere calls a form of "demonic interference," teacher and listener talking past each other because their underlying goals don't actually match — which is what makes this alignment itself a benefit to the teacher. The listener benefits in turn by receiving the teaching's genuine depth rather than skimming its surface, which produces real joy and, through that joy, further strengthens the very roots of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom that make continued practice possible — a benefit that compounds rather than merely comforts.

The second pair concerns practice itself: a practitioner who genuinely cultivates the path — precepts, concentration, wisdom, the thirty-seven factors of awakening — is, whether they think of it this way or not, offering the single truest form of homage a buddha ever asked for, since a buddha's entire purpose in teaching was to see beings actually walk the path, not merely admire it from a distance; genuine practice is itself the offering, more so than any material gift. And that same practice benefits the practitioner directly, carrying them, stage by stage, toward the "cool stillness" of a trainee's partial freedom and finally the complete "coolness" of an arhat's total freedom from affliction's heat. The fifth benefit then names where all of this ultimately arrives: at the moment of an arhat's death, entering nirvāṇa without any remainder at all, suffering itself reaches its absolute limit and simply ends — the single point past which, the treatise stresses, there is nothing further to reach at all. With these five benefits named, the third branch — nirvāṇa held as the governing aim — closes in full.

9. The Fourth Branch: Ten Steps That Ripen Calm-Abiding and Insight Into Genuine Liberation

The fourth branch, "the ripening of liberating wisdom," first explains a small but important puzzle — why both calm-abiding (奢摩他) and insight-observation (毗鉢舍那) are each separately called "wisdom's ripening" — and then lays out, in exact sequence, the ten concrete steps by which both are actually brought to maturity.

Chinese Source

所以者何？定心中慧，於所知境清淨轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this so? Because it is precisely the wisdom present within a concentrated mind that operates purely with regard to what is known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this single line as the treatise's own working definition of genuine cultivation: insight-observation is called wisdom's ripening for the obvious reason that it directly discerns the nature of things, but calm-abiding earns the same name because insight can only operate purely and clearly once the mind is actually settled — at minimum in the preparatory stage of concentration — so that observation happening within that settled state, not mere discursive thinking about doctrine, is what genuinely wears away affliction. He is pointed about the implication: reciting scripture, bowing, and chanting with a scattered mind are all worthwhile, but none of it by itself constitutes the kind of cultivation this ground is describing unless it is eventually joined to actual stillness and insight working together — a caution against mistaking sincere devotional activity for the different, further step of genuine meditative transformation.

He then walks through the ten steps in their given sequence, each building on the last: first, relying from the very outset on a genuine spiritual friend — whether a living teacher or the accumulated wisdom of the scriptures themselves — since insight cannot develop in isolation; second, grounding calm-abiding in complete precepts, since concentration reliably stalls when the underlying conduct is compromised, and a stalled practice is often itself a sign calling for renewed confession and correction; third, developing a genuine wish to realize the true nature of things, the motivating spark behind all further effort; fourth, learning to accept correction gracefully when more experienced companions in practice point out a fault, whether witnessed directly, reported, or merely suspected — genuine community only functions when its members can hear this without defensiveness; fifth, delighting in hearing the teaching and following that delight into active questioning, since understanding rarely deepens through passive listening alone; sixth, through sustained, uninterrupted hearing, watching the teaching's meaning grow steadily clearer and long-standing doubts dissolve; seventh, as that clarity grows, beginning to see the real cost hidden inside everything the world prizes — status, beauty, longevity, wealth, comfort, reputation, eloquence — and feeling genuine disenchantment with it; eighth, that disenchantment maturing from a mere insight into an actual loss of desire for any of it; ninth, forming the explicit resolve to sever the causes of rebirth in the lower realms entirely; and tenth, cultivating the specific remedies for every affliction in turn and aspiring to the resulting fruit — stream-entry's permanent closing of the lower realms, and finally the arhat's complete purification of mind. Master Cháng Bǎi closes by noting these ten form a genuine progression rather than a checklist to complete in any order — each step's fruit becomes the next step's starting condition, carrying a practitioner in one continuous motion from first meeting a teacher to full liberation.

10. Three Branches, One Set of Causes and Conditions — and a New Branch Opens

The treatise closes its account of branches two through four by naming them, together, the complete causes and conditions for yogic cultivation itself — and then opens the fifth branch, "cultivating remedies against affliction," with a threefold classification of practitioners and the specific afflictions each must overcome.

Chinese Source

又此三支，當知即是修瑜伽因緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

These three branches, it should be known, are precisely the causes and conditions for yogic cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that complete access to hearing the teaching, holding nirvāṇa as the aim, and the ten-step ripening of calm-abiding and insight together supply everything genuine cultivation actually needs — insight-observation functioning as the direct cause, calm-abiding as its indispensable supporting condition. He cross-references a fourfold description of what yogic cultivation actually accomplishes once these causes are in place: the gradual dissolving of whatever coarse, burdened quality still clings to body and mind; the corresponding gradual arising of genuine physical and mental ease in its place; a comprehensive grasp of both the specific and shared nature of whatever is being contemplated; and, finally, a genuine, self-sustaining delight in the practice itself — no longer something forced by discipline or comparison with others, but something the practitioner is drawn toward from the inside, wanting nothing more than the next opportunity to sit and observe.

With this framing complete, the fifth branch opens: cultivating actual remedies against affliction. The treatise first sorts practitioners into three situations — householders, monastics, and solitary retreatants — since each faces a different dominant obstacle, and then names ten specific afflictions across the three, the first two belonging to householders alone: sexual craving toward one's own spouse, and attachment to the pleasure of relatives, socializing, and the pursuit of wealth. Master Cháng Bǎi notes both are so consuming, wherever they take hold, that they leave no genuine room for practice at all — the classic warning that lustful craving unresolved makes escaping the dust of ordinary life simply impossible, and the equally practical observation that a life spent pursuing family obligations and income, however legitimate in itself, structurally forecloses the sustained attention cultivation requires.

11. The Monastic's Own Affliction, and the Two Remedies That Answer Both

Going forth does not itself dissolve the household's two afflictions — it simply changes their shape into rumination about the life left behind — and the treatise now names the two specific contemplations that answer both the householder's and the monastic's version of the same underlying attachment.

Chinese Source

設得出家，由此尋思之所擾動為障礙故，不生喜樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Even having gone forth, one is disturbed and obstructed by this same rumination, and so no joy arises.

Chinese Source

如是二種所對治法，隨其次第修不淨想、修無常想，當知是彼修習對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

For these two afflictions, cultivating the contemplation of impurity and the contemplation of impermanence, in their respective order, is the remedy that must be cultivated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a monastic freed from the outward circumstances of household life can still be pulled back by rumination over family and homeland — replaying how relatives are faring, whether they've prospered or declined, whether they've reunited or parted, and feeling the resulting swings of longing and regret, sometimes even wondering whether staying home might have been the better choice. This inner tug, he stresses, blocks the genuine joy of renunciation just as effectively as active household entanglement did, precisely because it keeps the mind oriented backward rather than toward the practice at hand.

He then explains why the two remedies are matched precisely to their causes: contemplating the body's actual impurity — its fluids, its inevitable decay, everything ordinary attraction glosses over — directly counters sexual craving, since that craving depends on a distorted, idealized image the contemplation dissolves; and contemplating impermanence — that nothing, including the people and wealth one might long for, can actually be held onto or controlled — directly counters both the householder's attachment to relatives and possessions and the monastic's homesick rumination about the very same things, since longing for something already known to be slipping away loses its grip once that fact is genuinely absorbed rather than merely acknowledged. With these two afflictions and their two remedies named, the treatise turns to what a monastic should actively do, day to day, rather than merely what to avoid — beginning with the first of four recurring tasks: sustained practice itself, undertaken specifically to master the mind's habitual craving and to genuinely realize the true nature of suffering.

12. Four Things a Monastic Must Always Do, and the Affliction Hidden Inside Each

Beyond avoiding specific afflictions, the treatise names four ordinary, recurring tasks that structure a monastic's entire day — sustained practice, holding nirvāṇa as a living aspiration rather than an abstraction, the alms round, and genuine solitude — and pairs each with the particular affliction most likely to corrupt it, and the specific remedy that answers that affliction.

Chinese Source

於此四種所作事中，當知有四所對治法。於初所作，有懶惰懈怠；於第二所作，有薩迦耶見；於第三所作，有愛味貪；於第四所作，有世間種種樂欲貪愛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within these four tasks, four afflictions to be remedied should be recognized. In the first, laziness and indolence; in the second, the view of a real self; in the third, craving for flavor; in the fourth, craving for the world's various pleasures.

Chinese Source

如是四種所對治法，如其次第，亦有四種修習對治。一、於無常修習苦想；二、於眾苦修無我想；三、於飲食修厭逆想；四、於一切世間修不可樂想。

Ru-Yi Translation

For these four afflictions, in their respective order, four cultivations serve as remedy: contemplating suffering within impermanence; contemplating non-self within the manifold sufferings; contemplating revulsion toward food; and contemplating the unloveliness of everything in the world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the fourth task, dwelling in genuine seclusion, as guarding against exactly the danger the first three tasks might otherwise create in a communal setting: monastics and laypeople clustered together without a shared aim toward liberation naturally generate the ordinary noise, gossip, and sensory distraction that disturbs anyone nearby genuinely trying to concentrate — a caution to protect others' practice as much as one's own. He then maps each of the four tasks to its characteristic corrupting affliction and remedy in turn: sustained practice itself can decay into laziness — not developing new virtue, not maintaining virtue already gained — countered by reflecting that death could come before the work is finished, which sharpens urgency; the aspiration toward nirvāṇa can be quietly undermined by residual self-view, especially in a bodhisattva's private worry that constant giving to others must somehow cost the self something, countered by genuinely seeing the five aggregates as without any self to protect in the first place, so the worry dissolves rather than needing to be argued away; the alms round can slide into ordinary craving for flavor, favoring tasty food and neglecting balanced nutrition, countered by contemplating food's actual nature — reflecting, as one classical example puts it, on the cook's own sweat falling unnoticed into the dish, or on how quickly any food becomes waste once swallowed, deflating its appeal without denying the body's real need for nourishment; and dwelling in seclusion can still be undermined by lingering craving for worldly pleasure even while physically alone, countered by seeing clearly that nothing the world offers is actually worth the craving it provokes.

13. Four More Afflictions That Surface in Solitary Practice, and Their Remedies

Whether monastic or lay, anyone actually sitting down to practice in seclusion faces four further afflictions specific to the moment of meditation itself — confusion about the method, attachment to the pleasure of concentration, subtle craving for a "better" future rebirth, and the endlessly rationalized postponement of practice to some later day — each with its own precise remedy.

Chinese Source

一、於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那品有闇昧心。二、於諸定有隨愛味。三、於生有隨動相心。四、推後後日，顧待餘時，隨不死尋，不能熾然勤修方便。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, a mind clouded with confusion regarding calm-abiding and insight. Second, craving that clings to states of concentration. Third, a mind still stirred by attraction toward future rebirth. Fourth, pushing practice off to some later day, waiting for another time, following the thought "I will not die yet" — and so failing to apply oneself with real intensity.

Chinese Source

如是四種所對治法，當知亦有四種修習對治。一、修光明想；二、修離欲想；三、修滅想；四、修死想。

Ru-Yi Translation

For these four afflictions, four cultivations likewise serve as remedy: cultivating the perception of light; cultivating the perception of freedom from craving; cultivating the perception of cessation; and cultivating the perception of death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first affliction as sitting down to meditate without ever having genuinely resolved basic doubts about the method itself — not knowing whether a given experience in meditation is progress or a dead end, whether the whole approach even works — leaving a practitioner to sit for years without truly settling any of it, sometimes abandoning practice entirely out of confusion rather than any lack of sincerity; the remedy, "the perception of light," is not literal brightness but genuine doctrinal clarity, patiently studied until the basic map of the path is no longer in doubt. The second affliction, craving that clings to concentration, he flags as more dangerous than ordinary sensory desire precisely because it feels virtuous — a practitioner who has tasted real stillness can become so attached to that stillness that further study or further insight-practice starts to feel unnecessary, mistaking a pleasant plateau for the destination; the remedy is contemplating that liberation itself, not any level of absorption, is what's genuinely supreme and peaceful, so that concentration is used rather than settled into.

The third affliction, a mind still stirred toward future rebirth, means failing to see the five aggregates of clinging as impermanent and without self clearly enough to stop unconsciously hoping for a better one next time — the remedy is contemplating cessation itself, nirvāṇa without remainder, until that hope for continuation genuinely loses its pull. And the fourth, endless postponement, is simply believing, at some barely conscious level, that death is comfortably far off — "I'll start tomorrow, or next year, once things settle down" — a pattern Master Cháng Bǎi notes crosses every walk of life, monastic and lay alike; its remedy is contemplating one's own body as, quite literally, already a thing that will die, with no guaranteed number of tomorrows left to spend. He closes this survey of the eight afflictions across the three practitioner-positions (two for householders, four each for monastics in ordinary duties and in seclusion) by noting that the treatise now turns to unpack three of these remedies — the perceptions of impurity, light, and death — in far greater technical detail, since they are the ones a practitioner is most likely to need concrete instruction on rather than simply being told to "contemplate" in the abstract.

14. Impurity-Contemplation Unpacked: Twelve Obstacles at Two Stages of Practice

The treatise now gives its fullest technical treatment of a single remedy: impurity-contemplation, the antidote to sexual craving named earlier, broken into two stages — one still at the level of reflection, one already resting on real concentration — each with its own list of specific obstacles and matching correctives.

Chinese Source

又不淨想略有二種。一、思擇力攝，二、修習力攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Impurity-contemplation, in brief, is of two kinds: that grounded in the power of reflection, and that grounded in the power of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains reflection-grounded impurity-contemplation as belonging to a practitioner still at the hearing-and-thinking stage, not yet resting on genuine concentration, and names five obstacles it must overcome: proximity to the opposite sex, the most basic precaution — a monk staying physically distant from women and a nun from men, since the tradition's own image is a person wearing straw clothing near an open flame, one spark from disaster, no matter how confident the wearer feels; losing mindfulness while out in public, letting the eyes linger on an attractive stranger encountered on the alms round rather than guarding the senses on sight; private indulgence in seclusion, entertaining an attractive mental image when no one is watching, on the mistaken assumption that unwitnessed thought carries no consequence; habituation, the deeper, harder-to-break version of the same pull that develops once either public or private indulgence becomes a settled pattern rather than an occasional lapse; and simple derailment of intention, where a practitioner genuinely trying to contemplate impurity finds the mind sliding back toward an attractive image anyway, since old habituation often reasserts itself hardest exactly when actively opposed.

Cultivation-grounded impurity-contemplation, resting on real meditative stability, faces seven further obstacles: a scattered mind still pulled toward the ten disturbing signs (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the mental afflictions of craving, aversion, delusion, and attraction to male or female form); that scattering compounding into active pursuit once the mind starts actively dwelling on and reacting to those signs; clumsy technique, from never having humbly sought instruction on how to actually visualize a corpse's stages of decay — discoloration, bloating, decomposition, down to bare bones and their joints — from someone genuinely skilled in the practice; residual mental disturbance even in solitude, from habitually leaving the senses unguarded elsewhere in daily life; physical imbalance from not eating in moderation, leaving the body without the steady energy concentration requires; reluctance to settle into inner stillness, kept restless by the same wandering thoughts about family, homeland, or status surveyed earlier in this fascicle; and, finally, an inability to sustain genuine insight-observation at all, simply because an unwell body cannot support the sustained attention insight requires. Master Cháng Bǎi tallies these as twelve obstacles in total (five plus seven), or fourteen if the two enabling "powers" themselves are counted alongside them — and stresses that each has an exact, named remedy: staying physically apart from the opposite sex answers the first; promptly re-establishing mindfulness answers the second; refusing to entertain the image even in private answers the third; deliberately breaking the habitual pattern before it hardens answers the fourth; and returning immediately to the actual object of contemplation, without following the mind's drift, answers the fifth. For the seven cultivation-stage obstacles, the remedies are, respectively: settling the mind back on its object rather than letting it wander; refusing to let that wandering develop into active grasping; humbly and persistently seeking a qualified teacher's concrete instruction; genuinely guarding the senses at all other times so seclusion isn't undone by habits carried in from outside it; eating in careful moderation to keep the body capable of sustained practice; recognizing that restlessness itself is the very obstacle calm-abiding exists to resolve, rather than a reason to abandon the attempt; and, since a well body is the precondition for genuine insight, addressing physical imbalance before expecting insight-observation to succeed. Twelve obstacles, twelve matched remedies — Master Cháng Bǎi's point in laying them out this exhaustively is that "just try harder" is never, in this treatise's own accounting, actually sufficient guidance.

15. Contemplating Suffering Within Impermanence: Six Obstacles to Overcoming Laziness

The remedy for laziness in sustained practice — contemplating that everything is impermanent, and therefore bound up with suffering — faces its own six specific obstacles, ranging from simple inertia to a subtler failure of placing one's confidence in the right thing at all.

Chinese Source

又於無常所修苦想，略有六種所對治法。

Ru-Yi Translation

For contemplating suffering within impermanence, there are, in brief, six obstacles to be remedied.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the six in turn: laziness that fails to even begin cultivating virtue not yet present — precepts, concentration, and wisdom that a practitioner has not yet developed at all — for lack of any real urgency; indolence that, having made some genuine progress, fails to protect and further develop it, letting hard-won stability quietly erode from neglect; failing to sustain the habit of humbly and repeatedly seeking a teacher's guidance when questions remain unresolved, whether from false confidence or simple inertia; lacking what he calls "pure faith" in the Dharma itself, as distinct from mere personal devotion to a particular teacher — a crucial distinction, since confidence placed in a person rather than in the teaching itself is fragile, collapsing the moment that teacher proves imperfect or is no longer present, whereas confidence placed correctly in the teaching's own soundness can carry a practitioner forward regardless of who happens to be teaching it at any given moment; the resulting inability, absent that properly placed faith, to sustain practice at all; and finally, plain inner heedlessness — greed, aversion, and delusion left unchecked, breaking the continuity that regular practice depends on.

He stresses the fourth obstacle as the one most easily missed: a student who follows a teacher out of simple personal attachment, rather than because the teaching itself has been genuinely tested and found sound, has not actually placed their trust anywhere stable — every teacher, however accomplished, remains an ordinary mortal subject to illness, error, and death, and confidence anchored there inevitably wavers along with them, while confidence anchored in the Dharma's own demonstrated capacity to resolve suffering does not. The remedy for all six, he notes, is simply their direct reversal: eagerly cultivating what has not yet arisen, protecting and building on what has, staying in genuine dialogue with capable teachers, grounding confidence in the teaching rather than the teacher, sustaining practice on that foundation, and guarding the mind against heedlessness throughout.

16. Light-Perception: What "Light" Actually Means in This Practice

The treatise now unpacks the remedy for confusion about method — "light-perception" — clarifying that while literal light (starlight, sunlight, a glowing jewel) can genuinely help settle a dull or drowsy mind, the perception this ground actually means is something more specific: the mind never losing hold of the teaching it has learned.

Chinese Source

謂如所聞已得究竟不忘念法，名法光明。與彼俱行彼相應想，應知名光明想。

Ru-Yi Translation

The teaching one has heard, thoroughly retained and never forgotten, is called the light of the teaching. The perception that arises together with and corresponds to that light should be known as light-perception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes ordinary luminous objects — moon and stars for the night, sunlight for the day, a glowing jewel for either — genuinely useful for countering literal drowsiness in meditation, from the specific sense this ground has in mind: "the light of the teaching," meaning whatever doctrine a practitioner has genuinely absorbed through hearing and repeated reflection, held so thoroughly that it stays instantly available rather than fading under pressure — whether that's the essential line "all that has marks is illusory; see the marks as no-marks and you see the Tathāgata" carried through a session of impurity-contemplation, or any other teaching a practitioner has made genuinely their own. What actually counts as "darkness" in this specific sense, he explains, is not literal dimness but forgetting the very method one set out to practice — losing the thread of counting the breath, drifting mid-contemplation without noticing — and what counts as its opposite, "light," is simply catching that lapse and returning cleanly to the object, whatever it is. A practitioner need not wait for confusion to strike before noticing it either, he adds: the same returning motion, promptly and without self-recrimination, is itself the whole of what "light-perception" means in practice.

He adds that "the light of the teaching" also covers a second, complementary technique: the six recollections — of the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, one's own past generosity, one's own precepts, and the heavens — deliberately called to mind whenever dullness sets in, since genuine joy at recalling one's own good conduct or the qualities of the Three Jewels naturally dispels the very heaviness that light-perception exists to counter.

17. Light-Perception Unpacked in Full: Eleven Obstacles Across Reflection and Cultivation

The treatise gives light-perception the same exhaustive treatment already given to impurity-contemplation — four obstacles specific to the reflection stage, seven specific to actual meditative cultivation — eleven in total, each threatening the same basic capacity: staying clear about what one is doing and why, rather than drifting into confusion or misdirected effort.

Chinese Source

又第一義思所成慧及修所成慧俱光明想，有十一法為所對治。謂思所成慧俱光明想有四法，修所成慧俱光明想有七法。

Ru-Yi Translation

In its fullest sense, light-perception joined with reflection-wisdom and with cultivation-wisdom has eleven things to be remedied: four belonging to light-perception joined with reflection-wisdom, and seven belonging to light-perception joined with cultivation-wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the four reflection-stage obstacles first: failing to examine and settle one's understanding clearly enough, so that genuine doubt lingers over basic questions — whether a self exists at all, or what exactly the Four Noble Truths mean — rather than being resolved through careful study or a teacher's guidance; wasting the night hours in excessive sleep out of simple laziness rather than sustained study; impairing the body through poor or unsuitable food during the day, leaving it too unwell to support careful examination of the teaching; and keeping company with a mixed, undisciplined crowd of lay and monastic people whose conversation never rises above idle chatter, leaving no real opportunity for sustained reflection on what has actually been heard. Left unresolved, he stresses, these four keep a practitioner's understanding forever shallow — however much doctrine has technically been heard, it never becomes genuinely clear or reliable knowledge.

The seven cultivation-stage obstacles then map onto specific meditative errors: over-application of the energizing factor, straining so hard toward alertness that the mind becomes agitated and sleep-deprived rather than genuinely awake; the opposite over-application of the calming factor, settling so completely into stillness that the mind grows sluggish and slow to respond rather than genuinely still; becoming attached to the pleasant taste of equanimity itself once concentration is genuinely achieved, wanting only to remain in that pleasant state rather than using it to progress further; fear or aversion at the very thought of nirvāṇa — either genuine dread at the prospect of the self's cessation, or a quieter reluctance that saps the will to practice, both rooted in not actually understanding what the treatise itself glosses nirvāṇa as: an old commentarial fourfold definition worth remembering — permanent exit from every realm of rebirth, freedom from the "stench" of affliction and karma, escape from the dense thicket of the three calamities and the three marks of birth-old-age-death, and the end of any further weaving of karmic thread into future suffering, none of which is remotely something to fear; sustained argumentative debate and doctrinal point-scoring carried into meditation time, which exhausts the mind through sheer volume of thought rather than settling it; ordinary sensory craving toward attractive sights, sounds, smells, tastes, or textures surfacing during or after sitting, scattering the mind that meditation just settled; and, finally, forcing the mind to dwell on genuinely unanswerable or unhelpful questions — a list the treatise borrows nearly whole from an older discourse: the ultimate nature of self, of other beings, and of the world; the full workings of karma; a meditator's or a buddha's own inner experience (both simply beyond a listener's capacity to verify secondhand); the fourteen classical unanswerable questions the Buddha himself declined to answer (whether the world is eternal or not, finite or not, what happens to a Buddha after death, and whether self and body are the same or different — each traditionally posed as a four-way question); anything belonging to a mistaken teaching; and anything that simply feeds affliction the more it's turned over, such as replaying a grievance until anger deepens rather than resolves. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in listing all nine "unthinkable" topics so precisely is practical rather than merely doctrinal: a practitioner who catches the mind wandering into any of them has a concrete signal to disengage, rather than mistaking restless rumination for genuine inquiry.

He adds that dwelling on any of these nine produces three specific costs — a genuinely scattered mind, no accumulation of merit, and no progress in wholesome qualities — while their remedy, simply declining to entertain them, produces the mirror benefits: settledness, merit, and genuine wholesome growth. With all eleven obstacles named, the remedy in each case is simply its direct reversal, and the treatise closes branch five, "cultivating remedies against affliction," by naming three further qualities that make consistent practice of these remedies "greatly productive": the genuine wish to eliminate whatever affliction is present, refusing to let a manifesting affliction take hold so it dissolves quickly rather than festering, and sustained dwelling in the remedy itself rather than only reaching for it reactively. With this, branch five — covering ten total obstacle-sets across householders, monastics, and solitary retreatants, with impurity-contemplation and light-perception unpacked in full technical detail — closes, matching, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, everything scattered piecemeal across the wider canon on this subject with nothing left over and nothing missing.

18. The Sixth Branch Opens: Mundane Purity, and the Three Stages of Genuine Meditative Mastery

The sixth branch, "mundane purity," addresses the first of the ground's two fruits: full mastery of meditative concentration itself, unfolding in three stages — actually attaining concentration, perfecting it through the full ladder of absorption, and finally becoming completely at ease within it, free of the subtle afflictions that can otherwise still ride along with even accomplished meditative attainment.

Chinese Source

云何世間一切種清淨？當知略有三種。一、得三摩地，二、三摩地圓滿，三、三摩地自在。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is mundane purity of every kind? It should be known, in brief, to be threefold: attaining concentration, perfecting concentration, and mastery over concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why this branch on meditation comes before the treatise's final branch on supramundane liberation: genuine insight can only operate purely within a settled mind, so mastering concentration itself is the necessary foundation everything else builds on. "Attaining concentration" means reaching at least the preparatory stage just short of the first absorption — the minimum concentration needed, for a hearer-disciple, to begin cutting affliction at all. "Perfecting concentration" means carrying that preparatory stability all the way up through the four form-realm absorptions. And "mastery over concentration" addresses something subtler still: even a practitioner who has reached the fourth absorption can remain quietly entangled in craving for, wrong views about, or conceit regarding their own attainment, together with the simple technical skill of entering, sustaining, and emerging from each level of concentration at will — genuine mastery requires purifying all of this, not merely reaching the stability itself. He notes pointedly that this three-stage structure is exactly where Buddhist meditation practice diverges from non-Buddhist systems capable of reaching the very same four absorptions and four formless attainments: only this third stage, mastery free of affliction, is unique to a practice actually aimed at liberation rather than stability for its own sake.

The treatise then opens the first stage, attaining concentration, with an unusually long and concrete inventory: twenty distinct obstacles capable of blocking a practitioner from reaching genuine concentration in the first place, the first of which is simply keeping company with fellow practitioners who have no real interest in cutting affliction at all — companions absorbed in worldly success and worldly setback alike, neither of which they've learned to see as impermanent and worth relinquishing. Master Cháng Bǎi is blunt about the practical cost of this first obstacle: shared practice requires a genuine shared aim, and a community lacking it drags down even a sincere individual practitioner's own momentum, however strong their private resolve.

19. Twenty Obstacles Between a Practitioner and Genuine Concentration

Having named unhelpful companionship as the first obstacle, the treatise works methodically through nineteen more — a remarkably candid, almost clinical inventory of everything that can go wrong on the way to attaining even the preliminary stage of concentration, moving from external circumstances through personal character flaws to the subtlest failures of balance in practice itself.

Chinese Source

此中最初有二十種得三摩地所對治法，能令不得勝三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

At the outset there are twenty things to be remedied that can prevent one from attaining excellent concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi groups the twenty roughly by where the failure originates. Several concern the teaching relationship itself: a teacher who, however well-meaning, actually gives faulty instruction on method, mixing up what should be seen as unattractive with what should be seen as worth pursuing; a listener whose own motivation is too weak to actually attend, present in body but mentally elsewhere; a listener whose native capacity or unrefined intellect simply can't absorb what's taught even with full attention; and, further along, refusing correction out of pride or resentment, or genuinely trying to apply the teaching but misunderstanding its sequence and misapplying it as a result, or simply forgetting what was taught soon after hearing it.

A cluster of others concern character and circumstance: craving reputation and material support, which pulls a practitioner into constant social obligation; genuine difficulty being satisfied with little, leading to chronic want and the endless activity needed to chase it; abandoning consistent effort out of simple laziness, particularly excessive attachment to comfortable sleep; illness, injury, or hostile interference of any kind, from chronic sickness to venomous bites to outright persecution; inability to tolerate ordinary discomforts like heat and cold; keeping company with an undisciplined mixed crowd of lay and monastic acquaintances; and an unsuitable physical location — the treatise names five specific marks of a genuinely suitable retreat setting (a clean, pleasant, well-tended site; free of excessive noise, insects, and heat; free of dangerous animals or hostile people; within reasonable reach of life's basic necessities; and near a genuine teacher or capable fellow practitioners) with the absence of any one counting as an obstacle.

The remaining obstacles are subtler, arising even once the basic conditions are otherwise sound: unguarded senses in solitude producing exactly the eight kinds of distracting rumination surveyed earlier in this fascicle — Master Cháng Bǎi adds here the well-known instruction the teacher Milarepa is said to have given his student Gampopa, that dwelling on a fellow practitioner's faults is itself one of the most corrosive habits a community can develop, and should be replaced with actively noticing others' good qualities instead; physical heaviness and lack of capability from poor, imbalanced diet; the specific hindrance of excessive drowsiness, which the treatise distinguishes carefully from outright sleep — a dulled, foggy half-presence at the meditation object rather than either full clarity or full unconsciousness, often unnoticed by the person experiencing it even as posture visibly slumps; disinterest in calm-abiding itself, for a naturally restless mind that finds settling on one object simply too boring to sustain; and, mirroring that last one exactly, disinterest in insight-observation for a mind that has grown comfortable with stillness alone and never developed the habit of actively examining what it observes. Master Cháng Bǎi's closing observation is that the sheer number and specificity of this list is itself instructive: genuine concentration fails for identifiable, addressable reasons far more often than for any mysterious lack of talent, which is exactly why the treatise catalogues them this precisely rather than simply urging more effort.

20. Twenty Obstacles Reduce to Four Causes — and "Attaining Concentration" Closes

The treatise closes its account of the first stage, "attaining concentration," by compressing the twenty obstacles into four underlying causes, naming their twenty mirror-image remedies, and specifying exactly what has actually been reached once these obstacles are cleared: access concentration, the threshold just short of the first absorption proper.

Chinese Source

又此二十種所對治法，略由四相，於所生起三摩地中，堪能為障。一、於三摩地方便不善巧故；二、於一切修定方便全無加行故；三、顛倒加行故；四、加行慢緩故。

Ru-Yi Translation

These twenty things to be remedied can, in brief, obstruct the arising of concentration through four causes: lacking skill in the means of concentration; making no effort toward it at all; applying effort in a mistaken way; and applying effort too slowly and half-heartedly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the fourth cause — effort applied too slowly to ever accumulate — with the image of trying to boil water by heating it to only twenty degrees each day and then stopping: however many days pass, it never reaches the boil, not because the method is wrong but because it is never sustained long enough at any one sitting to compound. He then restates all twenty obstacles as their direct mirror opposites — good companions, a genuinely skilled teacher, real motivation to listen, adequate capacity to understand, freedom from craving reputation, contentment with little, a manageable workload, sustained diligence, freedom from illness and interference, tolerance of discomfort, receptivity to correction, accurate application of what's taught, retained memory of it, disciplined rather than mixed company, a genuinely suitable location, disciplined rather than wandering thought, a body kept light and capable through balanced diet, moderate rather than excessive sleep, and genuine enthusiasm for both calm-abiding and insight-observation in turn — noting that a monastic's workload deserves particular nuance: someone genuinely serving the community through teaching or administrative duty is not thereby failing this standard, since such service itself builds the conditions for others' eventual support in return, though the underlying principle — trim away whatever activity isn't actually necessary — still applies.

He closes by naming exactly what has been reached once all twenty obstacles are cleared: not the first absorption itself, but "access concentration" — a genuine but still preliminary stability bordering the first absorption without yet actually entering it, marked by real but partial mental and physical ease. This is deliberately called "attaining concentration" rather than "concentration perfected," since it remains, in the treatise's own words, difficult to gain and easy to lose without sustained further cultivation — the necessary foundation for what comes next, but not yet the finished structure built on it.

21. Perfecting Concentration: Ten Marks on the Way to the First Absorption and Beyond

The second stage, "perfecting concentration," carries a practitioner from the still-fragile access concentration just described all the way through the four form-realm absorptions proper, marked by ten distinct signs of genuine progress rather than mere aspiration.

Chinese Source

復次，如是已得三摩地者，於此少小殊勝定中，不生喜足。於勝三摩地圓滿，更起求願。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, one who has attained concentration does not rest content with this small measure of excellence, but arouses a further aspiration toward the perfection of superior concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the ten marks as a single continuous arc. First, the practitioner who has reached access concentration refuses to settle for it, aspiring instead toward the genuine first absorption; second, they come to see, often through direct testimony or the treatise's own account, real superior virtue in that goal — the treatise's classic seven-stage sequence of attention (recognizing the object through study; gaining a powerful working grasp of it; an initial withdrawal from the desire realm's coarsest afflictions; growing genuine delight and easier practice as middling afflictions subside; checking one's own progress by testing whether attractive objects still stir anything; a final, thorough effort that subdues even the subtlest remaining desire-realm afflictions; and finally the fruit of that effort — genuine entry into the first absorption, where joy and bliss born of seclusion from desire are said to fill the entire body without a single gap). Third, seeing this clearly enough to actually want it produces genuine, sustained diligence rather than passive hope.

Fourth and fifth, the practitioner honestly recognizes that access concentration alone has not actually eliminated craving connected to attractive form, and that they remain, in a real sense, still losable to affliction rather than having genuinely defeated it — an important corrective against premature self-satisfaction. Sixth and seventh, once the first absorption is genuinely reached, the practitioner refuses to be seduced by the prospect of the correspondingly excellent rebirth it could produce in the pure form-realm heavens, recognizing this as still just a subtler form of "seeking existence" rather than actual liberation, and instead keeps applying the same calming, energizing, and equanimizing techniques already learned, carrying the same disciplined process forward through the second, third, and fourth absorptions in turn — releasing each level's own remaining agitation (initially thought itself, then joy, then pleasure) to advance to the next. Eighth, that same sustained practice — moving freely in and out of these deepening states — naturally produces genuine interest in learning more, prompting the practitioner to actively seek instruction on the paranormal insight the tradition associates with deep concentration, itself grounded in the same ordinary discipline of careful, attentive observation any skill requires, whether reading a person's true feeling from a flicker of expression or learning to distinguish one bird's call from another's — ultimately aimed, in its highest sense, at the wisdom that exhausts every corruption rather than mere supernatural ability. Ninth and tenth, this whole progression yields, at last, genuine delight in calm-abiding itself and genuine delight in insight-observation applied to the Four Noble Truths — both, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, now arising as real appetite for the practice rather than dutiful obligation. With all ten marks present, the treatise says, a practitioner has reached the root absorptions in full: perfecting concentration is complete.

22. Mastery Opens: Even the Root Absorptions Still Carry Hidden Flaws

The third and final stage, "mastery over concentration," opens with an important corrective: reaching the actual first, second, third, and fourth absorptions is a genuine accomplishment, but the mind attaining them can still be quietly stained by craving, conceit, wrong view, doubt, and ignorance riding along with that very attainment — meaning the work is not yet finished simply because the absorptions themselves have been reached.

Chinese Source

復次，雖已證得根本三摩地故，名三摩地圓滿；其心猶為三摩地生愛味、慢、見、疑、無明等諸隨煩惱之所染汙，未名圓滿清淨鮮白。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, although one has already attained the root concentrations — called "perfecting concentration" — the mind may still be defiled by secondary afflictions arising in connection with that very concentration: craving for it, conceit over it, wrong views regarding it, doubt, and ignorance. Such attainment cannot yet be called complete, pure, and radiant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the four specific ways this happens: craving arises simply because the absorption's own bliss so far exceeds anything the desire realm offers; wrong view arises when unusual experiences surface within deep concentration and get misinterpreted, especially by a practitioner who entered meditation without first establishing sound view at all; conceit arises from the quiet thought that one has achieved something others haven't; and doubt persists over the Three Jewels and the Four Truths despite the attainment. Genuine mastery, by contrast, means being able to enter, remain in, and emerge from concentration entirely at will, with none of these five riding along — the difference, he stresses, between the merely mundane concentration available to any sufficiently disciplined non-Buddhist meditator and the uniquely purified concentration this treatise is actually describing.

To purify concentration of these hidden defects, the treatise sets out an unusually concrete self-examination framework: twenty-two specific marks organized under four broad commitments a monastic makes at ordination — accepting a humble outward form, deportment, and simplicity of possessions; accepting the restraining precepts; accepting sustained, unbroken cultivation of wholesome qualities; and, cutting across all three, recognizing that failing any of them still leaves a practitioner pursued by suffering. The first of the four, examined through five marks, covers accepting a humble form: a shaved head and simple, unglamorous robes, deliberately foregoing any investment in physical appearance; humble conduct on the alms round, meaning no craving connected to sight, sound, or any other sense arising during the round, and no pride when entering a donor's home — Master Cháng Bǎi notes the classical image of approaching each house "like the moon," letting one's own conduct wax steadily purer rather than waning toward carelessness, and carefully distinguishing which sights along the way are worth attending to (illness, aging, death — anything that deepens the sense of impermanence) from which aren't (anything merely attractive); and simple, unaccumulated requisites, taking only what is offered and needed rather than stockpiling comfort.

The second commitment, precepts, is examined through five further marks: recognizing that full ordination itself already means relinquishing two things — attachment to family, friends, and possessions, and indulgence in entertainment, socializing, and sensory pleasure; genuinely not breaking the precepts oneself; not hastily accusing a fellow practitioner of breaking theirs, since appearances can mislead and careless accusation is itself a fault — Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Sixth Chan Patriarch's own teaching that seeing fault in others is itself already a fault; and, should a lapse occur on either side, sincerely correcting one's own through confession, and receiving a fellow practitioner's correction of oneself without resentment, recognizing it as help rather than attack. Together these first two commitments — form and precepts — already reveal, Master Cháng Bǎi observes, just how much subtler work remains even after the absorptions themselves have been genuinely attained.

23. The Twenty-Two Marks Complete: What Remains Undone Even After the Absorptions

The third commitment — sustained, unbroken cultivation of wholesome qualities — and the fourth — honestly recognizing which sufferings still pursue an otherwise accomplished meditator — complete the full twenty-two-mark self-examination, closing with the treatise's own precise definition of what actually counts as genuine renunciation.

Chinese Source

謂時時間諮受讀誦論量決擇，勤修善品，如是乃應受他信施；又樂遠離，以正方便修諸作意；又復晝夜，於退分、勝分二法，知斷修習；又於生死見大過失；又於涅槃見勝功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regularly seeking instruction, reading, reciting, discussing, and discerning the teaching, diligently cultivating what is wholesome — only thus does one deserve to receive others' faithful offerings. Delighting in seclusion, applying right effort to the stages of meditative attention. Day and night, recognizing and severing what causes decline while cultivating what brings genuine progress. Seeing the great fault in saṃsāra. And seeing the surpassing virtue in nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the third commitment's five marks as together forming what the tradition calls "the resources for concentration": genuinely seeking a teacher's instruction and actually following it rather than substituting one's own opinion, reading and memorizing the relevant texts, and discussing their meaning with fellow practitioners until real discernment develops — all of which, he stresses, is what actually earns the right to accept a donor's support, since receiving alms while not genuinely practicing leaves a debt that eventually has to be repaid in suffering; delighting in genuine seclusion and applying the same seven-stage attention sequence already described to advance through the absorptions, resting on a suitable physical location and disciplined deportment, and on being truly withdrawn both physically (not mixing carelessly with lay or monastic company) and mentally (free of any defiled or purposeless mental activity); a daily, even hourly habit of checking whether one's precepts, concentration, and wisdom are currently declining or advancing, cutting off the former the moment it's noticed and reinforcing the latter; and finally two motivating perspectives held together — clearly seeing saṃsāra's real cost and nirvāṇa's real value — which supply the actual pull that keeps effort from flagging.

The fourth commitment then asks the meditator to honestly check which of four specific forms of suffering, expanded into seven concrete marks, still pursue them despite everything already accomplished: the danger of falling to a lower realm, since only genuine realization of the Four Truths — not meditative attainment alone — permanently closes off rebirth there; the fourfold suffering internal to the body's own nature (birth, aging, sickness, and death, each traditionally analyzed into five defining marks — birth as already entangled in suffering and affliction, aging as five-fold decline in appearance, strength, faculty, enjoyment, and lifespan, sickness as bodily breakdown and its attendant misery, death as the loss of everything and everyone one has ever loved); the suffering of losing whatever is loved, since nothing held dear, however cherished, escapes eventual separation; and the suffering inherent simply in having aggregates shaped by one's own past karma at all, which keep generating further affliction and further karma in an unbroken cycle. Reflecting honestly on all seven, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, is meant to produce a specific realization: that reaching even the fourth absorption, however genuine, has not actually finished the work — only direct realization of the Four Truths closes these gaps for good.

The treatise then names the resulting resolve explicitly: a practitioner who has genuinely undertaken humble form, precepts, and sustained cultivation, yet still finds these four sufferings unresolved, should refuse to rest content with concentration alone or let their effort quietly lapse partway through. Only sustained reflection carried this far, the treatise stresses, earns the name "true renunciant thought" and "true seeker's thought" — glossed elsewhere in the canon as actually freeing oneself from every unwholesome quality still present in body and mind, and as embodying the eightfold noble path in substance rather than merely wearing its outward form. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in walking through all twenty-two marks this exhaustively is that genuine renunciation, in this treatise's own accounting, is demonstrated by this kind of sustained, specific self-examination — not simply by having taken ordination and reached deep states of concentration, however real those achievements are in themselves.

24. Mastery Achieved: Five Further Practices, and Branch Six Closes Entirely

Having examined itself through the twenty-two marks, the treatise names five further practices that actually resolve what that examination reveals — and with mastery achieved, closes the entire sixth branch, "mundane purity," declaring this whole three-stage structure unique to genuine Buddhist practice.

Chinese Source

彼由如是正修行故，於三摩地獲得自在。依止彼故，其心清白，無有瑕穢，離隨煩惱，廣說乃至獲得不動，能引一切勝神通慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through practicing correctly in this way, one attains mastery over concentration. Resting on this, the mind becomes pure and unstained, free of secondary afflictions, reaching what is called "the unmoving," able to give rise to every kind of superior wisdom born of paranormal insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names five practices, resting on the seven-stage attention already mastered, that finally resolve the flaws named earlier: genuine wish to sever whatever affliction the absorptions themselves have stirred up, rather than treating stability alone as the finish line; continued, unbroken practice aimed specifically at freedom of movement within concentration, not merely holding a static attainment; freedom from both craving the material support and admiration genuine attainment tends to attract, and from resentment toward hostility or disrespect from those who don't share one's faith — meeting mistreatment with goodwill rather than retaliation; sustained mindfulness that keeps the four foundations active even within deep absorption, so that craving, conceit, wrong view, doubt, and ignorance find no opening to resurface; and, crucially, never mistaking concentration itself for full attainment, and never disclosing one's own meditative achievements to others — since doing so invites exactly the material attention and social entanglement that undoes the seclusion attainment depends on.

He adds two supporting practices from daily conduct: genuine contentment with whatever robes, food, or bedding a patron happens to provide, without either craving something better or resenting something plain; and eating, dressing, and using every requisite with clear awareness of its actual purpose — sustaining the body and enabling continued practice, never for vanity, status, or sensory indulgence, always within careful moderation. Together, he explains, these resolve the flaws the earlier self-examination surfaced, yielding a mind genuinely purified, unstained, and — reaching what the fourth absorption specifically is called, "the unmoving," untouched by the three cosmic calamities or the eight ordinary disturbances that trouble every state below it — capable, from that stability, of giving rise to the mundane superknowledges. With this, mastery is complete, and the treatise closes its account of the sixth branch entirely: attaining concentration, perfecting it, and finally mastering it, taken together, constitute what it calls "unsurpassed mundane purity of every kind" — a designation, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, the treatise reserves specifically for genuine Buddhist practice, since a non-Buddhist meditator may well reach the same four absorptions, but without this same purification lacks any reliable way to keep the attainment from decaying, let alone to build genuine liberation on top of it.

25. The Seventh and Final Branch Opens: Supramundane Purity in Five Stages

With mundane purity — full mastery of meditative concentration — now complete, the treatise opens its seventh and final branch: supramundane purity, the genuine liberation this entire ground has been building toward, laid out in five sequential stages from first breakthrough to final, complete attainment.

Chinese Source

云何出世間一切種清淨？當知略有五種。一、入聖諦現觀；二、入聖諦現觀已，離諸障礙；三、入聖諦現觀已，為欲證得速疾通慧，作意思惟諸歡喜事；四、修習如所得道；五、證得極清淨道，及果功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is supramundane purity of every kind? It should be known, in brief, to be fivefold: entering direct realization of the noble truths; having entered that realization, removing every remaining obstacle; having entered that realization, applying the mind to what brings genuine joy in order to attain swift, penetrating wisdom; cultivating the path already attained; and attaining the utterly pure path together with its fruit of merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the five stages as tracing a hearer-disciple's entire journey from stream-entry to arhatship. The first is the moment of breakthrough itself — direct, non-conceptual realization of the Four Noble Truths at the path of seeing, the entry into stream-entry proper. The second acknowledges something easy to overlook: even a genuine stream-enterer still carries real obstacles that must be actively removed, a corrective he attributes to the compassionate precision only a bodhisattva of Maitreya's caliber would think to spell out — sainthood is not instantaneous completeness. The third describes the resulting practitioner's own motivation: having tasted genuine realization, and wanting to reach the swift, unobstructed wisdom that exhausts every remaining corruption, they deliberately dwell on what genuinely inspires them onward — the felt lightness, joy, and freedom from coarseness that direct realization already brought, used as fuel for continued effort rather than complacency. The fourth is the sustained cultivation of that same path already glimpsed, carrying the practitioner from stream-entry through the intermediate fruits. And the fifth is the culmination: reaching the utterly pure path in full, together with its complete fruit — arhatship itself, the end of this entire ground's long arc from a merely favorable rebirth all the way to complete liberation.

26. Stage One Unpacked: Even Mundane Purity Is Still "Easy Ground" Compared to Nirvāṇa

The treatise's account of "entering direct realization of the noble truths" opens with a sobering reminder: everything achieved so far — full mastery of the four absorptions, completely free of contaminating afflictions — still counts as the "easy," conceptually graspable territory compared to nirvāṇa itself, which cannot be approached the same way at all.

Chinese Source

彼於戲論界易可安住，謂於世間一切種清淨；於無戲論界難可安住，謂於出世間一切種清淨。是故於彼厭惡而住，非不厭惡。

Ru-Yi Translation

One can readily abide in the domain of conceptual elaboration — mundane purity of every kind — but can only abide with difficulty in the domain free of such elaboration — supramundane purity of every kind. One must therefore dwell in genuine revulsion toward the former; it will not do to remain without such revulsion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a practitioner entering this stage has already genuinely mastered concentration, free of craving, conceit, wrong view, doubt, and ignorance — a real achievement, but still conditioned, still expressible in concepts, and therefore still "easy ground" by comparison with nirvāṇa itself, which is realized only directly, admits no conceptual elaboration, and cannot be reached simply by extending the same meditative skill further. Genuine progress from here, he stresses, requires actively cultivating revulsion toward remaining in that easier, still-conditioned territory — not because concentration itself is bad, but because comfort with it can quietly substitute for the harder work of actually breaking through to direct realization.

That revulsion is then given a precise, fivefold structure: a practitioner examines their own life and finds it still bound to three kinds of defilement — the defilement of an unresolved relationship with death itself, since without genuine realization even a skilled meditator still dies in the ordinary, unsettled way; the defilement of falling, after death, back into the "great pit" of ignorance that underlies all further affliction; and the defilement of the unwholesome karma ignorance's own momentum still generates, capable of leading to a genuinely frightening future rebirth. Alongside these three, the practitioner also recognizes three ways they remain disconnected from genuine purity: not yet having reached the fearless, secure state that comes only from actually severing every affliction; not yet resting on the specific, uncontaminated concentration that makes liberating insight possible; and not yet possessing the correct view, born of that same insight, that leads all the way to complete liberation. Recognizing both — what one is still bound to, and what one has not yet reached — produces exactly the kind of clear-eyed dissatisfaction, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, that finally motivates the harder work of genuine breakthrough, rather than letting hard-won meditative comfort quietly become the end of the road.

27. The Fivefold Revulsion Expanded: Thirteen Further Reasons Not to Settle

The treatise then expands its fivefold self-examination into much finer detail — three further costs of remaining defiled, five further ways purity remains unreached, and five further reasons genuine attainment is hard-won — before naming the resolve that all of this is actually meant to produce: firm, sustained diligence rather than either despair or complacency.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi details the further costs of remaining defiled: rebirth itself is the root of aging, sickness, and death, so remaining unliberated guarantees all three will recur; the "intrinsic suffering" of formations themselves — the underlying instability the tradition calls the suffering of conditioning — can still land a future rebirth in a "region without opportunity," somewhere lacking any Buddhist teaching at all, whether because of location or circumstance, leaving no chance to hear the Dharma even if reborn as a human being; and every possible destination among the five realms remains subject to impermanence regardless of how favorable or how long-lived, since nothing there is exempt from eventual dissolution.

He then details five further ways a practitioner still falls short of genuine purity: an ongoing inability to guarantee never being reborn in a place without the Dharma; an ongoing inability to guarantee never falling to a lower realm; for a layperson, an ongoing vulnerability to committing one of the five acts of immediate retribution or their close equivalents, since only genuine liberating wisdom actually forecloses that possibility; for a monastic, an ongoing vulnerability to any of the sixty-two classical wrong views catalogued in the tradition, absent the same protective wisdom; and, most fundamentally, never having actually put an end to the beginningless, endless cycle of birth and death itself — even reaching the very peak of conditioned existence through meditation alone, as the treatise stresses elsewhere, changes nothing about this, since the underlying causal chain remains untouched by concentration alone.

A third round names five further reasons genuine purity is difficult to accomplish, meant to dispel any illusion that attainment might come easily or by proxy: no one attains liberation without genuinely doing the work themselves — there is no such thing as an "already enlightened by nature" shortcut; unlike many ordinary tasks, no one else can complete this work on one's behalf, no matter how devoted a teacher or how sincere a request, since only medicine one takes oneself cures one's own illness; the work is therefore something a practitioner must resolve to actually undertake, since remaining as one currently is guarantees continued exposure to every form of suffering; simply not committing fresh wrongdoing today is not the same as having purified past karma already accumulated, since karmic causes do not expire merely through the passage of time absent an actual antidote; and only a genuine noble path — from stream-entry through arhatship — actually manifests real purity, nothing short of it. Master Cháng Bǎi closes this whole extended self-examination by naming its intended fruit: not discouragement, but the arousal of firm, unwavering diligence specifically aimed at attainment — a corrective against exactly the laziness that might otherwise creep in once initial meditative comfort has already been reached.

28. Twenty Marks of "Skillful Cultivation," and the Five Causes That Open Onto "Good Abiding"

The treatise now tallies its own extended self-examination as exactly twenty marks in total, explains how repeated reflection on them deepens from ordinary revulsion into intense revulsion, and then opens a second, complementary framework — "good abiding" — naming the five underlying causes, traced from deepest to most foundational, that actually carry a practitioner into the moment of breakthrough itself.

Chinese Source

如是彼以由厭俱行想，於五處所，以二十種相作意思惟故，名善修治。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, applying the mind to twenty marks across five domains, joined with the perception of genuine revulsion, is called skillful cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tallies the twenty marks exactly: three for being bound to defilement, three for disconnection from purity, three for defilement's own further costs, five for purity's own further unattained aspects, five for the difficulty of genuine attainment, and one more — firm diligence itself, deliberately aroused as the sixth and final component holding the other nineteen together. He explains that this reflection is meant to deepen through repetition: a first pass produces ordinary revulsion, fear, and self-correction, but returning to the same reflection again and again — not because the first pass failed, but because sustained practice always works this way — produces genuinely intensified revulsion, intensified fear of remaining bound, and intensified resolve, exactly as contemplating impermanence and suffering more deeply naturally sharpens rather than dulls their impact.

With "skillful cultivation" complete, the treatise opens a second, complementary framework: "good abiding," meant to carry a practitioner from this cultivated readiness into the actual breakthrough of genuine, non-conceptual realization — a state the treatise calls entering the exhaustion of craving, quiescent nirvāṇa itself. Five causes make this possible, and Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to note the treatise presents them in reverse order, deepest and most immediate first: penetrating attention itself, the direct, uninterrupted insight that leads straight into freedom from affliction-based birth; the concentration that attention rests on; the specific object used as the "gateway" into that concentration, whichever it happens to be for a given practitioner — mind itself for one, form for another; the accumulated resources of merit and wisdom that make reaching that concentration possible at all; and, most foundational of all, the preparatory means — the initial groundwork — that make accumulating those resources possible in the first place. Presented forward, from foundation to fruit, the order would run the reverse: preparatory means, then resources, then the gateway object, then the supporting concentration, then penetrating attention itself — but the treatise deliberately states it backward, leading with what matters most directly, precisely because that is the piece most immediately responsible for the breakthrough itself.

29. Twenty Marks, Five Causes: Walking the Full Path to Breakthrough

The treatise now spells out all twenty marks belonging to the five causes — beginning with the subtlest, most dangerous obstacle of all: a trace of pride that can survive almost to the very threshold of the noble path.

Chinese Source

謂於空、無願、無相加行中，於隨入作意微細現行，有間無間隨轉我慢俱行心相能障現觀作意，正通達故。既通達已；於作意俱行心任運轉中，能善棄捨，令無間滅。依無間滅心，由新所起作意，以無常等行如實思惟。由此作意修習、多修習故，所緣能緣平等平等智生。彼於爾時，能障現觀我慢亂心便永斷滅，證得心一境性。便自思惟：我已證得心一境性，如實了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the preparatory practice of emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, a subtle mental state bound up with self-conceit arises — with or without interruption — following along with the attention that enters those gates, and it can obstruct the very attention needed for breakthrough realization; by genuinely penetrating it, [this obstacle is overcome]. Having penetrated it, in the mind's spontaneous flow together with that attention, one skillfully lets it go, causing it to cease without interruption. Resting on this mind of uninterrupted cessation, through newly arisen attention, one truly contemplates through the practice of impermanence and the rest. Through cultivating — and repeatedly cultivating — this attention, the wisdom of object and subject as utterly equal arises. At that time, the confused mind of self-conceit that obstructs direct realization is permanently cut off, and one attains one-pointedness of mind. One then reflects: "I have attained one-pointedness of mind," and knows this as it truly is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five marks as the innermost cause, "penetrating attention," and lays out its danger with real precision. Even a practitioner sincerely absorbed in the three liberation-gate practices — cultivating emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness in preparation for breakthrough — can still harbor a residue of self-conceit so subtle it goes almost unnoticed: a quiet, half-formed thought pattern that runs something like, "I have wandered saṃsāra so long, and now I alone truly see how to end it — I see suffering as suffering, its origin as its origin, cessation as cessation, the path as the path, exactly as it is, in a way others still do not." This is not gross arrogance; it is subtle self-congratulation riding along with genuine insight, and the 聲聞地 (Śrāvaka-bhūmi) warns that it can persist right up to the very edge of the breakthrough itself, precisely because comparing oneself favorably to others is such deeply ingrained habit. Recognizing this pattern for what it is — an obstruction, not a mark of attainment — allows it to be released; from that release, a newly arisen attention takes up the sixteen aspects of the four noble truths (impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self for suffering; cause, arising, accumulation, condition for origin; cessation, peace, excellence, freedom for cessation; path, suchness, progress, deliverance for the path) and applies them without interruption, like rain falling drop after drop on the very same spot. Where self-conceit had blocked genuine investigation before, now, for the first time, wisdom penetrates cleanly — and with repeated cultivation, the sense of a knowing subject and a known object dissolve into full equality, at which point the aggregates' true nature as empty and selfless is directly, non-conceptually realized. Master Cháng Bǎi adds that at the level of consciousness-only practice this corresponds to first realizing that meditative images are mind-made appearances, and then, going further, realizing that even "suchness" itself cannot be grasped as an object — genuine entry into the ultimate nature of mere-consciousness leaves no conceptual elaboration standing at all. What follows this breakthrough is simply direct, wordless self-knowledge, exactly like knowing for oneself whether water is warm or cold — which is why the scriptures describe an arhat's self-knowing declaration ("my births are ended, the holy life is established, what had to be done is done, no further becoming will follow") as needing no outside confirmation.

30. Causes Two Through Five: Concentration, Object, Resources, and Means

Having unpacked the first, deepest cause in full, the treatise moves rapidly through the remaining four, each supplying a further set of marks that steady the mind on its way toward breakthrough.

Chinese Source

又若先以世間道得三摩地，亦得圓滿，亦得自在。彼或於入三摩地相……或於住三摩地相……或於出三摩地相……安住其心，入諦現觀。若得三摩地，而未圓滿，亦未自在。彼或思惟止相、或思惟舉相、或思惟捨相，安住其心，入諦現觀。又有二法，於修現觀極為障礙：一、不正尋思所作擾亂，心不安靜；二、於所知事其心顛倒。……謂為對治第一障故，修阿那波那念；為對治第二障故，修諸念住。又於妙五欲樂習近者，於聖法毗奈耶，非所行處……既遠離已，依諸念住樂斷樂修……又復修習佛隨念等，令心清淨。又復安住諸聖種中。……自於契經阿毗達磨，讀誦受持，修正作意，於蘊等事令極善巧。……依他師教……於時時間，教授教誡，攝受依止。又正加行作意思惟，當知是名第三方便……謂尸羅淨所有作意，名正加行作意思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Again, if one has already attained concentration by a mundane path, whether fully perfected and mastered — steadying the mind through the marks of entering concentration, of abiding in concentration, and of emerging from concentration — one enters the direct realization of the truths. If concentration is attained but not yet perfected or mastered, one steadies the mind instead through the marks of calming, of uplifting, and of equanimity, and so enters the direct realization of the truths. There are, further, two things that greatly obstruct the cultivation of direct realization: disturbance caused by improper reflection, leaving the mind unsettled; and a mind inverted regarding what should be known. …To counter the first obstruction, one cultivates mindfulness of the breath; to counter the second, one cultivates the applications of mindfulness. Again, whoever grows habituated to the pleasures of the fine five sense-desires has strayed from the domain proper to the noble Dharma and discipline. …Having become free of this, resting on the applications of mindfulness, one delights in relinquishing and delights in cultivating. …One further cultivates recollection of the Buddha and the rest, purifying the mind. One further abides within the noble lineages. …One reads, recites, and upholds the discourses and the treatises oneself, cultivating correct attention until fully skilled regarding the aggregates and the rest. …One relies on a teacher's instruction — a master, a preceptor, an instructor — receiving, from time to time, their teaching and admonition, taking refuge in their guidance. And correctly applying attention itself should be known as the third means. …This means that attention devoted to the purity of moral discipline is called correctly applying attention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second cause, the underlying concentration, contributes six marks depending on how advanced that concentration already is: a practitioner who has already perfected and mastered the four form-realm absorptions steadies the mind through three marks — knowing what brings one into concentration, what sustains one in it, and what brings one properly out of it — while a practitioner who has only reached access concentration, not yet perfected or mastered, works instead with three balancing marks: calming an agitated mind, rousing a sunken one, and resting in equanimity once neither fault is present.

The third cause, the "gateway object" one enters concentration through, contributes two marks corresponding to its two chief obstacles. Improper reflection — thoughts of desire, hatred, harm, homeland, family, status, contempt, or anxious calculation about not dying — unsettles the mind, and is countered by mindfulness of the breath, counting each inhalation and exhalation until the mind steadies. A mind inverted about body, feeling, mind, and mental phenomena — mistaking the impure for pure, suffering for pleasure, the impermanent for permanent, the selfless for a self — is countered instead by the four applications of mindfulness. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the 聲聞地 treats these two practices as the foundational entry points for calming the coarsest afflictions before concentration itself can even be reached.

The fourth cause, the resources gathered along the way, contributes four marks: staying away from places bound up with fine sensory pleasure — being content with whatever robes, food, and bedding circumstance provides, and neither inflated by receiving support and veneration nor troubled by receiving little, following the example in the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* of a practitioner who reminds themselves that offerings honor the practice, not the person; delighting in relinquishing affliction and delighting in cultivating the applications of mindfulness, observing throughout the day and night how all worldly rise and fall — one's own and others' — inspires genuine dispassion; cultivating the six recollections (of the Buddha, the Dharma, the saṅgha, moral discipline, generosity, and the heavens) to purify and uplift the mind; and abiding in the four noble lineages — contentment with whatever robes, food, and bedding one receives, together with a genuine delight in relinquishing affliction and cultivating the path — which the treatise calls the most excellent of all provisions for the path, without claiming to have covered every possible resource.

The fifth and final cause, the preparatory means themselves, contributes three marks: study, mastering the scriptures and treatises through recitation and reflection until genuinely skilled in the aggregates, elements, sense-fields, dependent origination, the possible and impossible, and the faculties; reliance on a teacher, receiving a master's or preceptor's periodic instruction and admonition when self-study alone is not enough, following the eight forms of instruction and five forms of admonition the 菩薩地 describes bodhisattvas offering with an unattached mind to disciples of every disposition; and correct application of attention itself, understood specifically as attention devoted to purity of moral discipline — reflecting honestly on whether one's discipline is intact, so that freedom from regret gives rise to joy, joy to ease, and ease, finally, to a mind that settles naturally into correct concentration. Discipline, in other words, is what makes concentration possible at all — the final and most immediate of the "means," even though logically it comes first.

31. Milestone: The Twenty Marks Complete — Entering the Direct Realization of the Noble Truths

With all twenty marks now accounted for — five, six, two, four, and three, drawn from the five causes in turn — the treatise draws the entire "good abiding" framework to its close, describing the moment of breakthrough itself: settled, fearless dwelling within the exhaustion of craving, the peace of nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

彼於爾時，由此五因二十種相攝持其心，於愛盡寂滅涅槃界中，令善安住，無復退轉，心無驚怖。謂我我今者何所在耶？當於如是心安住時，應知已名入諦現觀。如是名入聖諦現觀。又此聖諦現觀義，廣說應知。謂心厭患相有二十種，心安住相亦二十種。除此更無若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, holding the mind by these five causes and twenty marks, one dwells well, without further regression, within the realm of nirvāṇa — the peace of exhausted craving — the mind free of alarm, no longer wondering, "Where, now, has my self gone?" When the mind abides in this way, this should be known as entering the direct realization of the truths. This is called entering the direct realization of the noble truths. This meaning of entering the direct realization of the noble truths should, in full, be understood thus: the marks of the mind's revulsion number twenty, and the marks of the mind's abiding likewise number twenty. Beyond these there are no more, and none is superfluous.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the formal close of the entire framework that began several sections earlier: twenty marks of revulsion (skillful cultivation) paired with twenty marks of abiding (good abiding), forty in total, deliberately exhaustive — the treatise states plainly that nothing further needs to be added and nothing here is excessive. What results is not a state of anxious self-monitoring but the opposite: a settled fearlessness in which the old habitual worry, "where has my self gone?" — the reflexive grasping that assumes a self must be found somewhere — simply no longer arises, because the direct realization of no-self has already answered it. This moment marks stream-entry itself: through the sixteen-fold pattern of patient acceptance and knowledge regarding suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path (the "eight forbearances and eight knowledges" comprising a sixteen-mind sequence), a practitioner directly, non-conceptually cognizes the four noble truths for the first time and attains the first of the four fruits. Master Cháng Bǎi observes that Maitreya presents this teaching with evident confidence — laying out, without hedging, exactly how many causes and conditions must be fulfilled for the noble path to be reached, and asserting that this account is complete.

32. Stage Two Opens: After Breakthrough, Two New Kinds of Obstacle

Stage one of the seven-branch framework — entering direct realization of the noble truths — is now complete. The treatise opens stage two: even after stream-entry, a practitioner still faces obstacles on the way to the higher fruits, sorted into two broad categories, obstacles of daily activity and obstacles of formal practice itself.

Chinese Source

云何入聖諦現觀已，離諸障礙？當知此障略有二種：一、行處障，二、住處障。行處障者，謂如聖弟子，或與眾同居，隨其生起僧所作事，棄捨善品，數與眾會。或復安住常乞食法，而愛重飲食。或兼二處，好樂營為衣鉢等事。或為讀誦經典，而好樂談話。或居夜分，而樂著睡眠。或居晝分，樂王賊等雜染言論。或於是處，有親戚交遊談謔等住，而於是處不樂遠離。……住處障者，謂處空閑修奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，總名為住。依奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，當知復有四種障礙：一、毗鉢舍那支不隨順性，二、奢摩他支不隨順性，三、彼俱品念不隨順性，四、處所不隨順性。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, after entering the direct realization of the noble truths, does one remove the obstacles? These obstacles should be known, in brief, as two kinds: obstacles of daily activity, and obstacles of formal practice. Obstacles of daily activity are these: a noble disciple dwelling among the community may be drawn into whatever monastic business arises, setting aside wholesome practice for frequent assemblies; may settle into constant almsround yet remain attached to food; may, in either setting, take pleasure in busying themselves with robes, bowls, and the like; may, under the pretext of reading and reciting the scriptures, take pleasure in mere conversation; may, through the night, take pleasure in sleep; may, through the day, take pleasure in worldly talk of kings and bandits and the like; or may, in a place filled with relatives and companions and joking, find themselves unwilling to leave it. …Obstacles of formal practice are those that arise where one dwells in solitude cultivating calm-abiding and clear-seeing, called simply "dwelling." In reliance on calm-abiding and clear-seeing, four further obstacles should be known: an unfavorable disposition of the clear-seeing factor; an unfavorable disposition of the calm-abiding factor; an unfavorable disposition of the mindfulness shared by both; and an unfavorable disposition of the place itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these seven activity-obstacles as a candid, almost gentle acknowledgment that ordinary monastic life keeps pulling even an accomplished stream-enterer away from formal practice: communal business and the six kinds of harmony it requires; lingering attachment to food while on almsround, since only intellectually-formed afflictions, not the deeply conditioned ones, have yet been cut; busyness over robes and bowls whether in activity or even during quiet sitting, where the mind still calculates and plans; discussing scripture until it slides into ordinary chatter; sleep, still a subtle, innate hindrance even once the coarsest five hindrances have been suppressed; talk of kings, thieves, food, clothing, and worldly affairs that stirs up greed, hatred, and delusion; and finally the deep, beginningless habituation to enjoying the company of relatives and friends, a habit so old and strong it makes solitude itself feel undesirable. None of these are gross transgressions — they are simply the ordinary texture of communal life, which is precisely why they remain obstacles even for someone who has already broken through to direct realization.

The four practice-obstacles are more technical: pride in one's own cleverness that refuses further instruction blocks clear-seeing; restless, undisciplined conduct of body, speech, and mind — repeated transgressions, followed by regret, followed by an unsettled mind that cannot rest in its object — blocks calm-abiding; forgetfulness strong enough that sinking, agitation, distraction, and clinging go unguarded blocks the mindfulness shared by both; and an unsuitable dwelling place — one of five kinds of flawed lodging, whether too clamorous by day, too plagued by insects at night, too dangerous or disease-ridden, too poor in basic supplies, or too full of unwholesome companions and empty of good ones — blocks practice through the setting itself. Taken together, seven activity-obstacles and four practice-obstacles complete the full account of what a stream-enterer must still learn to set aside.

33. Two Remedies: Cultivating the Joy of Concentration

Having named all eleven obstacles, the treatise turns immediately to their remedy — two broad causes capable of removing both kinds of obstacle at once, the first of which, cultivating abundant joy in concentration, it now begins to unpack in detail.

Chinese Source

又此二障，當知總有二種因緣，能為遠離：一、多諸定樂，二、多諸思擇。多諸定樂，應知略有六種。謂若有已得三摩地，而未圓滿，未得自在；彼應修習止、舉、捨三種善巧，由此發生多諸定樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding these two kinds of obstacle, two causes and conditions, in general, are capable of removing them: abundant joy in concentration, and abundant discernment. Abundant joy in concentration should be understood, in brief, to have six approaches. For one who has already attained concentration but not yet perfected or mastered it, one should cultivate the three skills of calming, uplifting, and equanimity, and thereby give rise to abundant joy in concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the logic plainly: the eleven obstacles just listed persist largely because concentration has not yet become a genuine source of pleasure, and because wisdom has not yet been sufficiently exercised — so the remedy is simply to cultivate both more fully. Deepening one's joy in concentration through calming an agitated mind, rousing a sunken one, and resting in balanced equanimity once neither fault remains is the first of six approaches the treatise will unpack; once concentration itself becomes genuinely pleasurable, the pull toward mundane distraction — communal chatter, food, sleep, worldly talk, attachment to companions — naturally loosens, since a mind already resting in stable joy has far less appetite for those lesser pleasures.

34. The Remaining Marks of Concentration-Joy, and the Second Remedy: Discernment

Where the first three marks of "abundant joy in concentration" suited a practitioner still working toward mastery, the treatise now adds three more for a practitioner who has already perfected and mastered the four absorptions — then turns to the second remedy altogether, discernment, explaining exactly what it examines and why it works.

Chinese Source

若有於三摩地已得圓滿，亦得自在；彼應修習入、住、出定三種善巧，由此發生多諸定樂。云何名為多諸思擇？謂勝善慧，名為思擇。由此慧故，於晝夜分自己所有善法增長，如實了知；不善法增長，如實了知；善法衰退，如實了知；不善法衰退，如實了知。又彼如於晝夜若行、若住，習近衣服、飲食命緣。由習近故，不善法增長、善法衰退，或善法增長、不善法衰退，皆如實了知。即此思擇為依止故，於所生起諸不善法，由不堅著方便道理，驅擯遠離。於諸善法能勤修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

For one whose concentration is already perfected and mastered, one should cultivate the three skills of entering, abiding in, and emerging from concentration, and thereby give rise to abundant joy in concentration. What is called abundant discernment? Excellent, superior wisdom is called discernment. Through this wisdom, day and night, one knows as it truly is whether one's own wholesome states are growing, whether unwholesome states are growing, whether wholesome states are declining, and whether unwholesome states are declining. Likewise, whether walking or standing, day or night, as one draws near to the conditions that sustain life — clothing, food — one knows as it truly is whether, through that drawing-near, unwholesome states grow while wholesome states decline, or wholesome states grow while unwholesome states decline. Resting on this discernment, whatever unwholesome states arise are driven out and set at a distance, through the method of not clinging to them; and the wholesome states are diligently cultivated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi clarifies that the "six marks" of concentration-joy break down as calming, uplifting, and equanimity for a practitioner still shy of full mastery, paired with entering, abiding, and emerging for one who has already reached it — the second group meant simply to keep re-entering that joy often enough that mundane distraction never gets a foothold. Discernment, the second remedy, works differently: it is a superior, self-monitoring wisdom trained on two things. First, in general, whether one's own wholesome qualities — the sense of shame before oneself and before others, reverent affection for the Three Jewels and one's teachers, pure faith, correct reflection, correct mindfulness, correct awareness, guarding the senses, guarding one's precepts, freedom from regret, and the joy and ease that follow from it — are growing or declining day by day, and likewise for their unwholesome counterparts. Second, more specifically, whether the ordinary, unavoidable business of using life's necessities — putting on robes, taking food — happens to be quietly strengthening greed, hatred, and delusion, or, conversely, becoming itself an occasion for reflecting on impermanence and thereby strengthening discipline, concentration, and wisdom. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the two are directly correlated: wholesome growth means concentration itself grows stronger, wholesome decline means it weakens, and the same holds in reverse for the unwholesome side. Once a practitioner can see this clearly and habitually, unwholesome states lose their grip simply because they are no longer clung to unreflectively, and wholesome states are cultivated with real diligence — discernment functioning less as a rulebook than as an honest, continuous mirror held up to one's own conduct.

35. Milestone: Stage Two Completes — Ten Skills Overcome Eleven Obstacles

The treatise now draws stage two to its formal close: ten skills in total — six belonging to concentration-joy, four to discernment — are declared sufficient to dissolve every one of the eleven obstacles named earlier, with the same closing formula used to seal stage one now repeated here.

Chinese Source

如是二處十種善巧，於二處所十一種障，能令斷滅；隨所生起，即便遠離。如是名為遠離障礙。又此遠離障礙義，廣說應知如所說相。除此更無若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus these ten skills, belonging to the two domains, are able to dissolve the eleven obstacles belonging to those same two domains; whatever arises is immediately removed. This is called removing the obstacles. This meaning of removing the obstacles should, in full, be understood exactly as stated. Beyond this there are no more, and none is superfluous.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tallies the full accounting: six skills of concentration-joy (calming, uplifting, equanimity, entering, abiding, emerging) belong to the practice-place domain, and four skills of discernment (knowing wholesome growth, unwholesome growth, wholesome decline, unwholesome decline — doubled across the general case and the case of daily requisites) belong to the activity-place domain, for ten skills answering the seven activity-obstacles and four practice-obstacles named earlier, eleven in total. Just as with stage one's forty marks, the treatise again insists the account is complete — nothing more needs to be added. Master Cháng Bǎi observes that this whole stage carries a quiet but important teaching: practice is never confined to the meditation seat. Because unwholesome states grow precisely where discipline, concentration, and wisdom are allowed to slip, and wholesome states grow precisely where they are tended, every waking activity — walking, standing, eating, dressing — belongs to the path just as much as formal sitting does.

36. Milestone: Stage Three — Four Grounds for the Joy That Speeds Liberation

With the first two stages complete, the treatise opens the third: contemplating four sources of genuine joy, in pursuit of what it calls "swift, penetrating wisdom" — the wisdom of full liberation an arhat alone possesses — since a joyful, encouraged mind is what actually sustains a practitioner through the remaining ground yet to be covered.

Chinese Source

謂聖弟子，已見聖諦，已得證淨。即以證淨為依止故，於佛法僧勝功德田，作意思惟發生歡喜。又依自增上生事，及決定勝事，謂己身財寶所證盛事，作意思惟發生歡喜。又依無嫉，如於自身，於他亦爾。又依知恩，謂有恩者。念大師恩，作意思惟發生歡喜。由依彼故，遠離眾苦及與苦因，引發眾樂及與樂因。如是思惟隨順修道歡喜事故，便能證得速疾通慧。又此思惟隨順修道歡喜事義，廣說應知如所說相。除此更無若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

A noble disciple who has already seen the noble truths has attained confirmed purity of faith. Resting on this confirmed purity, one applies the mind to the excellent field of merit that is the Buddha, the Dharma, and the saṅgha, and joy arises. Again, resting on one's own flourishing in higher rebirth and one's decisive excellence — the flourishing attained through one's own store of [noble] wealth — one applies the mind, and joy arises. Again, resting on freedom from envy, just as toward oneself, so too toward others. Again, resting on gratitude — recollecting the kindness of the Teacher — one applies the mind, and joy arises. In reliance on this, one grows distant from all manner of suffering and its cause, and draws forth all manner of happiness and its cause. Contemplating in this way the matters of joy that accord with the cultivation of the path, one is thereby able to attain swift, penetrating wisdom. This meaning of contemplating the matters of joy that accord with the cultivation of the path should, in full, be understood exactly as stated. Beyond this there are no more, and none is superfluous.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all four grounds for joy. The first rests on the "four confirmed purities" — unshakeable faith in the Buddha, the Dharma, the saṅgha, and the moral discipline shared with noble ones — attained the moment direct realization of the four truths occurs; unlike an ordinary person's faith, which wavers and can be abandoned the moment affliction stirs, a stream-enterer's faith in the Three Jewels, likened to a fertile field capable of yielding boundless merit and wisdom, never again wavers. The second rests on one's own "flourishing" — both the ordinary sense of rebirth improving life after life, and the decisive excellence of having entered the irreversible path toward nirvāṇa itself — measured concretely through the seven kinds of noble wealth: faith, moral discipline, a sense of shame before oneself, a sense of shame before others, learning, generosity, and wisdom. The third rests on freedom from envy: rejoicing in a fellow practitioner's attainment exactly as one would rejoice in one's own, since envy only ever brings unhappiness (there is always someone further along), while freed of it, another's every attainment becomes a further source of joy. The fourth rests on gratitude toward the Buddha specifically, recollecting that had the Buddha not appeared and taught, and had generations of disciples not carried that teaching forward, there would have been no path to walk at all — Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Buddha's vow in the Lotus Sūtra to labor endlessly for beings' liberation, likening it to a parent's love but without a parent's limits or conditions, since even when beings act wrongly and generate affliction, the Buddha never abandons them the way a disappointed parent might. Contemplating these four together, the treatise says, generates enough joy to carry a practitioner swiftly toward the wisdom of full liberation — and, echoing the closing formula used for stage one and stage two, declares stage three's account likewise complete.

37. Milestone: Stage Four Opens — Cultivating the Path Already Attained

With three of the five stages now complete, the treatise opens the fourth: a stream-enterer does not simply rest in what has been attained, but continues cultivating the very same path more deeply, in pursuit of the second, third, and fourth fruits, beginning with what nurtures a genuine, aspirational longing for the path's final completion.

Chinese Source

云何修習如所得道？謂彼如是所生廣大無罪歡喜，溉灌其心，為趣究竟，於現法中心極思慕。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one cultivate the path already attained? The vast, blameless joy thus generated irrigates the mind, and, in pursuit of the final goal, gives rise, in this very life, to the mind's most intense longing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats stage four as, in one sense, simply a continuation of the very same path already walked to reach stream-entry — the difference now is depth, since the afflictions still remaining to be cut are progressively subtler than the coarse, view-based ones already severed. The vast joy cultivated in stage three is not left behind but put to work here: it becomes, quite literally, an irrigating force, watering a longing so intense that a practitioner in this very life yearns for nothing short of the path's ultimate completion. Far from the fervor cooling into complacency once stream-entry secures freedom from the lower realms, the treatise's framing suggests genuine attainment sharpens aspiration rather than dulling it.

38. Stage Four Completes: Four Supports, Five Perfections

The longing just described unfolds into three further supports — a repeatedly-arising wish for liberation, vigorous unbroken effort applied to the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, a genuine love of solitude, and, crucially, never resting content with partial attainment — and these four together bring five distinct dimensions of the path to their own full perfection.

Chinese Source

彼由如是心生思慕，出離樂欲數數現行。如是欲樂生已，發勤精進，無間常委，於三十七菩提分法，方便勤修。又彼如是勤精進故，不與在家、出家眾相雜住，習近邊際諸坐臥具，心樂遠離。又彼如是發生欲樂、發勤精進，樂遠離已，不生喜足；於諸善法轉上、轉勝、轉微妙處，希求而住。由此四法攝受修道，極善攝受……最極損減方便道理，煩惱斷故，獲得殊勝所證法故，亦令喜悅修得圓滿。又修所斷惑品麤重已遠離故，獲得輕安；輕安生故，身心清涼，極所攝受。又此有學，金剛喻定到究竟故，修得圓滿。是名修習如所得道。除此更無若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this longing that has arisen in the mind, the wish for liberation repeatedly manifests. Once this wish has arisen, vigorous effort is aroused — unbroken and thoroughgoing — diligently applied through skillful means to the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment. Because of this vigorous effort, one no longer mingles with householders or renunciants indiscriminately, drawing near only the barest, simplest seat and bedding, the mind delighting in solitude. Having thus given rise to the wish for liberation, aroused vigorous effort, and come to delight in solitude, one no longer rests content with partial attainment, but abides seeking what is ever higher, ever more excellent, ever more subtle among wholesome states. By these four factors, the cultivation of the path is most excellently sustained. …Through the ultimate, most thoroughgoing means by which the afflictions are cut off, one attains the excellent realization, and by this the perfection of delight is likewise accomplished. Further, because the coarse residue of the afflictions cut off through cultivation has already been removed, one attains ease of body and mind; because this ease has arisen, body and mind become cool and clear, most fully embraced by it. Further, this trainee, having reached the diamond-like concentration in full, thereby perfects cultivation. This is called cultivating the path already attained. Beyond this there are no more, and none is superfluous.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as a candid admission that even a stream-enterer needs continual self-encouragement — otherwise complacency creeps in, since technically a stream-enterer is guaranteed liberation within, at most, seven more rounds of rebirth, and could simply coast. The four supports counter exactly that: a repeatedly-renewed wish for liberation, vigorous and thoroughgoing effort applied to the classic thirty-seven factors of enlightenment (the four applications of mindfulness, four right efforts, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of awakening, and the eightfold path), a genuine delight in solitude that naturally follows from serious effort, and — most important — never mistaking partial success for the finish line. These four then ripen into five perfections: delight (in what has already been accomplished — the four confirmed purities, the flourishing described earlier), a related but distinct delight tied specifically to the afflictions actually being cut off (Master Cháng Bǎi explains the afflictions to be cut through cultivation come in nine progressively subtler grades, and only the very subtlest, "lowest of the low," marks the true completion of this process), ease and coolness of body and mind once that coarse residue is gone, and finally the "diamond-like concentration" — so called because, like a diamond, it can sever any affliction whatsoever, however subtle — which brings a trainee's path to its absolute completion. As with every stage before it, the treatise insists this account leaves nothing out and adds nothing superfluous.

39. Milestone: Stage Five Begins — The Fruit of Utmost Purity

The fifth and final stage of branch seven now opens: attaining the path and fruit of utmost purity, arhatship itself. The treatise begins by diagnosing exactly where affliction still lies dormant even in a trainee — in every one of the three basic feeling-tones — and names the two defilement-generating forces that must be permanently uprooted before that dormancy can end.

Chinese Source

謂於三位，樂位、苦位、不苦不樂位，為諸煩惱之所隨眠。有二種補特伽羅，多分所顯。一者、異生，二者、有學。又有二種能發起雜染品：一者、取雜染品，二者、行雜染品。即為斷此二雜染品，入善說法毗奈耶時，能為障礙所有煩惱。此諸煩惱，能為隨眠，深遠入心，又能發生種種諸苦。若能於此無餘永斷，名為證得極淨道果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the three feeling-positions — pleasant, painful, and neither-pleasant-nor-painful — the afflictions lie dormant within them. Two kinds of persons chiefly display this: the ordinary being, and the trainee. There are, further, two forces capable of generating defilement: the defilement of grasping, and the defilement of karmic activity. It is precisely in order to cut off these two defilements that, upon entering the well-taught Dharma and discipline, the afflictions present themselves as obstacles. These afflictions, functioning as dormant tendencies, penetrate deeply into the mind, and further give rise to manifold sufferings. If one is able to cut these off entirely, without remainder, this is called attaining the fruit of utmost purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as, in effect, a final diagnosis of where affliction still hides. Every contact between the six senses and their objects produces one of three feelings — pleasant, painful, or neutral — and each carries its own characteristic affliction riding along beneath the surface: craving tends to arise with pleasant feeling, aversion with painful feeling, and delusion with neutral feeling, since a mind that is neither pleased nor pained is also the mind least likely to remember that all such feelings are impermanent, unsatisfactory, and selfless. This pattern shows up, to varying degrees, in ordinary beings and in trainees alike — a trainee has already cut the coarser afflictions bound up with wrong views, but has not yet uprooted the subtler, innate ones. Master Cháng Bǎi tells the well-known story of ascetics testing Śāriputra by dousing him with water and hurling abuse as he walked the road, expecting to provoke him — and finding him entirely unmoved, his composure itself becoming proof that arhatship is genuinely different from ordinary equanimity. The two roots of this residual affliction are craving, which fuels the fourfold "grasping" (at sense-pleasures, at views, at mistaken rules and rites, and at a sense of self bound up with pride), and ignorance, which fuels karmic activity itself. Only when both are cut off entirely — the wisdom-liberation that follows ignorance's ending, and the mind-liberation that follows craving's ending — does a practitioner attain what the treatise calls the "fruit of utmost purity": arhatship.

40. The Path of Utmost Purity: Ten Factors, Five Aggregates

Alongside the fruit just described, the treatise names the path itself that an arhat embodies — ten factors beyond further training, gathered into the five aggregates of the one beyond training, more traditionally known as the five-part dharma-body.

Chinese Source

又十無學支所攝五無學蘊，所謂戒蘊、定蘊、慧蘊、解脫蘊、解脫知見蘊，名極清淨道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the five aggregates of the one beyond training, comprised by the ten factors beyond training — namely the aggregate of moral discipline, the aggregate of concentration, the aggregate of wisdom, the aggregate of liberation, and the aggregate of the knowledge and vision of liberation — this is called the path of utmost purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the ten factors beyond further training — the eightfold path (correct view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration) joined by correct liberation and correct knowledge — onto the five traditional aggregates: correct speech, action, and livelihood form the aggregate of moral discipline; correct mindfulness and concentration form the aggregate of concentration; correct view, intention, and effort form the aggregate of wisdom; correct liberation forms its own aggregate; and correct knowledge — the arhat's knowledge that the afflictions are exhausted and that no further rebirth will follow — forms the aggregate of the knowledge and vision of liberation. Together these five are traditionally called the "five-part dharma-body": not five separate things, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, but five dimensions of a single, fully accomplished life, discipline sustained effortlessly, concentration free of outflow, wisdom that directly penetrates the four truths and dependent origination, liberation itself — both the active severing of affliction and its resulting freedom from any further conditioning — and finally the clear knowledge that sees all things as empty and dream-like, illusory in the precise sense the Dharma intends.

41. Ten Faults an Arhat Never Has, and the Two Kinds of Nirvāṇa

The treatise closes stage five — and, with it, the entire five-stage path from stream-entry to arhatship — by naming ten faults that never again arise once this path is complete, and by distinguishing the two forms nirvāṇa itself takes: one still supported by a body, one entirely free of any further support.

Chinese Source

所謂依外諸欲所有愁、歎、憂、苦種種惱亂，苦苦相應過失。又有依內不護諸根過失……又有愛味樂住過失……又有行住放逸過失……又有外道不共，即彼各別邪見所起語言、尋思、追求三種過失……又有依靜慮邊際過失……又有緣起所攝發起取雜染品過失。又有發起行雜染品過失……若於如是十種過失永不相應；唯有最後身所任持，第二餘身畢竟不起，於最寂靜涅槃界中，究竟安住……是故說名住聖所住。又若彼果、若極淨道、若彼功德，如是一切，總略說名證得極清淨道及果功德。除此更無若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

These are: the fault, bound to sorrow, of the various afflictions — grief, lamentation, distress, and pain — that depend on external sense-desires; the fault of leaving the senses unguarded within; the fault of savoring and clinging to the pleasure of meditative dwelling; the fault of laxity in both activity and stillness; the three faults, not shared with the noble teaching, of speech, deliberation, and pursuit that arise from various non-Buddhist wrong views; the fault of [mistaking] the outer limit of meditative absorption [for liberation]; the fault of generating the defilement of grasping, comprised within dependent origination; and the fault of generating the defilement of karmic activity. Whoever is permanently free of all ten of these faults — sustained only by this final body, with no second body ever to arise again — abides ultimately within the realm of utterly quiescent nirvāṇa. …This, therefore, is called abiding where the noble ones abide. Whether it is this fruit, this path of utmost purity, or this excellent quality — all of this together is called, in brief, attaining the path and fruit of utmost purity. Beyond this there are no more, and none is superfluous.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps each of the ten faults to the specific factor of the noble path that removes it — correct view removes attachment to external desires, correct mindfulness removes an unguarded mind, correct concentration removes savoring meditative pleasure, correct effort removes laziness, correct speech-action-livelihood together remove the three faults distinctive of non-Buddhist wrong view, correct liberation removes the mistake of taking the fourth absorption itself for final liberation, and correct knowledge removes both remaining defilement-generators. He retells the classic 聲聞地 image of an arhat's "six ever-abiding qualities": seeing a form, hearing a sound, smelling, tasting, touching, or cognizing a mental object, and in every case remaining without elation or distress, resting in supreme equanimity, mindful and clearly aware — so that even when painful contact does occur through the residue of past karma, only the "first arrow" of physical pain is felt, never the "second arrow" of mental suffering layered on top of it, which is exactly the difference the Buddha once pointed to when asked what distinguishes his disciples from ordinary people.

Finally, Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes nirvāṇa's two modes: while the body that carried a practitioner to arhatship still lives, one abides in nirvāṇa "with residue" (有餘依涅槃) — every affliction extinguished, yet the physical aggregates, the residue of past karma, still continuing their course; when that body's lifespan finally ends and no second body ever arises to replace it, one abides in nirvāṇa "without residue" (無餘依涅槃), the utterly quiescent nirvāṇa beyond which, the treatise says, there is nothing higher and nothing more excellent — the true end of the entire chain of ignorance, craving, and grasping (the "path of affliction"), karmic formation and becoming (the "path of karma"), and consciousness through birth, aging, and death (the "path of suffering") that has driven the beginningless cycle of rebirth all along.
