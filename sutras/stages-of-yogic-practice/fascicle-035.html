The Ground of the Bodhisattva Begins

The Ground of the Hearer-Disciples and the Ground of the Solitary Buddha now both complete, the treatise opens its fifteenth and by far its largest ground — the Ground of the Bodhisattva, spanning fascicles 35 through 50. Ten topics carry the entire bodhisattva path from its earliest cause to buddhahood's furthest fruit; this fascicle begins with the very first: the lineage that makes bodhisattva practice possible at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 35 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Ground of the Bodhisattva Opens — Ten Topics Spanning the Entire Path to Buddhahood

The treatise's largest ground by far, occupying fascicles 35 through 50, opens with a summary verse naming ten topics that together carry the bodhisattva path from its earliest seed — lineage — all the way to the hundred and forty unique qualities of buddhahood itself.

Chinese Source

如是已說獨覺地。云何菩薩地？……嗢柁南曰：初持次相分，增上意樂住，生攝受地行，建立最為後。有十法具攝大乘菩薩道及果。何等為十？一者、持，二者、相，三者、分，四者、增上意樂，五者、住，六者、生，七者、攝受，八者、地，九者、行，十者、建立。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Ground of the Solitary Buddha has now been fully explained. What is the Ground of the Bodhisattva? … Summary verse: retention first, then marks, and portions; strengthened resolve, then abidings; birth, embracing, grounds, and conduct; establishment comes very last. Ten topics together comprise the great vehicle's bodhisattva path and its fruit. What are the ten? First, retention; second, marks; third, portions; fourth, strengthened resolve; fifth, abidings; sixth, birth; seventh, embracing; eighth, grounds; ninth, conduct; tenth, establishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps out the sheer scale of what lies ahead: the Ground of the Bodhisattva is organized into four major divisions called "yogic processes" — an initial process (fascicles 35–46, fully three-quarters of the entire ground, covering lineage, the arising of bodhicitta, and the factors of awakening across eighteen chapters), a process according with the teaching (fascicle 47, the marks of a genuine bodhisattva and the states of lay and monastic practice), a final process (fascicles 48–50, the actual fruits attained across the ten bodhisattva grounds), and a process of sequence (the back half of fascicle 50, explaining why the preceding twenty-seven chapters are ordered as they are). The ten topics named in this opening verse map onto that structure: retention (種性/lineage, 發心/arising of the resolve, 覺分/factors of awakening — the ground's largest topic by far); marks (what genuinely identifies a bodhisattva); portions (lay versus monastic bodhisattvas); strengthened resolve (the compassionate resolve marking entry into the first ground); abidings (thirteen stages from an ordinary aspirant all the way to a tathāgata's own abiding); birth (a bodhisattva's deliberately chosen rebirths, ground by ground); embracing (six ways of gathering beings toward liberation); grounds (the ten bodhisattva grounds reorganized into seven broader stages); conduct (four kinds of practice — the perfections, the factors of awakening, superknowledges, and the ripening of other beings — carried out at every ground); and establishment (the hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha, distinguishing awakening from every lesser attainment). He notes the shift in scale this ground represents: where the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples traced one person's path to personal liberation across roughly fifteen fascicles, the Ground of the Bodhisattva will trace the far larger path of a being vowed to liberate all beings, across sixteen.

2. What "Retention" Actually Means — Three Supports Carrying a Being All the Way to Buddhahood

The treatise opens its first major topic, retention, by explaining what the term itself means: three things — lineage, the initial resolve, and the factors of awakening — that each support and carry forward the next, together bearing the entire weight of the path to buddhahood.

Chinese Source

云何名持？謂諸菩薩自乘種性、最初發心，及以一切菩提分法，是名為持。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called retention? A bodhisattva's own lineage, the initial arising of the resolve, and every factor of awakening practiced — these together are called retention.

Chinese Source

以諸菩薩自乘種性為依止故、為建立故，有所堪任、有大勢力，能證無上正等菩提，是故說彼自乘種性，為諸菩薩堪任性持。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because a bodhisattva's own lineage serves as the basis and the foundation, one has the capacity and the great strength to attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening — for this reason, that lineage is called the retention of capacity for all bodhisattvas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "retention" (持) through three complementary senses drawn from the commentarial tradition: a cause retaining its eventual fruit (lineage is the cause that will bear buddhahood as its fruit); a capacity retaining itself (a being with bodhisattva lineage genuinely has what it takes to carry the path, the way a vessel must be capable of holding what is poured into it); and each earlier link retaining and supporting the next (lineage supports the arising of the resolve, which supports cultivating the factors of awakening, which supports buddhahood itself) — three links in a single chain, none dispensable. He explains why lineage alone, even before any resolve is made, already counts as a form of retention: a being with the capacity for buddhahood is already different in kind from one without it, the way an acorn already differs from a pebble even before either is planted. The resolve, once made, then retains the six perfections and both accumulations (merit and wisdom) as its own support — Master Cháng Bǎi notes each perfection's paired double function running throughout: giving breaks stinginess and produces wealth; precepts still unwholesome conduct and produce a fortunate rebirth; patience dissolves anger and produces stability for oneself and others; diligence overcomes laziness and produces boundless wholesome growth; concentration settles distraction and produces inner stability; and wisdom dispels wrong views and produces genuine discernment of reality — six practices ordinary people, hearers, and solitary buddhas can all perform in some measure, but which only a bodhisattva's lineage carries all the way to the far shore of buddhahood, hence "perfection" (pāramitā) applying uniquely to this same six when a bodhisattva performs them. Finally, the factors of awakening themselves — the thirty-seven wings to awakening and beyond — retain and complete the whole structure, since without lineage supporting the resolve and the resolve supporting sustained practice, no factor of awakening could ever be cultivated to completion in the first place.

3. Milestone: Two Kinds of Lineage — Innate and Cultivated, and Why Some Beings Can Never Become Buddhas

The treatise now defines lineage itself: an innate kind, present since beginningless time in a being's own six sense-faculties, and a cultivated kind, built through sustained practice — and candidly names a third category, those with no such lineage at all, for whom buddhahood is permanently out of reach.

Chinese Source

云何種性？謂略有二種。一、本性住種性，二、習所成種性。本性住種性者，謂諸菩薩六處殊勝有如是相，從無始世展轉傳來，法爾所得，是名本性住種性。習所成種性者，謂先串習善根所得，是名習所成種性。此中義意，二種皆取。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is lineage? In brief, there are two kinds: the lineage abiding by nature, and the lineage accomplished through habituation. The lineage abiding by nature is this: bodhisattvas possess a distinctive quality in their six sense-faculties, carried forward from beginningless time, naturally so without contrivance — this is called the lineage abiding by nature. The lineage accomplished through habituation is that obtained through previously cultivated wholesome roots — this is called the lineage accomplished through habituation. Here, both kinds are intended together.

Chinese Source

住無種性補特伽羅，無種性故，雖有發心及行加行為所依止，定不堪任圓滿無上正等菩提。由此道理，雖未發心、未修菩薩所行加行，若有種性，當知望彼而得名持。

Ru-Yi Translation

A person abiding with no lineage at all, precisely because of lacking that lineage, even should such a person generate the resolve and undertake bodhisattva practices as a basis, will definitely never be capable of perfecting unsurpassed, perfect awakening. By this same reasoning, even one who has not yet generated the resolve nor cultivated bodhisattva practices, if possessed of the lineage, should be understood — by comparison with such a person — to already have retention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the innate lineage as genuinely present in the six sense-faculties themselves — not some separate substance hovering apart from a bodhisattva's ordinary eyes and ears and mind, but a distinctive quality already showing up in how those faculties naturally function (an instinctive delight in benefiting others, a compassion arising unforced), lodged since beginningless time in the storehouse consciousness and expressing itself no matter which of the five destinies or what stage of life a being currently occupies; searching for this lineage apart from the six faculties, he stresses, would be searching in the wrong place entirely, since it is neither identical to them nor separate from them. The cultivated lineage builds on this same innate basis through the accumulated force of sustained practice, so the two are not rival theories about what lineage is but two ways of naming a single reality — a seed already present, and that same seed watered and strengthened over time. He then turns to the treatise's genuinely difficult claim: some beings, entirely lacking either kind of lineage, cannot become buddhas no matter how much effort they exert, resolve they generate, or practice they undertake — a position Master Kuījī's commentary carefully distinguishes into three readings (a being with truly no lineage at all can never even generate the resolve in the first place; a being definitively fixed in the hearer or solitary-buddha lineage counts as equally without the bodhisattva lineage, even if circumstance briefly provokes an aspiration; and a being with no lineage who does manage to generate some resolve will inevitably fall back from it, unable to complete the path) — and notes the position is not despair but honesty: it explains why lineage alone, even wholly unexercised, already counts for something real, since a being who merely possesses the capacity for buddhahood is already in an entirely different position than one who does not, whether or not either has yet done anything about it.

4. Milestone: Why Bodhisattva Lineage Surpasses Every Other — Two Purifications, Four Comparisons

The treatise now explains precisely why bodhisattva lineage outranks even the lineages of hearers and solitary buddhas: it alone leads to purifying not one but two kinds of obstruction, and it surpasses the other two vehicles across four further measures — faculty, conduct, skillfulness, and fruit.

Chinese Source

略有二種淨，一、煩惱障淨，二、所知障淨。一切聲聞、獨覺種性，唯能當證煩惱障淨，不能當證所知障淨；菩薩種性，亦能當證煩惱障淨，亦能當證所知障淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, two kinds of purification: purification from the afflictive obstruction, and purification from the cognitive obstruction. Every hearer and solitary-buddha lineage is able only to attain purification from the afflictive obstruction, never purification from the cognitive obstruction; the bodhisattva lineage is able to attain both — purification from the afflictive obstruction, and purification from the cognitive obstruction as well.

Chinese Source

復由四事，當知菩薩勝於一切聲聞、獨覺。何等為四？一者、根勝，二者、行勝，三者、善巧勝，四者、果勝……言根勝者，謂諸菩薩本性利根，獨覺中根，聲聞軟根……言行勝者……菩薩亦能自利，亦能利他……善巧勝者，聲聞、獨覺於蘊、界、處、緣起、處非處中能修善巧；菩薩於此及於其餘一切明處能修善巧……言果勝者，聲聞能證聲聞菩提，獨覺能證獨覺菩提，菩薩能證阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by four measures, one should know a bodhisattva surpasses every hearer and solitary buddha. What are the four? First, superiority of faculty; second, of conduct; third, of skillfulness; fourth, of fruit. Superiority of faculty: bodhisattvas are by nature sharp-facultied, solitary buddhas middling, hearers dull … superiority of conduct: bodhisattvas are able to benefit both themselves and others … superiority of skillfulness: hearers and solitary buddhas cultivate skillfulness only in the aggregates, sense-bases, elements, dependent origination, and valid-and-invalid reasoning; bodhisattvas cultivate skillfulness in these and in every other field of knowledge besides … superiority of fruit: a hearer attains a hearer's awakening, a solitary buddha a solitary buddha's awakening, but a bodhisattva attains unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two-purifications distinction as the deepest reason for bodhisattva lineage's superiority: a hearer or solitary buddha, having severed the afflictive obstruction (craving, hatred, and their kin), reaches genuine liberation from suffering, but leaves untouched the cognitive obstruction — the subtler habit of taking conceptually constructed appearances as ultimately real, obstructing full omniscience rather than mere personal freedom — while only bodhisattva practice, oriented toward realizing both the emptiness of self and the emptiness of phenomena, is capable of removing both. He unpacks the four comparative measures concretely: faculty is measured paradoxically by how long the path takes, not how quickly it's finished — a solitary buddha needs four lifetimes to a hundred eons, a hearer as few as three lifetimes, but a bodhisattva commits to three incalculable eons, precisely because sharper faculties are willing to undertake the harder, longer task rather than seek the fastest exit; conduct distinguishes the hearer and solitary buddha's exclusively self-directed aim (ending their own suffering) from the bodhisattva's dual-directed aim (benefiting self and others at once, out of compassion for the world); skillfulness distinguishes hearers and solitary buddhas' narrower field of study — the aggregates, sense-bases, elements, dependent origination, and valid reasoning, all oriented toward personal liberation — from the bodhisattva's five full fields of knowledge, adding medicine, logic, linguistics, and practical craft, since a bodhisattva committed to helping all beings needs far more than liberation theory alone; and fruit simply names the different destinations these different vehicles actually reach. He stresses this is not a claim that hearers or solitary buddhas fail at what they attempt — both genuinely reach real liberation — only that what a bodhisattva attempts, and thus what a bodhisattva's lineage must be capable of, is categorically larger.

5. The Marks of Giving — How a True Bodhisattva Is Recognized Through Generosity

The treatise now turns from lineage's abstract superiority to something concretely observable: six sets of marks, one for each perfection, by which others can actually recognize a genuine bodhisattva. Giving comes first — not merely the act itself, but the motivation, the manner, and the scope that distinguish bodhisattva generosity from any ordinary act of charity.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩本性樂施，於諸現有堪所施物，恆常無間性能於他平等分布，心喜施與，意無追悔……施物雖少，而能均布；惠施廣大，而非狹小……無所惠施，深懷慚恥。常好為他讚施勸施，見能施者心懷喜悅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas are by nature inclined to give; with respect to whatever suitable object is presently at hand to give, they constantly and without interruption distribute it equally to others, the mind delighting in the giving, with no regret afterward … even when what is given is little, it is still distributed evenly; the intention to give is vast, never narrow or small … when there is nothing to give, one feels deep shame; one constantly delights in praising others' giving and encouraging it, and, seeing another able to give, the heart fills with joy.

Chinese Source

受他寄物，未嘗差違。若負他債，終不抵誑。於共財所，亦無欺罔……其性好樂廣大財位，於彼一切廣大資財心好受用；樂大事業，非狹小門。於諸世間酒色、博戲、歌舞、倡妓種種變現耽著事中，速疾厭捨，深生慚愧。得大財寶，尚不貪著，何況小利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Receiving something entrusted by another, one never mishandles it; owing a debt to another, one never denies or deceives; in property jointly held, there is likewise no cheating … by nature one delights in vast wealth and standing, taking genuine pleasure in putting all such vast resources to use; one delights in great undertakings, not narrow or petty ones. Amid the world's intoxicating entanglements — drink, sensual pleasure, gambling, song, dance, entertainment — one swiftly recognizes the danger and abandons it, feeling deep shame; even great wealth, once obtained, is never clung to, much less some small gain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes giving's marks into motivation and actual conduct. On motivation: joy before, during, and after giving — never the reluctant, calculating generosity of someone parting with something precious, but a mind already inclined toward it — extended equally to every recipient regardless of whether they are friend, stranger, enemy, virtuous, flawed, fortunate, or suffering, since a bodhisattva regards every being alike as a field for cultivating merit, not a candidate to be screened by personal preference; even a trivial gift is given with a genuinely expansive heart, so scale of intention matters more than scale of the object given. On conduct: three kinds of giving in practice — giving the Dharma itself, whether asked or not, using eight distinct qualities of skillful speech (properly sequenced words, coherent support for the teaching, following the topic's natural order, clear and bright expression, genuinely reaching the listener's mind, matching the listener's own capacity, aligned with ultimate truth and timely circumstance, and patient, sustained instruction in the factors of awakening) so that the teaching actually lands rather than merely being delivered; giving fearlessness, rescuing those genuinely terrified of rulers, thieves, flood, or fire according to one's actual ability; and giving material wealth with scrupulous honesty — never mishandling an entrusted object, never denying a debt, never taking advantage of jointly-held property, and gently correcting others' confusion about the value of jewels and treasures rather than exploiting it. He notes the treatise's own sharp point about wealth itself: a bodhisattva may outwardly appear to delight in vast resources, but the delight is specifically in what those resources make possible for other beings, evidenced by an equally swift and genuine revulsion toward the world's usual traps of intoxication, gambling, and idle entertainment — wealth pursued as a tool for giving, never as an end enjoyed for its own sake.

6. The Marks of Precepts — A Discipline Measured by Benefit to Others, Not Rule-Following Alone

Precepts' marks unfold across three registers already familiar from earlier fascicles' ethical teaching — restraining harm, actively cultivating good, and benefiting other beings — but here reframed entirely around what makes a bodhisattva's discipline recognizably different: warmth, honest speech, and a discipline that bends toward helping others rather than merely avoiding wrongdoing.

Chinese Source

於恩有情，知恩知報……如法求財，不以非法，不以卒暴……性常喜樂修諸福業……若見若聞他所受苦……於是等苦過於自受，重於法受及重後世。於少罪中尚深見怖，何況多罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward beings who have shown kindness, one knows that kindness and repays it … one seeks wealth lawfully, never unlawfully, never through violence … by nature one constantly delights in cultivating meritorious deeds … seeing or hearing of another's suffering … one feels that suffering more keenly than one's own, weighing the karmic consequence and future life more heavily still; even the smallest wrongdoing is deeply feared, let alone a greater one.

Chinese Source

其心溫潤、其心純淨，恚心、害心不久相續，隨生隨捨，起賢善心……尊重實語，不誑惑他，不離他親……言常柔軟，無有麤獷；於己僮僕尚無苦言，況於他所。敬愛有德，如實讚彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mind is warm and gentle, pure and clear; anger and the wish to harm never last long or continue, arising only to be released, replaced by a wholesome heart … one values truthful speech, never deceiving others, never dividing those who are close … speech is always soft, never coarse; not even a harsh word for one's own servants, let alone anyone else. One honors and loves the virtuous, praising them exactly as they truly are.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes precepts' marks across the same three-fold discipline named in earlier fascicles — restraint from harm, active cultivation of good, and benefit to beings — but stresses how far the third category is pushed here: knowing and repaying kindness in full or in greater measure than received; earning a livelihood only by lawful, gentle means, never violence or exploitation; delighting in giving, precept-keeping, and meditation as the three grounds of merit, and eagerly encouraging others toward the same; and, most distinctively, feeling another's suffering more sharply than one's own — witnessing violence or cruelty done to someone else and being moved more deeply than if it had happened to oneself, precisely because a bodhisattva weighs karmic consequence and the arc of future lives more heavily than momentary personal pain, and so dreads even trivial wrongdoing the way an ordinary person dreads only grave ones. He notes the treatise's own gloss on this apparent excess of empathy: an ordinary being fears the result of wrongdoing (suffering, once it arrives); a bodhisattva fears the cause itself (the wrongdoing, before any suffering has even begun), since correct insight into cause and effect makes the seed just as alarming as the fruit it will inevitably bear. The register then shifts to demeanor: warmth quick to release any flash of anger rather than nursing it, unfailingly truthful and never divisive speech, and a gentleness of tone extended even to one's own servants — precepts here measured less by which specific rules are kept than by whether the whole disposition genuinely bends toward others' benefit, restraint from harm being simply the necessary floor beneath a discipline whose real center of gravity is generosity of spirit.

7. Patience's Marks, and Diligence's Five Faces — From Rising Before Dawn to Never Being Satisfied

Patience's mark is stated with striking brevity — absorbing harm without retaliation, forgiving readily, and never nursing a grudge. Diligence then unfolds across five distinct faces, from simple early rising through to a striking refusal ever to consider one's effort sufficient.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，性於他所遭不饒益，無恚害心，亦不反報。若他諫謝，速能納受，終不結恨，不久懷怨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, by nature, when harmed by another, hold no anger or wish to injure in return, nor do they retaliate. If the other later repents and apologizes, one accepts it readily, never harboring resentment or nursing a grudge for long.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，性自翹勤，夙興晚寐，不深耽樂睡眠倚樂……於所作事勇決樂為，不生懈怠，思擇方便，要令究竟……於諸廣大第一義中，心無怯弱，不自輕懱……或入大眾，或與他人共相擊論，或餘種種難行事業，皆無畏憚。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, by their very nature, exert themselves — rising early, retiring late, never indulging deeply in the pleasure of sleep or reclining ease … whatever is undertaken is done boldly and gladly, never with laziness, choosing the right means beforehand so the task reaches genuine completion … with respect to the vast, ultimate goal, the mind holds no timidity, never belittling itself … whether entering a large assembly, debating others directly, or facing any other difficult undertaking, there is no fear or hesitation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats patience's mark as deliberately simple by comparison with giving and precepts' elaborate detail: absorb harm without either anger or retaliation, and release an apology the instant it is offered, rather than making the offender wait out a cooling-off period a bodhisattva has no interest in enforcing. Diligence then unfolds across five distinct faces the text develops one by one: armor-like diligence, the basic willingness to rise early and forgo comfortable sleep rather than let ease dictate one's schedule; methodical diligence, choosing the right approach before acting and carrying any undertaking through to genuine completion rather than abandoning it partway; undaunted diligence, refusing to think oneself incapable of the vast goal of buddhahood itself, since underestimating one's own capacity is itself a subtle failure of diligence; unshakeable diligence, entering a large assembly, debating opponents directly, or taking on any other genuinely difficult task without hesitation, since fear of a hard task is simply diligence wavering under pressure; and — about to be introduced — a fifth, insatiable diligence, which refuses to ever regard effort already expended as sufficient. He notes the progression's logic: each face addresses a different way effort can quietly fail — comfort undermining basic willingness, carelessness undermining follow-through, self-doubt undermining ambition, fear undermining courage under scrutiny — building toward the final face, where the danger is not any of these but premature satisfaction with progress already made.

8. Concentration's and Wisdom's Marks — Solitude Genuinely Loved, Knowledge Spanning Every Field

Diligence's fifth face — never regarding effort as sufficient, however vast the task — closes that perfection. Concentration's marks follow: a genuine, not merely dutiful, love of solitude, thin afflictions, and compassion reaching even to enemies; wisdom's marks then close the whole sixfold portrait with an innate intelligence spanning all five fields of knowledge.

Chinese Source

能引義利大事務中，尚無深倦，何況小事……謂諸菩薩，性於法義能審思惟，無多散亂……便生是念：是處安樂，出離、遠離。常於出離及遠離所深生愛慕……於其怨品，尚能速疾安住慈心，況於親品及中庸品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Amid great undertakings that bring genuine benefit to beings, one feels no deep weariness whatsoever — how much less over small matters … bodhisattvas, by nature, are able to reflect carefully on the meaning of the teaching, with little scattered distraction … one gives rise to the thought: this place is peaceful, a place of renunciation and seclusion — and comes to deeply cherish renunciation and seclusion always … toward even an enemy, one is quickly able to abide in a mind of loving-kindness — how much more so toward a friend, or someone neutral.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩成俱生慧，能入一切明處境界。性不頑鈍、性不微昧、性不愚癡。遍於彼彼離放逸處有力思擇。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas possess an innate wisdom, able to enter every field of knowledge. By nature neither dull, nor obscured, nor deluded, they are everywhere able, with real strength, to discern and step away from occasions of heedlessness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes diligence's fifth and final face, insatiability, as refusing to treat even a task as vast as guiding all beings to liberation as ever quite finished — a bodhisattva feels no fatigue even facing that scale of work, which sets a standard no lesser task could ever exhaust. Concentration's marks then unfold in four movements: a genuine, unforced delight in physically remote, quiet places — the kind other people don't frequent, glimpsed and instantly recognized as "peaceful, a place of renunciation," distinguishing renunciation's own joy (leaving a cramped, confining household life) from seclusion's joy (dhyāna's own first taste of ease); naturally thin afflictions, so that concentration comes easily rather than being wrestled into place against constant inner resistance; compassion so genuine it reaches an enemy before it even needs to reach a friend, together with real grief at others' suffering and an eager wish to help; and patient endurance of loss — family, wealth, even imprisonment or exile — met without collapse, paired with unusually strong memory supporting sustained meditative practice. Wisdom's marks then complete the sixfold set with an innate intelligence, built from accumulated practice across past lives, spanning all five classical fields of knowledge: the inner teaching itself (scripture's own structure and meaning), logic (the discipline of establishing a position and refuting a rival's), medicine (diagnosing and treating illness), linguistics (language, writing, and literary composition), and practical craft (everything from agriculture to cooking to civic administration) — a bodhisattva's intelligence oriented outward toward genuine competence in the world, not merely toward liberation theory. Master Cháng Bǎi notes pointedly that this wisdom shows up even amid the very wealth that tempts most people toward complacency: born into riches, with every sensory pleasure available, a bodhisattva still sees clearly enough to recognize indulgence's danger and turn away from it — proof that renunciation here is a matter of insight, not merely of circumstance.

9. Milestone: Why Buddhahood Itself Is Genuinely Inconceivable — Five Aspects, Two Reasons

Having catalogued the six perfections' observable marks, the treatise closes the lineage chapter's portrait of bodhisattva virtue by naming what these marks are actually building toward — and pauses to explain, with striking philosophical precision, exactly why the buddhahood they lead to cannot be captured in thought or word at all.

Chinese Source

是故能與難得最勝不可思議無動無上如來果位為證得因，應正道理，餘不應理。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, this lineage alone is able to serve as the cause for attaining the Tathāgata's fruit — a fruit hard to attain, most excellent, inconceivable, unmoving, and unsurpassed. This accords with correct principle; any other claim does not.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks "inconceivable" through the commentarial tradition's own five aspects and two underlying reasons. The five aspects: buddhahood's own nature is inconceivable, since it can be said neither to simply be the five aggregates nor to be separate from them, neither straightforwardly existent in the ordinary sense nor simply nonexistent — the *Diamond Sūtra*'s "all marks are illusory; seeing the marks as no-marks is seeing the Tathāgata" pointing at exactly this same knot; its location is inconceivable, since it cannot be pinned to any one of the three realms, any one destiny, or any one direction; its manner of abiding is inconceivable, whether described through the bliss of present dhyāna, through calm-abiding itself, through being in a state with or without conceptual activity, or through the three abidings (noble, heavenly, or divine); its unity or multiplicity is inconceivable, since every buddha shares the identical realization of suchness, so that asking whether Amitābha and Śākyamuni are "the same buddha" or "different buddhas" is a question with no coherent answer either way; and its manner of activity is inconceivable, since a buddha benefits beings continuously and effortlessly, without the deliberate exertion that benefiting others usually requires. Underlying all five, two deeper reasons: buddhahood's reality is severed from the reach of language and concept altogether — quite literally beyond what can be spoken, thought, or written — and, being fully beyond the ordinary, worldly frame of reference entirely, no worldly analogy is even available to gesture toward it. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses the practical upshot the treatise itself draws: only the bodhisattva lineage — able to purify both obstructions, unshaken by any rival philosophy, uniquely fitted to something this far beyond ordinary comprehension — can actually serve as buddhahood's cause; claiming a hearer or solitary-buddha lineage could reach the identical destination, however admirable those paths genuinely are on their own terms, simply does not hold up.

10. Even a Fallen Bodhisattva Differs — Brief Suffering, Swift Return, Compassion Kindled Rather Than Crushed

The lineage chapter closes by candidly addressing an uncomfortable possibility: a bodhisattva whose conduct becomes genuinely corrupted by affliction can indeed fall into a lower rebirth like anyone else — yet even there, the underlying lineage still marks a real and observable difference from an ordinary being's fall.

Chinese Source

若被染汙，如是白法皆不顯現，或於一時生諸惡趣。菩薩雖生諸惡趣中，由種性力，應知與餘生惡趣者有大差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

If corrupted by affliction, none of these wholesome qualities appear, and one may, at some point, be reborn into a lower destiny. Yet even reborn there, by the sheer force of that lineage, one should know a bodhisattva differs greatly from any other being reborn in such a place.

Chinese Source

謂彼菩薩久處生死，或時時間生諸惡趣，雖暫生彼，速能解脫；雖在惡趣，而不受於猛利苦受，如餘有情生惡趣者；雖觸微苦，而能發生增上厭離，於生惡趣受苦有情，深起悲心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such a bodhisattva, long abiding in the cycle of birth and death, may from time to time be reborn into a lower destiny — but even reborn there only briefly, is swiftly able to gain release; and though present there, does not undergo the fierce, sharp suffering that other beings reborn there endure; and though touched by some slight suffering, is thereby moved to heightened revulsion, and gives rise to deep compassion for the suffering beings reborn there.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage as the treatise's own honest acknowledgment that lineage is not a guarantee against every misstep — a bodhisattva can still be corrupted, at least temporarily, by heedlessness, wrong views, distraction, or entanglement (the four specific afflictions to be named next), and can genuinely fall into a lower rebirth exactly as an ordinary being might. What lineage guarantees instead is the quality of that fall: brief rather than protracted, since the underlying capacity for awakening reasserts itself quickly; muted rather than crushing, so the sharpest edge of suffering other beings there endure never quite reaches a bodhisattva in the same place; and, most tellingly, productive rather than merely painful — even a small taste of suffering there is enough to provoke a heightened revulsion toward the causes that led there, and, remarkably, compassion for the other suffering beings sharing that same rebirth rather than despair over one's own plight. He notes the underlying logic: lineage is not a shield against affliction's temporary power, but it is a floor beneath how far and how permanently that affliction can actually drag a being down — the seed of buddhahood, once present, continues quietly working even through a fall it did not manage to prevent.

11. Four Afflictions That Corrupt Lineage, and Four Conditions That Slow Its Fruit

The lineage chapter closes by naming exactly what corrupts a bodhisattva's lineage into temporary dormancy — heedlessness, wrong views, distraction, and entanglement — and, separately, four further conditions that, even without full corruption, can still delay buddhahood's swift attainment.

Chinese Source

謂放逸者，由先串習諸煩惱故，性成猛利、長時煩惱……又愚癡者、不善巧者，依附惡友……又為尊長、夫主、王賊及怨敵等所拘逼者，不得自在，其心迷亂……又資生具有匱乏者，顧戀身命，是名第四隨煩惱性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Heedlessness: through previously habituated afflictions, one's afflictions become fierce and long-lasting … delusion and lack of skill: relying on bad companions … being restrained or coerced by superiors, a spouse, a ruler, bandits, or enemies, one loses freedom and the mind becomes confused … and being deprived of life's basic necessities, one clings anxiously to body and life — this is the fourth kind of derivative affliction.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩雖具種性，由四因緣，不能速證阿耨多羅三藐三菩提……先未值遇諸佛菩薩真善知識……雖遇善友為說正道，而顛倒執……雖遇善友為說正道……而於加行方便慢緩、懈怠、懶惰……然諸善根猶未成熟，菩提資糧未得圓滿……若無種性補特伽羅，雖有一切，一切一切種，當知決定不證菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, though possessing the lineage, may fail to swiftly attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening for four reasons: not yet having encountered a genuine spiritual friend among the buddhas and bodhisattvas … having encountered one, yet clinging to a distorted understanding of what is taught … having encountered one and understood correctly, yet remaining slack, lazy, and indolent in actual practice … or having understood correctly and practiced diligently, yet with wholesome roots still unripe. But a person entirely without the lineage — whatever conditions may be present, in whatever category of being — should be understood as certain never to attain awakening at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes these two lists carefully: the four afflictions explain why lineage's wholesome marks temporarily stop showing up at all — heedlessness lets old habituated afflictions run unchecked; wrong views, absorbed from bad company, corrupt understanding at the root, illustrated by nihilist outsiders who deny giving or killing carry any consequence at all; distraction comes from external coercion (a demanding superior, a captor, a hostile power) that leaves no room for a settled mind; and entanglement, detailed into five specific varieties (clinging to one's own body, clinging to sensory pleasure, delighting in unsuitable company, lacking access to teaching that actually accords with the path, and — subtly — being satisfied with only a small attainment rather than pressing on toward the full goal), quietly binds a bodhisattva to worldly concerns before genuine transcendent practice ever gets underway. The four further conditions, by contrast, don't corrupt lineage so much as simply slow its fruit even when nothing has gone wrong: lacking a genuine teacher at all; having one but misunderstanding the teaching; understanding correctly but practicing half-heartedly; or practicing diligently and correctly, yet with the two accumulations of merit and wisdom still short of ripening — each condition assumes the previous one is already resolved, so a bodhisattva can be stalled at any of four distinct points along an otherwise correct path. Master Cháng Bǎi underscores the chapter's closing insistence, repeated for emphasis: however many of these four conditions are fully met, a being genuinely without the bodhisattva lineage in the first place will never reach awakening regardless — lineage is not one condition among the four to be optimized, but the precondition beneath all of them.

12. Milestone: The Chapter on Arising the Resolve Opens — Five Aspects of Bodhicitta

Lineage alone is only the seed; retention's second great topic, the arising of the resolve (bodhicitta), now opens — the decisive moment a being with bodhisattva lineage actually commits, in words and in heart, to becoming a buddha for every being's sake. The treatise analyzes this resolve through five aspects: its own nature, its mode, its object, its virtue, and its supremacy.

Chinese Source

菩薩最初發心，於諸菩薩所有正願是初正願，普能攝受其餘正願，是故發心以初正願為其自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva's initial resolve is, among all a bodhisattva's genuine aspirations, the very first — and it comprehensively gathers in every aspiration that follows. For this reason, the arising of the resolve has this first aspiration as its own nature.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩起正願心求菩提時，發如是心，說如是言：願我決定當證無上正等菩提，能作有情一切義利，畢竟安處究竟涅槃及以如來廣大智中。如是發心，定自希求無上菩提，及求能作有情義利，是故發心以定希求為其行相。

Ru-Yi Translation

When bodhisattvas raise this genuine aspiration and seek awakening, they give rise to this very thought and speak these very words: may I definitely attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening, be able to accomplish every benefit for beings, and ultimately settle them in final nirvāṇa and in the Tathāgata's vast wisdom. Since such an arising of the resolve is a definite wish for unsurpassed awakening and for the capacity to benefit beings, the arising of the resolve has this definite wish as its mode.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the arising of the resolve as the second of five aspirations a bodhisattva will eventually hold (the resolve itself, the vow governing future rebirths, the vow to actually practice, right aspiration in general, and the great vow), with this first resolve gathering in and giving direction to all the rest — its own nature is simply being the very first genuine aspiration a bodhisattva makes. He distinguishes ordinary "worldly" arising of the resolve (an unenlightened being's sincere, repeatedly-confirmed commitment, formed through hearing, reflecting, and practicing the teaching until it becomes an unshakeable vow rather than a passing wish) from the rarer arising accompanied by direct realization of reality itself, reserved for a bodhisattva already on the grounds — this chapter addressing the former, ordinary case, the starting point genuinely open to anyone with the lineage. He quotes the classical formula for actually taking this vow before a witness, spoken three times: "from this day forward, I resolve on unsurpassed, perfect awakening, for the benefit of all beings; whatever giving, precept-keeping, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom I now cultivate, all of it is for the sake of attaining that awakening" — a spoken commitment, not merely a private feeling, precisely because a vow that can be revised at will is not yet a genuine vow. The resolve's mode is this same definite wish stated in that formula; its content, spelled out further, names the destination in full: unsurpassed awakening itself (glossed elsewhere as perfectly pure wisdom, unobstructed omniscience across all times and categories, and the hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha), the capacity to genuinely benefit every being, and settling all beings not merely in personal liberation but in the Tathāgata's own vast wisdom — a scope categorically larger than a hearer or solitary buddha's own aspiration, which seeks liberation for the self alone.

13. The Resolve's Remaining Three Aspects — Its Object, Its Virtue, and Why Nothing Surpasses It

The arising of the resolve is not a wish floating free of any object, nor a bare intention without further merit, nor merely one good aspiration among many comparable ones. The treatise closes its five-aspect analysis by naming precisely what the resolve is aimed at, what it produces, and why it outranks every other aspiration, worldly or otherwise.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩緣大菩提，及緣有情一切義利，發心希求，非無所緣，是故發心以大菩提及諸有情一切義利為所緣境。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas take great awakening, and every benefit for beings, as the object of this resolve and wish — it is never a wish with no object at all. For this reason, the arising of the resolve has great awakening and every being's benefit as its object.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩最初發心，能攝一切菩提分法殊勝善根為上首故，是善、極善，是賢、極賢，是妙、極妙……又諸菩薩最初發心所起正願，於餘一切希求世間、出世間義妙善正願，最為第一，最為無上。

Ru-Yi Translation

The initial resolve is able to gather in every excellent wholesome root belonging to the factors of awakening as its very forefront — it is wholesome, and supremely wholesome; virtuous, and supremely virtuous; sublime, and supremely sublime … among every other aspiration seeking excellent, wholesome ends, mundane or supramundane, the genuine aspiration that arises with a bodhisattva's initial resolve is foremost, is unsurpassed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the resolve's object as ruling out a common misunderstanding: this is not a vague, diffuse yearning but a wish with two entirely concrete targets — great awakening itself, and the actual welfare of every being — held together rather than pursued in sequence, since a bodhisattva's awakening is never sought as a private achievement apart from what it makes possible for others. The resolve's virtue names what this same wish, once genuinely arisen, actually gathers around itself: the five wholesome roots (faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, wisdom) surpassing even a hearer or solitary buddha's equivalent, a temperament naturally drawn to good company, and outward marks already described in the lineage chapter — an open, smiling face, gentle speech, full ethical discipline — intensifying stage by stage from merely wholesome to supremely so, precisely because the resolve, once made, continues drawing every subsequent practice into its own orbit. Supremacy then compares this same aspiration against every other wish a being might hold, worldly or otherwise: it surpasses ordinary worldly aspirations (for wealth, status, a fortunate rebirth) by sharing in, and completing, whatever genuine good those aspirations already aim at; and it surpasses even a hearer or solitary buddha's aspiration for liberation, since only the bodhisattva's resolve aims at purifying the cognitive obstruction as well as the afflictive one — the same two-purifications distinction already established as lineage's own deepest superiority now shown to be already present, in seed form, at the very first moment the resolve is made. With all five aspects — nature, mode, object, virtue, and supremacy — now named, the treatise has fully defined what the arising of the resolve actually is before turning to how it comes about.

14. What the Resolve Actually Does — Entry, Root, Compassion's Overflow, and the Ground of All Training

Having defined the resolve's own nature, the treatise now names what it functionally accomplishes: it counts as entry into the bodhisattva path (though not yet genuine attainment), it is the root from which awakening eventually grows, it is compassion's direct overflow, and it becomes the very ground on which every subsequent bodhisattva training rests.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩初發心已，即名趣入無上菩提，預在大乘諸菩薩數。此據世俗言說道理，是故發心趣入所攝……又諸菩薩要發心已，方能漸次速證無上正等菩提，非未發心，是故發心能為無上菩提根本。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once bodhisattvas first raise the resolve, they are immediately said to have entered upon unsurpassed awakening, already reckoned among the great vehicle's bodhisattvas — this is stated according to conventional usage. For this reason, the resolve is included as entry itself … only once the resolve has been raised are bodhisattvas gradually able to swiftly attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening — never without it. For this reason, the resolve serves as the very root of unsurpassed awakening.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩悲愍一切有苦眾生，為欲濟拔，發菩提心，是故發心是悲等流……又諸菩薩以初發心為所依止、為建立故，普於一切菩提分法及作一切有情義利菩薩學中皆能修學，是故發心是諸菩薩學所依止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, out of compassion for every suffering being, wishing to rescue them, raise the resolve for awakening — for this reason, the resolve is compassion's own overflow … bodhisattvas, taking the initial resolve as basis and foundation, are thereby able to train fully in every factor of awakening and in every training that benefits beings — for this reason, the resolve is the very basis of a bodhisattva's training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful about the first function's precise scope: raising the resolve counts, by convention, as entry into "the bodhisattva count," but not yet as being a genuine bodhisattva in the fullest sense reserved for one who has actually realized reality on the grounds — a distinction the classical recitation formula itself makes plain, phrased as a sincere aspiration rather than a claim of attainment already achieved. He borrows a compact four-part formula from the *Compendium of the Great Vehicle* for what actually gets a bodhisattva through the full three incalculable eons this path requires: purity (the wholesome roots of merit and wisdom genuinely established), strengthening power (the resolve itself, which alone keeps drawing a practitioner into contact with genuinely accomplished teachers rather than drifting without guidance), a firm mind (a vow strong enough that no rival philosophy, no discouragement, and no demonic obstruction can uproot it), and forward advance (sustained diligence carrying the six perfections toward actual completion rather than stalling) — all four already implicit in what a genuine resolve sets in motion. He then unpacks the resolve as compassion's overflow quite literally: not compassion as a separate motivating feeling that happens to accompany the resolve, but the resolve itself understood as nothing but that same compassion taking the specific, structured form of a vow to attain awakening for every suffering being's sake — and, finally, as the basis of all training, borrowing the classical four-element analysis of matter itself (arising, dependence, establishment, and sustaining) to show how every subsequent bodhisattva practice depends on this same founding resolve exactly as visible form depends on the four elements beneath it — abandon the resolve, and the whole edifice of training built upon it correspondingly weakens.

15. Two Kinds of Resolve, and Why Some Falls Are Final While Others Are Not

Not every resolve, once made, actually holds. The treatise distinguishes a resolve that never again reverts from one that eventually does — and, among those that falter, a permanent fall that never recovers from one that keeps renewing itself, tracing the difference back to lineage itself.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩最初發心略有二種。一者、永出，二、不永出……言永出者，謂發心已畢竟隨轉，無復退還。不永出者，謂發心已不極隨轉，而復退還。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva's initial resolve is, in brief, of two kinds: permanently departing, and not permanently departing … permanently departing means that, once the resolve is raised, it continues without exception, never again reverting. Not permanently departing means that, once raised, it does not continue in full, and reverts once again.

Chinese Source

此發心退復有二種。一者、究竟，二、不究竟……究竟退者，謂一退已，不能復發求菩提心……不究竟者，謂退已後，數數更發求菩提心。

Ru-Yi Translation

This falling-back of the resolve is again of two kinds: final, and not final … final falling-back means that, once fallen, one is no longer able to raise the aspiration for awakening again … not final means that, having fallen, one again and again raises the aspiration for awakening once more.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces both distinctions back to lineage itself, already established many sections earlier as the underlying determinant of what a being's practice can ultimately accomplish. A being definitively fixed in the hearer or solitary-buddha lineage may, under the right circumstances, sincerely raise the bodhisattva resolve — moved by a teacher, a text, genuine admiration for the great vehicle — yet, lacking the lineage capable of sustaining three incalculable eons of committed practice, will eventually and permanently abandon it, settling back into their own vehicle's more modest, quicker liberation; this is final falling-back, a door that closes and stays closed. A being who genuinely carries the bodhisattva lineage, by contrast, may still stumble and lose the resolve under difficult enough circumstances — discouragement, poor company, a season of confusion — but the underlying capacity keeps reasserting itself, and the resolve reliably reawakens again and again whenever favorable conditions return; this is not-final falling-back, a door that reopens precisely because the lineage beneath it was never actually gone. He notes the comfort embedded in this teaching for anyone who has genuinely lost their own resolve and worries the loss is permanent: the very fact that one keeps being drawn back to raise it again is itself evidence of which kind of falling-back this actually is.

16. Milestone: Four Conditions That Spark the Resolve — Wonder, Teaching, Loss, and a Degenerate Age's Suffering

The treatise now asks what actually triggers a being to raise the bodhisattva resolve in the first place, naming four distinct conditions — witnessing a buddha's miraculous power, hearing the Dharma taught directly, fearing the Dharma's genuine loss, and, most movingly, simply looking honestly at a degenerate age's suffering and its five distinctive corruptions.

Chinese Source

謂善男子或善女人，若見諸佛及諸菩薩，有不思議甚奇希有神變威力……由此見聞增上力故，於大菩提深生信解，因斯發起大菩提心。是名第一初發心緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a son or daughter of good family see the buddhas and bodhisattvas display inconceivable, wondrous, rare, and powerful miraculous transformations … by the strengthened power of this seeing or hearing, deep confidence arises in great awakening, and from this the great resolve for awakening is raised. This is the first condition for the initial resolve.

Chinese Source

或有一類，雖不見聞如前所說神變威力，而聞宣說依於無上正等菩提微妙正法菩薩藏教……由聞正法及與深信增上力故，於如來智深生信解，為得如來微妙智故，發菩提心。是名第二初發心緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is another who, without seeing or hearing such wondrous power, hears instead the sublime true teaching of the bodhisattva canon, resting on unsurpassed, perfect awakening itself … by the strengthened power of hearing the true teaching and the deep confidence it brings, profound confidence arises in the Tathāgata's wisdom, and, wishing to attain that same sublime wisdom, the resolve for awakening is raised. This is the second condition.

Chinese Source

或有一類，雖不聽聞如上正法，而見一切菩薩藏法將欲滅沒……菩薩藏法久住於世，能滅無量眾生大苦，我應住持菩薩藏法，發菩提心，為滅無量眾生大苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is another who, without having heard such teaching directly, sees the entire bodhisattva canon on the verge of vanishing from the world … [and reflects:] if the bodhisattva canon remains long in the world, it is able to end boundless beings' great suffering; I should uphold and preserve the bodhisattva canon, raising the resolve for awakening, in order to end boundless beings' great suffering.

Chinese Source

或有一類，雖不觀見正法欲滅，而於末劫、末世、末時，見諸濁惡眾生身心，十隨煩惱之所惱亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is another who, without observing the true teaching itself on the verge of vanishing, sees, in a degenerate eon, a degenerate age, a degenerate time, beings whose bodies and minds are corrupted and afflicted, disturbed by ten kinds of derivative affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all four sparks in turn, each requiring progressively less direct exposure to the extraordinary before compassion and conviction ignite: the first condition needs an actual encounter, seen or reliably reported, with a buddha's genuine miraculous power (classically threefold — displaying miraculous feats, reading others' minds, and instructing according to a listener's actual capacity) — proof so striking it directly convinces someone that awakening's own scale of accomplishment is real and worth pursuing. The second needs only hearing the bodhisattva canon's own teaching taught well, its content alone — the path to the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, unobstructed wisdom — sufficient to inspire deep confidence without any miracle required. The third needs neither miracle nor direct teaching, only the alarming sight of the genuine teaching being crowded out by counterfeit versions — teachers offering something that merely resembles the Dharma while actually distorting it — moving a person to resolve on awakening specifically in order to preserve the authentic teaching for others, quoting the *Diamond Sūtra*'s own vow to liberate every kind of being, born from egg, womb, moisture, or transformation, into final nirvāṇa, as the scale of vow this motivation aims at. The fourth and most universally accessible spark needs no direct contact with anything extraordinary at all — merely honest observation of an ordinary degenerate age, unpacked into five classical corruptions Master Cháng Bǎi lists in full: a shortened lifespan (people once living a hundred years now dying young); a corruption of beings themselves (children no longer recognizing parents, no fear of wrongdoing in this life or the next, no interest in generosity or precepts); a corruption of affliction (rampant unchecked greed, violence, deceit becoming simply how a society now operates); a corruption of views (counterfeit teachings multiplying, genuine truth crowded out by confident-sounding falsehood); and a corruption of the age itself (famine, plague, and war arriving in succession) — precisely the five turbidities named in the *Amitābha Sūtra*'s own description of this present world. He notes what unites all four sparks despite their vastly different starting points: each ends in the identical resolve, proving that awakening's motivation can be kindled equally by wonder at the extraordinary or by clear-eyed grief at the ordinary — neither route more valid than the other, since both, followed honestly, arrive at the same vow.

17. Ten Ways a Degenerate Age Disturbs the Mind, and Why Rarity Itself Becomes a Reason to Vow

The fourth condition closes by naming ten specific ways a degenerate age's turbidities actually disturb beings' minds, then draws its own striking conclusion: precisely because even the lesser awakening of a hearer or solitary buddha becomes rare in such an age, a bodhisattva resolves to become the example others can follow.

Chinese Source

謂多愚癡、多無慚愧、多諸慳嫉、多諸憂苦、多諸麤重、多諸煩惱、多諸惡行、多諸放逸、多諸懈怠、多諸不信。

Ru-Yi Translation

Much delusion, much shamelessness, much stinginess and envy, much grief and distress, much unwieldiness of body and mind, much affliction, much wrongdoing, much heedlessness, much laziness, much lack of faith.

Chinese Source

見是事已，便作是念：大濁惡世，於今正起諸隨煩惱所惱亂時，能發下劣聲聞、獨覺菩提心者，尚難可得，況於無上正等菩提能發心者。我當應發大菩提心，令此惡世無量有情，隨學於我起菩提願。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having seen this, one thinks: in this vast, degenerate world, now disturbed by these derivative afflictions, even those able to raise the lesser resolve of a hearer or solitary buddha are hard to find — how much more so anyone raising the resolve for unsurpassed, perfect awakening. I myself should raise the great resolve for awakening, so that the boundless beings of this degenerate world may follow my example and likewise raise the aspiration for awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the ten disturbances briefly — confusion about self and phenomena; no inner or outer sense of shame for wrongdoing; stinginess with resources paired with resentment at others' good fortune; grief when what is loved is lost or spoiled; a body and mind so unwieldy that thirty-seven distinct varieties are classically distinguished (from the sheer weight of past karma itself, through the five hindrances, distracting thoughts, and even the subtle "conditioned suffering" that pervades even neutral feeling); outright agitation of body, speech, and mind; unrestrained wrongdoing; failure to guard against evil or cultivate good; addiction to comfort and sleep that wastes both day and night; and a mind that cannot settle into genuine confidence in the three treasures or the four truths — together painting a complete portrait of a mind in genuine disarray, not merely an unlucky external circumstance. He draws out the fourth condition's own striking logic: rather than being discouraged by how few beings in such an age manage even the comparatively modest aspiration to end their own suffering, a bodhisattva reasons in the opposite direction — precisely because genuine aspiration has become this rare, someone willing to model it publicly, at the largest possible scale, is needed all the more, so as to give others still capable of it something concrete to follow. Scarcity, in other words, becomes the argument for stepping forward rather than for giving up — a strikingly practical, non-mystical account of why compassion sometimes chooses the hardest possible venue rather than the easiest.

18. Milestone: Four Causes Behind the Resolve — Lineage, a Genuine Teacher, Compassion, and Fearlessness Before Suffering

Distinct from the four external conditions that spark the resolve, the treatise now names four underlying causes that must actually be present for it to take hold: the lineage itself, a genuinely qualified teacher (defined by four precise marks), a deep-running compassion that deliberately seeks out suffering worlds rather than avoiding them, and an unflinching willingness to endure whatever difficulty the path requires.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩種性具足，是名第一初發心因。又諸菩薩賴佛菩薩善友攝受，是名第二初發心因。又諸菩薩於諸眾生多起悲心，是名第三初發心因。又諸菩薩於極長時種種猛利無間無闕生死大苦難行苦行無有怯畏，是名第四初發心因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas possessing complete lineage — this is the first cause of the initial resolve. Bodhisattvas relying on being embraced by a genuine spiritual friend among the buddhas and bodhisattvas — this is the second cause. Bodhisattvas frequently giving rise to compassion for beings — this is the third cause. Bodhisattvas facing, over an extremely long time, fierce, unbroken, unremitting suffering in birth and death, and difficult practices, without any fear — this is the fourth cause.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩所遇善友，性不愚鈍，聰明黠慧，不墮惡見，是名第一善友具足……終不教人行於放逸……終不教人行於惡行……終不勸捨增上信、欲、受學、精進、方便功德，而復勸修下劣……功德，是名第四善友具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva's genuine spiritual friend is by nature neither dull nor slow, but intelligent and discerning, never falling into wrong views — this is the first mark of a genuine friend … never teaching another to be heedless … never teaching another to do wrong … and never urging one to abandon superior confidence, aspiration, training, diligence, or skillful means in favor of something inferior — this is the fourth mark.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，雖有十方無量無邊無苦世界，而生有苦諸世界中。於中恆有眾苦可得，非無眾苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, though there exist boundless worlds throughout the ten directions entirely free of suffering, choose instead to be born into worlds where suffering exists — worlds where suffering is constantly to be found, never absent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes these four causes from the four conditions just covered: conditions are the external trigger that sparks the resolve at a particular moment, while causes are what must genuinely be present beneath the surface for that spark to actually take. Lineage, already established at length, is simply restated as the first and most fundamental cause. A genuine teacher is defined with unusual precision through four marks, each guarding against a different way a teacher can fail a student: intelligence and freedom from wrong view (someone confused about reality cannot reliably guide anyone else out of confusion); never encouraging indulgence or supplying its means (a teacher who provides fine food, entertainment, or comfort as if these were harmless is quietly undermining the very practice they claim to support); never encouraging or enabling wrongdoing (never supplying what makes harm easier — the classical example given is poison, fire, weapons, or drink); and, most subtly, never suggesting a student settle for less than what they are actually capable of — never encouraging someone to trade contemplative wisdom for mere reflection, reflection for mere listening, listening for ordinary merit-making, or precepts for mere giving, since a genuine teacher only ever builds on a student's current level, never quietly lowers the bar. The third cause, compassion, is illustrated with striking directness: a bodhisattva with the power to be reborn anywhere, including boundless worlds entirely free of suffering, deliberately chooses instead to be reborn precisely where suffering is guaranteed to be encountered — the eight classical sufferings (birth, aging, sickness, death, meeting the disliked, losing the loved, not getting what is sought, and the raw fact of grasping at conditioned existence) named as what such a world constantly, never briefly, provides — a choice that only makes sense if easing that suffering is the actual point of being there at all. The fourth cause, fearlessness before difficulty, closes the set: a willingness to face this same suffering, over an incalculably long stretch of time, without the eventual buckling that would abandon the whole enterprise partway through.

19. Fearlessness Before Suffering Closes the Four Causes — Eight Kinds of Hardship, Willingly Faced

The fourth and final cause, fearlessness, is unpacked into four sub-reasons — innate courage, sharp discernment, unshakeable confidence in the goal, and compassion deep enough to make hardship bearable — and illustrated with eight concrete categories of difficulty a bodhisattva commits to enduring without flinching.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩性自勇健，堪忍有力……又諸菩薩性自聰敏，能正思惟，具思擇力……又諸菩薩能於無上正等菩提成就上品清淨信解……又諸菩薩於諸眾生成就上品深心悲愍，當知是名第四因緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas are by nature courageous, capable of enduring, and strong … by nature sharp and intelligent, able to reflect correctly, possessing the power of careful discernment … able to establish supreme, pure confidence and conviction in unsurpassed, perfect awakening … and able to establish supreme, profound compassion for beings — this last is the fourth condition for fearlessness.

Chinese Source

難行苦行尚無怯畏，何況小苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Even the hardest, most difficult practices bring no fear whatsoever — how much less any lesser hardship.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the eight categories of hardship the treatise names as things a bodhisattva must be able to bear without wavering: hardship from the basic requisites themselves (accepting whatever clothing, food, shelter, or medicine is actually available, however coarse, without resentment); hardship from the ordinary vicissitudes of worldly life (the classical eight worldly conditions — gain and loss, honor and disgrace, praise and blame, pleasure and pain); hardship from sustained physical posture (long stretches of walking, standing, sitting, or lying down maintained without complaint); hardship from the practices of gathering others (serving the three treasures and one's elders, receiving and then teaching the Dharma to others, reciting it aloud, reflecting on it in solitude, and actually cultivating calm-abiding and insight); hardship specific to a monastic's daily begging round; hardship from sheer diligent exertion itself; hardship incurred specifically in benefiting others; and hardship from one's ordinary livelihood, whether a monastic's simple upkeep or a layperson's trade, farming, or public service. He notes the striking case cited from the classical literature — the Buddha's own past life as a Brahmin youth who gave his body to a hungry demon in exchange for half a verse of teaching — offered not as an exotic legend but as the outer edge of what this same fearlessness, taken to its logical completion, actually looks like. With this fourth cause closed, all four causes underlying the resolve — complete lineage, a genuine teacher, deep compassion, and fearlessness before hardship — now stand fully defined, alongside the four conditions that spark it.

20. Milestone: Four Powers Behind the Resolve — and Why Some Vows Hold Firm While Others Waver

Closing the whole "conditions, causes, powers" framework, the treatise names four distinct powers that can actually deliver the resolve — one's own strength, another's influence, accumulated past habituation, and present effort — then draws a sharp and practically important conclusion: resolves arising from different combinations of these powers differ in how firmly they actually hold.

Chinese Source

云何四力？一者、自力，二者、他力，三者、因力，四者、加行力……謂諸菩薩由自功力，能於無上正等菩提深生愛樂，是名第一初發心力。又諸菩薩由他功力……是名第二初發心力。又諸菩薩宿習大乘相應善法……即能速疾發菩提心……是名第三初發心力。又諸菩薩於現法中，親近善士，聽聞正法，諦思惟等……長時修習種種善法，由此加行發菩提心，是名第四初發心力。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the four powers? First, one's own power; second, another's power; third, the power of past cause; fourth, the power of present effort … through one's own power, deep affection for unsurpassed awakening arises — this is the first … through another's power, this same affection arises — this is the second … through wholesome practices habituated toward the great vehicle in past lives, merely glimpsing the buddhas and bodhisattvas, or hearing them praised, is enough to swiftly raise the resolve — this is the third … through drawing near a genuine teacher in this very life, hearing the true teaching, reflecting on it carefully, and sustaining long practice of wholesome qualities, the resolve is raised through this very effort — this is the fourth.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩依上總別四緣、四因，或由自力、或由因力、或總二力而發心者，當知此心堅固無動……或由他力、或加行力、或總二力而發心者，當知此心不堅不固，亦非無動。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas raise the resolve based on the conditions and causes described above, through their own power, or the power of past cause, or both together, one should know that resolve is firm and unshakeable … but if raised through another's power, or the power of present effort, or both together, one should know that resolve is neither firm nor stable, and not, in fact, unshakeable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing distinction as the single most practically important point in the entire discussion of the resolve's arising: a resolve rooted in one's own genuine conviction, or in a wholesome habituation already carried over from past lives — both, at root, expressions of the underlying lineage reasserting itself — proves stable precisely because nothing external needs to keep sustaining it. A resolve raised mainly because of another person's influence (a beloved teacher's encouragement, a moving example) or through present-life effort alone (diligent practice not yet backed by deep habituation) can be entirely genuine in the moment, yet remains vulnerable, since it depends more heavily on conditions — a teacher's continued presence, an effort that might eventually flag — that are not guaranteed to persist. He is careful to note this is not a judgment that other-power or effort-power resolves are false or worthless; both are entirely real and can mature over time into the same firm stability the self-power and cause-power resolves already have — but a practitioner who understands which kind their own resolve currently is can watch for exactly the right danger: not doubting the resolve's sincerity, but recognizing that a resolve still leaning on external support needs that support reinforced, or needs the deeper habituation still to be built, rather than assuming firmness that isn't there yet. With this, the entire "four conditions, four causes, four powers" framework for how the bodhisattva resolve actually arises is now complete.

21. What Makes Resolve Retreat, and Two Marvels Unique to the Bodhisattva Alone

The mirror image of the four causes names four conditions that cause the resolve to retreat instead — then the treatise turns to describing what firm, unretreating resolve actually looks like in practice, starting with two qualities shared with no other being: embracing literally everyone as kin, and doing so without the possessiveness that ordinarily accompanies close attachment.

Chinese Source

有四因緣能令菩薩退菩提心……一、種姓不具；二、惡友所攝；三、於諸眾生悲心微薄；四、於極長時種種猛利無間無闕生死大苦難行苦行，其心極生怯畏驚怖。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four conditions can cause a bodhisattva's resolve for awakening to retreat: lacking the lineage; being taken in by bad companions; having only thin compassion for beings; and, faced with the fierce, unbroken, unremitting suffering and difficult practice demanded over an extremely long time, the mind gives rise to genuine timidity, fear, and alarm.

Chinese Source

最初發心堅固菩薩，略有二種不共世間甚希奇法。何等為二？一者、攝諸眾生皆為眷屬，二者、攝眷屬過所不能染。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva whose initial resolve is firm has, in brief, two marvelous qualities shared with no other being at all. What are the two? First, embracing every being as kin; second, being untouched by the faults that ordinarily accompany embracing kin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the four causes of retreat map exactly onto the reverse of the four causes of firm resolve already established — lacking lineage, keeping bad company, thin compassion, and fear of the path's sheer length — nothing new to add beyond recognizing the same four factors work in both directions. He then draws out why the two "unshared marvels" genuinely startle: ordinary affection is inherently selective, embracing those we like and keeping distance from those we don't, yet a bodhisattva's resolve, the instant it arises, extends kinship to literally every being without exception — friend, stranger, and enemy treated with the identical warmth ordinarily reserved only for one's closest family. What makes this even more remarkable is the second marvel: ordinary kinship almost always comes bundled with its own subtle corruption — favoring whichever "family member" is more agreeable, resenting whichever one causes trouble, the very love parents feel for their children still shading into partiality between siblings. A bodhisattva's embrace of all beings as kin carries none of this: neither extra warmth for the compliant nor withdrawal from the difficult, precisely because the motivation was never personal preference to begin with, only an unwavering wish for every being's genuine benefit, applied with the same steadiness whether the being in question is grateful or hostile in return.

22. Firm Resolve in Practice — Two Aspirations, Two Kinds of Effort, and a Superiority in Both Cause and Fruit

Beyond its two unshared marvels, firm resolve manifests concretely through two aspirations (wishing benefit and wishing happiness for beings), two kinds of sustained effort (deepening the aspiration itself, and actually carrying it out), and a superiority — over every other being's wholesome practice — that holds true in both cause and fruit at once.

Chinese Source

最初發心堅固菩薩，於諸眾生發起二種善勝意樂。一者、利益意樂，二者、安樂意樂。利益意樂者，謂欲從彼諸不善處，拔濟眾生，安置善處。安樂意樂者，謂於貧匱無依無怙諸眾生所，離染汙心，欲與種種饒益樂具。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva of firm initial resolve gives rise to two excellent, superior aspirations toward beings: the aspiration to benefit, and the aspiration to bring happiness. The aspiration to benefit is the wish to rescue beings from unwholesome states and settle them in wholesome ones. The aspiration to bring happiness is the wish, free of any defiling motive, to give beneficial things to beings who are destitute, without support, and without protection.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩所攝善法，皆是無上正等菩提能證因故，所證無上正等菩提是此果故，比餘一切聲聞、獨覺所攝善法尚為殊勝，何況比餘一切有情所攝善法。是故菩薩所攝善法，比餘一切所攝善法，因果俱勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wholesome practices a bodhisattva takes up are all causes capable of realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening, and that same awakening is their fruit — surpassing even the wholesome practices of hearers and solitary buddhas, let alone those of every other being. For this reason, a bodhisattva's wholesome practices surpass every other being's in both cause and fruit alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi pairs the two aspirations with compassion's classical two functions: bringing benefit corresponds to removing suffering (a mother's instinct to pull a child back from danger), while bringing happiness corresponds to giving joy (a mother's instinct to actually provide for a child's wellbeing) — benefit as the cause, happiness as its natural result. He then describes the two kinds of sustained effort that keep this pair of aspirations alive rather than letting them fade into good intentions never acted on: aspiration-effort, simply continuing to deepen the wish itself day and night through repeated reflection (citing the classical principle that karmic force, wholesome or unwholesome, only strengthens through repeated rehearsal — a bodhisattva's compassion grows the same way any habituated intention grows, through practice, not through a single moment of feeling); and actual-practice-effort, matching that same growing aspiration with real conduct — cultivating the six perfections to ripen one's own capacity for awakening while simultaneously, according to whatever ability is actually available, benefiting others in fact rather than merely in wish. Ripening itself is illustrated with three classical similes: a boil finally ready to be lanced, unfired clay finally fired into a usable vessel, and fruit finally ripe enough to eat — each showing that spiritual maturity, like these ordinary processes, cannot be rushed past its own necessary stages. He closes by explaining why the treatise insists a bodhisattva's wholesome practice surpasses every rival practice in cause and fruit together: an ordinary being's good deed produces its own limited good result and stops there, while a bodhisattva's identical-seeming act of generosity or patience is simultaneously building toward the single most far-reaching fruit available to any being — a superiority not of effort or sincerity, which other paths share, but of scale, since only this path's cause is oriented toward that particular fruit at all.

23. Milestone: The Concrete Blessings of Firm Resolve — Universal Reverence, and Protection Beyond a King's

The chapter's account of firm resolve closes with something startlingly concrete: the moment the resolve genuinely takes hold, a bodhisattva becomes a field of merit worthy of everyone's reverence, a spiritual parent to every being — and gains a cluster of tangible protections, from supernatural safekeeping exceeding even a universal monarch's, to a body strikingly resistant to illness and fatigue.

Chinese Source

最初發心堅固菩薩，略有二種發心勝利。一者、初發菩提心已，即是眾生尊重福田，一切眾生皆應供養；亦作一切眾生父母。二者、初發菩提心已，即能攝受無惱害福。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva of firm initial resolve gains, in brief, two benefits from that resolve. First, the very moment the resolve for awakening is raised, one becomes a field of merit deserving all beings' reverence, worthy of everyone's offering, and, in effect, a parent to every being. Second, the very moment the resolve is raised, one gains harmless merit.

Chinese Source

由此菩薩成就如是無惱害福，得倍輪王護所守護；由得如是護所護故，若寢、若寤、若迷悶等，一切魍魎、藥叉、宅神、人非人等不能嬈害。又此菩薩轉受餘生……少病、無病，不為長時重病所觸……於諸眾生所作義利，能以身語勇猛而作。常為眾生宣說正法，身無極倦，念無忘失，心無勞損。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this harmless merit, the bodhisattva is protected by a guard exceeding even a universal monarch's; sleeping, waking, or even unconscious, no goblin, spirit, household deity, human, or nonhuman being is able to cause harm. In whatever future rebirth is taken, this same merit ensures little or no illness, never touched by prolonged serious sickness … whatever benefits beings, one acts on boldly with body and speech; constantly teaching beings the true Dharma, the body never grows exhausted, mindfulness never fails, and the mind never wears down.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first benefit as simply following from what the resolve already accomplishes: having embraced literally every being as kin the instant the resolve arose, a bodhisattva becomes worthy of reverence not through personal achievement yet but through sheer scope of intention alone — likened to a parent whose singular, undivided love for every being resembles the intense devotion ordinarily reserved for an only child, extended here to all beings equally. The second benefit, harmless merit, he unpacks through eight concrete marks, the first four already given: supernatural protection exceeding even a universal monarch's own retinue of guardian spirits (classically five hundred for a monarch, a full thousand for a bodhisattva, per one commentarial count), since a being this vast in compassionate intention is naturally shielded by forces no ordinary malice can penetrate; a body strikingly resistant to illness across successive rebirths, simply because sustained wholesome action leaves its own trace on physical vitality; an inexhaustible capacity to teach — illustrated with the example of an elderly teacher still lecturing on the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra* into their nineties, undiminished by age; and progressively lightened "grossness" — the technical term for whatever leaves body and mind sluggish, unresponsive, or incapable, itself rooted in affliction's own seeds and already thinner even before the resolve, thinner still once it takes hold. He notes what unites all these blessings: none of them are magical rewards handed down externally, but the entirely natural, this-worldly consequence of what compassion this genuine, sustained this long, actually does to a being's body, reputation, and vitality over time.
