Fascicle 35 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十五 · The Ground of the Bodhisattva Opens

The Ground of the Bodhisattva Begins

The Ground of the Hearer-Disciples and the Ground of the Solitary Buddha now both complete, the treatise opens its fifteenth and by far its largest ground — the Ground of the Bodhisattva, spanning fascicles 35 through 50. Ten topics carry the entire bodhisattva path from its earliest cause to buddhahood's furthest fruit; this fascicle begins with the very first: the lineage that makes bodhisattva practice possible at all.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 35 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Ground of the Bodhisattva Opens — Ten Topics Spanning the Entire Path to Buddhahood

The treatise's largest ground by far, occupying fascicles 35 through 50, opens with a summary verse naming ten topics that together carry the bodhisattva path from its earliest seed — lineage — all the way to the hundred and forty unique qualities of buddhahood itself.

Chinese Source

如是已說獨覺地。云何菩薩地？……嗢柁南曰：初持次相分，增上意樂住，生攝受地行，建立最為後。有十法具攝大乘菩薩道及果。何等為十？一者、持，二者、相，三者、分，四者、增上意樂，五者、住，六者、生，七者、攝受，八者、地，九者、行，十者、建立。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Ground of the Solitary Buddha has now been fully explained. What is the Ground of the Bodhisattva? … Summary verse: retention first, then marks, and portions; strengthened resolve, then abidings; birth, embracing, grounds, and conduct; establishment comes very last. Ten topics together comprise the great vehicle's bodhisattva path and its fruit. What are the ten? First, retention; second, marks; third, portions; fourth, strengthened resolve; fifth, abidings; sixth, birth; seventh, embracing; eighth, grounds; ninth, conduct; tenth, establishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps out the sheer scale of what lies ahead: the Ground of the Bodhisattva is organized into four major divisions called "yogic processes" — an initial process (fascicles 35–46, fully three-quarters of the entire ground, covering lineage, the arising of bodhicitta, and the factors of awakening across eighteen chapters), a process according with the teaching (fascicle 47, the marks of a genuine bodhisattva and the states of lay and monastic practice), a final process (fascicles 48–50, the actual fruits attained across the ten bodhisattva grounds), and a process of sequence (the back half of fascicle 50, explaining why the preceding twenty-seven chapters are ordered as they are). The ten topics named in this opening verse map onto that structure: retention (種性/lineage, 發心/arising of the resolve, 覺分/factors of awakening — the ground's largest topic by far); marks (what genuinely identifies a bodhisattva); portions (lay versus monastic bodhisattvas); strengthened resolve (the compassionate resolve marking entry into the first ground); abidings (thirteen stages from an ordinary aspirant all the way to a tathāgata's own abiding); birth (a bodhisattva's deliberately chosen rebirths, ground by ground); embracing (six ways of gathering beings toward liberation); grounds (the ten bodhisattva grounds reorganized into seven broader stages); conduct (four kinds of practice — the perfections, the factors of awakening, superknowledges, and the ripening of other beings — carried out at every ground); and establishment (the hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha, distinguishing awakening from every lesser attainment). He notes the shift in scale this ground represents: where the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples traced one person's path to personal liberation across roughly fifteen fascicles, the Ground of the Bodhisattva will trace the far larger path of a being vowed to liberate all beings, across sixteen.

2. What "Retention" Actually Means — Three Supports Carrying a Being All the Way to Buddhahood

The treatise opens its first major topic, retention, by explaining what the term itself means: three things — lineage, the initial resolve, and the factors of awakening — that each support and carry forward the next, together bearing the entire weight of the path to buddhahood.

Chinese Source

云何名持？謂諸菩薩自乘種性、最初發心，及以一切菩提分法，是名為持。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called retention? A bodhisattva's own lineage, the initial arising of the resolve, and every factor of awakening practiced — these together are called retention.

Chinese Source

以諸菩薩自乘種性為依止故、為建立故，有所堪任、有大勢力，能證無上正等菩提，是故說彼自乘種性，為諸菩薩堪任性持。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because a bodhisattva's own lineage serves as the basis and the foundation, one has the capacity and the great strength to attain unsurpassed, perfect awakening — for this reason, that lineage is called the retention of capacity for all bodhisattvas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "retention" (持) through three complementary senses drawn from the commentarial tradition: a cause retaining its eventual fruit (lineage is the cause that will bear buddhahood as its fruit); a capacity retaining itself (a being with bodhisattva lineage genuinely has what it takes to carry the path, the way a vessel must be capable of holding what is poured into it); and each earlier link retaining and supporting the next (lineage supports the arising of the resolve, which supports cultivating the factors of awakening, which supports buddhahood itself) — three links in a single chain, none dispensable. He explains why lineage alone, even before any resolve is made, already counts as a form of retention: a being with the capacity for buddhahood is already different in kind from one without it, the way an acorn already differs from a pebble even before either is planted. The resolve, once made, then retains the six perfections and both accumulations (merit and wisdom) as its own support — Master Cháng Bǎi notes each perfection's paired double function running throughout: giving breaks stinginess and produces wealth; precepts still unwholesome conduct and produce a fortunate rebirth; patience dissolves anger and produces stability for oneself and others; diligence overcomes laziness and produces boundless wholesome growth; concentration settles distraction and produces inner stability; and wisdom dispels wrong views and produces genuine discernment of reality — six practices ordinary people, hearers, and solitary buddhas can all perform in some measure, but which only a bodhisattva's lineage carries all the way to the far shore of buddhahood, hence "perfection" (pāramitā) applying uniquely to this same six when a bodhisattva performs them. Finally, the factors of awakening themselves — the thirty-seven wings to awakening and beyond — retain and complete the whole structure, since without lineage supporting the resolve and the resolve supporting sustained practice, no factor of awakening could ever be cultivated to completion in the first place.

3. Milestone: Two Kinds of Lineage — Innate and Cultivated, and Why Some Beings Can Never Become Buddhas

The treatise now defines lineage itself: an innate kind, present since beginningless time in a being's own six sense-faculties, and a cultivated kind, built through sustained practice — and candidly names a third category, those with no such lineage at all, for whom buddhahood is permanently out of reach.

Chinese Source

云何種性？謂略有二種。一、本性住種性，二、習所成種性。本性住種性者，謂諸菩薩六處殊勝有如是相，從無始世展轉傳來，法爾所得，是名本性住種性。習所成種性者，謂先串習善根所得，是名習所成種性。此中義意，二種皆取。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is lineage? In brief, there are two kinds: the lineage abiding by nature, and the lineage accomplished through habituation. The lineage abiding by nature is this: bodhisattvas possess a distinctive quality in their six sense-faculties, carried forward from beginningless time, naturally so without contrivance — this is called the lineage abiding by nature. The lineage accomplished through habituation is that obtained through previously cultivated wholesome roots — this is called the lineage accomplished through habituation. Here, both kinds are intended together.

Chinese Source

住無種性補特伽羅，無種性故，雖有發心及行加行為所依止，定不堪任圓滿無上正等菩提。由此道理，雖未發心、未修菩薩所行加行，若有種性，當知望彼而得名持。

Ru-Yi Translation

A person abiding with no lineage at all, precisely because of lacking that lineage, even should such a person generate the resolve and undertake bodhisattva practices as a basis, will definitely never be capable of perfecting unsurpassed, perfect awakening. By this same reasoning, even one who has not yet generated the resolve nor cultivated bodhisattva practices, if possessed of the lineage, should be understood — by comparison with such a person — to already have retention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the innate lineage as genuinely present in the six sense-faculties themselves — not some separate substance hovering apart from a bodhisattva's ordinary eyes and ears and mind, but a distinctive quality already showing up in how those faculties naturally function (an instinctive delight in benefiting others, a compassion arising unforced), lodged since beginningless time in the storehouse consciousness and expressing itself no matter which of the five destinies or what stage of life a being currently occupies; searching for this lineage apart from the six faculties, he stresses, would be searching in the wrong place entirely, since it is neither identical to them nor separate from them. The cultivated lineage builds on this same innate basis through the accumulated force of sustained practice, so the two are not rival theories about what lineage is but two ways of naming a single reality — a seed already present, and that same seed watered and strengthened over time. He then turns to the treatise's genuinely difficult claim: some beings, entirely lacking either kind of lineage, cannot become buddhas no matter how much effort they exert, resolve they generate, or practice they undertake — a position Master Kuījī's commentary carefully distinguishes into three readings (a being with truly no lineage at all can never even generate the resolve in the first place; a being definitively fixed in the hearer or solitary-buddha lineage counts as equally without the bodhisattva lineage, even if circumstance briefly provokes an aspiration; and a being with no lineage who does manage to generate some resolve will inevitably fall back from it, unable to complete the path) — and notes the position is not despair but honesty: it explains why lineage alone, even wholly unexercised, already counts for something real, since a being who merely possesses the capacity for buddhahood is already in an entirely different position than one who does not, whether or not either has yet done anything about it.

4. Milestone: Why Bodhisattva Lineage Surpasses Every Other — Two Purifications, Four Comparisons

The treatise now explains precisely why bodhisattva lineage outranks even the lineages of hearers and solitary buddhas: it alone leads to purifying not one but two kinds of obstruction, and it surpasses the other two vehicles across four further measures — faculty, conduct, skillfulness, and fruit.

Chinese Source

略有二種淨，一、煩惱障淨，二、所知障淨。一切聲聞、獨覺種性，唯能當證煩惱障淨，不能當證所知障淨；菩薩種性，亦能當證煩惱障淨，亦能當證所知障淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, two kinds of purification: purification from the afflictive obstruction, and purification from the cognitive obstruction. Every hearer and solitary-buddha lineage is able only to attain purification from the afflictive obstruction, never purification from the cognitive obstruction; the bodhisattva lineage is able to attain both — purification from the afflictive obstruction, and purification from the cognitive obstruction as well.

Chinese Source

復由四事，當知菩薩勝於一切聲聞、獨覺。何等為四？一者、根勝，二者、行勝，三者、善巧勝，四者、果勝……言根勝者，謂諸菩薩本性利根，獨覺中根，聲聞軟根……言行勝者……菩薩亦能自利，亦能利他……善巧勝者，聲聞、獨覺於蘊、界、處、緣起、處非處中能修善巧；菩薩於此及於其餘一切明處能修善巧……言果勝者，聲聞能證聲聞菩提，獨覺能證獨覺菩提，菩薩能證阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by four measures, one should know a bodhisattva surpasses every hearer and solitary buddha. What are the four? First, superiority of faculty; second, of conduct; third, of skillfulness; fourth, of fruit. Superiority of faculty: bodhisattvas are by nature sharp-facultied, solitary buddhas middling, hearers dull … superiority of conduct: bodhisattvas are able to benefit both themselves and others … superiority of skillfulness: hearers and solitary buddhas cultivate skillfulness only in the aggregates, sense-bases, elements, dependent origination, and valid-and-invalid reasoning; bodhisattvas cultivate skillfulness in these and in every other field of knowledge besides … superiority of fruit: a hearer attains a hearer's awakening, a solitary buddha a solitary buddha's awakening, but a bodhisattva attains unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two-purifications distinction as the deepest reason for bodhisattva lineage's superiority: a hearer or solitary buddha, having severed the afflictive obstruction (craving, hatred, and their kin), reaches genuine liberation from suffering, but leaves untouched the cognitive obstruction — the subtler habit of taking conceptually constructed appearances as ultimately real, obstructing full omniscience rather than mere personal freedom — while only bodhisattva practice, oriented toward realizing both the emptiness of self and the emptiness of phenomena, is capable of removing both. He unpacks the four comparative measures concretely: faculty is measured paradoxically by how long the path takes, not how quickly it's finished — a solitary buddha needs four lifetimes to a hundred eons, a hearer as few as three lifetimes, but a bodhisattva commits to three incalculable eons, precisely because sharper faculties are willing to undertake the harder, longer task rather than seek the fastest exit; conduct distinguishes the hearer and solitary buddha's exclusively self-directed aim (ending their own suffering) from the bodhisattva's dual-directed aim (benefiting self and others at once, out of compassion for the world); skillfulness distinguishes hearers and solitary buddhas' narrower field of study — the aggregates, sense-bases, elements, dependent origination, and valid reasoning, all oriented toward personal liberation — from the bodhisattva's five full fields of knowledge, adding medicine, logic, linguistics, and practical craft, since a bodhisattva committed to helping all beings needs far more than liberation theory alone; and fruit simply names the different destinations these different vehicles actually reach. He stresses this is not a claim that hearers or solitary buddhas fail at what they attempt — both genuinely reach real liberation — only that what a bodhisattva attempts, and thus what a bodhisattva's lineage must be capable of, is categorically larger.

5. The Marks of Giving — How a True Bodhisattva Is Recognized Through Generosity

The treatise now turns from lineage's abstract superiority to something concretely observable: six sets of marks, one for each perfection, by which others can actually recognize a genuine bodhisattva. Giving comes first — not merely the act itself, but the motivation, the manner, and the scope that distinguish bodhisattva generosity from any ordinary act of charity.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩本性樂施，於諸現有堪所施物，恆常無間性能於他平等分布，心喜施與，意無追悔……施物雖少，而能均布；惠施廣大，而非狹小……無所惠施，深懷慚恥。常好為他讚施勸施，見能施者心懷喜悅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas are by nature inclined to give; with respect to whatever suitable object is presently at hand to give, they constantly and without interruption distribute it equally to others, the mind delighting in the giving, with no regret afterward … even when what is given is little, it is still distributed evenly; the intention to give is vast, never narrow or small … when there is nothing to give, one feels deep shame; one constantly delights in praising others' giving and encouraging it, and, seeing another able to give, the heart fills with joy.

Chinese Source

受他寄物，未嘗差違。若負他債，終不抵誑。於共財所，亦無欺罔……其性好樂廣大財位，於彼一切廣大資財心好受用；樂大事業，非狹小門。於諸世間酒色、博戲、歌舞、倡妓種種變現耽著事中，速疾厭捨，深生慚愧。得大財寶，尚不貪著，何況小利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Receiving something entrusted by another, one never mishandles it; owing a debt to another, one never denies or deceives; in property jointly held, there is likewise no cheating … by nature one delights in vast wealth and standing, taking genuine pleasure in putting all such vast resources to use; one delights in great undertakings, not narrow or petty ones. Amid the world's intoxicating entanglements — drink, sensual pleasure, gambling, song, dance, entertainment — one swiftly recognizes the danger and abandons it, feeling deep shame; even great wealth, once obtained, is never clung to, much less some small gain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes giving's marks into motivation and actual conduct. On motivation: joy before, during, and after giving — never the reluctant, calculating generosity of someone parting with something precious, but a mind already inclined toward it — extended equally to every recipient regardless of whether they are friend, stranger, enemy, virtuous, flawed, fortunate, or suffering, since a bodhisattva regards every being alike as a field for cultivating merit, not a candidate to be screened by personal preference; even a trivial gift is given with a genuinely expansive heart, so scale of intention matters more than scale of the object given. On conduct: three kinds of giving in practice — giving the Dharma itself, whether asked or not, using eight distinct qualities of skillful speech (properly sequenced words, coherent support for the teaching, following the topic's natural order, clear and bright expression, genuinely reaching the listener's mind, matching the listener's own capacity, aligned with ultimate truth and timely circumstance, and patient, sustained instruction in the factors of awakening) so that the teaching actually lands rather than merely being delivered; giving fearlessness, rescuing those genuinely terrified of rulers, thieves, flood, or fire according to one's actual ability; and giving material wealth with scrupulous honesty — never mishandling an entrusted object, never denying a debt, never taking advantage of jointly-held property, and gently correcting others' confusion about the value of jewels and treasures rather than exploiting it. He notes the treatise's own sharp point about wealth itself: a bodhisattva may outwardly appear to delight in vast resources, but the delight is specifically in what those resources make possible for other beings, evidenced by an equally swift and genuine revulsion toward the world's usual traps of intoxication, gambling, and idle entertainment — wealth pursued as a tool for giving, never as an end enjoyed for its own sake.

6. The Marks of Precepts — A Discipline Measured by Benefit to Others, Not Rule-Following Alone

Precepts' marks unfold across three registers already familiar from earlier fascicles' ethical teaching — restraining harm, actively cultivating good, and benefiting other beings — but here reframed entirely around what makes a bodhisattva's discipline recognizably different: warmth, honest speech, and a discipline that bends toward helping others rather than merely avoiding wrongdoing.

Chinese Source

於恩有情，知恩知報……如法求財，不以非法，不以卒暴……性常喜樂修諸福業……若見若聞他所受苦……於是等苦過於自受，重於法受及重後世。於少罪中尚深見怖，何況多罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward beings who have shown kindness, one knows that kindness and repays it … one seeks wealth lawfully, never unlawfully, never through violence … by nature one constantly delights in cultivating meritorious deeds … seeing or hearing of another's suffering … one feels that suffering more keenly than one's own, weighing the karmic consequence and future life more heavily still; even the smallest wrongdoing is deeply feared, let alone a greater one.

Chinese Source

其心溫潤、其心純淨，恚心、害心不久相續，隨生隨捨，起賢善心……尊重實語，不誑惑他，不離他親……言常柔軟，無有麤獷；於己僮僕尚無苦言，況於他所。敬愛有德，如實讚彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mind is warm and gentle, pure and clear; anger and the wish to harm never last long or continue, arising only to be released, replaced by a wholesome heart … one values truthful speech, never deceiving others, never dividing those who are close … speech is always soft, never coarse; not even a harsh word for one's own servants, let alone anyone else. One honors and loves the virtuous, praising them exactly as they truly are.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes precepts' marks across the same three-fold discipline named in earlier fascicles — restraint from harm, active cultivation of good, and benefit to beings — but stresses how far the third category is pushed here: knowing and repaying kindness in full or in greater measure than received; earning a livelihood only by lawful, gentle means, never violence or exploitation; delighting in giving, precept-keeping, and meditation as the three grounds of merit, and eagerly encouraging others toward the same; and, most distinctively, feeling another's suffering more sharply than one's own — witnessing violence or cruelty done to someone else and being moved more deeply than if it had happened to oneself, precisely because a bodhisattva weighs karmic consequence and the arc of future lives more heavily than momentary personal pain, and so dreads even trivial wrongdoing the way an ordinary person dreads only grave ones. He notes the treatise's own gloss on this apparent excess of empathy: an ordinary being fears the result of wrongdoing (suffering, once it arrives); a bodhisattva fears the cause itself (the wrongdoing, before any suffering has even begun), since correct insight into cause and effect makes the seed just as alarming as the fruit it will inevitably bear. The register then shifts to demeanor: warmth quick to release any flash of anger rather than nursing it, unfailingly truthful and never divisive speech, and a gentleness of tone extended even to one's own servants — precepts here measured less by which specific rules are kept than by whether the whole disposition genuinely bends toward others' benefit, restraint from harm being simply the necessary floor beneath a discipline whose real center of gravity is generosity of spirit.

7. Patience's Marks, and Diligence's Five Faces — From Rising Before Dawn to Never Being Satisfied

Patience's mark is stated with striking brevity — absorbing harm without retaliation, forgiving readily, and never nursing a grudge. Diligence then unfolds across five distinct faces, from simple early rising through to a striking refusal ever to consider one's effort sufficient.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，性於他所遭不饒益，無恚害心，亦不反報。若他諫謝，速能納受，終不結恨，不久懷怨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, by nature, when harmed by another, hold no anger or wish to injure in return, nor do they retaliate. If the other later repents and apologizes, one accepts it readily, never harboring resentment or nursing a grudge for long.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，性自翹勤，夙興晚寐，不深耽樂睡眠倚樂……於所作事勇決樂為，不生懈怠，思擇方便，要令究竟……於諸廣大第一義中，心無怯弱，不自輕懱……或入大眾，或與他人共相擊論，或餘種種難行事業，皆無畏憚。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, by their very nature, exert themselves — rising early, retiring late, never indulging deeply in the pleasure of sleep or reclining ease … whatever is undertaken is done boldly and gladly, never with laziness, choosing the right means beforehand so the task reaches genuine completion … with respect to the vast, ultimate goal, the mind holds no timidity, never belittling itself … whether entering a large assembly, debating others directly, or facing any other difficult undertaking, there is no fear or hesitation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats patience's mark as deliberately simple by comparison with giving and precepts' elaborate detail: absorb harm without either anger or retaliation, and release an apology the instant it is offered, rather than making the offender wait out a cooling-off period a bodhisattva has no interest in enforcing. Diligence then unfolds across five distinct faces the text develops one by one: armor-like diligence, the basic willingness to rise early and forgo comfortable sleep rather than let ease dictate one's schedule; methodical diligence, choosing the right approach before acting and carrying any undertaking through to genuine completion rather than abandoning it partway; undaunted diligence, refusing to think oneself incapable of the vast goal of buddhahood itself, since underestimating one's own capacity is itself a subtle failure of diligence; unshakeable diligence, entering a large assembly, debating opponents directly, or taking on any other genuinely difficult task without hesitation, since fear of a hard task is simply diligence wavering under pressure; and — about to be introduced — a fifth, insatiable diligence, which refuses to ever regard effort already expended as sufficient. He notes the progression's logic: each face addresses a different way effort can quietly fail — comfort undermining basic willingness, carelessness undermining follow-through, self-doubt undermining ambition, fear undermining courage under scrutiny — building toward the final face, where the danger is not any of these but premature satisfaction with progress already made.
