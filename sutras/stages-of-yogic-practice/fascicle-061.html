Fascicle 61 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十一 · Three Sutras Cited in Full

The King Who Felt Neither Wounded Nor Flattered

With the abstract analysis of affliction, karma, and the opening of rebirth defilement now behind it, the treatise turns to a different register entirely, citing three sutras at length to ground everything already established in concrete human terms: a king's own true virtues and faults, the eight sufferings every being faces, and the differing capacities of lesser, middling, and superior persons — opening with King Udayana's own visit to the Buddha, seeking to understand why neither false blame nor false praise ever genuinely moves him.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 61 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A King Immune to Both False Blame and False Praise

Having completed, across fascicles 58 through 60, the full deciding discernment of the three stages of examination and reflection through their own establishing realms and the three kinds of defilement, affliction, karma, and rebirth, the treatise now turns to a fourth and final portion of this same topic, drawing directly on the Buddha's own recorded teaching to ground everything already established through vivid example: sutra citations illustrating defilement's own faults and the merits of giving, moral discipline, and meditation; a detailed account of a king's own virtues and faults; the distinguishing marks of the eight sufferings; and the differing capacities of lesser, middling, and superior persons — all offered to guide beings toward genuinely wholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind. The treatise opens with King Udayana's own visit to the Buddha, bowing at his feet and posing a question drawn from his own practice: whenever some ascetic or brahmin comes before him and rebukes him for some fault he does not actually possess, his own mind feels no regret, no distress, and no sorrow whatsoever, since, examining himself honestly, he simply finds no trace of the alleged fault to be found; and whenever some ascetic or brahmin instead praises him for some virtue he does not actually possess, his own mind feels no elation or excitement either, since, examining himself just as honestly, he finds no trace of the alleged virtue there either.

Chinese Source

復次，如佛世尊為出愛王所說經言：彼王一時往詣佛所，頂禮佛足，白言：世尊！有一沙門、若婆羅門來至我所，以不真實過失，現前呵諫於我，我於爾時，其心不生悔惱憂慼。何以故？觀此過失，於我自身都不見故。又有沙門、若婆羅門來至我所，以不真實功德，現前讚勸於我，我於爾時，心亦不生歡喜踊躍。何以故？觀此功德，於我自身都不見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, as the Blessed One taught in the sutra spoken for King Udayana: that king once went to where the Buddha was, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said: Blessed One, when some ascetic or brahmin comes before me and rebukes me to my face with an untrue fault, my mind at that time feels no regret, distress, or sorrow. Why is this so? Because, examining this fault, I find no trace of it in myself. And when some ascetic or brahmin comes before me and praises me to my face with an untrue virtue, my mind at that time likewise feels no joy or elation. Why is this so? Because, examining this virtue, I find no trace of it in myself either.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats King Udayana's own opening observation as a genuinely sophisticated piece of self-knowledge for a ruler to have arrived at on his own, since it requires the same honest, unflinching self-examination the treatise has praised throughout this entire four-fascicle stretch, applied here not to a monastic but to a king still fully immersed in worldly power and its usual flatteries and slanders alike. He highlights the king's own practical motive for eventually seeking out the Buddha, wanting to actually learn what his own true faults and true virtues are rather than simply dismissing every external judgment, as the very opposite of complacency, a genuinely humble use of his own initial equanimity as a foundation for further, deeper self-improvement rather than an excuse to stop examining himself at all.

2. Section: The Buddha Names Ten Faults of a King

Reflecting alone afterward in a quiet place, the king wondered how he might come to know a ruler's own genuine faults and genuine virtues, resolving to abandon the former and cultivate the latter once he understood them clearly, and asked himself which ascetic or brahmin could possibly explain this to him in full. Recognizing that only the Blessed One, knowing and seeing all things without exception, could truly answer such a question, he went directly to the Buddha and posed it there: what, in truth, are a king's own genuine faults, and what are a king's own genuine virtues? The Blessed One answered by first naming six topics he would address in turn: a king's own faults, a king's own virtues, the very gateways through which a king's own position declines, the skillful means by which a king may govern well, the qualities that make a king genuinely beloved, and the means by which those very qualities are actually brought about. Taking up the first of these, the Buddha explained that a king's faults are, in brief, of ten kinds, and that a king who falls into any of these, however vast his treasury, however capable his ministers, and however powerful his armies, does not deserve his people's genuine trust and reliance: being of low birth; lacking genuine self-mastery; being naturally cruel by disposition; being quick to fierce, uncontrolled anger; being stingy and sparing in rewarding others; heeding flattering, deceitful counsel; acting without careful thought and without regard for proper protocol; disregarding wholesome conduct entirely; failing to distinguish who deserves what and forgetting kindnesses already received; and indulging, entirely and without restraint, in heedless self-indulgence.

爾時，世尊告出愛王曰：大王！大王今者應當了知王之過失、王之功德、王衰損門、王方便門、王可愛法，及能引發王可愛法。云何名為王之過失？大王當知！王過失者略有十種。王若成就如是過失，雖有大府庫、有大輔佐、有大軍眾，然不可歸仰。何等為十？一、種姓不高，二、不得自在，三、立性暴惡，四、猛利憤發，五、恩惠奢薄，六、受邪佞言，七、所作不思不順儀則，八、不顧善法，九、不知差別忘所作恩，十、一向縱任專行放逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Blessed One told King Udayana: Great King, you should now know a king's own faults, a king's own virtues, the gateways of a king's own decline, the skillful means of a king, the qualities beloved of a king, and what draws forth those beloved qualities. What is called a king's own fault? Great King, you should know that a king's faults are, in brief, of ten kinds. If a king possesses such faults, though he has a great treasury, great ministers, and a great army, he does not deserve reliance. What are the ten? First, low birth. Second, lack of self-mastery. Third, a naturally cruel disposition. Fourth, fierce, uncontrolled anger. Fifth, sparing and stingy kindness. Sixth, heeding deceitful counsel. Seventh, acting without thought and without regard for proper protocol. Eighth, disregarding wholesome dharma. Ninth, failing to distinguish and forgetting kindness received. Tenth, wholly indulgent and heedless conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this entire extended teaching, addressed originally to a ruler navigating the very real pressures of worldly power, remains strikingly applicable well beyond kingship itself, since anyone holding real authority over others, whether a business leader, an administrator, or the head of a household, faces essentially the same temptations toward cruelty, stinginess, flattery-seeking, and heedless self-indulgence this list names one by one. He highlights forgetting kindness already received as a fault worth particular attention, since it names a failure not of judgment but of memory and gratitude, the kind of ordinary human forgetfulness that can quietly corrode a leader's own relationships with those who have genuinely supported them long before any dramatic wrongdoing ever becomes visible.

3. Section: Five Shades of Royal Cruelty, and the Gravest of Them All

The Buddha explains each of the ten faults in turn. Low birth belongs to a king born into some inferior royal lineage rather than one of long-standing eminence, or one born within a royal house yet through some concubine or lesser wife rather than the true consort, resembling the ruling line only in name, or one who has risen instead from among ministers, chancellors, royal preceptors, or ordinary officials rather than genuine royal stock. Lack of self-mastery belongs to a king controlled by his own ministers, chancellors, preceptors, and officials, unable to act, reward, or freely enjoy even the finest pleasures according to his own genuine wish. A naturally cruel disposition unfolds across five distinct shades: cruelty displayed openly to another's face, banishing and berating them the moment the slightest displeasing thing occurs, scowling and erupting in coarse, furious language; cruelty displayed instead behind another's back, before everyone but the very person concerned; a brooding, resentful cruelty kept entirely private, harboring real malice and grudge yet not maintained for very long before it passes; and this same brooding, resentful cruelty sustained instead over a long stretch of time, nursed and never actually released. Of these, the Buddha states plainly, it is specifically the long-sustained, brooding cruelty that constitutes the truly grave fault, every other shade of cruelty falling short of this same severity.

云何名王種姓不高？謂有國王，隨一下類王家而生，非宿尊貴；或雖於此王家而生，賤女之子，不相似子；或是大臣、輔相、國師、群官等子。如是名王種姓不高。云何名王不得自在？謂有國王，為諸大臣、輔相、國師、群官所制，不隨所欲作所應作，錫賚群臣；於妙五欲，亦不如意歡娛遊戲。如是名王不得自在。謂有國王，諸群臣類或餘人等，隨於一處現行少小不如意事，即便對面擯黜，發麤惡言，咆勃忿恚，顰蹙而住，時生憤發。設不對面，背彼向餘而作於前擯辱等事。設不對面、亦不背彼向餘而作於前黜罵等事。然唯內意憤恚鬱怏，懷惱害心、懷怨恨心，然不長時持憤恚心相續不捨。復有內意憤恚鬱怏，懷惱害心、懷怨恨心，亦於長時持憤恚心相續不捨。大王當知！長久暴惡名獲大罪，非是餘者。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called a king's low birth? A king born into some inferior royal house, not one of long-standing eminence; or, though born within such a house, the child of a lowly woman, not resembling the true heir; or the child of a minister, chancellor, royal preceptor, or official. This is called a king's low birth. What is called a king's lack of self-mastery? A king controlled by his own ministers, chancellors, preceptors, and officials, unable to act and reward according to his own wish, nor freely enjoy the finest pleasures. This is called lack of self-mastery. A king whose ministers or others, wherever some slight displeasure arises, are immediately banished to his face, met with coarse language, furious shouting, and a scowling countenance, erupting in anger. Or, not to their face, doing the same behind their back before others. Or, neither to their face nor behind their back, yet inwardly harboring resentment and malice, though not sustained for long. Or further, inwardly harboring resentment and malice, sustained over a long time without release. Great King, you should know that it is specifically this long-sustained cruelty that constitutes a grave fault, not the others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own careful gradation of cruelty, from a public outburst quickly spent to a private grudge nursed indefinitely, as a genuinely perceptive piece of psychology, since the loudest, most visible display of temper is not, in the end, identified as the gravest fault at all, while the quiet, sustained, never-released resentment, invisible to everyone but the one who carries it, is named as the true danger. He highlights this ranking as a useful corrective for anyone inclined to judge character purely by outward volume, since a leader who shouts and then genuinely lets go may, by this very measure, be less dangerous than one who never raises their voice at all yet never once forgives.

4. Section: Rewards Given Only After Resentment Has Already Set In

Fierce, uncontrolled anger belongs to a king who, faced with only the smallest fault or minor overstepping among his ministers, responds by stripping away their salary and rank, seizing their wives and concubines for himself, or subjecting them to severe punishment. Sparing, stingy kindness belongs to a king whose ministers serve him faithfully and well, genuinely satisfying his every wish, and who responds only with faint, half-hearted words of comfort rather than genuine praise, granting rank, salary, and reward for real accomplishment only incompletely, contrary to proper custom, whether giving something already worn or depleted, delaying the reward for a long stretch of time, first extracting some further exhausting labor before finally granting it, or withholding it until the recipient has already grown resentful and only then, grudgingly, giving it at all. Heeding deceitful counsel belongs to a king whose ministers, though genuinely lacking real wisdom, carry themselves with an arrogant pretense of wisdom, driven by greed, factional corruption, and unfamiliarity with proper law, secretly plotting rebellion and governing poorly, and who nonetheless listens to, trusts, and acts upon whatever advice these very ministers offer, his own governance, treasury, reputation, and administration all declining as a direct result. And acting without thought and without regard for proper protocol belongs to a king who neither investigates nor carefully considers his own ministers before assigning them duty, entrusting sensitive responsibilities to those unfit for them while overlooking those genuinely capable, commanding the unsuited while leaving the suited idle, and rewarding fault while punishing merit — a king, further, who fails to uphold his own predecessors' court protocols, so that ministers interrupt one another mid-speech at the royal assembly, show neither respect nor restraint in bringing forward disputes, and neither faithfully carry out the king's own decrees nor properly abide by his commands at all.

云何名王猛利憤發？謂有國王，諸群臣等有小愆過、有少違越，便削封祿，奪去妻妾，或以重罰而刑罰之。如是名王猛利憤發。云何名王恩惠奢薄？謂有國王，諸群臣等供奉侍衛，雖極清淨，善稱其心，而以微劣軟言慰諭；頒賜爵祿、酬賞勳庸不能圓滿，不順常式。或損耗已、或稽留已、或推注已、或怨恨已，然後方與。如是名王恩惠奢薄。云何名王受邪佞言？謂有國王，諸群臣等實非聰叡，有聰叡慢，貪濁偏黨，不閑憲式，情懷謀叛，不修善政，聽受信用如是輩人所進諫議，由此因緣，王務、財寶、名稱、善政并皆衰損。如是名王受邪佞言。云何名王所作不思不順儀則？謂有國王，不能究察、不審究察、不能思擇、不審思擇諸群臣輩，於彼彼務機密事中，不堪委任而委任之，堪委任者而不委任。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called a king's fierce, uncontrolled anger? A king whose ministers, for even the smallest fault or minor overstepping, has his rank and salary stripped, his wives and concubines seized, or is subjected to severe punishment. This is called fierce, uncontrolled anger. What is called a king's sparing, stingy kindness? A king whose ministers serve him purely and to his genuine satisfaction, yet who offers only faint, feeble words of comfort, granting rank and reward for merit incompletely, contrary to custom, whether depleted, delayed, extracted through further labor, or given only once resentment has already arisen. This is called sparing, stingy kindness. What is called a king's heeding deceitful counsel? A king whose ministers, lacking real wisdom yet carrying an arrogant pretense of it, driven by greed and faction, unfamiliar with proper law, secretly plotting rebellion and governing poorly, are nonetheless trusted and heeded, his own governance, treasury, reputation, and administration all declining as a result. This is called heeding deceitful counsel. What is called a king's acting without thought and without regard for protocol? A king who neither investigates nor carefully considers his ministers, entrusting sensitive duties to the unfit while overlooking the fit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the description of a reward given only once the recipient has already grown resentful as one of the sutra's own sharpest psychological observations, since it names a genuinely common failure of leadership, technically fulfilling an obligation while completely forfeiting whatever goodwill that same obligation, promptly honored, might otherwise have earned. He highlights the parallel drawn between heeding flattering counsel and a broader failure of judgment in appointing and rewarding others as two faces of the very same underlying weakness, a ruler's own failure to look carefully at the people around him, whether trusting the wrong advisors or misplacing responsibility among the wrong officials, both ultimately traceable to the same absence of careful, honest observation.

5. Section: Forgetting the Old Servants Who Gave Their Whole Lives

Disregarding wholesome dharma belongs to a king who neither believes in future lives nor understands them, and who, precisely because of this disbelief, cannot genuinely accept that present wholesome or unwholesome deeds bring about desirable or undesirable results to come, and so, feeling no shame at all, freely indulges unwholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind, never pausing to give charity, cultivate merit, observe the fasting days, or study the precepts. Failing to distinguish and forgetting kindness received belongs to a king whose own mind is so distorted that he cannot properly recognize which of his ministers are genuinely loyal, skilled, and wise, mistaking the disloyal for the loyal and the loyal for the disloyal, the untalented for the talented and the talented for the untalented, the foolish for the wise and the wise for the foolish, and who, on this very distortion, comes to honor and reward exactly the wrong ministers while holding the genuinely worthy in contempt; this same king further forgets those officials who, now aged and enfeebled, once served him faithfully through long years of devoted attendance, and, recognizing they no longer carry influence, strength, or vigor, withholds the very rank, salary, and reward they are owed, abandoning them entirely, unmoved even should they later be humiliated by others. And heedless self-indulgence belongs to a king utterly sunk in the five exquisite sensory pleasures, given over entirely to amusement and delight, never rousing himself to attend to his own proper duties or reward his own deserving ministers. Should a king possess all ten of these faults, however vast his treasury, however capable his ministers, and however powerful his armies, he does not deserve reliance — the first of the ten being a fault of lineage alone, the remaining nine genuine faults of character.

And ministers now aged and enfeebled, who once served him faithfully through long years of attendance, knowing they lack influence, strength, and vigor, are no longer cherished, granted rank or salary, or rewarded; should they be humiliated by others, they are abandoned and ignored. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the abandonment of aged, faithful servants once their usefulness has passed as one of the sutra's own most quietly devastating faults, precisely because it requires no dramatic cruelty at all, only ordinary neglect, forgetting a debt owed to someone no longer capable of collecting on it. He highlights the sutra's own closing clarification, that only the very first fault concerns birth while all nine others concern character, as an important piece of moral reassurance: a king born into humble circumstances is not thereby condemned, since nine-tenths of what actually determines whether a ruler deserves trust lies entirely within their own power to cultivate, regardless of the family into which they happened to be born.

6. Section: Ten Virtues, the Exact Mirror of Ten Faults

The Buddha then turns to a king's own virtues, in brief also ten in number, and explains that a king possessing these deserves genuine reliance even should his treasury, ministers, and army all prove modest: noble lineage, great self-mastery, freedom from cruelty, only mild anger when provoked, generous and forceful kindness, heeding honest and upright counsel, acting only after careful thought and in full accordance with proper protocol, genuinely valuing wholesome dharma, skillfully distinguishing worthy from unworthy ministers while remembering kindness received, and never falling into heedless self-indulgence — each the exact positive mirror of the ten faults already named. Noble lineage belongs to a king born into a truly eminent royal house, resembling his own forebears in bearing and conduct, honored by all and never the target of scorn, his family's own distinguished lineage stretching back across generations. Great self-mastery belongs to a king able to act, reward his ministers, and enjoy the finest pleasures entirely according to his own genuine wish, every command he issues to his ministers, chancellors, preceptors, and officials carried out without the slightest obstruction. And freedom from cruelty belongs to a king who, even when his ministers act in ways genuinely displeasing to him, responds with real patience and tolerance rather than any of the four faces of cruelty already described: never banishing them to their face, never disparaging them behind their back, never secretly harboring resentment against them, and never nursing a grudge across any long stretch of time at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sutra's own decision to build its account of virtue as the precise mirror image of the vice already catalogued, rather than as an entirely separate list, as a genuinely effective teaching structure, since a reader who has already absorbed exactly what cruelty looks like in its four distinct shades now immediately recognizes what its absence actually requires, patience sustained not merely in the loud, visible moment but across every one of those same four occasions where resentment might otherwise have taken root. He highlights this parallel construction as a fitting reminder that virtue is rarely something wholly new to be learned, so much as the genuine, sustained absence of the very faults already so carefully named.

7. Section: A Punishment Scaled to the Actual Fault

Mild anger belongs to a king who, even when his ministers commit some genuinely serious fault or overstep, does not strip away their entire rank and salary, seize their wives and concubines, or subject them to severe punishment, but instead metes out censure or penalty scaled proportionately to the actual gravity of the offense. Forceful, generous kindness belongs to a king whose ministers serve him with genuine sincerity and devotion, and who, in turn, offers them correct, complete, and gentle words of comfort in due season, fully and properly granting rank, salary, and reward for merit without ever letting it be depleted, delayed, wrung from further exhausting labor, or given only once resentment has set in, so that serving such a king is genuinely easy and welcome rather than burdensome. Heeding honest counsel belongs to a king whose ministers are truly wise, free of arrogance, free of corruption and factional bias, well versed in proper law, harboring no rebellious intent, and delighting in wholesome practice, and who listens to, trusts, and acts upon precisely this kind of counsel, his own governance, treasury, reputation, and wholesome practice all flourishing as a direct result. And acting with careful thought and in full accordance with protocol belongs to a king who genuinely investigates and carefully considers his own ministers before assigning duty, entrusting the unfit to nothing while properly employing the fit, rewarding merit correctly and punishing fault correctly, acting only after real deliberation rather than in haste, and who further upholds his own predecessors' court protocols so completely that his ministers wait their proper turn to speak, raise objections only with genuine respect and restraint, and faithfully carry out every one of his own decrees.

8. Section: Still Sharing Rewards With Servants Long Past Their Prime

9. Section: Ministers Who Learn to Hide Their Own Talent

The Buddha names five gateways through which a king's own position genuinely declines: taking in ministers without carefully observing them first; carefully observing and taking in worthy ministers, yet showing them no genuine kindness except at the wrong, too-late moment; falling into heedless indulgence and neglecting important state affairs; falling into heedless indulgence and failing to guard the royal treasury; and falling into heedless indulgence and neglecting the practice of the Dharma. The first belongs to a king who, without genuinely investigating his own ministers' loyalty, skill, or wisdom, nonetheless takes them into his close confidence, showers them with favor, grants them lavish rank and reward, and entrusts them with the most sensitive affairs of state, comforting them constantly with gentle words — only to discover that these very ministers squander much of the wealth entrusted to them, and that, when facing an actual enemy, a hostile faction, or open battle, they lose their nerve before the fighting even begins, retreat in fear, hide behind others rather than leading from the front, and resort to deceptive schemes that genuinely damage the king's own governance. The second belongs to a king who, though he does genuinely investigate and correctly identify which ministers are truly loyal, skilled, and wise, nonetheless fails to show them proper favor or grant them reward proportionate to their real contribution, entrusting them with nothing sensitive and offering no genuine word of encouragement — until, at last, facing a real enemy, a hostile faction, open battle, or even mortal danger to his own throne, he suddenly turns to these same ministers with belated affection and soothing words; and the ministers themselves, recognizing this warmth for what it actually is, remark to one another that the king favors them only now, under genuine threat, with no real lasting sincerity behind it, and thereafter quietly conceal their own true loyalty, skill, and wisdom rather than offer it freely.

11. Section: Five Gateways of Decline Closed, Five Gateways of Skillful Means Open

12. Section: Ministers Who Give Everything Because They Were Truly Seen

The second gateway of skillful means belongs to a king who, having genuinely and carefully observed his own ministers, takes the loyal, skilled, and wise among them into his close confidence, favoring them, granting rank and reward proportionate to their real contribution, entrusting them with sensitive affairs, and consulting them directly, constantly encouraging them with genuine words of praise for work well and diligently done — so that when this same king later faces a genuine enemy, a hostile faction, open battle, or even mortal danger to his own throne, these very ministers respond by giving everything they have, their full loyalty, skill, and wisdom, even to the point of sacrificing their own lives to save him. The third gateway belongs to a king who, regarding every matter that calls for alliance, for severance, for charity, for military deployment, or for drawing powerful factions into partnership, regularly sets aside quiet time alone, or together with wise counselors, to properly reflect on, weigh, and examine the right course of action in each case, remaining entirely free of the very heedlessness that would otherwise leave such genuinely important affairs neglected and adrift.

14. Section: Five Practices That Actually Draw Forth What a King Truly Wants

The Buddha names five practices that draw forth these very qualities: nurturing the world with genuine kindness, possessing full heroic capability, skillful and expedient means, properly enjoying one's own circumstances, and diligently practicing the Dharma. Nurturing the world with genuine kindness belongs to a king naturally content, cautious rather than greedy regarding wealth, accumulating only what is proper without excessive pursuit; who, free of both greed and stinginess, draws on his own treasury to give according to his own means to the poor and the orphaned; and who governs with real gentleness and patience, admonishing his people mostly through kind words, rewarding his ministers proportionately to their genuine contribution, never assigning them tasks beyond their capacity or genuinely degrading work, forgiving forgivable faults with real compassion while justly punishing, at the proper time and according to the actual facts, whatever faults are genuinely unforgivable — and it is specifically through practicing this threefold kindness that a king comes to be respected and loved by the world. Possessing full heroic capability belongs to a king who plans state affairs diligently and without laziness, whose military strategy is genuinely accomplished, subduing what remains unconquered while protecting what has already submitted, broadly and skillfully managing state enterprise exactly as already described, never falling into excessive indulgence in gambling or idle amusement, and who correctly discerns what should and should not be granted, correctly punishing what deserves punishment and correctly caring for what deserves care — and it is through practicing this very capability that a king comes to enjoy ever-increasing self-mastery and authority.

15. Section: Eating Alone Is a Fault Even for a King

Skillful, expedient means belongs to a king who correctly understands how to handle every matter calling for alliance, exactly as already described, through matters calling for drawing in powerful factions, correctly grasping the proper approach to each in turn — and it is through practicing this very skill that a king comes to overcome every enemy who opposes him. Freedom from fault in this enjoyment means eating what is actually suitable during illness and avoiding what is not, treating moderation itself as medicine; once genuinely recovered, eating further food only once what came before has properly digested, since food eaten before digestion is complete, or food that passes through the body too quickly to be absorbed, should not be eaten at all; and, when eating alongside others who ought properly to share the meal, actually sharing it with them, rather than selfishly consuming the finest delicacies alone while contriving some excuse to exclude everyone else from the very same table.

16. Section: Five Qualities of Genuine Dharma Practice, and a Quick Awakening From One's Own Lapse

Through practicing proper enjoyment of his own circumstances, a king comes to skillfully maintain his own bodily health. The Buddha then turns to the fifth and final practice, diligently practicing the Dharma, comprising five qualities in full: pure faith, pure precepts, pure learning, pure giving, and pure wisdom. Pure faith belongs to a king who genuinely believes in future lives, and in the future ripening of pure and impure deeds into desirable and undesirable results alike. Pure precepts belong to a king who keeps entirely free of killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, and every intoxicated or heedless state. Pure learning belongs to a king who listens carefully to, receives, studies, recites until fully fluent, and diligently investigates every wondrous teaching bearing on both present and future benefit, coming to see and understand it thoroughly. Pure giving belongs to a king who, even surrounded by others still bound by miserliness, keeps his own mind constantly pure and free of that same stain, freely offering his own wealth with an open hand while still living the ordinary life of a householder, delighting in supporting worthy causes and cultivating merit, giving fully and joyfully with an even, impartial heart toward all. And pure wisdom belongs to a king who genuinely understands what is wholesome and unwholesome, what is blameworthy and blameless, what should and should not be cultivated, and the workings of dependent origination in full detail — and who, even should he momentarily lose mindfulness and give rise to greed, hatred, anger, resentment, concealment, vexation, stinginess, envy, deceit, flattery, shamelessness, or wrong view, quickly becomes aware of it, refusing to remain stuck within these very afflictions once they have already arisen. It is through practicing this fivefold Dharma conduct that a king comes, in the end, to reach the very fortunate destinies.

17. Section: "Follow This, and You Are Called a Dharma King" — And a New Sutra Begins

The Buddha closes his teaching to King Udayana with a final exhortation: having now explained, in brief, a king's own faults, virtues, gateways of decline, gateways of skillful means, beloved qualities, and the very means that draw those qualities forth, the king should diligently take up this entire teaching in practice, specifically distancing himself from every fault and every gateway of decline while cultivating every virtue and every gateway of skillful means, aspiring genuinely toward the qualities that make a king beloved and actually putting into practice the very means that draw them forth — and doing so, the Buddha promises, will bring about every benefit and every happiness there is. The tradition records that the Buddha further called this teaching the secret instruction of kings, promising that a ruler who reads and follows it each morning becomes, in the fullest sense, both a sage-king and a dharma-king, receiving the constant day-and-night protection of buddhas, bodhisattvas, and the eightfold host of celestial beings, enjoying favorable weather, an end to warfare, tribute freely offered by neighboring lands rather than owed to them, boundless merit, an unusually long and peaceful reign, and a genuinely long life. With this, the treatise closes its citation of the sutra spoken for King Udayana, also known as the Discourse the Buddha Delivered to King Udayana on the Governance of Kings, and turns to a second sutra entirely, deciding the distinguishing marks of the truth of suffering by citing the Sutra of the Eight Sufferings, also known as the Sutra of the Five Kings, opening with birth's own suffering, ultimately reducible, in brief, to nothing other than the suffering of the five clinging-aggregates themselves, the very foundation every other kind of suffering ultimately rests upon.

18. Section: Five Marks of the Suffering of Being Born at All

Birth's own suffering, the treatise explains, unfolds through five distinguishing marks. It is accompanied by many sufferings, since a being born into hell, or into the hungry-ghost realm's own uniformly painful existence, or as certain animals, whether through womb-birth or egg-birth, is followed from that very first moment by sorrow upon sorrow. It is accompanied by coarse-heaviness, since a being born anywhere within the three realms carries, from birth onward, the very seeds of affliction lodged within the mind, making that mind inherently rigid and uncontrolled, entirely lacking the freedom needed to sever affliction and accomplish the noble path. It is the very basis of many sufferings, since old age, sickness, death, separation from what is loved, union with what is disliked, and failing to obtain what is wanted, all depend entirely on the aggregates having been born in the first place, and could never arise at all without that birth. It is the very basis of affliction, since a being, once born, inevitably comes to crave whatever seems agreeable, resent whatever provokes anger, and grow confused before whatever confuses, the mind thereby losing its own stillness and the body and mind together left in constant, unsettled discomfort. And it is the very impossibility of avoiding an unwanted final separation, since every being that is born will also die, death marking the absolute boundary and final limit of any life, an outcome no being genuinely welcomes, and it is precisely this same inescapable, unwanted parting from everything and everyone loved that makes birth itself, from its very first moment, already suffering.

19. Section: A Lamp Whose Oil Is Nearly Spent

Old age's own suffering likewise unfolds through five marks: the decline of one's own once-radiant appearance, glossy vitality giving way to graying hair and a wrinkled face no mirror can hide; the decline of one's own physical strength and vigor, a body once full of energy now struggling even to lift a simple bowl of rice; the decline of the sense faculties themselves, eyes grown dim, ears grown dull, the nose no longer distinguishing fragrance from foulness, the tongue no longer savoring flavor, the body slow to respond, and the mind itself, in its most severe form, sliding into genuine confusion, scarcely able to distinguish itself from another; the decline of one's own capacity to enjoy the world's own pleasant circumstances, no longer able to wander freely in spring or swim in summer, to sing or dance as one once did; and the decline of one's own remaining lifespan itself, each passing day leaving less than the day before, the years after sixty growing steadily thinner, the months after seventy thinner still, the very days after eighty and the very hours after ninety each diminishing further, until, by a hundred, little remains at all. The Nirvana Sutra compares this condition to a lamp's own wick, sustained only by its own oil, which, once nearly exhausted, can no longer keep the flame burning for very much longer; and the Udānavarga records the Buddha's own verse on the very same subject: a youthful mind once vigorous and strong is eventually overtaken by old age, the body withering until it can walk only leaning on a staff, its own vital energy nearly spent.

20. Section: "One Comes Alone and Departs Alone" — Death, Hatred, and Separation

Death's remaining marks complete its own fivefold suffering: separation from one's own body itself, a body inhabited and called one's own for an entire lifetime suddenly relinquished all at once, with no way of knowing what form awaits next; and the extreme distress endured at the very moment of dying, described elsewhere as resembling a tortoise torn from its own shell. The Nirvana Sutra likens death to a journey through treacherous terrain without provisions and without companions, traveled day and night with no visible end, entered through a door that cannot be found even though a destination surely exists; and the Infinite Life Sutra observes that a person comes alone into this life and departs from it alone as well, the very joys and sorrows of that departure belonging to that person and no other, parting even from the most cherished companions with no promise of ever meeting again, while the living merely sing and weep at the departed's own funeral, unaware that the dead already inhabit an entirely different place. The Udānavarga records the Buddha's own verse: a life waiting to ripen constantly fears its own falling, since every birth already carries suffering within it, and only what is fully ripened can ever hope to escape death. Turning next to the suffering of encountering what is hated, this too unfolds through five marks: the sorrow felt simply in the enemy's own presence; the fear of retaliation or punishment; the fear of slander and damaged reputation; the fear of death itself at the enemy's hand; and the fear of falling into the evil destinies through whatever unlawful conduct such hostility provokes — the Sutra of the Five Kings describing how ordinary rivalry can escalate until both parties sharpen their own blades and finally confront one another, bows drawn, uncertain who will prevail, gripped by boundless terror in that very moment. And the suffering of separation from what is loved unfolds through its own five marks: the sorrow of simply not being together; the resentment and lament this separation provokes; the bodily distress it causes; the burning longing that follows remembering the departed's own good qualities; and the sheer loss of whatever shared enjoyment once existed — the same sutra recalling entire families scattered by plunder and war, father separated east, mother south, child and spouse each carried in a different direction, their final farewells offered with no certainty of ever meeting again.

What is the suffering of separation from what is loved? It should be known that this too has five marks: sorrow at not being together; resentment and lament arising from this cause; bodily distress arising from this cause; burning affliction of mind arising from longing for that being's own good qualities; and the loss of whatever mutual enjoyment ought to have continued.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Infinite Life Sutra's own image of the living singing and weeping at a funeral the departed can no longer perceive as one of the most quietly devastating details in this entire catalogue, since it captures precisely how completely death severs even the most devoted farewell, every ritual of mourning ultimately addressed to someone no longer capable of receiving it. He highlights the Sutra of the Five Kings' account of families scattered by war as this whole section's own reminder that separation's suffering rarely announces itself gently, arriving instead, for countless beings throughout history, as sudden, violent rupture rather than a peaceful parting anyone had time to prepare for.

21. Section: The Eighth Suffering — And a Deep Digression Into the Suffering of Formations Itself

The suffering of not obtaining what is wanted follows this very same fivefold pattern already explained for separation from what is loved, the Sutra of the Five Kings illustrating it through the desperate pursuit of wealth, officials and commoners alike driven by urgent need to seek riches by any means, sometimes resorting to unlawful methods that, once exposed, cost them not only rank but even their own lives — proof, as an old saying has it, that the freest person is the one who wants for nothing at all. The eighth and final suffering, that of the five clinging-aggregates themselves, unfolds through its own five marks: being the very vessel of birth's suffering, generating suffering as its own object and drawing forth every suffering still to come; being the basis upon which birth's own vessel depends, since old age and sickness alike depend on these very aggregates already existing; being the vessel of the suffering of pain, carrying within it the coarse-heaviness seeds of old age, sickness, and death; being the vessel of the suffering of change, carrying likewise the coarse-heaviness seeds of pleasant feeling's own inevitable loss; and being, in its own very nature, the suffering of formations, the aggregates' own ceaseless flowing and changing already constituting suffering in itself. The treatise then turns to an extended examination of this final mark, the suffering of formations, in far greater depth, explaining that formations produced by the karma and affliction driving future existence, present within every kind of embodied being without exception, are capable of generating further affliction and suffering in turn, and that this very capacity, embedded as a pervasive coarse-heaviness within the storehouse consciousness itself, follows a being into whichever of the three realms they are reborn, since the storehouse consciousness belonging to any one realm already carries within it the latent seeds of all three realms together.

Chinese Source

It should be known that this too has five marks: being the vessel of birth's suffering, being the basis of birth's own vessel, being the vessel of the suffering of pain, being the vessel of the suffering of change, and being, in nature, the suffering of formations. Moreover, as the Blessed One spoke of three kinds of suffering nature, what here is called the suffering of formations? Formations produced by the karma and affliction of future existence, within each and every embodied being, capable of giving rise to every affliction and every suffering in turn, established as belonging entirely to pervasive coarse-heaviness, also called coarse-heaviness, is the very nature of the suffering of formations. It is on the basis of this suffering of formations that the Blessed One said, in brief, that the five clinging-aggregates are all called suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this deep excursion into the suffering of formations, according to the treatise's own commentary tradition, was actually relocated here by the redactor Hán Qīngjìng from its own original position much further ahead in the text, precisely because it completes the discussion of the five clinging-aggregates' own suffering so naturally at exactly this point, an editorial judgment he treats as a genuinely helpful piece of scholarly care rather than an arbitrary rearrangement. He highlights the storehouse consciousness's own remarkable property, carrying the latent seeds of all three realms simultaneously regardless of which realm a being is actually reborn into, as the technical foundation beneath this entire discussion, explaining precisely why the suffering of formations follows a being everywhere, into any realm whatsoever, since the very seeds capable of generating it are never actually left behind.

22. Section: Why Only Three Poisons — Mapped Onto Pleasure, Pain, and Neutrality

The treatise completes its analogy: the formations produced by karma and affliction, established as coarse-heaviness within the storehouse consciousness, genuinely resemble a hot abscess; within this very suffering of formations, pleasant feeling resembles the abscess touched by something cool, painful feeling resembles it touched by hot ash, and neutral feeling, free of either touch, resembles the abscess's own intrinsic suffering finally becoming clearly apparent on its own. It is specifically within pleasant feeling that greed increases, a being growing attached the moment ease arises; specifically within painful feeling that hatred increases, aversion arising the moment discomfort is met; and specifically within neutral feeling that delusion increases, manifesting as four inverted views, mistaking the impermanent for permanent, the inherently suffering for pleasant, the impure for pure, and the selfless for a genuine self. Pleasant feeling, joined with greed, drives unwholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind that leads to the evil destinies, or else, through craving for continued existence, ripens into suffering within whichever destiny a being is next reborn; painful feeling, joined with hatred, likewise drives unwholesome conduct leading to the evil destinies, generating both present and future suffering alike; and neutral feeling, joined with delusion's own fourfold inversion, produces an unwillingness to ever release these very aggregates, a being clinging instead to conditioned existence itself. It is precisely for this reason, the treatise explains, that the Blessed One, despite naming countless afflictions and secondary afflictions elsewhere, established only three root afflictions, greed, hatred, and delusion, mapped directly onto pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling in turn — and it is with this same hidden intention that pleasant feeling should be observed as itself suffering-generating, painful feeling should be observed as though a poisoned arrow, and neutral feeling should be observed as impermanent, a dissolving thing that can never ultimately escape either kind of suffering.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Pleasant feeling generates much attachment, and so greed is said to increase within it. Painful feeling generates much hatred, and so hatred is said to increase within it. What is established, within the coarse-heaviness manifested by neither pain nor pleasure, as inverted views computing permanence in the impermanent, pleasure in what is entirely suffering, purity in the impure, and self in the selfless, is why delusion is said to increase within neutral feeling. Therefore, though there are countless afflictions and secondary afflictions, the Blessed One established only three root afflictions: greed, hatred, and delusion. With this same hidden intention, the Blessed One said: pleasant feeling should be observed as itself a suffering-generating dharma; painful feeling should be observed as though a poisoned arrow; and neutral feeling should be observed as impermanent, a dissolving dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this completed mapping, three poisons onto three feelings, as one of the treatise's own most elegant unifying structures, resolving what might otherwise look like an arbitrary shortlist of afflictions into a genuinely systematic account grounded in nothing more exotic than how a mind ordinarily responds to pleasant, painful, and neutral experience alike. He highlights the instruction to view neutral feeling as itself impermanent and dissolving as this section's own most subtle and demanding teaching, since neutral feeling offers no obvious motivation to examine it closely the way pain or pleasure naturally does, making delusion's own quiet increase within it correspondingly the hardest of the three poisons to actually notice and address.

23. Section: Even the Highest Heaven Holds No Appeal to a Noble One

The treatise turns to how the suffering of the five clinging-aggregates is subsumed within the four noble truths themselves, beginning with the truth of suffering: genuinely observing the aggregates' own impermanent nature gradually severs every one of the four inverted views in turn, and these very formations, seen this way, constitute the truth of suffering in its own ultimate sense — a truth so thoroughly realized by noble ones and those approaching genuine wisdom that even rebirth in the very highest, most tranquil of all conditioned states, the peak of existence itself, holds no appeal for them whatsoever, let alone rebirth within the wretched, suffering-ridden hells far beneath it. The treatise then turns to the truth of origination: it is precisely this same karma and affliction, capable of generating future existence, that constitutes the truth of origination, understood specifically by way of its own dependent, conditioned characteristic, arising through cause and never possessing any fixed, independent nature of its own — a designation the treatise's own commentary tradition explains in two complementary ways, either as ultimately empty and identical with suchness itself, provisionally labeled karma and affliction only through conventional language, or simply as the general characteristic shared by whatever actually gathers together and gives rise to a future result. The treatise then turns to reveal a further, even more precise point regarding this same truth of origination: among every affliction capable of generating future existence, it is craving alone that constitutes its very foremost and most essential mark.

Ru-Yi Translation

If one can truly observe the nature of impermanence, one gradually severs every inverted view. These very formations are the truth of suffering in its ultimate sense. Having observed this with noble wisdom, noble and worthy ones do not wish even for the clinging-aggregates of the very peak of existence, its own most extreme stillness, let alone the wretched hells beneath it. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the claim that even the peak of existence holds no genuine appeal for a noble one as this whole section's own most radical implication, since that very state represents the highest, most refined pleasure any conditioned being can possibly attain within saṃsāra, and its explicit rejection here shows that genuine liberation is never a search for a better or subtler form of pleasant feeling at all, but a complete stepping outside the entire framework in which pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling are even meaningful categories to chase or avoid. He notes that the treatise's own promised further precision, that craving specifically, among every affliction capable of generating future existence, constitutes the origination-truth's own foremost mark, directly echoes a teaching already given at length back in fascicle 58, the treatise here circling back to reaffirm, from within this new context of the eight sufferings, a conclusion its own earlier analysis had already independently reached.

24. Section: Two Kinds of Cessation, and a Third Sutra on Three Kinds of Persons

The treatise explains why the Blessed One, within the sutras themselves, singles out craving alone as the truth of origination: based on the most evident, readily recognized principle, craving is what most directly and visibly draws forth a future result, even though, considered by way of its own underlying characteristic, every affliction and karma capable of generating future existence equally qualifies as origination in the fullest sense. Turning to the truth of cessation, the treatise names two kinds: cessation established through nirvana with residue, the permanent extinguishing of the very seeds of affliction belonging to the three realms while the karmically produced body itself still remains; and cessation established through nirvana without residue, the permanent severing of both the cause and the very result of suffering together, karmic action having been fully exhausted, no future rebirth arising, and the present life's own remaining formations simply, naturally dissolving away entirely, this second cessation representing suffering's own truly final and complete end. And the truth of the path belongs to genuine right view realizing ultimate truth itself, together with every noble practice guided by that very right view in turn, spanning the entire progression from the earliest stage of accumulating provisions all the way through to the stage beyond any further learning. Having explained why the coarser marks of suffering, birth, aging, sickness, and death, are taught before the far subtler suffering of the five clinging-aggregates itself, precisely because that subtlest suffering is hardest of all to genuinely grasp, the treatise brings its entire citation of the Sutra of the Eight Sufferings to its own close, and turns to a third and final sutra, deciding the differences among persons by establishing three kinds, lower, middling, and superior, through numerous distinct gateways in turn — the very first gateway distinguishing them by whether their own conduct benefits themselves, benefits others, both, or neither: a lower person benefits neither themselves nor others; a middling person benefits either themselves or others, but not both together; and a superior person genuinely benefits both themselves and others at once.

Moreover, the permanent extinguishing of this affliction class's own coarse-heaviness is the truth of cessation established through the increase of nirvana with residue. Moreover, wishing that suffering be comprehended, origination permanently severed, cessation directly realized, and the path cultivated, the marks of the noble truths are briefly established. And so that the coarse marks of the truth of suffering serve as a foundation for gradually entering its subtle marks, the various sufferings beginning with birth are set forth first, and only afterward is the suffering of the five clinging-aggregates shown. Moreover, according to differences in conduct, three kinds of person are established: lower, middling, and superior. One without self-benefiting or other-benefiting conduct is a lower person; one with either self-benefiting or other-benefiting conduct alone is a middling person; one with both self-benefiting and other-benefiting conduct is a superior person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between nirvana with and without residue as the treatise's own precise account of liberation's own two genuine stages, a first, real freedom from affliction's own seed still lived out within an ordinary body, and a second, truly final freedom in which even that body's own natural course finally completes itself, neither stage lesser than the other but each representing something authentically different. He highlights the very first gateway distinguishing lower, middling, and superior persons, benefiting neither self nor other, one or the other alone, or both together, as a deceptively simple standard that immediately reveals something uncomfortable: a person who diligently improves only their own life while never once helping another remains, by this very measure, only middling at best, genuine excellence requiring both directions of benefit at once.

25. Section: Doing Evil Without Delighting in It, and Delighting in Evil Without Doing It

The treatise names a second gateway distinguishing persons: four kinds exist with respect to doing evil and delighting in evil. Some do evil yet do not delight in it: genuinely believing that evil deeds bring about undesirable future results, they nonetheless commit unwholesome acts through a momentary lapse of mindfulness, through heedlessness, or through the influence of bad companions. Others delight in evil yet do not actually do it: habituated to evil across past lives and still drawn by unwholesome desire, they nonetheless, through the good fortune of encountering genuine friends, hearing correct teaching, and relying on proper reflection, come to recognize that evil deeds bring undesirable future results, and so exert real effort to distance themselves from evil regardless of their own lingering inclination toward it. Still others both do evil and delight in it, naturally inclined toward evil and making no effort whatsoever to distance themselves from it, the worst of the four. And others neither do evil nor delight in it at all, naturally good by disposition and genuinely free of evil throughout, the very fewest mistakes marking a life most easily brought to spiritual accomplishment, since a mind unburdened by extensive wrongdoing requiring confession and purification settles into concentration and wisdom far more readily. Mapped onto the three tiers, one who both does evil and delights in it is a lower person; one who does evil without delighting in it, or delights in evil without doing it, is a middling person; and one who neither does evil nor delights in it at all is a superior person.

If, habituated to evil since past lives, one delights in evils, drawn by evil desire, yet, through nearness to a good person, hearing correct teaching, and relying on correct attention, sees that evil conduct brings undesirable future results, and exerts effort to distance oneself from evil, this is called delighting in evil without doing it. If one is by nature inclined to evil and does not distance from it, this is called both doing evil and delighting in it. If one is by nature not inclined to evil and is able to distance from it, this is called neither doing evil nor delighting in it. Among these, one who both does evil and delights in it is called a lower person; one who does evil without delighting in it, or delights in evil without doing it, is called a middling person; and one who neither does evil nor delights in it is called a superior person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second and third kinds of person, doing evil without delighting in it and delighting in evil without doing it, as the treatise's own genuinely encouraging middle ground for most sincere practitioners, since almost no one arrives at practice already free of every lingering evil inclination, and this classification insists that active restraint in the face of real temptation still counts as meaningfully better than surrendering to it, even if full inner purity has not yet been reached. He highlights the observation that fewer past mistakes make concentration and wisdom easier to develop as a practical, rather than merely moral, reason to take this classification seriously, genuine spiritual progress and a relatively unburdened conscience turning out to be closely, causally connected rather than separate concerns.

26. Section: What a Person Values Most, and a King's Three Kinds of Completeness

A third gateway distinguishes three kinds of person by what they most value: valuing sensory desire above all else, valuing worldly enterprise above all else, and valuing the true Dharma above all else, the first named a lower person, the second a middling person, and the third a superior person, since the treatise makes plain that anyone who genuinely prizes the Dharma above the world's own pleasures and ambitions already ranks among the superior. A fourth gateway distinguishes three kinds by what a person actually treats as their own proper business: one who sustains their own life through unwholesome conduct, killing, stealing, and the like, treats what is genuinely improper as their own affair, and is a lower person; one who, fearing the consequences of wrongdoing, instead cultivates wholesome conduct, treats their own genuine affair as their own affair, and is a middling person; and a bodhisattva, who treats every other being's own affairs as though they were entirely their own, sharing fully in another's suffering and another's joy with no thought of self left over, is a superior person. A fifth gateway distinguishes kings specifically through three kinds of completeness: completeness of ripened result, being born into wealth and nobility, enjoying long life and little illness, belonging to an accomplished lineage, and possessing innate, unlearned intelligence from birth; completeness of capability, governing state affairs skillfully and heroically, subduing what remains unconquered while protecting what has already submitted, broadly managing enterprise without falling into indulgence, and discerning ministers and commoners alike with real clarity; and completeness of virtue, upholding and abiding in the true Dharma, whether called a dharma-king for sustaining it or a great king for abiding within it, and joining together with the entire royal household, ministers, and people alike in giving, cultivating merit, observing the fasting days, and keeping the precepts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that anyone genuinely engaged in studying this very treatise already qualifies, by the third gateway's own standard, as a superior person, since sincere engagement with the Dharma is itself the defining mark this classification names, a gentle but real encouragement folded into what might otherwise read as pure abstraction. He highlights the fourth gateway's own description of the bodhisattva, treating another's affairs entirely as one's own, as this whole section's own clearest statement yet of what genuinely distinguishes the superior tier throughout every one of these classifications, a consistent willingness to let another being's own good become, without remainder, one's own personal concern.

27. Section: Three Fruits for Three Kinds of King, and Three Kinds of Minister

Each of a king's three completenesses draws forth its own corresponding fruit: completeness of ripened result is enjoying the very fruit of pure karma cultivated in a past life; completeness of capability is enjoying the beloved fruit of this present life itself; and completeness of virtue is enjoying, still to come, the complete fruit of pure karma being cultivated even now for lives yet ahead. Mapped onto the three tiers, a king lacking all three kinds of completeness is a lower person; a king possessing ripened result alone, capability alone, or both together, is a middling person; and a king lacking none of the three completenesses at all is a superior person, one whose past, present, and future alike are all genuinely well-founded, having cultivated pure karma before, cultivating it now, and thereby securing pure results still to come. A sixth gateway turns from kings to their own ministers, naming three kinds in turn: one who is genuinely loyal and trustworthy, yet possesses neither real skill nor real wisdom; one who is loyal and skilled, yet still lacks genuine wisdom; and one who possesses loyalty, skill, and wisdom all together in full.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three-fruit structure mapped onto past, present, and future completeness as a fitting echo of the treatise's own recurring three-times framework used throughout this entire fascicle, a superior king's own life cohering across an entire span of lifetimes rather than being judged by any single moment's own success or failure alone. He highlights the newly opened threefold classification of ministers as a natural companion to the earlier classification of kings, the same three-tiered logic, capacity building upon capacity, now applied downward to those who actually carry out a ruler's own decisions, suggesting that good governance ultimately depends on getting this same classification right at every level of an entire administration.

28. Section: Four Kinds of Speech, Sharp and Sweet

Gateway six closes by mapping the three kinds of minister onto the three tiers directly, a loyal minister lacking both skill and wisdom counted a lower person, one possessing loyalty and skill but still lacking wisdom counted a middling person, and one possessing loyalty, skill, and wisdom together counted a superior person, with the text adding a fourth, still lower case beneath even the first, a minister lacking loyalty, skill, and wisdom all at once, untrustworthy and without ability or judgment alike. A seventh gateway turns to speech itself, noting that beings of the desire realm, driven by investigation and deliberation, inevitably speak, and that speech itself divides into four kinds: words that seem loving but are not, being smooth and pleasing yet unhelpful, never truly urging the listener toward abandoning evil and cultivating good; words that seem unloving but are not, being harsh or displeasing to hear yet genuinely well-suited to help, as when a teacher's sharp rebuke or a parent's angry scolding is spoken entirely out of love, meant only to correct and uplift; words that are neither loving nor helpful, harsh and displeasing while also arising from real anger with no genuine care behind them; and words that are both loving and helpful, gentle, pleasing, and genuinely suited to the listener's own good, landing like a spring breeze on the face, striking a well-trained horse as lightly as the mere shadow of a raised whip.

Chinese Source

初名下士，次名中士，後名上士。若不忠信，無有技能，亦無智慧，當知此臣下中之下。又有四語。一、非愛似愛，二、愛似非愛，三、非愛似非愛，四、愛似愛。諸有語言辭句善順，然非所宜，是名初語。或有語言辭句勃逆，然是所宜，是第二語。或有語言辭句勃逆，亦非所宜，是第三語。或有語言辭句善順，亦是所宜，是第四語。若有宣說非愛似非愛、非愛似愛語者，是下士；若有宣說愛似非愛語者，是中士；若有宣說愛似愛語者，是上士。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first is a lower person, the second middling, the third superior. One lacking loyalty, skill, and wisdom alike is lower than the lowest. There are also four kinds of speech: unloving-seeming-loving, loving-seeming-unloving, unloving-seeming-unloving, and loving-seeming-loving. Speech whose words are smooth and pleasing, yet not truly helpful, is the first. Speech whose words are harsh, yet truly helpful, is the second. Speech whose words are harsh and also unhelpful is the third. Speech whose words are smooth and also truly helpful is the fourth. One who speaks the unloving-unhelpful or unloving-seeming-loving kind is a lower person; one who speaks the loving-seeming-unloving kind is a middling person; one who speaks the loving-seeming-loving kind is a superior person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on the Chan master's own thirty blows for a right answer and thirty blows for a wrong one alike, a teaching method that looks purely harsh from the outside yet is, by this fourfold framework's own definition, unmistakably the second kind of speech, loving in motive however unloving in appearance, since its entire aim is the student's own genuine progress. He notes that only a buddha or bodhisattva truly knows which of the four kinds a given listener needs in a given moment, since some beings soften at gentle words while others only wake up under a sharp rebuke, and reads this entire gateway as a caution against judging any teacher's own words by tone alone, without first asking what result they were actually spoken to produce.

29. Section: Three Ways of Enjoying Wealth and Desire

An eighth gateway distinguishes three kinds of person by how they enjoy the objects of desire. The first gathers wealth through unlawful and reckless means, unable even to properly support themselves or their own spouse and children, let alone give anything to renunciants or learned brahmins as a field of merit, and is a lower person. The second gathers wealth through a mixture of lawful and unlawful, careful and reckless means, and although this suffices to support themselves, their family, and their friends comfortably, they still give nothing to renunciants or brahmins, and are a middling person. The third gathers wealth exclusively through lawful and careful means, supporting themselves and their family well while also giving generously to renunciants and brahmins and cultivating merit, and is a superior person, this threefold distinction turning entirely on whether wealth is gathered rightly and, once gathered, whether any of it is shared beyond one's own household.

Chinese Source

復有三種受諸欲者。或有受欲，非法孟浪積集財寶，不能安樂正養己身及與妻子，廣說乃至不於沙門、婆羅門所修殖福田。或有受欲，法或非法、孟浪或非積集財寶，能以安樂正養己身、妻子、眷屬及知友等，不於沙門、婆羅門所修殖福田。或有受欲，一向以法及不孟浪積集財寶，能以安樂正養己身，廣說乃至能於沙門、婆羅門所修殖福田。此三種中，初名下士，次名中士，後名上士。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also three kinds of persons who enjoy desire. One gathers wealth unlawfully and recklessly, unable to properly support themselves and their spouse, let alone give to renunciants and brahmins as a field of merit. One gathers wealth by lawful or unlawful, reckless or careful means, able to support themselves, their spouse, their family, and their friends, yet gives nothing to renunciants and brahmins. One gathers wealth exclusively by lawful and careful means, able to properly support themselves, and also gives to renunciants and brahmins. Of these three, the first is a lower person, the second middling, the third superior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi points out that this gateway names two separate axes at once inside a single classification, how wealth is earned and what becomes of it afterward, and that a person's own tier depends on both together, since even lawfully earned wealth kept entirely within one household still falls short of the superior standard set here. He connects this back to the earlier gateway on treating others' affairs as one's own, noting that a willingness to share one's own livelihood with renunciants and the wider community is simply that same generosity, now made concrete in the specific terms of everyday household economics.

30. Section: Three Ways of Eating, from Gluttony toward Freedom

A ninth gateway turns to the manner of eating itself, opening with the first of three kinds of person. The first eats with greedy attachment, indulging and gorging without restraint, seeing no fault in it at all and knowing nothing of freedom from it, the very picture of the ancient mythical beast called tāo-tiè, headless-bodied and purely ravenous, whose name later came to mean gluttony itself, greed for wealth called tāo and greed for food called tiè. The second is a practitioner who eats only after careful reflection, neither craving nor clinging nor gorging nor losing themselves in the food, clearly seeing the fault in unrestrained eating and knowing well how freedom from it is reached, yet not having actually severed or fully realized this freedom, since eating still nourishes the six sense faculties and the mind along with its mental factors, and in turn feeds the contact of pleasant sensation and the volition behind it, which together moisten consciousness itself and keep the wheel of birth and death turning without rest, so that this second person, though possessing real restraint through reflection, has not yet uprooted the underlying seed of craving that eating itself keeps watered.

Chinese Source

復有三人。一者、有人貪染而食，愛著、饕餮乃至耽湎，不見過患、不知出離。二者、有人思擇而食，不染不著，亦不饕餮、吞吸、迷悶、堅住、耽湎，深見過患，善知出離，而於此食未斷未知。三者、有人思擇而食，不生貪染，廣說乃至深見過患，善知出離，又於此食已斷已知。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also three kinds of person. First, one who eats with greed and attachment, indulging and gorging without restraint, seeing no fault in it and knowing nothing of freedom from it. Second, one who eats after reflection, without craving or clinging or gorging, deeply seeing the fault in it and knowing well the freedom from it, yet not having severed or fully realized this freedom. Third, one who eats after reflection, giving rise to no craving at all, deeply seeing the fault in it and knowing well the freedom from it, and having already severed and fully realized this freedom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the etymology of tāo-tiè at some length, a headless, bodiless beast of pure appetite from ancient legend, and treats its presence here as the text's own vivid warning against letting so ordinary an act as eating become the very shape of one's entire character. He highlights the second person's own situation as spiritually significant precisely because it is incomplete, real restraint achieved through reflection and clear seeing, yet the underlying seed of craving still present and unsevered, a state he describes as genuinely on the path but not yet arrived, still needing the deeper power of meditative insight to reach what the third person has already fully realized.
