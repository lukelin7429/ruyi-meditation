Fascicle 61 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十一 · Three Sutras Cited in Full

The King Who Felt Neither Wounded Nor Flattered

With the abstract analysis of affliction, karma, and the opening of rebirth defilement now behind it, the treatise turns to a different register entirely, citing three sutras at length to ground everything already established in concrete human terms: a king's own true virtues and faults, the eight sufferings every being faces, and the differing capacities of lesser, middling, and superior persons — opening with King Udayana's own visit to the Buddha, seeking to understand why neither false blame nor false praise ever genuinely moves him.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 61 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A King Immune to Both False Blame and False Praise

Having completed, across fascicles 58 through 60, the full deciding discernment of the three stages of examination and reflection through their own establishing realms and the three kinds of defilement, affliction, karma, and rebirth, the treatise now turns to a fourth and final portion of this same topic, drawing directly on the Buddha's own recorded teaching to ground everything already established through vivid example: sutra citations illustrating defilement's own faults and the merits of giving, moral discipline, and meditation; a detailed account of a king's own virtues and faults; the distinguishing marks of the eight sufferings; and the differing capacities of lesser, middling, and superior persons — all offered to guide beings toward genuinely wholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind. The treatise opens with King Udayana's own visit to the Buddha, bowing at his feet and posing a question drawn from his own practice: whenever some ascetic or brahmin comes before him and rebukes him for some fault he does not actually possess, his own mind feels no regret, no distress, and no sorrow whatsoever, since, examining himself honestly, he simply finds no trace of the alleged fault to be found; and whenever some ascetic or brahmin instead praises him for some virtue he does not actually possess, his own mind feels no elation or excitement either, since, examining himself just as honestly, he finds no trace of the alleged virtue there either.

Chinese Source

復次，如佛世尊為出愛王所說經言：彼王一時往詣佛所，頂禮佛足，白言：世尊！有一沙門、若婆羅門來至我所，以不真實過失，現前呵諫於我，我於爾時，其心不生悔惱憂慼。何以故？觀此過失，於我自身都不見故。又有沙門、若婆羅門來至我所，以不真實功德，現前讚勸於我，我於爾時，心亦不生歡喜踊躍。何以故？觀此功德，於我自身都不見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, as the Blessed One taught in the sutra spoken for King Udayana: that king once went to where the Buddha was, bowed at the Buddha's feet, and said: Blessed One, when some ascetic or brahmin comes before me and rebukes me to my face with an untrue fault, my mind at that time feels no regret, distress, or sorrow. Why is this so? Because, examining this fault, I find no trace of it in myself. And when some ascetic or brahmin comes before me and praises me to my face with an untrue virtue, my mind at that time likewise feels no joy or elation. Why is this so? Because, examining this virtue, I find no trace of it in myself either.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats King Udayana's own opening observation as a genuinely sophisticated piece of self-knowledge for a ruler to have arrived at on his own, since it requires the same honest, unflinching self-examination the treatise has praised throughout this entire four-fascicle stretch, applied here not to a monastic but to a king still fully immersed in worldly power and its usual flatteries and slanders alike. He highlights the king's own practical motive for eventually seeking out the Buddha, wanting to actually learn what his own true faults and true virtues are rather than simply dismissing every external judgment, as the very opposite of complacency, a genuinely humble use of his own initial equanimity as a foundation for further, deeper self-improvement rather than an excuse to stop examining himself at all.

2. Section: The Buddha Names Ten Faults of a King

Reflecting alone afterward in a quiet place, the king wondered how he might come to know a ruler's own genuine faults and genuine virtues, resolving to abandon the former and cultivate the latter once he understood them clearly, and asked himself which ascetic or brahmin could possibly explain this to him in full. Recognizing that only the Blessed One, knowing and seeing all things without exception, could truly answer such a question, he went directly to the Buddha and posed it there: what, in truth, are a king's own genuine faults, and what are a king's own genuine virtues? The Blessed One answered by first naming six topics he would address in turn: a king's own faults, a king's own virtues, the very gateways through which a king's own position declines, the skillful means by which a king may govern well, the qualities that make a king genuinely beloved, and the means by which those very qualities are actually brought about. Taking up the first of these, the Buddha explained that a king's faults are, in brief, of ten kinds, and that a king who falls into any of these, however vast his treasury, however capable his ministers, and however powerful his armies, does not deserve his people's genuine trust and reliance: being of low birth; lacking genuine self-mastery; being naturally cruel by disposition; being quick to fierce, uncontrolled anger; being stingy and sparing in rewarding others; heeding flattering, deceitful counsel; acting without careful thought and without regard for proper protocol; disregarding wholesome conduct entirely; failing to distinguish who deserves what and forgetting kindnesses already received; and indulging, entirely and without restraint, in heedless self-indulgence.

Chinese Source

爾時，世尊告出愛王曰：大王！大王今者應當了知王之過失、王之功德、王衰損門、王方便門、王可愛法，及能引發王可愛法。云何名為王之過失？大王當知！王過失者略有十種。王若成就如是過失，雖有大府庫、有大輔佐、有大軍眾，然不可歸仰。何等為十？一、種姓不高，二、不得自在，三、立性暴惡，四、猛利憤發，五、恩惠奢薄，六、受邪佞言，七、所作不思不順儀則，八、不顧善法，九、不知差別忘所作恩，十、一向縱任專行放逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Blessed One told King Udayana: Great King, you should now know a king's own faults, a king's own virtues, the gateways of a king's own decline, the skillful means of a king, the qualities beloved of a king, and what draws forth those beloved qualities. What is called a king's own fault? Great King, you should know that a king's faults are, in brief, of ten kinds. If a king possesses such faults, though he has a great treasury, great ministers, and a great army, he does not deserve reliance. What are the ten? First, low birth. Second, lack of self-mastery. Third, a naturally cruel disposition. Fourth, fierce, uncontrolled anger. Fifth, sparing and stingy kindness. Sixth, heeding deceitful counsel. Seventh, acting without thought and without regard for proper protocol. Eighth, disregarding wholesome dharma. Ninth, failing to distinguish and forgetting kindness received. Tenth, wholly indulgent and heedless conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this entire extended teaching, addressed originally to a ruler navigating the very real pressures of worldly power, remains strikingly applicable well beyond kingship itself, since anyone holding real authority over others, whether a business leader, an administrator, or the head of a household, faces essentially the same temptations toward cruelty, stinginess, flattery-seeking, and heedless self-indulgence this list names one by one. He highlights forgetting kindness already received as a fault worth particular attention, since it names a failure not of judgment but of memory and gratitude, the kind of ordinary human forgetfulness that can quietly corrode a leader's own relationships with those who have genuinely supported them long before any dramatic wrongdoing ever becomes visible.

3. Section: Five Shades of Royal Cruelty, and the Gravest of Them All

The Buddha explains each of the ten faults in turn. Low birth belongs to a king born into some inferior royal lineage rather than one of long-standing eminence, or one born within a royal house yet through some concubine or lesser wife rather than the true consort, resembling the ruling line only in name, or one who has risen instead from among ministers, chancellors, royal preceptors, or ordinary officials rather than genuine royal stock. Lack of self-mastery belongs to a king controlled by his own ministers, chancellors, preceptors, and officials, unable to act, reward, or freely enjoy even the finest pleasures according to his own genuine wish. A naturally cruel disposition unfolds across five distinct shades: cruelty displayed openly to another's face, banishing and berating them the moment the slightest displeasing thing occurs, scowling and erupting in coarse, furious language; cruelty displayed instead behind another's back, before everyone but the very person concerned; a brooding, resentful cruelty kept entirely private, harboring real malice and grudge yet not maintained for very long before it passes; and this same brooding, resentful cruelty sustained instead over a long stretch of time, nursed and never actually released. Of these, the Buddha states plainly, it is specifically the long-sustained, brooding cruelty that constitutes the truly grave fault, every other shade of cruelty falling short of this same severity.

Chinese Source

云何名王種姓不高？謂有國王，隨一下類王家而生，非宿尊貴；或雖於此王家而生，賤女之子，不相似子；或是大臣、輔相、國師、群官等子。如是名王種姓不高。云何名王不得自在？謂有國王，為諸大臣、輔相、國師、群官所制，不隨所欲作所應作，錫賚群臣；於妙五欲，亦不如意歡娛遊戲。如是名王不得自在。謂有國王，諸群臣類或餘人等，隨於一處現行少小不如意事，即便對面擯黜，發麤惡言，咆勃忿恚，顰蹙而住，時生憤發。設不對面，背彼向餘而作於前擯辱等事。設不對面、亦不背彼向餘而作於前黜罵等事。然唯內意憤恚鬱怏，懷惱害心、懷怨恨心，然不長時持憤恚心相續不捨。復有內意憤恚鬱怏，懷惱害心、懷怨恨心，亦於長時持憤恚心相續不捨。大王當知！長久暴惡名獲大罪，非是餘者。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called a king's low birth? A king born into some inferior royal house, not one of long-standing eminence; or, though born within such a house, the child of a lowly woman, not resembling the true heir; or the child of a minister, chancellor, royal preceptor, or official. This is called a king's low birth. What is called a king's lack of self-mastery? A king controlled by his own ministers, chancellors, preceptors, and officials, unable to act and reward according to his own wish, nor freely enjoy the finest pleasures. This is called lack of self-mastery. A king whose ministers or others, wherever some slight displeasure arises, are immediately banished to his face, met with coarse language, furious shouting, and a scowling countenance, erupting in anger. Or, not to their face, doing the same behind their back before others. Or, neither to their face nor behind their back, yet inwardly harboring resentment and malice, though not sustained for long. Or further, inwardly harboring resentment and malice, sustained over a long time without release. Great King, you should know that it is specifically this long-sustained cruelty that constitutes a grave fault, not the others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own careful gradation of cruelty, from a public outburst quickly spent to a private grudge nursed indefinitely, as a genuinely perceptive piece of psychology, since the loudest, most visible display of temper is not, in the end, identified as the gravest fault at all, while the quiet, sustained, never-released resentment, invisible to everyone but the one who carries it, is named as the true danger. He highlights this ranking as a useful corrective for anyone inclined to judge character purely by outward volume, since a leader who shouts and then genuinely lets go may, by this very measure, be less dangerous than one who never raises their voice at all yet never once forgives.

4. Section: Rewards Given Only After Resentment Has Already Set In

Fierce, uncontrolled anger belongs to a king who, faced with only the smallest fault or minor overstepping among his ministers, responds by stripping away their salary and rank, seizing their wives and concubines for himself, or subjecting them to severe punishment. Sparing, stingy kindness belongs to a king whose ministers serve him faithfully and well, genuinely satisfying his every wish, and who responds only with faint, half-hearted words of comfort rather than genuine praise, granting rank, salary, and reward for real accomplishment only incompletely, contrary to proper custom, whether giving something already worn or depleted, delaying the reward for a long stretch of time, first extracting some further exhausting labor before finally granting it, or withholding it until the recipient has already grown resentful and only then, grudgingly, giving it at all. Heeding deceitful counsel belongs to a king whose ministers, though genuinely lacking real wisdom, carry themselves with an arrogant pretense of wisdom, driven by greed, factional corruption, and unfamiliarity with proper law, secretly plotting rebellion and governing poorly, and who nonetheless listens to, trusts, and acts upon whatever advice these very ministers offer, his own governance, treasury, reputation, and administration all declining as a direct result. And acting without thought and without regard for proper protocol belongs to a king who neither investigates nor carefully considers his own ministers before assigning them duty, entrusting sensitive responsibilities to those unfit for them while overlooking those genuinely capable, commanding the unsuited while leaving the suited idle, and rewarding fault while punishing merit — a king, further, who fails to uphold his own predecessors' court protocols, so that ministers interrupt one another mid-speech at the royal assembly, show neither respect nor restraint in bringing forward disputes, and neither faithfully carry out the king's own decrees nor properly abide by his commands at all.

Chinese Source

云何名王猛利憤發？謂有國王，諸群臣等有小愆過、有少違越，便削封祿，奪去妻妾，或以重罰而刑罰之。如是名王猛利憤發。云何名王恩惠奢薄？謂有國王，諸群臣等供奉侍衛，雖極清淨，善稱其心，而以微劣軟言慰諭；頒賜爵祿、酬賞勳庸不能圓滿，不順常式。或損耗已、或稽留已、或推注已、或怨恨已，然後方與。如是名王恩惠奢薄。云何名王受邪佞言？謂有國王，諸群臣等實非聰叡，有聰叡慢，貪濁偏黨，不閑憲式，情懷謀叛，不修善政，聽受信用如是輩人所進諫議，由此因緣，王務、財寶、名稱、善政并皆衰損。如是名王受邪佞言。云何名王所作不思不順儀則？謂有國王，不能究察、不審究察、不能思擇、不審思擇諸群臣輩，於彼彼務機密事中，不堪委任而委任之，堪委任者而不委任。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called a king's fierce, uncontrolled anger? A king whose ministers, for even the smallest fault or minor overstepping, has his rank and salary stripped, his wives and concubines seized, or is subjected to severe punishment. This is called fierce, uncontrolled anger. What is called a king's sparing, stingy kindness? A king whose ministers serve him purely and to his genuine satisfaction, yet who offers only faint, feeble words of comfort, granting rank and reward for merit incompletely, contrary to custom, whether depleted, delayed, extracted through further labor, or given only once resentment has already arisen. This is called sparing, stingy kindness. What is called a king's heeding deceitful counsel? A king whose ministers, lacking real wisdom yet carrying an arrogant pretense of it, driven by greed and faction, unfamiliar with proper law, secretly plotting rebellion and governing poorly, are nonetheless trusted and heeded, his own governance, treasury, reputation, and administration all declining as a result. This is called heeding deceitful counsel. What is called a king's acting without thought and without regard for protocol? A king who neither investigates nor carefully considers his ministers, entrusting sensitive duties to the unfit while overlooking the fit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the description of a reward given only once the recipient has already grown resentful as one of the sutra's own sharpest psychological observations, since it names a genuinely common failure of leadership, technically fulfilling an obligation while completely forfeiting whatever goodwill that same obligation, promptly honored, might otherwise have earned. He highlights the parallel drawn between heeding flattering counsel and a broader failure of judgment in appointing and rewarding others as two faces of the very same underlying weakness, a ruler's own failure to look carefully at the people around him, whether trusting the wrong advisors or misplacing responsibility among the wrong officials, both ultimately traceable to the same absence of careful, honest observation.
