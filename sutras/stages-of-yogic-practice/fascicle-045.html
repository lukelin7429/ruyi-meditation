Fascicle 45 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十五 · The Factors of Awakening, Continued

Four Reliances, Four Understandings, the Provisions for Awakening

Continuing directly from fascicle 44's opening of the seventeenth chapter, this fascicle carries the factors of awakening through nine further topics: the four correct reliances, the four unhindered understandings, the provisions for awakening, the thirty-seven classical factors of awakening themselves, cultivating calming and insight, skillful means, wondrous mnemonic retention, one's own correct aspiration, and the three concentrations.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 45 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle 45 Opens — the Factors of Awakening Continue With the Sixth of Fifteen Topics, the Four Correct Reliances

Fascicle 45 opens as the second of three installments of the seventeenth chapter, the factors of awakening — the first five topics already completed in fascicle 44, the final topic still to come in fascicle 46. The sixth topic, the four correct reliances, opens by naming its first reliance: relying on meaning rather than on ornamental phrasing, so that a bodhisattva listening to the teaching seeks the substance a teacher is actually conveying rather than the polish of their language, receiving the meaning with genuine reverence even when it arrives through the plainest, most ordinary words.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處菩提分品第十七之二。云何菩薩修正四依？謂諸菩薩為求義故，從他聽法，不為求世藻飾文辭。菩薩求義不為求文而聽法時，雖遇常流言音說法，但依於義，恭敬聽受。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter seventeen, part two: the factors of awakening. How do bodhisattvas correctly cultivate the four reliances? Bodhisattvas who, seeking the meaning of the teaching, listen to the dharma from another, not seeking worldly, ornamental phrasing — when bodhisattvas seeking meaning rather than phrasing listen to the teaching, even when they encounter it delivered in the most ordinary, common speech, they nonetheless rely on its meaning, listening to and receiving it with reverence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi's opening note situates this fascicle within the chapter's own larger architecture: the fifteen topics of the factors of awakening were already named in outline at the very start of chapter seventeen in fascicle 44 — conscience and shame, firm resolve, a tireless mind, skill in treatises, skill in worldly knowledge, and then the nine topics beginning here in fascicle 45, with the fifteenth and final topic, the four dharma-summaries, held over for fascicle 46. "Reliance" itself, he notes, functions here as a corrective principle rather than a merely descriptive category: each of the four names a specific, easily-made mistake and the correct standard that guards against it.

He grounds the first reliance in the treatise's own earlier chapter on the stage of meditative equipoise, which explains that this reliance exists specifically to correct a common failing among ordinary listeners: a fondness for elegant, well-turned phrasing that can eclipse actual comprehension of what that phrasing means, so that beautiful language becomes mistaken for genuine understanding, or plain language is dismissed as though it could carry nothing of value. A bodhisattva relying on meaning listens past the surface texture of any given explanation, plain or polished alike, and receives with reverence whatever substance is actually being conveyed — since the dharma's own value never resided in its literary ornament to begin with. This closes the first reliance; the treatise turns next to the second, relying on the teaching itself rather than on whoever happens to be delivering it.

2. Milestone: Relying on the Teaching, Not on Whoever Happens to Deliver It — Recognizing Provisional and Complete Teaching for What Each Actually Is

The second reliance: correctly recognizing provisional teaching for what it is and complete teaching for what it is, then relying on the underlying principle itself rather than on the reputation of whoever speaks — not simply believing something because an elder, a well-known figure, or even someone attributed to the Buddha or the community said it. Resting on principle this way, a bodhisattva's own confidence in genuine truth remains unshaken, immune to being pulled away by any other influence.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩如實了知闇說、大說；如實知已，以理為依，不由耆長、眾所知識補特伽羅、若佛、若僧所說法故即便信受，是故不依補特伽羅。如是菩薩以理為依，補特伽羅非所依故，於真實義心不動搖，於正法中他緣匪奪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly recognize provisional teaching and complete teaching for what each is; having recognized this correctly, they rely on principle, not believing and accepting something as true merely because an elder, a well-known figure, or the Buddha or the community are said to have taught it — and for this reason they do not rely on the person. In this way, bodhisattvas rely on principle rather than on the person, and their mind remains unshaken regarding genuine truth, never pulled away by some other influence within the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "provisional teaching" and "complete teaching" through two readings preserved in the tradition: read against the two vehicles, provisional teaching names what the Buddha taught to śrāvakas eager only for their own swift release from birth and death — the truth of non-self alone, without yet opening the vaster teaching of emptiness in phenomena — while complete teaching names the great vehicle's own full, unreserved disclosure of every dimension of the path. Read according to Master Kuiji's alternative account, provisional teaching instead names the mistaken and deceptive claims of non-Buddhist teachers generally, while complete teaching names correct, wholesome teaching of every kind found within the Buddhist tradition itself.

He treats this reliance's real target as a specific vulnerability the treatise's own chapter on meditative equipoise names directly: certain flattering, deceptive teachers who rely on personal charisma or borrowed authority rather than genuine soundness of doctrine, counting on a listener's automatic deference to a respected-looking figure to secure belief without actual scrutiny. A bodhisattva relying on principle instead tests any teaching against its own coherence and its correspondence to complete versus provisional truth, refusing to accept a claim on the strength of the speaker's reputation alone — a discipline, he notes, that leaves a bodhisattva's grasp of the actual truth so firmly settled that no rival doctrine can subsequently dislodge it, precisely because that grasp was never borrowed from someone else's authority in the first place. This closes the second reliance; the treatise turns next to the third, relying on scriptures of complete and final meaning rather than those still provisional.

3. Milestone: Relying on Scriptures of Complete and Final Meaning, Never on Those Still Provisional — Why a Provisional Teaching Can Always, in Principle, Be Overturned

The third reliance: a bodhisattva whose faith in the Tathāgata has been deeply, purely planted relies exclusively on scriptures of complete and final meaning, never on those still provisional, so that nothing can shake what the Buddha has genuinely taught. The treatise explains exactly why this distinction matters — provisional scriptures explain the underlying nature of things through varying, situational approaches without yet reaching final settlement, still capable of provoking doubt, while a bodhisattva who fails to make this distinction remains vulnerable to having even genuine teaching later overturned by whatever provisional statement seems, on its own limited terms, to contradict it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於如來所深殖正信、深殖清淨，一向澄清；唯依如來了義經典，非不了義。了義經典為所依故，於佛所說法毗奈耶不可引奪。何以故？以佛所說不了義經，依種種門辯本性義，猶未決定，尚生疑惑，非了義故。若諸菩薩於了義經不決定者，於佛所說法毗奈耶猶可引奪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas who have deeply planted correct faith and deeply planted purity toward the Tathāgata, entirely and thoroughly clarified, rely exclusively on the Tathāgata's scriptures of complete and final meaning, never on those of provisional meaning. Because they rely on scriptures of complete and final meaning, nothing can pull them away from what the Buddha has taught in the dharma and the discipline. Why is this so? Because the provisional scriptures the Buddha spoke explain the underlying nature of things through varying approaches, not yet settled, still capable of provoking doubt, since they are not of complete and final meaning. Should bodhisattvas remain unsettled regarding scriptures of complete and final meaning, they remain vulnerable to being pulled away, even within what the Buddha has taught in the dharma and the discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "deeply planted purity, entirely and thoroughly clarified" as pointing to a bodhisattva who has already attained the four confirmed faiths toward the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and the precepts — a settled confidence, rooted in genuine spiritual attainment, that no future circumstance can uproot — before turning to why exactly a provisional teaching remains structurally vulnerable in a way a complete teaching is not: provisional scriptures explain reality's own nature through a variety of situational lenses — sometimes through the aggregates, sometimes through the sense-bases, sometimes through dependent origination or the four foundations of mindfulness — teaching existence at one moment and emptiness at another according to whichever framing best suits a given audience's own present capacity, and it is precisely this context-dependent variability that leaves such teaching, however genuinely useful in its own setting, still capable of generating doubt when compared against a different provisional statement elsewhere.

He treats the great vehicle's own complete teaching, by contrast, as resolving this instability at its root: the conventionally-labeled nature belonging to the aggregates, sense-bases, dependent origination, and the rest is genuinely without independent existence — neither falsely affirmed as real nor nihilistically denied altogether, since the non-conceptual nature underlying that conventional labeling, and the perfected nature it ultimately discloses, are genuinely present and real. Because this complete teaching commits to neither extreme of existence or nonexistence, it leaves nothing further to reconcile, and hence generates no genuine doubt at all — which is precisely why the treatise insists that a bodhisattva who has not clearly grasped this fourth distinction between provisional and complete meaning remains at risk of having even correctly received teaching subsequently unsettled by some seemingly contradictory provisional statement encountered later. This closes the third reliance; the treatise turns next to the fourth and final reliance — relying on wisdom actually realized through cultivation, never on the mere consciousness that learning and reflection alone can produce.

4. Milestone: Relying on Realized Wisdom, Never on Mere Learning — Closing the Four Reliances, the Four Standards They Establish, and Their Power to Guide Beings Fully Out of Affliction

The fourth reliance closes the set: a bodhisattva regards only wisdom genuinely realized through direct cultivation as true, not the understanding gained merely through learning and reflection, since what learning and reflection deliver still falls short of what cultivation alone can verify directly — and, having correctly recognized this, never disparages even the most profound teaching once actually heard. A closing summary distills all four reliances into four standards — meaning, principle, the genuine teacher, and realized wisdom — and confirms that a bodhisattva grounded in all four can guide beings, without any confusion whatsoever, along the six factors that lead genuinely out of affliction: loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity, signless absorption, and freedom from the conceit of self.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於真證智見為真實，非於聞思但識法義非真證智。是諸菩薩如實了知修所成智所應知者，非唯聞思所成諸識所能了達。如實知已，聞如來說最極甚深所有法義，終不誹毀。是名菩薩修正四依。依正四依善修習故，略顯四量。謂所說義、正理、大師、修所成慧真實證智。又諸菩薩一切四依為所依止，精勤發起正加行故，於出要道明了開示，無有迷惑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas regard wisdom genuinely realized through direct insight as true, not the mere understanding of the teaching's meaning gained only through learning and reflection, which is not genuinely realized wisdom. Such bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly recognize that what should be known through wisdom accomplished by cultivation is not something the consciousnesses accomplished only by learning and reflection are capable of fully comprehending. Having correctly recognized this, upon hearing the Tathāgata teach the most extremely profound dharma of every kind, they never disparage it. This is called a bodhisattva's correct cultivation of the four reliances. Because bodhisattvas skillfully cultivate the four correct reliances, four standards of measure are thereby, in brief, revealed: the meaning that is spoken, correct principle, the genuine teacher, and the true, realized wisdom accomplished by cultivation. Furthermore, bodhisattvas who take all four reliances as their basis, diligently arousing correct practice, are able to clearly explain the essential path leading out of affliction, entirely without confusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this final reliance to the treatise's own diagnosis, again drawn from the chapter on meditative equipoise, of a specific complacency it exists to correct: a listener who treats hearing the teaching as itself the finish line, content with intellectual comprehension alone and never pressing on into direct cultivation — like the poor man in the classical parable who spends all day counting another's treasure without ever gaining a coin of it for himself, comprehending the dharma's words without ever converting that comprehension into the wisdom that actually liberates. Only wisdom born of genuine cultivation, transforming ordinary consciousness into realized understanding, counts here as truly reliable; the treatise's own instruction not to disparage even the most profound teaching guards specifically against the temptation to dismiss as incomprehensible whatever cultivation has not yet caught up to explaining.

He treats the four resulting standards — meaning over phrasing, principle over person, the genuine teacher's complete teaching over provisional statement, and realized wisdom over mere learned consciousness — as together forming a single coherent method of measuring any teaching's own trustworthiness, rather than four unrelated rules. He closes by unpacking the six factors leading out of affliction that a bodhisattva grounded in these four reliances can then teach without confusion: loving-kindness overcoming hostility, compassion overcoming the impulse to harm, sympathetic joy overcoming envious displeasure at others' good fortune, equanimity overcoming both craving and aversion, signless absorption overcoming attachment to fixed characteristics, and freedom from the conceit of self overcoming the underlying view of a fixed personal identity that generates doubt about liberation itself — each addressed, he notes, by cultivating its own specific antidote in turn: loving-kindness for anger, compassion for the wish to harm, sympathetic joy for jealousy, equanimity for craving and aversion together, signless absorption for grasping at characteristics, and the view of no-self for self-conceit. This closes the sixth of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, the four correct reliances, in full; the treatise turns next to the seventh topic, the four unhindered understandings.

5. Milestone: The Four Unhindered Understandings — Teaching, Meaning, Language, and Eloquence, Each Resting on Both the Comprehensive Detail and the Essential Nature of Every Phenomenon

The seventh topic, the four unhindered understandings, names four distinct capacities, each grounded in the identical undefiled, unregressing wisdom accomplished through cultivation, and each spanning both the exhaustive particulars and the underlying essential nature of every phenomenon: unhindered understanding of the teaching itself, mastering every doctrinal approach the Buddha has taught across every audience; unhindered understanding of meaning, penetrating the actual significance beneath any given wording; unhindered understanding of language, commanding the vocabulary and idiom needed to explain any teaching fluently; and unhindered eloquence, delivering all of this to others without the slightest obstruction or hesitation.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩所修菩薩四無礙解？謂諸菩薩，於一切法一切異門，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩法無礙解。又諸菩薩，於一切法一切異相，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩義無礙解。又諸菩薩，於一切法一切釋辭，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩辭無礙解。又諸菩薩，於一切法一切品別，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩辯無礙解。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the four unhindered understandings bodhisattvas cultivate? Bodhisattvas who, regarding every teaching-approach to every phenomenon, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature, relying on an unhindered, unregressing wisdom accomplished through cultivation — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered understanding of the teaching. Bodhisattvas who, regarding every distinct aspect of every phenomenon, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature through this same wisdom — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered understanding of meaning. Bodhisattvas who, regarding every mode of explanation for every phenomenon, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature through this same wisdom — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered understanding of language. Bodhisattvas who, regarding every category and distinction among phenomena, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature through this same wisdom — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered eloquence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "exhaustive particulars" and "essential nature," the same technical pair introduced earlier in this chapter's account of skill in worldly knowledge, through the treatise's own later chapter of determinations: exhaustive particulars names the full range of specific, differentiated instances within any given category of phenomena, defiled or pure — the five aggregates, the six inner and outer sense-bases, and every classification the Āgamas apply to form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness across past, future, present, coarse, subtle, superior, inferior, distant, and near; essential nature names the shared, underlying suchness common to all of them, the fact that everything within that same category is equally impermanent, suffering, empty, and without fixed self. The two together give a genuinely complete grasp of any phenomenon — exhaustive particulars supplying the detailed factual picture, essential nature supplying the underlying principle those particulars all instantiate.

He treats "the teaching" as covering the Buddha's own many doctrinal approaches taught to different audiences according to their capacity — sometimes the śrāvaka path, sometimes the solitary-realizer's path, sometimes Madhyamaka, sometimes Yogācāra, sometimes tathāgatagarbha teaching — with unhindered understanding of the teaching meaning genuine mastery across every one of these approaches rather than fluency in only one; he cites the late Elder Miaojing's own observation that a classical commentator's capacity to organize and subdivide an entire scripture into its proper sections and topics is itself concrete proof of unhindered understanding of meaning, since correctly dividing a text into its constituent parts requires first genuinely grasping what each part actually means. Language, he notes, further extends this into command of vocabulary and regional idiom sufficient to explain any teaching fluently across different audiences and dialects, while eloquence gathers everything the first three have established into an actual capacity to deliver it aloud, without hesitation or obstruction, to whoever is listening. He notes the cross-reference the treatise makes to the ninth ground's own account of this fourfold mastery, since it is specifically at that advanced stage that a bodhisattva correctly understands, without exception, every possible variety of defiled and purified states, their causes, and their objects across the entire range of a teacher's own activity. This closes the four unhindered understandings' own intrinsic natures; the treatise turns next to their concrete benefit — the five kinds of skillful mastery they make possible, and the awakening they alone can bring to genuine completion.

6. Milestone: The Four Unhindered Understandings' Benefit — Five Kinds of Skillful Mastery, and the Awakening That Nothing Beyond Them Can Produce

Grounded in the four unhindered understandings, a bodhisattva obtains five kinds of immeasurable, supreme skillful mastery: mastery of the aggregates, correctly seeing that no fixed self can be found apart from the five aggregates' own combination; mastery of the elements, correctly discerning the distinct seeds and functions of the six sense faculties, their objects, and consciousness; mastery of the sense-bases, correctly discerning the three conditions by which perception actually arises; mastery of dependent origination, correctly discerning the twelvefold chain's own impermanent, painful, and selfless nature; and mastery of the possible and impossible, correctly discerning which causes genuinely produce which effects and which do not. The treatise then declares that nothing beyond these four unhindered understandings can bring a bodhisattva to genuine, complete awakening, nor equip them to teach others without distortion.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩依是菩薩四無礙解，應知獲得無量最勝五處善巧。一、蘊善巧，二、界善巧，三、處善巧，四、緣起善巧，五、處非處善巧。菩薩由此四種行相，於一切法自能妙善現正等覺，亦善為他無倒開示。此上無有自能妙善現正等覺，況善為他無倒開示。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who rely on these four unhindered understandings should be understood to obtain immeasurable, supreme skillful mastery in five domains: first, mastery of the aggregates; second, mastery of the elements; third, mastery of the sense-bases; fourth, mastery of dependent origination; fifth, mastery of the possible and the impossible. Through these four modes of understanding, bodhisattvas are themselves able, regarding every phenomenon, to wondrously and skillfully manifest complete, correct awakening, and are also able to skillfully explain it to others without any distortion. Beyond this, there is nothing capable of enabling a bodhisattva to wondrously and skillfully manifest complete, correct awakening themselves — let alone skillfully explain it to others without distortion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all five kinds of skillful mastery, cross-referencing the treatise's own Śrāvaka Stage and later chapters of determination for their full technical definitions: mastery of the aggregates rests on the meaning of "accumulation" itself, correctly seeing that form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness are only ever found as mere aggregated phenomena, never as a separate, permanent self standing apart from or within them; mastery of the elements rests on the meaning of "cause," "seed," "inherent nature," "lineage," "subtlety," and "sustaining," correctly discerning the distinct seeds and distinct natures underlying the eighteen elements of the six sense faculties, their objects, and their resulting consciousnesses; mastery of the sense-bases rests on the meaning of "the gateway through which mind and its associates arise," correctly discerning the three conditions — the dominant condition, the object condition, and the immediately preceding condition — that actually govern how the six inner and six outer sense-bases give rise to perception; mastery of dependent origination rests on eight distinct aspects of causal meaning — freedom from a fixed being, impermanence, momentary duration, dependence on other factors, freedom from any independent agent, unbroken causal continuity, causal resemblance across the chain, and being one's own karmic product — correctly discerning through all eight the wisdom of impermanence, of suffering, or of non-self within the twelvefold chain; and mastery of the possible and impossible rests on distinguishing where a given cause and effect genuinely cohere without contradiction from where they do not, correctly discerning the causal principle of equity that never simply happens by chance.

He treats the treatise's own emphatic closing statement — that nothing beyond these four unhindered understandings can bring a bodhisattva to genuine awakening or equip them to teach others without distortion — as the strongest possible claim this topic makes for its own necessity: the Cheng Weishi Lun's own account holds that a bodhisattva above the first ground possesses only a partial measure of these four, the ninth ground's own bodhisattva possesses them in full completion, and only buddhahood itself brings them to their absolute, perfected extent — meaning that even a bodhisattva who has not yet fully awakened to the ultimate truth genuinely cannot teach others without some risk of distortion, since teaching correctly presupposes having first understood correctly, and there is no shortcut around that sequence. This closes the seventh of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, the four unhindered understandings, in full; the treatise turns next to the eighth topic, the provisions for awakening.

7. Milestone: The Provisions for Awakening Open — Merit and Wisdom, Ripening in Three Tiers Across Three Incalculable Eons

The eighth topic, the provisions for awakening, names two kinds in brief: the provision of merit and the provision of wisdom, cross-referenced to the treatise's own earlier chapter on benefiting self and others, where merit is identified with the three perfections of generosity, discipline, and patience, wisdom with the single perfection of wisdom itself, and diligence and meditative concentration understood as belonging to either category depending on which a given bodhisattva emphasizes. The treatise then ranks both provisions across three tiers matched to the three incalculable great eons of a bodhisattva's own path: what is cultivated during the first such eon counts as the lesser tier, the second as the middling tier, and the third as the supreme tier.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩菩提資糧？當知如是菩提資糧略有二種。一者、福德資糧，二者、智慧資糧。此二資糧廣分別義，如前所說自他利品，應知其相。又此福德、智慧資糧，菩薩於初無數大劫所修習者，應知名下；若於第二無數大劫所修習者，應知名中；若於第三無數大劫所修習者，應知名上。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are a bodhisattva's provisions for awakening? One should understand that these provisions for awakening are, in brief, of two kinds: first, the provision of merit; second, the provision of wisdom. The full, detailed meaning of these two provisions is as already explained in the earlier chapter on benefiting self and others, and their characteristics should be understood accordingly. Furthermore, regarding this provision of merit and provision of wisdom, whatever bodhisattvas cultivate during the first incalculable great eon should be understood as the lesser tier; whatever is cultivated during the second incalculable great eon should be understood as the middling tier; and whatever is cultivated during the third incalculable great eon should be understood as the supreme tier.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own earlier chapter for the precise division of the two provisions among the six perfections: merit is, in brief, the three perfections of generosity, discipline, and patience together, while wisdom is the single perfection of wisdom alone — and diligence and meditative concentration are said to belong to whichever category a given bodhisattva's own practice happens to emphasize, since sustained effort and settled concentration can each be directed either toward accumulating merit, as when diligence supports generosity or meditation supports the four immeasurables, or toward accumulating wisdom, as when diligence and meditation instead support investigation into dependent origination, the four noble truths, or the emptiness of phenomena. Rather than assigning these two perfections a fixed, single category, the treatise treats them as genuinely dual in character, following the actual direction a bodhisattva's own cultivation takes.

He treats the threefold ranking across three incalculable great eons as this topic's own concrete measure of a bodhisattva's own developmental scale: the same two provisions, merit and wisdom, are cultivated throughout the entire length of the path, yet what counts as an accomplished level of practice during the very first of the three incalculable eons is only the beginning relative to what the same provisions look like once matured across the second eon, and lesser again compared to their supreme culmination across the third — a bodhisattva's own progress measured not by achieving some entirely new quality partway through the path, but by the same two provisions of merit and wisdom deepening continuously across an almost unimaginable span of cultivation. This closes the eighth of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics' opening statement; the treatise turns next to the ninth topic, the thirty-seven classical factors of awakening themselves, now examined through a distinctly great-vehicle lens.

8. Milestone: The Thirty-Seven Classical Factors Reconceived — A Bodhisattva Understands Them Fully Without Ever Rushing to Realize Them

The ninth topic opens with a striking reframing: bodhisattvas, relying on the four unhindered understandings and the wondrous wisdom skillful means makes possible, correctly and thoroughly understand the classical thirty-seven factors of awakening — the four establishments of mindfulness, four correct efforts, four bases of miraculous power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment, and eightfold noble path — yet deliberately decline to realize them, since doing so would cut off the very rebirth needed to keep guiding beings. This understanding spans both the śrāvaka vehicle's own approach to these thirty-seven factors, already explained in the Śrāvaka Stage, and the great vehicle's own distinct approach still to come.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩於三十七菩提分法精勤修習？謂諸菩薩，依止菩薩四無礙解，由善方便所攝妙智，於三十七菩提分法如實了知，而不作證。是諸菩薩，普於一切二乘理趣三十七種菩提分法皆如實知。謂於聲聞乘理趣及於大乘理趣三十七種菩提分法皆如實知。於聲聞乘理趣三十七種菩提分法如實了知，如聲聞地，如前所說一切應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do bodhisattvas diligently cultivate the thirty-seven factors of awakening? Bodhisattvas who, relying on the four unhindered understandings, through the wondrous wisdom encompassed by skillful means, correctly and thoroughly understand the thirty-seven factors of awakening, yet do not realize them. Such bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know the thirty-seven factors of awakening across both approaches belonging to the two vehicles — that is, they correctly and thoroughly know the thirty-seven factors of awakening according to both the śrāvaka vehicle's own approach and the great vehicle's own approach. Their correct and thorough knowledge of the thirty-seven factors of awakening according to the śrāvaka vehicle's approach is exactly as already explained in the Śrāvaka Stage, and should be understood accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "correctly understanding without realizing" as this topic's own central paradox, resolved through the treatise's own earlier chapter on ultimate truth: a bodhisattva living out life after life within saṃsāra cultivates the very understanding of emptiness that would, if fully actualized into realization, cut off further rebirth entirely — yet deliberately withholds that final step, retaining just enough residual affliction to keep taking birth for beings' sake, using this same wisdom instead to mature both the Buddhist teaching itself and the beings who need it. He notes the treatise's own technical point that the thirty-seven factors function as the direct cause of comprehensive knowledge, whose fruit is the actual severing of afflictive and cognitive obstruction — realization, strictly speaking, meaning exactly this severing — and a bodhisattva's refusal to pursue that severing, while continuing to understand the very path leading to it in full, is precisely what the treatise names here as great compassion in its most demanding form.

He treats the pairing of śrāvaka approach and great-vehicle approach as this topic's own structural key: rather than treating the thirty-seven factors as a fixed, single teaching, the treatise insists a bodhisattva must genuinely master both how a śrāvaka practices them — already explained at length across the Śrāvaka Stage's own extensive chapters — and how a bodhisattva practices the identical thirty-seven factors through a distinctly great-vehicle lens, a difference the treatise is about to unfold in full. This closes the topic's opening framing; the treatise turns next to that great-vehicle approach itself, opening with how a bodhisattva practices the very first of the four establishments of mindfulness, mindfulness of the body, through ultimate truth.

9. Milestone: Mindfulness of the Body Through Ultimate Truth — Neither Grasping the Body as Real Nor Denying It Into Nothing

Where a śrāvaka's mindfulness of the body observes the body's own impurity, a bodhisattva's version, approached through ultimate truth, walks a careful middle path: never conceptually asserting that the body possesses some fixed, independent nature of its own, yet never asserting either that it possesses no nature whatsoever — correctly recognizing that the body's own conventionally-labeled nature is genuinely without substance, while the underlying nature beyond all verbal expression, which that labeling merely gestures toward, is not thereby erased or denied.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩於大乘理趣三十七種菩提分法如實了知？謂諸菩薩，能於其身住循身觀，不於其身分別有性，亦不分別一切種類都無有性。又於其身遠離言說自性、法性如實了知，當知名依勝義理趣，能於其身住循身觀，修習念住。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know the thirty-seven factors of awakening according to the great vehicle's own approach? Bodhisattvas who, abiding in mindfulness observing the body as such, never conceptually construct the body as possessing a fixed nature, nor conceptually construct it as possessing no nature of any kind whatsoever — and who correctly and thoroughly know both the body's own conventionally-labeled nature and its underlying nature beyond words, each free of the other's extreme — one should understand this to be called cultivating the establishment of mindfulness through the ultimate approach, abiding in mindfulness observing the body as such.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts the śrāvaka's own method directly: observing the body's own impurity from head to foot or foot to head, whether one's own living body or a corpse's decay. A bodhisattva, by contrast, observes the body just as thoroughly, head to foot and foot to head, yet adds the specifically great-vehicle recognition that this body is nothing beyond consciousness's own manifestation, projected by one's own karmic consciousness — neither asserting it as genuinely, independently real, nor swinging to the opposite extreme of denying it altogether and thereby falling into nihilism's own mistaken grasp at emptiness. The body is genuinely empty of any fixed self yet not therefore nonexistent as a dependently arisen appearance.

He unpacks the treatise's own precise gloss on this double avoidance: never constructing the body as having a fixed nature guards against the deluded belief that the conventionally-labeled self — the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and the rest as ordinarily conceived — is genuinely, substantially real, since that labeled nature carries no actual substance or characteristic whatsoever; never constructing it as having no nature at all guards against the opposite mistake of denying even the underlying, wordless nature that labeling merely points toward, since what that labeling depends on — the dependently originated process actually occurring — is a real process, however it may be beyond capture in fixed verbal terms. He offers a concrete illustration: saying the word "heart" produces no literal heart in the speaking, yet the heart's own actual function — circulating blood, delivering oxygen and nutrients, clearing metabolic waste — continues regardless of whatever words describe it, existing as a genuine causal process even though no fixed essence called "heart" can be pinned down within the label itself. Correctly avoiding both extremes at once — neither treating the label as substantially real nor treating the underlying process as nonexistent — is precisely what allows a bodhisattva to enter the fully accomplished nature beyond both extremes. This closes mindfulness of the body through ultimate truth; the treatise turns next to the same practice approached instead through conventional truth, and to how this same double approach extends to every remaining factor of awakening.

10. Milestone: Mindfulness of the Body Through Conventional Truth, and the Same Double Approach Extended to Every Remaining Factor of Awakening

Alongside the ultimate approach, a bodhisattva also practices mindfulness of the body through conventional truth: following the same broad, name-based framework ordinary teaching relies on, skillfully observing the body's own impurity, suffering, impermanence, and selflessness in the manner already familiar from the śrāvaka's own method. And exactly as with the body, so too with the remaining establishments of mindfulness — sensation, mind, and phenomena — and with every one of the remaining thirty-three factors of awakening: each is cultivated through both approaches together, ultimate and conventional, neither one replacing the other.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩隨順無量安立理趣妙智而轉，當知名依世俗理趣，能於其身住循身觀，修習念住。如於其身住循身觀，修習念住，如是所餘一切念住、所餘一切菩提分法，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who follow the wondrous wisdom of the boundless, name-based conventional approach — one should understand this to be called cultivating the establishment of mindfulness through the conventional approach, abiding in mindfulness observing the body as such. Just as mindfulness observing the body as such is cultivated in this way, one should understand that all the remaining establishments of mindfulness, and all the remaining factors of awakening, are cultivated in exactly the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the boundless, name-based conventional approach" as the treatise's own technical term for subsequently attained wisdom — wisdom that, having already realized the ultimate directly, returns to operate skillfully within ordinary, name-based convention, close in method to the śrāvaka's own familiar observation of impurity, suffering, impermanence, and non-self, yet never mistaking that conventional observation for the entire picture the way an unawakened practitioner might. He cross-references the treatise's own later chapter of determinations for the threefold structure underlying every kind of conventional truth: worldly convention, the ordinary labels of house, jar, army, forest, or number, and indeed "I" or "a being" themselves; doctrinal convention, the Buddha's own name-based teaching framework of the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases; and attainment convention, the labels applied to actual stages of realization such as stream-entry — all three genuinely provisional, yet all three still functionally necessary for teaching and practice to proceed at all.

He treats the extension to every remaining factor as this topic's own economical closing move: rather than repeating the identical double analysis thirty-six more times for sensation, mind, and phenomena, and then for the four correct efforts, four bases of miraculous power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment, and eightfold path, the treatise simply confirms that the same two-tiered method — ultimate truth's freedom from both extremes, and conventional truth's skillful use of ordinary, name-based practice — governs every one of the thirty-seven factors without exception. A bodhisattva's entire practice of the classical path, however familiar its individual components may look to a śrāvaka, is thus quietly transformed at its root by this doubled approach. This closes the establishments of mindfulness and their extension to every remaining factor; the treatise turns next to the identical double approach applied to the four noble truths themselves.

11. Milestone: The Four Noble Truths Reconceived — Ultimate and Conventional Approaches to Suffering, Its Cause, Its Cessation, and the Path, Closing the Ninth Topic

The same double approach closes this topic by turning to the four noble truths themselves. Through the ultimate approach, a bodhisattva neither conceptually constructs suffering, its cause, its cessation brought about through practice, nor the path leading to that cessation as fixed, nameable realities, yet correctly recognizes that the underlying nature of each truth beyond all verbal expression is not thereby denied. Through the conventional approach, a bodhisattva instead follows the boundless, name-based framework ordinary teaching relies on, cultivating the sixteen classical aspects of the four truths through focused, sustained calming and insight practice.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩，於身等法不分別苦、不分別集、不分別此所作斷滅、不分別此得滅因道。又即於此遠離言說自性、法性，若苦法性、若集法性、若滅法性、若道法性，如實了知。當知名依勝義理趣，修菩提分為所依止，緣諦修習。若諸菩薩隨順無量安立理趣妙智而轉，當知名依世俗理趣，緣諦修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, bodhisattvas, regarding the body and the other establishments of mindfulness, do not conceptually construct suffering, do not conceptually construct its cause, do not conceptually construct the cessation brought about through practice, and do not conceptually construct the path that is the cause of attaining that cessation. Furthermore, regarding exactly these truths, freed of conventionally-labeled nature, they correctly and thoroughly know the underlying nature beyond words belonging to suffering, to its cause, to cessation, and to the path. One should understand this to be called taking the ultimate approach as one's basis in cultivating the factors of awakening, practicing by way of the truths. Bodhisattvas who follow the wondrous wisdom of the boundless, name-based conventional approach — one should understand this to be called practicing by way of the truths through the conventional approach.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the ultimate approach's own application to each of the four truths in turn: suffering is the truth that body, sensation, mind, and phenomena, taken together, constitute genuine suffering, yet the conventionally-labeled "suffering" applied to them carries no fixed substance of its own, so a bodhisattva does not conceptually construct it as a fixed reality; the cause is the craving and afflicted view that accumulate this suffering, likewise without fixed substance once its conventional label is examined; cessation is the extinguishing of both affliction and suffering, its own conventional label equally without fixed substance; and the path is the very establishments of mindfulness and remaining factors of awakening that serve as cessation's own cause, its label too without fixed substance under the same analysis. In every case, the underlying nature beyond words that each label merely points toward is not thereby erased — suffering, cause, cessation, and path are all genuinely, dependently real as processes, even though none of them can be pinned to a fixed, independently existing essence.

He treats the conventional approach's own return to the classical sixteen aspects of the four truths — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness for the truth of suffering; cause, arising, coming-together, and condition for its origin; cessation, tranquility, excellence, and definitive escape for its ending; and path, correctness, achievement, and deliverance for the path itself — as confirming that a bodhisattva's practice never abandons the śrāvaka's own familiar structure, but rather holds it together with the ultimate understanding that structure alone could never fully disclose. With this, he notes, the ninth of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, the thirty-seven factors of awakening reconceived through both ultimate and conventional approaches, closes in full — the entire classical path of the lesser vehicle preserved intact, yet permeated throughout by the great vehicle's own understanding of emptiness free of both extremes. The treatise turns next to the tenth topic, cultivating calming and insight themselves.

12. Milestone: Calming and Insight Defined — Calming as Freedom From All Discrimination, Insight as Both the Wisdom That Directly Realizes and the Wisdom That Returns to Explain

The tenth topic, cultivating calming and insight, opens by defining each. Calming, in its simplest sense, is a bodhisattva's mind resting free of all conceptual discrimination toward whatever is observed; understood more deeply, it is the state reached once calming and insight operate jointly, the mind released even from the subtlest trace of naming, language, and mental reference. Insight is twofold: the genuinely realized wisdom that directly penetrates the ultimate approach to every phenomenon, and the wondrous, subsequently attained wisdom that skillfully engages the boundless, name-based conventional approach as well.

Chinese Source

此中菩薩即於諸法無所分別，當知名止。若於諸法勝義理趣如實真智，及於無量安立理趣世俗妙智，當知名觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, a bodhisattva's mind resting free of all discrimination toward every phenomenon — one should understand this to be called calming. The genuinely realized wisdom directly penetrating the ultimate approach to every phenomenon, together with the wondrous wisdom skillfully engaging the boundless, name-based conventional approach — one should understand this to be called insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi offers calming's own definition at two depths: at its simpler level, calming names a mind that has stopped conceptually elaborating on whatever object it observes, resting attentively without further discursive commentary; at its deeper level, reached once calming and insight are already operating together in genuine union, it names a mind entirely freed even from the subtlest residue of naming and verbal reference toward the sense faculties, sense objects, consciousness, and the physical world itself — no longer merely refraining from discrimination but genuinely beyond the reach of discrimination's own apparatus.

He treats insight's own twofold structure as encompassing the entire arc of a bodhisattva's wisdom: the four investigations and four kinds of genuine knowledge that approach realization still belong to preparatory, still-tainted wisdom; the direct, realized wisdom penetrating ultimate truth is the root non-discriminating wisdom itself; and the wondrous wisdom skillfully engaging boundless conventional truth is the subsequently attained wisdom that returns from direct realization equipped to explain the four noble truths, the aggregates, and every other name-based teaching to others. All three levels together, he stresses, are gathered under this one term "insight," since none of the three can function properly in isolation from the other two. This closes the intrinsic natures of calming and insight; the treatise turns next to calming's own fourfold unfolding in actual practice.

13. Milestone: Calming's Fourfold Unfolding — From Investigation-Led Approach Through Effortless Freedom From Elaboration to Resting in the Sameness of All Phenomena

The treatise names four stages through which a bodhisattva's calming actually unfolds. First, calming is led by insight into both ultimate and conventional truth, since genuine non-discrimination is reached only after first investigating names, things, provisional essence, and provisional distinction through insight's own four investigations — never simply willed into being from the outset. Second, calming is the fruit of that very investigation, the mind settling directly onto the object once its verbally-labeled nature is seen through, no longer needing further deliberation. Third, calming operates effortlessly across every conceptual elaboration, no longer clinging to any residue of verbal proliferation at all. Fourth, calming rests in phenomena stripped of every characteristic and every discrimination, the mind grown genuinely still and inclined toward the sameness and single, genuine nature shared by all phenomena.

Chinese Source

此中菩薩略有四行，當知名止。一、勝義世俗智前行；二、勝義世俗智果；三、普於一切戲論想中無功用轉；四、即於如是離言唯事，由無有相無所分別，其心寂靜，趣向一切法平等性、一味實性。由此四行，是諸菩薩止道運轉，漸次乃至能證無上正等菩提智見圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, bodhisattvas' calming has, in brief, four aspects. First, calming led by wisdom regarding ultimate and conventional truth. Second, calming as the fruit of that wisdom regarding ultimate and conventional truth. Third, calming operating effortlessly, universally, amid every conceptual elaboration. Fourth, calming resting precisely in this freedom from words, in mere dependently arisen fact alone, the mind, through being free of characteristics and free of discrimination, growing still and inclining toward the sameness and single, genuine nature of all phenomena. Through these four aspects, bodhisattvas' calming operates and unfolds, progressing gradually all the way to the complete perfection of supreme, correct awakening's own wisdom and vision.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all four stages as a genuine sequence rather than four alternative descriptions of the same state. The first stage requires insight's own four investigations to run first: investigating names, recognizing that "cup" is only a name with no cup-substance actually contained in the word itself; investigating things, recognizing that whatever thing is seen is seen simply as a thing, prior to any name being attached; investigating provisional essence, recognizing that attaching any name to a thing is itself a provisional act rather than a discovery of the thing's own inherent identity; and investigating provisional distinction, recognizing that further distinctions layered onto that name — calling one cup "large" only relative to a smaller one, which becomes "small" again beside a still larger cup — are equally provisional through and through. Calming genuinely begins only once this investigative groundwork has been laid, which is why great-vehicle calming is never simply blank non-thinking from the very first moment, but rather the fruit that wisdom itself produces, wisdom leading and calming following as its result.

He treats the second stage as the moment this investigation actually pays off: having examined the aggregates and the six sense faculties through the four investigations and found no substance corresponding to their names, the mind settles directly onto the very fact that this verbally-labeled nature is ungraspable, no longer needing to keep re-running the investigation once its conclusion has been genuinely absorbed — a settling he compares to no longer needing to "release" an object one recognizes was never actually held in the first place. The third stage marks a further deepening: even the subtle mental habit of elaborating further thoughts about that ungraspability itself falls away, the mind now clear of every residue of conceptual proliferation without needing continued effort to keep it that way. And the fourth stage, resting in the sameness and single, genuine nature of all phenomena, he identifies as the calming that accompanies the path of seeing itself, where the provisional, name-based nature mistakenly clung to and the dependently-originated nature actually present are recognized as equally empty of independent existence, and hence, in that specific sense, equal to one another — a stillness the treatise says continues developing across the second ground and every ground beyond it, all the way to complete, perfected buddhahood. This closes calming's own fourfold unfolding; the treatise turns next to insight's own matching fourfold unfolding.

14. Milestone: Insight's Fourfold Unfolding Closes the Tenth Topic — Freedom From Exaggerating and From Denying, and Skillful Investigation Across Boundless Phenomena

Insight likewise unfolds in four stages, closing this topic. First, insight is led by the calming already achieved, taking that settledness as its own necessary basis rather than proceeding without it. Second and third, insight steers a genuinely middle course, freeing itself from the extreme of exaggeration — wrongly imputing fixed, independent existence onto what is only dependently arisen or ultimately real — and equally from the extreme of denial — wrongly negating the genuine existence of what actually, dependently exists. And fourth, insight extends into skillfully investigating the boundless variety of phenomena the Buddha has named and distinguished, engaging every teaching framework fully while still avoiding both extremes throughout. A closing statement confirms that both calming and insight, cultivated this way together, carry a bodhisattva progressively all the way to the complete perfection of awakening's own wisdom and vision.

Chinese Source

此中菩薩略有四行，當知名觀。謂即四行止道前行，於一切法遠離增益不正執邊，遠離損減不正執邊，及與隨順無量諸法，差別安立理趣妙觀。由此四行，是諸菩薩觀道運轉，漸次乃至能證無上正等菩提智見圓滿。是名略說菩薩止觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, bodhisattvas' insight has, in brief, four aspects. First, insight taking those same four aspects of the path of calming as its own necessary basis. Second, insight freeing itself, regarding every phenomenon, from the extreme of incorrectly exaggerating existence. Third, insight freeing itself, regarding every phenomenon, from the extreme of incorrectly denying existence. Fourth, insight skillfully investigating, in accordance with the boundless variety of phenomena, their distinct, name-based approaches. Through these four aspects, bodhisattvas' insight operates and unfolds, progressing gradually all the way to the complete perfection of supreme, correct awakening's own wisdom and vision. This is, in brief, what is called a bodhisattva's calming and insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats insight's own first stage as confirming, from the opposite direction, the very same interdependence already established for calming: calming was shown to require insight's investigation to get started, and now insight is shown to require calming's own settledness as its necessary basis in turn — the two never actually functioning apart from one another despite being distinguished for the sake of analysis, exactly the treatise's own repeated insistence throughout this fascicle on calming and insight operating jointly rather than sequentially in some final, definitive sense.

He unpacks the second and third stages through the treatise's own later chapter of determinations: the extreme of exaggeration names the mistaken imputation of a provisionally-constructed, independent essence onto what is actually only dependently arisen or ultimately real — believing the label itself carries genuine substance; the extreme of denial names the opposite mistake of negating even the dependently arisen process or the perfected, ultimate nature that genuinely does exist, on the grounds that its provisional label cannot be pinned to any fixed essence — collapsing the entire structure into nihilism. A bodhisattva's insight steers carefully between both, neither reifying the provisional nor voiding the genuinely real. The fourth stage he treats as insight's own positive, constructive labor: having secured this middle path, a bodhisattva then actually investigates, through subsequently attained wisdom, the Buddha's own boundless variety of name-based teaching frameworks — the four noble truths, the twelvefold chain of dependent origination, and every other doctrinal structure — never treating this investigation as somehow beneath a bodhisattva already versed in ultimate emptiness, but as insight's own necessary completion. He closes by noting the treatise's own summary line: though a bodhisattva's practice still proceeds by way of names and concepts, it has genuinely transcended mere naming in the process — proof that emptiness and skillful engagement with conventional teaching were never actually in tension to begin with. This closes the tenth of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, cultivating calming and insight, in full; the treatise turns next to the eleventh and by far the longest topic remaining in this fascicle, skillful means.

15. Milestone: Skillful Means Opens — Twelve Kinds, Six Turned Inward Toward Realizing the Teaching Oneself, Six Turned Outward Toward Maturing Beings

The eleventh and by far the longest topic among the fifteen, skillful means, opens by naming twelve kinds in two matched sets of six: six directed inward, toward a bodhisattva's own realization of the entire teaching, and six directed outward, toward maturing sentient beings. The commentary clarifies an apparent overlap in this division at the outset — the inward six already involve holding beings continuously in mind, and the outward six already involve a bodhisattva's own continued cultivation — explaining that "inward" here means whatever a bodhisattva does specifically to develop their own mind toward awakening, even when beings are its object, while "outward" means whatever actually benefits beings directly, even when it also matures the bodhisattva's own path in the process.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩方便善巧？當知略說有十二種。依內修證一切佛法，有其六種；依外成熟一切有情，亦有六種。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's skillful means? One should understand that this is, in brief, of twelve kinds: six belonging to realizing the entire teaching by way of inward cultivation, and six belonging to maturing all sentient beings by way of outward activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi flags this topic's own scale at the outset: skillful means, the practical intelligence that lets a bodhisattva's compassion and wisdom actually accomplish something concrete in the world, receives by far the most extensive treatment of any topic in this entire chapter, spanning the remainder of this fascicle. He resolves the Yogācārabhūmi commentary's own noted puzzle over the inward-outward division directly: the six inward means already require holding sentient beings continuously in mind as their object, and the six outward means already mature the bodhisattva's own path as their side effect, so the real distinction is not about whose benefit results but about what the practice is fundamentally oriented toward — inward practice cultivates the bodhisattva's own mind and realization even while beings remain constantly present within that cultivation, while outward practice acts directly upon beings' own circumstances even though the bodhisattva's own merit and wisdom are thereby matured as well.

He treats this framing as worth holding in mind across everything still to come in this topic: skillful means is never a separate, additional virtue layered on top of compassion and wisdom, but rather names the concrete intelligence and resourcefulness through which compassion and wisdom actually translate into effective action, whether that action is aimed at deepening the bodhisattva's own realization or at directly helping others encounter, enter, mature within, and finally be liberated by the teaching. This closes the topic's opening framework; the treatise turns next to the six inward-turned means in full detail.

16. Milestone: The Six Inward-Turned Skillful Means — Three Paired Foundations Behind Refusing Nirvāṇa, Staying Unstained in Saṃsāra, and Blazing Diligence

The six inward-turned means name what actually sustains a bodhisattva's own practice across an entire path: holding compassion continuously toward beings, unable to abandon them; correctly and thoroughly knowing every conditioned activity's own causal structure; delighting deeply and constantly in the wondrous wisdom of supreme awakening; refusing to abandon saṃsāra specifically because of that continuous compassion for beings; remaining genuinely unstained while wandering through birth and death specifically because of that thorough knowledge of causal activity; and sustaining blazing, vigorous diligence specifically because of that delight in the Buddha's own wisdom. The treatise thus pairs the fourth means with the first, the fifth with the second, and the sixth with the third — each later means resting directly on an earlier one as its own foundation.

Chinese Source

云何依內修證一切佛法六種方便善巧？一者、菩薩於諸有情悲心俱行，顧戀不捨；二者、菩薩於一切行如實遍知；三者、菩薩恆於無上正等菩提所有妙智，深心欣樂；四者、菩薩顧戀有情為依止故，不捨生死；五者、菩薩於一切行如實遍知為依止故，輪轉生死而心不染；六者、菩薩欣樂佛智為依止故，熾然精進。當知是名菩薩依內修證一切佛法六種方便善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the six kinds of skillful means belonging to realizing the entire teaching by way of inward cultivation? First, bodhisattvas hold a mind joined with compassion toward sentient beings, cherishing them and unable to abandon them. Second, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know every conditioned activity exactly as it is. Third, bodhisattvas constantly delight deeply in the wondrous wisdom belonging to supreme, correct awakening. Fourth, taking their cherishing of sentient beings as their basis, bodhisattvas do not abandon birth and death. Fifth, taking their thorough knowledge of every conditioned activity as their basis, bodhisattvas wander through birth and death without their mind becoming stained. Sixth, taking their delight in the Buddha's own wisdom as their basis, bodhisattvas sustain blazing diligence. One should understand this to be called the six kinds of skillful means belonging to a bodhisattva's realization of the entire teaching by way of inward cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Avataṃsaka Sūtra's own famous formula — "the awakening mind as root, great compassion as leader" — for the first of the six, treating a bodhisattva's inability to abandon suffering beings as the emotional foundation everything else in this list ultimately rests upon: seeing beings caught in killing, theft, and false speech, bound for the lower realms through their own ignorance, a bodhisattva's compassion refuses to let go, vowing to liberate beings without limit and trusting in their own capacity to actually help. The second means he ties to the treatise's own account of skill in worldly knowledge already developed earlier in this chapter — thoroughly knowing the causal structure binding ignorance and craving to old age, sickness, and death, correctly grasping the truths of suffering and its origin as concrete, examinable processes rather than abstract doctrine.

He treats the third means, delight in the wisdom of awakening, as requiring a distinctly long view: becoming a buddha means cutting off not only self-grasping but grasping at phenomena as well, and severing that second, subtler grasping requires remaining within birth and death across an almost unimaginable span of continued practice — a task genuinely bearable only for a bodhisattva who takes deep, sustained delight in the destination itself rather than merely enduring the journey. He then walks through the three explicit pairings the treatise itself draws: the fourth means, refusing to abandon saṃsāra, rests directly on the first means, cherishing beings, since it is precisely that inability to abandon suffering beings that keeps a bodhisattva returning to samsara rather than seeking a swifter private escape; the fifth means, remaining unstained while wandering through birth and death, rests on the second means, thorough causal knowledge, since a bodhisattva equipped with genuine insight into emptiness finds every arising thought already seen through as empty, unable to provoke the craving, hostility, or delusion an ordinary being would otherwise develop from identical circumstances; and the sixth means, blazing diligence, rests on the third means, delight in awakening's own wisdom, since desire for a genuinely good outcome is itself, as the treatise elsewhere teaches, the very support that diligence depends upon to sustain itself at all. This closes the six inward-turned means in full; the treatise turns next to the six outward-turned means, named first in brief outline before each receives its own extensive treatment.

17. Milestone: The Six Outward-Turned Skillful Means Named — From the Smallest Seed of Merit to Beings Who Have Already Fully Ripened

The six outward-turned means name a graduated sequence covering a being's entire journey toward liberation: enabling beings to reap boundless fruit from only the smallest good deed; enabling beings to gather vast wholesome roots while expending only minimal effort; removing hostility from beings who actively resent the Buddhist teaching; drawing beings who are neutral toward it into genuine entry; maturing beings who have already entered; and, finally, bringing beings who have already fully matured to actual liberation.

Once such beings are genuinely able to receive and retain the teaching, bodhisattvas proceed further still, opening its correct meaning to them in full.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the six qualities of correctly taught dharma through the treatise's own commentarial gloss: suited to the occasion means matched to the actual audience gathered; easy to enter and understand means clear and unobscured; correctly timed and sequenced means delivered without interruption once hostility has genuinely been cleared; undistorted means following the text's own natural structure, whether summarized, explained, or elaborated in full; genuinely beneficial means producing real joy and confidence in the listener; and able to withstand challenge means the teacher's own capacity to resolve objections skillfully rather than being flustered by them.

He identifies the three miraculous displays directly with the Śrāvaka Stage's own classical threefold teaching of complete instruction: the miracle of supernormal power, inspiring such reverence in onlookers that they become genuinely receptive to the teaching that follows; the miracle of reading minds, allowing a teacher to seek out exactly what troubles a given listener and address it with real precision; and the miracle of instruction proper, teaching correctly matched to a listener's own faculties, conduct, and level of realization — the same threefold structure already introduced in fascicle 44's own account of both-worlds-happiness meditation. He treats the closing instruction on expanding brief teachings and compressing elaborate ones as the natural completion of genuine teaching skill: a bodhisattva's own eloquence flows freely in either direction, neither trapped in excessive brevity that loses the listener nor excessive elaboration that overwhelms them, posing questions and challenges specifically to keep the listener's own understanding actively engaged rather than passively received. This closes teaching and gradual taming; the treatise turns next to matching specific meditation objects to a listener's own particular affliction, before turning to the deeper doctrinal crisis a listener may feel on first encountering the Buddha's own teaching on emptiness.

25. Milestone: Matching Meditation to Affliction, and the Crisis of Faith Sparked by "All Dharmas Are Without Nature" — Why Some Mistake the Perfection of Wisdom for Something Other Than the Buddha's Own Word

A bodhisattva guides beings into meditative absorption by matching the object of practice to their own dominant affliction — contemplating the body's impurity for the strongly desirous, cultivating loving-kindness for the strongly hostile, contemplating dependent origination for the deeply confused, analyzing the elements for the arrogant, and mindfulness of breathing for the easily distracted — together with the five kinds of instruction and admonishment already introduced earlier in this chapter. The treatise then turns to a genuine crisis of faith: certain sutras teaching profound emptiness state that all phenomena are without inherent nature, without real substance, without arising or ceasing, equal to empty space, like illusion and dream — language some listeners, failing to grasp the Buddha's own hidden intent, find so disturbing that they conclude these teachings cannot possibly be the Buddha's own word at all, and denounce them as false.

Chinese Source

又於趣入遍緣一切三摩地門，能為隨順教授教誡，攝益有情令修利行。若諸有情，於佛所說甚深空性相應經典，不解如來密意義趣。於此經中，說一切法皆無自性、皆無有事、無生無滅，說一切法皆等虛空、皆如幻夢。彼聞是已，如其義趣不能解了，心生驚怖，誹謗如是一切經典，言非佛說。菩薩為彼諸有情類，方便善巧如理會通，如是經中如來密意甚深義趣，如實和會，攝彼有情。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, regarding entry into the entire range of meditative absorptions, bodhisattvas offer teaching and admonishment suited to each being, gathering them in and benefiting them so that they cultivate genuinely helpful conduct. Should beings, upon encountering the Buddha's own scriptures joined with profound emptiness, fail to understand the Tathāgata's own hidden intent — scriptures which state that all phenomena are without inherent nature, without real substance, without arising, without ceasing, and that all phenomena are equal to empty space and like illusion and dream — and, upon hearing this, being unable to grasp its intended meaning, become fearful and denounce all such scriptures as not being the Buddha's own word, bodhisattvas, for the sake of these beings, skillfully and correctly reconcile the Tathāgata's own hidden, profound intent within these very scriptures, genuinely harmonizing it so as to gather these beings in.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the five meditation objects matched to affliction as identical to the treatise's own earlier account, in this same chapter's discussion of a spiritual friend's teaching duties, of contemplating impurity for the desirous, loving-kindness for the hostile, dependent origination for the confused, the elements for the arrogant, and mindfulness of breathing for the distracted — confirming that skillful means here draws directly on tools already established rather than introducing entirely new technique.

He treats the crisis of faith that follows as this section's own genuinely serious concern, historically documented within the tradition itself: the Lotus Sūtra records five thousand śrāvaka practitioners actually walking out when the Buddha began teaching universal emptiness, unable to bear hearing that even nirvāṇa itself is empty of fixed essence, after investing their entire practice in a framework where the aggregates were simply impermanent, painful, empty, and selfless in a more straightforward sense. He explains the underlying resolution the Yogācāra tradition offers, dividing phenomena into three natures: the purely conceptually-constructed nature, genuinely without any substance whatsoever; the dependently arisen nature, a real, if illusion-like, causal process; and the perfectly accomplished nature, the genuinely real emptiness underlying both. When the Prajñāpāramitā literature declares "all phenomena are without inherent nature, without arising, without ceasing, fundamentally at peace," Master Cháng Bǎi explains, this hidden intent specifically targets the first nature alone — the conceptually-constructed, verbally-labeled nature that never had any substance to begin with — never denying the second and third natures' own genuine, if differently structured, reality. He notes the tradition's own account of five specific qualifications a listener needs before this teaching lands without crisis — having already planted superior wholesome roots, purified one's own obstructions, matured one's own continuum, cultivated deep conviction repeatedly, and accumulated a superior store of both merit and wisdom — explaining why the identical teaching that liberates one listener can genuinely terrify another not yet ready to hear it. This closes the setup of the crisis; the treatise turns next to how a bodhisattva actually reconciles it, beginning with what "all phenomena are without inherent nature" genuinely means.

26. Milestone: Reconciling the Crisis Begins — "All Phenomena Are Without Inherent Nature" Never Meant That Nothing Exists at All

A bodhisattva reconciling this crisis explains the sutra's own actual claim with precision: it never taught that phenomena themselves do not exist at all, only that their verbally-labeled, conceptually-constructed nature does not exist — the specific, fixed essence that ordinary language and thought project onto things is what is genuinely absent, not the dependently arisen phenomena those labels are projected onto. This is why the sutra states that all phenomena are without inherent nature — a claim about the emptiness of the label, not a claim that nothing whatsoever is actually happening.

Chinese Source

菩薩如是正會通時，為彼說言：此經不說一切諸法都無所有，但說諸法所言自性都無所有。是故說言一切諸法皆無自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

When bodhisattvas thus correctly reconcile this crisis, they explain to such beings: this sutra does not teach that all phenomena do not exist at all; it teaches only that the verbally-labeled nature of phenomena does not exist. This is why it is said that all phenomena are without inherent nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this single distinction — between denying phenomena outright and denying only their verbally-labeled nature — as the entire resolution's own hinge, everything that follows in this section merely elaborating the same basic point from different angles. He offers the same fire-example already used earlier in this chapter: dependently arisen fire genuinely possesses the capacity for warmth and burning, a real causal function actually present in the world, while the mere word "fire," spoken aloud, produces no heat and burns nothing — the label and the labeled thing are simply not the same kind of object, and the sutra's negation targets only the former.

He stresses that this reconciliation is not a clever reinterpretation invented after the fact to rescue an otherwise troubling teaching, but the treatise's own claim about what the sutra's compilers actually intended from the beginning — hidden intent, in this technical sense, meaning a deeper purpose requiring correct unpacking, not a deception to be exposed. A listener who grasps this distinction correctly need not choose between accepting emptiness and believing anything continues to exist at all; dependently arisen phenomena remain genuinely, functionally present, while only the fixed, independent essence habitually projected onto them turns out, upon examination, to have never actually been there. This closes the resolution of "all phenomena are without inherent nature"; the treatise turns next to the sutra's second troubling claim — that all phenomena are without real substance — and its own parallel resolution.

27. Milestone: "No Real Substance" and "No Arising, No Ceasing" — Two Further Resolutions, Each Denying Only the Verbal Label, Never the Underlying Process

Two further troubling claims receive the identical resolution. "All phenomena have no real substance" does not mean the dependently arisen basis for speech is absent — a microphone must genuinely exist before the word "microphone" can be applied to it — but only that, examined by ultimate truth, the very essence the word itself seems to name has no corresponding reality, no more real than a rabbit's horn. And "all phenomena neither arise nor cease" simply follows from this: since the verbally-labeled nature never existed to begin with, from the very beginning, there is nothing there that could subsequently arise, and hence nothing that could subsequently cease either.

Chinese Source

雖有一切所言說事，依止彼故諸言說轉，然彼所說可說自性，據第一義非其自性。是故說言一切諸法皆無有事。一切諸法所言自性，理既如是從本已來都無所有，當何所生、當何所滅。是故說言一切諸法無生無滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Although the dependently arisen basis for every verbal statement genuinely exists, and it is by relying on that basis that verbal statements actually proceed, still, the very essence that such statements purport to name, examined according to ultimate truth, is not itself their real essence. This is why it is said that all phenomena have no real substance. Since the verbally-labeled essence of all phenomena, being of this nature, has never existed at all from the very beginning, what could possibly arise, and what could possibly cease? This is why it is said that all phenomena neither arise nor cease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the layered process behind ordinary perception to make "no real substance" concrete: a microphone's actual appearance depends on the store-consciousness's own seed manifesting as an image, the eye consciousness apprehending that image through the eye faculty, and the sixth consciousness's own conceptual grasping fixing it as "a microphone" — the store-consciousness taking its own manifestation as its own object and then layering names on top of it. What is actually present throughout this entire chain is a real causal process; what is absent is only the fixed essence the resulting word seems to guarantee, no more substantially present than a tortoise's fur or a hare's horn, classical Buddhist images for something that language names but that corresponds to no genuine referent at all. He cites the Yogācārabhūmi commentary's own gloss directly: "no real substance" means no conceptually-constructed substance, never a denial of the dependently arisen event that the constructing mind mistakenly reifies.

He treats "no arising, no ceasing" as following automatically once this point is genuinely absorbed: both arising and ceasing presuppose that something with a fixed nature was actually there to begin with, and since the verbally-labeled essence in question never possessed any such nature from the start, the entire question of its arising or ceasing simply does not arise — not because reality freezes into changeless stasis, but because the mistaken premise generating the question has already been dissolved. He notes this closes the first, "earlier" set of the sutra's troubling statements, all resolved through this identical distinction between conceptually-constructed essence and dependently arisen process; the treatise turns next to the sutra's "later" set of statements — that all phenomena are equal to empty space, and that all phenomena are like illusion and dream — opening with the space comparison's own vivid double meaning.

28. Milestone: Like Space Itself — Colors and Movements Pass Freely Through Emptiness Without Ever Staining Its Own Underlying Purity

The treatise reconciles "all phenomena are equal to empty space" through space's own two familiar qualities. First, space accommodates: countless colors, shapes, and movements — objects coming, going, rising, falling, bending, and stretching — all occur freely within it. Second, space remains pure: the instant every one of those colors, shapes, and movements is removed, space's own colorless, untainted nature simply shows itself, exactly as it always was. Dependently arisen phenomena, genuinely empty of any fixed verbal essence yet capable of appearing vividly the moment conditions allow, work exactly the same way: mistaken, verbally-generated discrimination and conceptual elaboration pass through this space-like, wordless reality the way colored shapes pass through open sky, without ever actually staining the underlying purity those very appearances depend upon.

Chinese Source

譬如空中有眾多色、色業可得，容受一切諸色、色業。謂虛空中，現有種種若往、若來、若住，起墮屈伸等事。若於爾時，諸色、色業皆悉除遣；即於爾時，唯無色性清淨虛空其相顯現。如是即於相似虛空離言說事，有其種種言說所作邪想分別隨戲論著，似色業轉。又即如是一切言說邪想分別隨戲論著似眾色業，皆是似空離言說事之所容受。

Ru-Yi Translation

For example, within space, many kinds of form and the activity of form can be found, space accommodating every kind of form and its activity — within space there presently appear various comings, goings, restings, risings, fallings, bendings, and stretchings. Should all such forms and their activity at some point be entirely removed, at that very time, only the pure, colorless nature of space itself would appear. In just this way, within this reality beyond words that resembles space, various mistaken discriminations and conceptual elaborations produced by verbal speech occur, resembling the activity of form. And all such mistaken discriminations and conceptual elaborations resembling the activity of countless forms are themselves accommodated by this reality beyond words that resembles empty space.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the treatise's own careful catalogue of what space accommodates: visible color proper — blue, yellow, red, white, light, shadow, brightness, and darkness; shape — long, short, round, square, coarse, fine, high, and low; and expressive form — reaching, withdrawing, bending, stretching, and every posture of walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, each one an outward movement first set in motion by an inward intention. All of these pass through space continuously, yet the instant every one is removed, space's own colorless purity is what remains — not a purity achieved by effort, but simply what was quietly there underneath the entire display the whole time.

He treats this double image as directly mapping onto the dependently arisen mind's own two faces: the wordless, dependently arisen nature itself resembles space in being genuinely empty of any fixed form or color of its own, and mistaken, verbally-driven discrimination and conceptual elaboration resemble the countless shapes and movements passing through that space — real activity, genuinely occurring, yet never actually altering the underlying nature it occurs within, precisely as colored shapes drifting through open sky leave the sky itself untouched by their passage. A being caught in habitual conceptual proliferation experiences exactly this ceaseless traffic of thought and mistaken discrimination, mistaking that traffic for reality's own fixed nature, when the traffic itself is only ever passing through a deeper purity it can neither improve nor damage. This closes space's own accommodating and pure natures; the treatise turns next to how a noble bodhisattva's own realized wisdom actually witnesses this same purity directly.

29. Milestone: The Noble Mind Realizes Space's Own Purity — Yet Never Goes Hunting for Some Further "Thing Called Emptiness" Once the Search Is Done

Once a bodhisattva's own noble wisdom dispels every mistaken discrimination and conceptual elaboration that verbal speech produces, that same noble wisdom directly realizes phenomena's own reality beyond words — disclosing only the fact that the verbally-labeled essence itself has no corresponding nature, exactly as space's own pure aspect appears once every color and shape has been cleared away. And, crucially, this realization does not then go searching for some further, separate essence lying beyond that clearing — there is nothing more to find once the search for a fixed essence has been correctly abandoned. This is why the sutra can say that all phenomena are equal to empty space.

Chinese Source

若時菩薩以妙聖智，遣除一切言說所起邪想分別隨戲論著；爾時菩薩最勝聖者，以妙聖智證得諸法離言說事，唯有一切言說自性非性所顯，譬如虛空清淨相現。亦非過此有餘自性應更尋求。是故宣說一切諸法皆等虛空。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once bodhisattvas, through wondrous noble wisdom, dispel every mistaken discrimination and conceptual elaboration arising from verbal speech, at that very time, such supreme, noble bodhisattvas, through this wondrous noble wisdom, realize phenomena's own reality beyond words — a reality disclosing only that every verbally-labeled essence has no corresponding nature, exactly as space's own pure aspect appears. Nor is there, beyond this, some further essence still to be sought. This is why it is proclaimed that all phenomena are equal to empty space.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing caution — that nothing further remains to be sought once verbal essence has been seen through — as this reconciliation's own most practically important safeguard, quoting the Diamond Sūtra's own parallel warning that the Buddha "attained not the slightest thing whatsoever" in reaching complete awakening: "attaining nothing" here means only that no further contaminated appearance remains to be grasped, exactly as space, once cleared of every color, offers nothing further to hold onto — not that awakening consists in acquiring some new, separate possession called emptiness. He cites the Avataṃsaka Sūtra's own chapter on pure conduct making the same point from another angle, finding no fixed "buddha" locatable anywhere among body, bodily action, speech, verbal action, mind, or mental action, only conceptual discrimination projected on top of a process that was never actually graspable as a fixed thing to begin with.

He treats the Prajñāpāramitā's own famous formula — "no phenomena arise, yet wisdom itself arises" — as summarizing this entire point in miniature: precisely because no phenomenon carries a fixed, graspable characteristic, wisdom's own single defining mark is signlessness itself, and signlessness simply means ungraspability rather than blank absence. A bodhisattva who has genuinely realized this no longer searches for anything further once the search for fixed essence has correctly ended, since searching further would itself repeat the very mistake just seen through. This closes the reconciliation of "equal to empty space" in full; the treatise turns next to the sutra's final troubling claim — that all phenomena are like illusion and dream — opening with the classical warning against both grasping illusion as real and denying it altogether.

30. Milestone: Like Illusion and Dream — the Wild Fox Kōan, and Why Denying Karma Outright Is Exactly as Mistaken as Grasping It as Ultimately Real

The final reconciliation turns to "all phenomena are like illusion and dream." Just as a dream's own imagery should never be taken as genuinely, substantially real simply because it vividly appears, neither should its imagery and content be dismissed as utterly nonexistent — dream-fear and dream-delight are genuinely felt while the dream continues, whatever their ultimate status once one wakes. Every phenomenon works exactly the same way: unlike an ordinary, unlearned person who mistakes what habitual verbal conditioning presents as though it were straightforwardly real, a bodhisattva neither takes phenomena at this naive face value, nor swings to the opposite extreme of denying that their wordless, ultimate nature exists at all.

Chinese Source

又如幻夢，非如顯現如實是有，亦非一切幻夢形質都無所有。如是諸法，非如愚夫言說串習勢力所現如實是有，亦非一切諸法勝義離言自性都無所有。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, just as an illusion or a dream should not be taken as genuinely real simply because it vividly appears, neither should the very form and substance of that illusion or dream be taken as utterly nonexistent. In just this way, phenomena should not be taken as genuinely real merely as they are presented through an unlearned person's own habituated force of verbal speech, nor should the ultimate, wordless essence of all phenomena be taken as utterly nonexistent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this double warning against grasping dream-content as real and denying its own coherent content altogether as the identical middle path already threaded through every prior reconciliation in this section, now applied to its most vivid and memorable image: dream-fear and dream-affection genuinely occur, capable of provoking real anger or real longing while the dream continues, so a dream's content clearly is not "nothing whatsoever" — yet neither is it what it appears to be once the dreamer wakes. He offers the celebrated wild fox kōan as this section's own cautionary tale about the cost of getting this balance wrong: an old teacher, asked by a student whether a person of great accomplishment still falls under the law of cause and effect, wrongly answered "no, such a person is not subject to cause and effect," and was reborn five hundred lifetimes as a wild fox for that single mistaken denial — only rescued from the fox's body once he correctly reformulated the answer as "such a person is never confused about cause and effect," rather than exempt from it. The lesson, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is precisely this section's own recurring point: dependently arisen cause and effect, like the illusion or dream that appears vividly without ultimate substance, is genuinely, functionally operative, however illusion-like its own status — denying it outright to escape the discomfort of emptiness is every bit as mistaken as clinging to it as ultimately, independently real.

He treats the extension from illusion and dream to "all phenomena" as the treatise's own final generalizing move: the same double avoidance applies not merely to obviously dreamlike experiences but to every ordinary phenomenon whatsoever, form, sound, and the rest, none of which should be taken at naive face value as habitual verbal conditioning presents it, yet none of which should be denied its own dependently arisen, functionally real status either. This closes the setup of "like illusion and dream"; the treatise turns next to its formal conclusion, and to the overall summary that closes this entire reconciliation of the sutra's troubling language.

31. Milestone: The Reconciliation Closes — Genuine Knowledge That Neither Grasps Nor Abandons a Single Fraction of Reality, Neither Exaggerating Nor Denying

The reconciliation of "like illusion and dream" closes by naming the underlying nature both illusion and every phenomenon actually share: neither existent nor nonexistent, a single undivided nature beneath the apparent duality of appearing and not-appearing. A closing summary then names the entire mark of a bodhisattva's own genuine understanding across every one of these reconciliations: never grasping the smallest fraction of reality, never abandoning the smallest fraction of it, never exaggerating what is genuinely absent into existence, never denying what genuinely exists into nonexistence — knowing what is truly real as truly real, and what is truly unreal as truly unreal, and teaching accordingly. This closes the skillful means of accommodation and doctrinal reconciliation in its entirety.

Chinese Source

由此方便悟入道理，一切諸法非有非無，猶如幻夢，其性無二。是故宣說一切諸法皆如幻夢。如是菩薩，普於一切諸法法界不取少分、不捨少分、不作損減、不作增益、無所失壞。若法實有，知為實有；若法實無，知為實無；如其所知，如是開示。當知是名菩薩隨順會通方便善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through this skillful means, one enters correctly into the understanding that all phenomena are neither existent nor nonexistent, exactly like illusion and dream, their nature undivided. This is why it is proclaimed that all phenomena are like illusion and dream. In this way, bodhisattvas, throughout the entire realm of phenomena, neither grasp the smallest fraction of it, nor abandon the smallest fraction of it; they neither exaggerate what does not exist, nor deny what does exist; nothing is thereby damaged or lost. What is genuinely existent they know as genuinely existent; what is genuinely nonexistent they know as genuinely nonexistent; and, exactly as they know it, so do they teach it. One should understand this to be called a bodhisattva's skillful means of accommodation and reconciliation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own earlier chapter on ultimate truth for the precise technical meaning behind "not grasping" and "not abandoning": not grasping the smallest fraction means never seizing hold of some genuinely nonexistent, verbally-constructed essence as though it were a real, graspable thing; not abandoning the smallest fraction means never negating the dependently arisen and perfectly accomplished natures that genuinely, actually exist. Not exaggerating means never imputing real existence onto what is genuinely absent; not denying means never negating what genuinely exists — and freedom from both errors together is what the treatise calls being free of damage or loss, since exaggeration damages one's grasp of what is truly empty and denial damages one's grasp of what is truly, functionally present.

He treats this closing formula as the single thread running through every one of the reconciliations just completed — no real inherent nature, no real substance, no arising or ceasing, equal to space, like illusion and dream — each one, examined carefully, turning out to deny only the conceptually-constructed, verbally-labeled essence while leaving the dependently arisen and ultimately real natures genuinely intact. A bodhisattva equipped with this understanding never actually needs to choose between the Prajñāpāramitā's own uncompromising language of emptiness and ordinary, functioning cause and effect, since the two were never in genuine conflict once correctly understood — and it is precisely this settled clarity that lets a bodhisattva reassure a listener genuinely frightened by the sutra's own most challenging statements without softening or distorting what the sutra actually teaches. This closes the first of the six skillful means addressing hostile, neutral, entering, and maturing beings — accommodation and doctrinal reconciliation — in its entirety; the treatise turns next to the second, the skillful means of mutual agreement.

32. Milestone: Mutual Agreement Opens — Striking a Genuine Bargain: Material Support in Exchange for Honoring Parents and the Three Jewels

The second of the six skillful means, mutual agreement, has a bodhisattva strike an explicit bargain with beings who come asking for help. When a being requests any of the ten basic necessities of life — food, drink, transport, clothing, ornament, household goods, fragrance, entertainment, light, or attendants — a bodhisattva agrees to provide it specifically on the condition that the being first learn to recognize their debt to their own parents, honor them, and show equal reverence toward genuine renunciants and other worthy figures. The same bargain extends to requests for land, housing, marketplace dealings, government affairs, community matters, wealth, grain, skilled trades, or the five branches of classical learning, and even to requests for friendship, marriage, communal gatherings, or help running a business — in every case, the bodhisattva's material or social assistance is made explicitly conditional on the being first taking up ethical conduct.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩共立要契方便善巧？謂諸菩薩，若見有情求飲食等十資身具，即便共彼立要契言：汝等若能知父母恩，恭敬供養，及諸沙門婆羅門等，廣說如前，乃至若能受持淨戒；如是我當隨汝所欲，施飲食等諸資身具，如其不能，我不施汝。如是菩薩，若見有情來求種種田事、宅事、諸闤闠事、王事、域事、財事、穀事，或有來求諸工業處及諸明處，或有來求共為朋友，或有來求共結婚媾，或有來求共作邑會，或有來求助營事業，菩薩共彼立要契言：汝等若能知父母恩，恭敬供養，如前廣說；如是我當施汝田宅，廣說乃至助營事業。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's skillful means of mutual agreement? Bodhisattvas who, seeing a being request any of the ten basic necessities such as food and drink, immediately strike this agreement with them: "If you are able to recognize your debt to your parents and honor them with reverence, and likewise honor renunciants and brahmins" — stated in full as before — "up to being able to uphold pure precepts, then I will provide you with food, drink, and the other necessities according to your wish; if you are unable to do this, I will not provide them to you." Likewise, bodhisattvas who see a being come requesting land, housing, marketplace dealings, government affairs, community matters, wealth, or grain, or requesting instruction in some skilled trade or one of the branches of classical learning, or requesting friendship, marriage, a communal gathering, or help running a business, strike this same agreement with them: "If you are able to recognize your debt to your parents and honor them with reverence" — stated in full as before — "then I will provide you with land and housing," and so on up through help running a business.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this second skillful means as operating through a genuinely different mechanism than the first: rather than teaching correct doctrine to an already receptive or already hostile listener, a bodhisattva here works with a being's own ordinary, self-interested request as the lever, attaching a condition that quietly redirects that self-interest toward the beginning of ethical practice. He cross-references this fascicle's own earlier account of skill in worldly knowledge for exactly who else besides parents deserves this reverence — household elders, those who provide genuine benefit, and those who assist in worthy undertakings — confirming that the bargain's own moral content is not arbitrary but continuous with everything already established about proper social conduct.

He treats the underlying psychology as this method's own real insight: a being frightened of the consequences of wrongdoing in this life or the next naturally becomes willing to take up generosity, precept-keeping, and merit-making, and a bodhisattva's explicit bargain simply makes that willingness concrete and actionable rather than leaving it as a vague aspiration — the material help genuinely delivered once the condition is genuinely met, so that fulfilling one's end of an ordinary bargain becomes, without the being necessarily even noticing at first, the beginning of a lasting change in conduct. This closes the first two of six concrete applications; the treatise turns next to the remaining four — rescue from legal danger, fear, frustrated desire, and illness, each conditioned on this same underlying bargain.

33. Milestone: Mutual Agreement Closes — Rescue From Legal Danger, Fear, Frustrated Desire, and Illness, All Conditioned on the Very Same Bargain

The remaining four applications extend the identical bargain into genuine crisis. A being facing arrest, punishment, mutilation, humiliation, exile, or enslavement for some wrongdoing is offered rescue on the condition of future ethical conduct. A being terrorized by kings, bandits, flood, fire, human or nonhuman threat, starvation, or damaged reputation is offered protection on the same condition. A being desperate to be united with what they love or separated from what they loathe is offered help fulfilling that wish on the same condition. Master Cháng Bǎi treats these four remaining applications as together covering the full spectrum of genuine crisis a being might actually face — legal danger, physical terror, unmet longing, and bodily suffering — each one a moment when a being's own motivation to change is naturally at its highest, precisely because the stakes are suddenly, unmistakably real. He treats the closing line — that beings who strike this bargain go on to swiftly adopt wholesome conduct and abandon unwholesome conduct, with the bodhisattva then fulfilling every promised wish in return — as confirming that the bargain is never a bluff or a manipulative trick to be quietly abandoned once compliance is secured: a bodhisattva who has genuinely promised material help or rescue in exchange for a being's own change in conduct genuinely delivers on that promise once the condition is genuinely met, since the entire method depends on beings learning, through direct experience, that ethical conduct and genuine benefit are actually, reliably connected — a lesson that a broken promise would destroy far more thoroughly than it could ever teach. This closes the second of the six skillful means, mutual agreement, in its entirety; the treatise turns next to the third, the skillful means of differing aspiration — a bodhisattva's own deliberate, temporary withholding of help, undertaken purely for a being's genuine benefit.

34. Milestone: Skillful Withholding Opens — a Bodhisattva's Deliberate, Temporary Refusal, Compassion Never Actually Wavering Underneath It

The third skillful means, differing aspiration, addresses what happens when a being who has struck an agreement fails to keep their end of it: a bodhisattva then deliberately withholds the promised material support or rescue from danger, fear, frustrated desire, or illness — not from stinginess or genuine indifference, but purely as a further skillful means aimed at the being's own genuine benefit. The bodhisattva's outward refusal and the bodhisattva's inward compassion are, in this specific sense, "of differing aspiration" — the visible act and the actual underlying motivation genuinely diverge, even though both ultimately serve the identical compassionate purpose.

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses the crucial distinction this passage insists on repeatedly, almost defensively: the bodhisattva's refusal must never actually arise from stinginess, indifference, or genuine abandonment — the text explicitly denies any "other motivation" or "genuinely different motivation" behind the withholding — since a refusal motivated by real indifference would simply be ordinary neglect dressed up in doctrinal language, while a refusal motivated purely by compassion for the being's own genuine growth is something else entirely: a deliberate, temporary withholding of an immediate want in service of a deeper, more lasting benefit that immediate gratification would have undermined.

He treats "differing aspiration" as naming precisely this gap between outward act and inward motivation: to an outside observer, or even to the being on the receiving end, the bodhisattva's refusal looks identical to ordinary stinginess or abandonment, yet the actual aspiration driving it — genuine benefit for the being who has not yet kept their word — is entirely different from what the outward act would normally suggest. He notes this method requires real discernment to apply correctly, since withholding help from a genuinely needy being for insufficiently examined reasons would simply be harmful rather than skillful; the treatise's own insistence on "purely for the being's benefit" functions as a strict boundary condition rather than a loophole excusing ordinary neglect. This closes the setup of skillful withholding; the treatise turns next to how this same principle extends even to a bodhisattva's own close, long-standing friends, and to two genuinely startling examples of what compassionate firmness can license.

The same principle extends to beings who have no particular request or crisis at all, but who share a genuine, long-standing closeness with a bodhisattva: such beings receive gentle guidance suited to the occasion, encouraging them to recognize their debt to their parents and take up ethical practice. Should that gentle guidance be met with willful, careless disregard, a bodhisattva then displays genuine anger and reproach, opposes their conduct outright, or even inflicts something that appears, on the surface, genuinely harmful — never from actual anger, opposition, or the wish to harm, but purely as skillful means toward the being's own benefit. The commentary illustrates this with two striking examples drawn from the bodhisattva precepts themselves: forcibly removing a cruel, tyrannical official from power for the sake of the beings suffering under them, and forcibly reclaiming monastic or stūpa property that has been stolen and is being selfishly consumed by the thief, in both cases without any violation of the bodhisattva's own precepts, since the underlying motivation remains genuinely compassionate throughout.

He adds a contemporary illustration: monastics who have not properly arranged the disposition of monastery property before death sometimes see that property pass to lay relatives who, not understanding its proper status, simply use it themselves — a bodhisattva in such a case may need to persuade, or even compel, the return of that property, precisely to spare those relatives the very suffering such misuse would otherwise bring upon them. 36. Milestone: Skillful Means Born of Duress — Using Legitimate Authority to Threaten Real Consequences, Then Watching Closely to Make Sure the Threat Actually Works

The fourth skillful means applies specifically to a bodhisattva who genuinely holds legitimate authority — the head of a household or the ruler of a country — using that authority to warn their own family or subjects directly: fail to honor your parents, fail to revere renunciants, violate household or civic law, and real consequences will follow — withdrawn support, punishment, estrangement, or outright banishment. A bodhisattva then appoints a genuinely capable, alert person to keep ongoing watch, ensuring the warning is not merely issued once and forgotten. Beings governed this way take up wholesome conduct not from genuine desire to practice virtue but from real fear of real consequences — yet a bodhisattva's skillful pressure nonetheless succeeds in producing the change itself.

37. Milestone: Kindness and Its Repayment — the Truest Way to Repay a Debt Is Never Money, but Genuine Virtue

The fifth skillful means has a bodhisattva first extend genuine, unconditional kindness — providing necessities, rescuing from crisis, protecting from fear, fulfilling desire, or healing illness, exactly as already described — without attaching any prior condition. Only once a being, genuinely grateful, wishes to repay that kindness does a bodhisattva redirect the gratitude itself: refusing ordinary material repayment, and declaring instead that the only repayment genuinely worth offering is taking up reverence for one's own parents and the three jewels, and ultimately upholding pure precepts — reframing gratitude itself into the very motivation for ethical practice.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fifth skillful means as the precise mirror image of mutual agreement, the second skillful means already covered: where mutual agreement makes ethical conduct an explicit precondition stated up front, kindness and its repayment gives freely first, with no condition attached at all, and only afterward, once gratitude has genuinely arisen on its own, redirects that gratitude toward the identical goal. The sixth and final skillful means, ultimate purity, unfolds through the classical phases of a buddha's own descent into the world. 40. Milestone: Willingly Enduring Years of Futile Austerity — So That Ascetics Devoted to Needless Suffering Might Finally Let It Go

Having renounced the world, a bodhisattva then deliberately vows to undergo genuinely difficult ascetic practice — exactly as Śākyamuni undertook six years of austerity before his own awakening — not because such practice actually produces liberation, but specifically to reach beings who already believe deeply in the value of austerity for its own sake. Only a teacher who has personally, visibly undergone the identical practice they now recommend abandoning can actually persuade such ascetics to release their own attachment to needless suffering.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, this bodhisattva manifests a vow to undergo genuinely difficult ascetic practice. This is done specifically so that beings who place their faith and understanding in ascetic practice might release their own attachment to what they find so appealing in it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this third phase as this entire section's own most psychologically astute move: a teacher who simply denounces austerity from the outside, having never personally attempted it, carries little authority with practitioners already deeply committed to that path, however genuinely mistaken their approach may be — but a teacher who has personally undertaken the identical austerity, exhausting its own possibilities to their fullest extent, and only then concluded that this path does not lead to genuine liberation, speaks with an authority no outside critic could ever match. The six years of austerity the historical Buddha underwent before reaching the Bodhi tree, he stresses, were never wasted effort in this specific sense — they were the necessary credential that let his subsequent teaching of the middle way actually reach those who most needed to hear it.

He treats this phase as directly continuing the identical logic of the second: just as visible, voluntary renunciation of privilege made renunciation itself credible, visible, voluntary endurance of austerity — followed by its equally visible rejection as a path to genuine awakening — makes that rejection credible in a way mere doctrinal argument never could. This closes the third phase; the treatise turns next to the fourth — the bodhisattva's own manifestation of complete, genuine awakening itself.

41. Milestone: Manifesting Awakening Itself, Then Waiting for Brahmā's Own Plea Before Teaching a Single Word

Having exhausted austerity's own limits, a bodhisattva then manifests the actual attainment of supreme, complete awakening — kindling in every other being who shares the same aspiration toward awakening a genuine, joyful confidence that this same goal is actually reachable, since it has now visibly been reached. Yet upon reaching complete awakening, a bodhisattva does not immediately begin teaching; instead, the newly awakened buddha waits until the great Brahmā, ruler of the first meditative heaven, personally descends and formally requests that the teaching be given — a deliberate delay meant to cultivate in beings a heightened reverence for the teaching about to be delivered, since even the mighty Brahmā himself evidently regards it as something so precious that he comes in person to beg for it.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, this bodhisattva attains supreme, correct awakening. This is done so that other beings who share the identical aspiration toward awakening and liberation give rise to genuine joy at its supreme excellence. Furthermore, having attained supreme, correct awakening, this buddha does not immediately teach the true dharma to beings, but waits until the great Brahmā king personally comes to formally request it. This is done so that beings give rise to genuine reverence toward the true dharma, reflecting in this way: "The teaching about to be given must surely be supremely wondrous, since even the great Brahmā king now hopes the World-Honored One will teach it, and has come in person to make this request."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth phase, awakening itself, as the natural culmination of everything the previous three phases prepared: the bodhisattva's descent, renunciation, and austerity all built credibility and reverence, and now the actual attainment of awakening gives every other aspiring practitioner concrete proof that the destination they themselves seek genuinely exists and can genuinely be reached — not an abstract promise but a demonstrated fact.

He treats the fifth phase, waiting for Brahmā's own plea, as a carefully calculated piece of theater serving a genuine pedagogical purpose rather than false modesty: a newly awakened buddha could, in principle, simply begin teaching immediately, but choosing instead to wait for the ruler of an entire heavenly realm to descend and personally beg for the teaching sends an unmistakable signal about how precious that teaching actually is — ordinary beings reason that if even Brahmā himself values this teaching enough to make this formal request, it must be worth their own most serious attention as well. This closes the fourth and fifth phases; the treatise turns next to the sixth phase, in which the Buddha observes the world with compassion before ever actually beginning to teach, specifically guarding against a subtle but real misunderstanding of what has just occurred.

42. Milestone: Observing the World With Compassion Before Ever Turning the Wheel — Guarding Against the Claim That the Buddha's Compassion Was Merely Reactive

Before actually teaching, the newly awakened buddha surveys the entire world through the buddha-eye — precisely to forestall a specific, damaging misunderstanding some beings might otherwise form: that the Buddha taught only because Brahmā personally requested it, out of deference to Brahmā rather than from the Buddha's own genuine compassion, as though the Buddha lacked the independent wisdom to judge for himself whether beings were actually ready to receive the teaching. Having thus surveyed the world with the buddha-eye, on the Buddha's own initiative and by the Buddha's own judgment, the wheel of the unsurpassed teaching — never before turned anywhere in the world — is finally set in motion, followed by further teaching and the establishment of the actual training precepts.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the Buddha observes the world through the buddha-eye, so as to prevent beings from forming this kind of slander: "The Buddha proclaimed the true teaching only because the great Brahmā king personally came to request it, out of reverence for Brahmā, and not from any compassion arising within the Buddha's own being; the Buddha was merely provoked into it by another, and was not personally capable of judging what was actually fitting." In order to dispel this kind of mistaken grasping in certain beings, the Buddha first observes the world through the buddha-eye, and only then turns the wheel of the unsurpassed teaching, never before turned anywhere in the world. In this way the true teaching is further proclaimed, and the actual training precepts are established. This is called a bodhisattva's skillful means of ultimate purity. Among the skillful means already described, there is no further skillful means beyond or more wondrous than this; this is why it is called ultimate purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own chapter on the power of spiritual lineage for what the buddha-eye's own survey actually accomplishes: across all six periods of day and night, the Buddha continuously observes which beings' wholesome roots are increasing and which are declining, determining specifically in whom an as-yet-unplanted wholesome root should now be planted, all the way to determining in whom the supreme fruit of arhatship should now be established — a survey the Buddha performs continuously even now, not merely at the moment of this first historical teaching. The point of naming this survey explicitly here, immediately after Brahmā's own request, is to make unmistakably clear that the Buddha's decision to teach rests on the Buddha's own independent, complete wisdom regarding exactly who is ready to hear what, rather than on any external prompting — Brahmā's request created the occasion, but the Buddha's own compassionate wisdom, not deference to Brahmā, is what actually determined the content and timing of what followed.

He explains "turning the wheel of the teaching" through the treatise's own later chapter on compiling discourses: the Buddha transmits the very teaching personally realized into the first recipient, Kauṇḍinya, who in turn transmits it into further disciples, who transmit it onward again — a continuous, rolling transmission from body to body across generations, exactly resembling a wheel rolling from one place to the next, hence "wheel"; and since this transmission carries the very substance of the eightfold noble path, correct view and the rest, it is called specifically the wheel of the "dharma." He closes by stressing the treatise's own emphatic final claim: among every skillful means already described across this entire topic, nothing beyond or more excellent than this sixth, culminating example exists — which is precisely why it earns the name "ultimate purity," the very summit toward which every other skillful means in this topic has, in its own way, been building. This closes the sixth and final skillful means addressing the four kinds of being; the treatise turns next to a comprehensive closing summary of all six together, and then to the full closing of the eleventh topic, skillful means, in its entirety.

43. Milestone: The Eleventh Topic Closes in Full — Twelve Kinds of Skillful Means, Nothing Beyond Them Left to Add

A comprehensive closing summary gathers together everything this topic has covered: the six skillful means just detailed — accommodation and reconciliation, mutual agreement, differing aspiration, duress, kindness and repayment, and ultimate purity — together remove hostility from beings who resent the teaching, draw neutral beings into genuine entry, mature those who have entered, and liberate those already mature, whether stated briefly or explained in full detail — and nothing beyond these six can be added or improved upon. This closes the entire structure of twelve skillful means named at this topic's very outset: six turned inward toward a bodhisattva's own realization of the complete teaching, and six turned outward toward maturing every kind of sentient being — by far the most extensive of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩所說六種若略若廣方便善巧，能除憎背聖教有情所有恚惱；處中住者，令其趣入；已趣入者，令其成熟；已成熟者，令得解脫。除此無有若過若增。是名菩薩方便善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, bodhisattvas' six kinds of skillful means, whether stated briefly or explained in full, are able to remove all hostility from beings who resent the noble teaching; to draw those who remain neutral into genuine entry; to mature those who have already entered; and to bring those already mature to actual liberation. Beyond these, there is nothing further to add and nothing further to improve upon. This is called a bodhisattva's skillful means.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes this entire topic by recapping its full architecture one final time: six inward-turned means sustaining a bodhisattva's own progress toward realization — compassion unable to abandon beings, thorough causal knowledge, delight in awakening's own wisdom, and their three paired fruits of refusing private nirvāṇa, remaining unstained within saṃsāra, and blazing diligence — joined with six outward-turned means addressing beings at every stage of their own spiritual journey: enabling boundless fruit from the smallest good deed, enabling vast wholesome roots through fourteen concrete methods requiring minimal effort, and the culminating six shared methods — accommodation, agreement, differing aspiration, duress, kindness, and ultimate purity — reaching hostile, neutral, entering, and maturing beings in turn.

He treats the emphatic closing claim — that nothing beyond these twelve kinds of skillful means remains to be added — as this topic's own fitting conclusion to what has been, by a wide margin, the most extensive single topic among the seventeenth chapter's fifteen, spanning the majority of this entire fascicle: compassion and wisdom alone, however genuine, remain incomplete without the concrete, resourceful intelligence to actually translate them into effective help across every conceivable circumstance a being might present, and this topic's own exhaustive catalogue — from a single grain of rice dedicated to awakening, through reconciling a listener's genuine terror at hearing emptiness taught, to the Buddha's own culminating example of descent, renunciation, austerity, awakening, and teaching — is offered as nothing less than the complete toolkit skillful means actually requires. This closes the eleventh of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, skillful means, in its entirety; the treatise turns next to the twelfth topic, wondrous mnemonic retention.

44. Milestone: Wondrous Mnemonic Retention Opens — Retaining the Very Words of the Teaching Forever, and Retaining Its Boundless Meaning Just as Permanently

The twelfth topic, wondrous mnemonic retention, names four kinds: retention of the teaching itself, retention of its meaning, retention through mantra, and retention that produces a bodhisattva's own genuine patient acceptance of reality. The first, retention of the teaching, is a bodhisattva's mastery over letters and words so complete that even scripture never before heard, never rehearsed, and not yet genuinely familiar — countless sutras compiled through every possible sequence and combination — is retained without loss across an immeasurable span of time. The second, retention of meaning, works identically but targets not the words themselves but the boundless layers of significance those words convey, equally unforgotten across immeasurable time even without prior rehearsal.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩妙陀羅尼？當知如是妙陀羅尼略有四種。一者、法陀羅尼，二者、義陀羅尼，三者、呪陀羅尼，四者、能得菩薩忍陀羅尼。云何菩薩法陀羅尼？謂諸菩薩獲得如是念慧力持，由此力持，聞未曾聞，言未溫習、未善通利，名句文身之所攝錄，次第錯縱、次第結集無量經典，經無量時能持不忘。是名菩薩法陀羅尼。云何菩薩義陀羅尼？謂如前說。此差別者，即於彼法無量義趣，心未溫習、未善通利，經無量時能持不忘。是名菩薩義陀羅尼。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's wondrous mnemonic retention? One should understand that this is, in brief, of four kinds: first, retention of the teaching; second, retention of meaning; third, retention through mantra; fourth, retention that produces a bodhisattva's genuine patient acceptance. What is a bodhisattva's retention of the teaching? Bodhisattvas who obtain this power of mindful, wise retention, and who, through this power, retain without loss, across an immeasurable span of time, countless sutras compiled through every sequence and combination — even teaching never before heard, never rehearsed, and not yet genuinely mastered, composed of names, phrases, and syllables. This is called a bodhisattva's retention of the teaching. What is a bodhisattva's retention of meaning? It is as already described. The distinction is this: regarding the boundless significance conveyed by that same teaching, even without prior mental rehearsal or genuine mastery, it is retained without loss across an immeasurable span of time. This is called a bodhisattva's retention of meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "wondrous mnemonic retention" through its classical Sanskrit meaning, dhāraṇī, rendered "total holding" — a mindful, wise capacity so strong that once genuine sacred speech is encountered, it is never subsequently lost, and every wholesome quality it carries can be sustained while every unwholesome influence it might otherwise invite is held at bay. He cites the Xianyang Shengjiao Lun's own concise definition directly for the first two kinds together: a bodhisattva's genuine mastery over the letter itself, so complete that within any single syllable the entirety of its own defiled and purified significance can be drawn out, whether summarized briefly or unpacked in full detail, applicable equally to worldly and to transcendent teaching.

He treats the distinction between the two kinds as one of object rather than mechanism: retention of the teaching targets the actual words, names, phrases, and syllables themselves, holding the literal text secure across any length of time even without deliberate memorization beforehand; retention of meaning targets instead the layered significance those same words convey, a single passage of scripture yielding meaning upon meaning the more deeply it is examined, all of it likewise held secure without needing to have been consciously rehearsed in advance. Together, he notes, these first two kinds ensure that neither the letter nor the spirit of the teaching is ever genuinely lost to a bodhisattva who has attained this capacity — the literal container and its living content both permanently secured. This closes the first two kinds; the treatise turns next to the third, retention through mantra.

45. Milestone: Mantra-Retention — a Bodhisattva's Own Meditative Mastery Makes Even an Ordinary Formula Genuinely, Reliably Effective

The third kind, retention through mantra, rests on genuine mastery of meditative absorption — whether the four form-realm absorptions or the four formless attainments, or the undefiled concentration born of cultivating the four establishments of mindfulness. This mastery empowers mantras aimed at relieving beings' own afflictions and misfortunes — summoning rain, curing eye disease, neutralizing snake venom, and the like — so that these formulas actually, reliably produce their intended effect once channeled through a bodhisattva's own sustained mindful power, their efficacy never wasted and capable of dispelling every kind of affliction, not merely one.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's retention through mantra? Bodhisattvas who obtain this mastery of meditative equipoise, and who, through this mastery, empower the mantra formulas capable of relieving beings' own afflictions and misfortunes, so that these formulas become entirely, supremely efficacious, never wasted, and capable of dispelling not merely one but every kind of affliction and misfortune.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi directly connects this teaching to its own earlier appearance in fascicle 44's account of fulfilling-requests meditation: an ordinary practitioner reciting a mantra without genuine spiritual accomplishment behind it accomplishes relatively little, since the formula's own effectiveness depends heavily on the reciter's own accumulated meditative and spiritual power rather than the words alone — but a bodhisattva who has genuinely attained deep meditative mastery, whether through the form-realm's four absorptions, the formless realm's four attainments, or the undefiled concentration cultivated through mindfulness of the body, sensation, mind, and phenomena, channels that accumulated power directly into the mantra itself, making even an ordinarily modest formula produce reliable, supreme results.

He treats the specific examples named — summoning rain during drought, curing eye ailments, neutralizing snakebite — as concrete, practically recognizable illustrations rather than abstract claims, precisely the kind of tangible relief that makes this form of skillful assistance immediately legible to ordinary beings who might not yet grasp subtler doctrinal teaching at all. He stresses the closing emphasis on "not merely one kind" of affliction: a bodhisattva's own mantra-retention is not narrowly specialized to a single problem but genuinely versatile, its underlying power drawn from meditative mastery itself rather than from any single formula's own particular design. This closes retention through mantra; the treatise turns next to the fourth and richest kind, retention that produces a bodhisattva's own genuine patient acceptance of reality's true nature.

46. Milestone: The Fourth Retention Opens — an Unshakable Resolve Matured Across an Entire Incalculable Eon, Then a Life Stripped Down to Almost Nothing

The fourth and richest kind of retention, the retention producing a bodhisattva's own genuine patient acceptance, rests on a specific cause: a naturally firm, unshakable resolve matured across the entire first incalculable eon of the path, joined with the four kinds of genuine understanding that see through names, things, provisional essence, and provisional distinction. A bodhisattva possessing this foundation then withdraws entirely — dwelling alone in genuine solitude, speaking little to avoid disturbing concentration, eating only simply and in moderation, sleeping little, and devoting sustained, penetrating reflection specifically to certain mantra syllables the Buddha himself taught as a vehicle toward a bodhisattva's own genuine patient acceptance of reality.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's retention that produces genuine patient acceptance? Bodhisattvas who accomplish a naturally firm, resolute foundation of practice, complete with genuine, subtle wisdom. They dwell alone in solitude, silent and free of excessive speech, possessing nothing, walking a path already clearly seen. They eat in proper measure, never mixed with anything impure, taking food of a single, simple kind. They constantly cultivate the deepest meditative stillness, sleeping little and remaining wakeful through the greater part of the night. Toward the mantra syllables the Buddha has taught as leading to a bodhisattva's own genuine patient acceptance, they apply sustained, penetrating reflection.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the practitioner described here as a bodhisattva still on the path of conviction, having completed the first of the three incalculable great eons and standing at its very threshold, citing Asaṅga's own verse from the Compendium of the Great Vehicle: purifying power, an increasingly dominant force, a firm mind, and steady advancement together define this entire first incalculable eon of practice — "firm mind" meaning a great awakening-resolve so genuinely rooted that no hostile influence, however capable, can talk the practitioner out of it, and "steady advancement" meaning that wholesome roots, across this life and every life to come, only ever increase and never regress. This firmness, joined with the four investigations already introduced earlier in this fascicle's account of calming and insight, together constitute the "genuine, subtle wisdom" this passage names as its own necessary foundation.

He treats the description of this bodhisattva's actual way of life — solitude, minimal speech, simple food, minimal sleep — as itself already a form of teaching by example rather than mere incidental biographical detail: withdrawing from crowded, distracting company removes the specific obstacle to genuine concentration that ordinary social life constantly reintroduces, refraining from excessive talk removes the discursive thought that concentration cannot tolerate, and simple, moderate eating removes the specific craving and physical sluggishness that indulgent eating habits produce. "Possessing nothing" and "walking a path already clearly seen," he notes, describe a bodhisattva who has already genuinely realized the emptiness of phenomena and clearly perceives the ultimate path stretching ahead, no longer disturbed by attachment to material acquisition, social status, or the pursuit of ordinary worldly pleasure and companionship, all of which are recognized as impermanent, unsatisfying, and liable to collapse without warning. This closes the causal foundation for the fourth retention; the treatise turns next to the mantra syllables themselves, and to what happens when a bodhisattva actually reflects on them deeply.

47. Milestone: The Mantra Itself — Meaningless Syllables Recited Until Their Very Meaninglessness Becomes the Doorway to Truth

The treatise gives the actual mantra syllables the Buddha taught for this purpose, ending, as such formulas classically do, with the word "svāhā" — meaning stillness, and stillness in the specific sense of the cessation of misfortune, since when every undertaking is at peace and no calamity remains, everything is genuinely still. Reflecting on these very syllables, a bodhisattva investigates their meaning with sustained, careful attention, and — through this correct practice, discovered directly through their own inner reflection rather than told to them by anyone else — comes to realize that these mantra syllables actually have no meaning whatsoever: they are the perfectly accomplished nature in the sole and specific sense that they are simply, purely without meaning. Having genuinely realized their meaning to be precisely this absence of meaning, a bodhisattva seeks nothing further beyond this point — this exact realization is what it means to have skillfully and thoroughly penetrated the meaning of these mantra syllables.

Chinese Source

其呪詞曰：壹胝　密胝　吉胝毗　羼底（丁理反）　鉢陀膩　莎訶。即於如是呪章句義，審諦思惟、籌量、觀察。彼於如是呪章句義如是正行，不從他聞，自然通達；了知如是諸呪章句都無有義，是圓成實，但唯無義；如實了知此章句義，所謂無義。是故過此，不求餘義，齊此名為妙善通達呪章句義。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mantra syllables are: iti mi ti ki ti bhi kṣānti (pronounced with a soft "t") padāni svāhā. Reflecting on the meaning of exactly these mantra syllables with sustained, careful attention, investigation, and observation, and through this correct practice regarding their meaning — not heard from anyone else, but naturally penetrated through direct inner realization — a bodhisattva comes to know that these mantra syllables have no meaning at all; this is their perfectly accomplished nature, consisting purely and only in having no meaning. Correctly and thoroughly knowing the meaning of these syllables to be precisely this absence of meaning, one seeks nothing further beyond this point. This, and nothing more, is called skillfully and thoroughly penetrating the meaning of these mantra syllables.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites Master Kuiji's own commentary on the Heart Sūtra directly identifying this specific mantra as one Maitreya bodhisattva himself taught, capable of swiftly bringing a practitioner to a bodhisattva's own patient acceptance of the truth of non-arising — and glosses "svāhā," the word closing virtually every classical mantra, as meaning stillness in the sense of the actual cessation of misfortune, since once every affair rests genuinely at peace with no remaining calamity, everything is, in that specific sense, entirely still.

He treats the progression this passage describes with real precision: a bodhisattva first investigates the mantra's literal words with sustained attention, then moves from mere words toward a wordless dimension of understanding beneath them, discovering — crucially, through direct realization rather than being told — that the syllables carry no fixed, graspable content whatsoever: no real object corresponds to the word, and no real word genuinely captures any object either, so both what is named and the naming itself dissolve together into emptiness. Master Kuiji's own explanation, which Cháng Bǎi quotes directly, makes the pedagogical point explicit: these syllables carry no meaning at all, only names — and a bodhisattva still on the path leading up to the first ground who recites this meaningless mantra thereby directly uproots their own habitual conceptual construction, learning concretely never to generate attachment on the basis of a mere name alone.

He then extends this insight, following the treatise's own commentary, to ordinary speech itself: every word anyone ever speaks is, in exactly this sense, already a mantra — pure name without any corresponding graspable substance. Someone calling another person "lazy" points to no actual, findable thing called laziness anywhere in existence, only a word; yet an unenlightened person hearing a harsh word reacts as though something real had actually struck them, growing furious at criticism and elated at praise precisely because they mistake the mere name for something substantial and real. Once this is genuinely seen through, hearing even the harshest criticism becomes recognizable as simply another person "reciting a mantra" — a reminder Cháng Bǎi suggests can be genuinely, practically useful the next time an angry word arrives: recognizing on the spot that "this person is just reciting a mantra," and that the mantra's own meaning is precisely its lack of meaning, is often enough to prevent the affliction from actually taking hold. This closes the mantra's own literal reflection; the treatise turns next to how this same insight extends from the mantra alone to every phenomenon whatsoever.

48. Milestone: From "No Meaning" to the Wordless Nature of Every Phenomenon — Nothing Named by Any Word Was Ever Actually Real to Begin With

Having correctly penetrated the mantra's own meaninglessness, a bodhisattva then extends this identical realization, entirely on their own without needing to hear it from anyone else, to every phenomenon whatsoever: exactly as with the mantra syllables, whatever meaning ordinary speech attributes to any phenomenon at all turns out, upon the same investigation, never to have been genuinely real — only the wordless nature underlying every phenomenon, beyond what any verbal label could ever capture, is what genuinely constitutes that phenomenon's own actual nature. Once a bodhisattva has correctly penetrated to this wordless nature, there is nothing further beyond it left to seek.

Chinese Source

彼於如是呪章句義正通達已，即隨此義，不從他聞，自正通達一切法義。謂於此義如是通達，一切言說所說諸法自性之義皆不成實，唯有諸法離言自性是自性義。彼於諸法此自性義正通達已，過此更無餘義可求。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having correctly penetrated the meaning of these mantra syllables, bodhisattvas, following this same meaning, without hearing it from anyone else, correctly and thoroughly penetrate the meaning of every phenomenon on their own. That is to say, having correctly penetrated this meaning, they realize that the meaning attributed by every kind of verbal statement to the essential nature of any phenomenon whatsoever is never genuinely real; only the wordless essential nature of every phenomenon constitutes its own genuine essential meaning. Having correctly penetrated this essential meaning within every phenomenon, there is, beyond this, nothing further to seek.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this extension from mantra to "every phenomenon whatsoever" as the entire point this teaching has been building toward from the start: the mantra was never actually about mantras specifically, but a deliberately chosen, self-contained laboratory for discovering, in miniature, exactly the same emptiness that pervades every word ever spoken about any phenomenon at all — a table, a cup, a self, a friend. Once the principle is genuinely grasped through the mantra's own confined example, generalizing it to the whole of reality requires no further external instruction, since the underlying structure — verbal label projected onto a process that never actually corresponds to that label's own implied fixed essence — is identical in every single case.

He cross-references the treatise's own later chapter of determinations for the precise technical grounding of this claim: the mantra syllables carry no content available to conceptual discrimination whatsoever, and this very absence of content is itself their perfected nature "by way of demonstrative reasoning" — reasoning grounded in direct perception, inference, and the testimony of scripture together, arriving at a conclusion no longer open to genuine doubt. What is directly perceived in the mantra's own case — that these particular syllables carry no meaning — is then extended by valid inference to the parallel conclusion that the verbally-labeled essence of every other phenomenon is equally without genuine, fixed reality, and that only each phenomenon's own wordless nature constitutes what is actually, genuinely there. He treats "there is nothing further beyond this to seek" as this passage's own repeated insistence that emptiness, once correctly understood in this specific sense, is not an invitation to keep searching for some yet-deeper hidden truth, but the complete and sufficient resting point itself. This closes the extension from mantra to all phenomena; the treatise turns next to the boundless joy this realization actually produces, and to the specific, precise threshold of spiritual attainment it brings a bodhisattva to.

49. Milestone: Boundless Joy at the Very Threshold of the First Ground — Closing the Fourth Retention With the Supreme Worldly Dharma, One Instant From Genuine Realization

Genuinely penetrating this meaning produces, in a bodhisattva, the most excellent and vast joy imaginable — the treatise identifies this precisely as the joy proper to entering the first ground's own extremely joyous abiding. Attaining this specific mantra-based retention means a bodhisattva has already attained the patient acceptance these very syllables establish, and, having attained it, will very soon obtain the genuinely pure, excellent aspiration belonging to the first ground itself, already operating from the supreme tier of patient acceptance available within the stage of conviction. This closes the fourth and final kind of wondrous mnemonic retention.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas thereby obtain this retention, it should be said that they have already obtained the supreme patient acceptance these very mnemonic syllables establish. Having obtained this patient acceptance, such bodhisattvas will, before long, obtain the genuinely pure, excellent aspiration of the first ground, already operating from the supreme tier of patient acceptance within the stage of conviction. One should understand this to be called a bodhisattva's own retention that produces genuine patient acceptance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the most excellent, vast joy" directly with the classical name of the first bodhisattva ground itself, the extremely joyous ground — a joy this specific, he stresses, because it marks the very first time, across the entirety of an incalculable eon of prior practice, that a bodhisattva has actually, directly touched emptiness rather than merely understood it as doctrine: countless prior lifetimes of conceptual activity, never once resting, never once glimpsing genuine cessation, finally give way, in this single moment, to a mind that has actually seen through to the unconditioned. He quotes the Pure Land tradition's own aspiration verse — "the lotus opens, the buddha is seen, one awakens to the unborn" — noting that this exact experience of directly touching the unconditioned need not wait for some future rebirth in a pure land at all: genuinely realizing that all phenomena are without meaning is already, in that very instant, an encounter with the unborn nature of reality, since every being and every circumstance rest equally in that same underlying emptiness — a pure land, in this specific sense, already present the moment this realization genuinely dawns.

He treats the closing technical description — "the supreme tier of patient acceptance within the stage of conviction" — as identifying this attainment precisely as the classical "supreme worldly dharma," the very highest point reachable while still, strictly speaking, an unenlightened being: the topmost tier of the patience stage within the path of preparation, immediately followed, in the next instant of consciousness, by direct entry into the path of seeing itself. A bodhisattva at this exact point has not yet technically attained the pure, excellent aspiration unique to the first ground — that remains, by definition, still to come — yet stands only a single instant, some hundredth of a second, away from it, every last subtle residue of self- and phenomena-grasping about to be permanently severed. This closes the fourth and richest of wondrous mnemonic retention's four kinds, and with it the treatise's own full account of the four kinds together; the treatise turns next to an overall comparison of what the four kinds of retention each actually attain, and the specific merits each one produces.
