Fascicle 45 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十五 · The Factors of Awakening, Continued

Four Reliances, Four Understandings, the Provisions for Awakening

Continuing directly from fascicle 44's opening of the seventeenth chapter, this fascicle carries the factors of awakening through nine further topics: the four correct reliances, the four unhindered understandings, the provisions for awakening, the thirty-seven classical factors of awakening themselves, cultivating calming and insight, skillful means, wondrous mnemonic retention, one's own correct aspiration, and the three concentrations.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 45 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle 45 Opens — the Factors of Awakening Continue With the Sixth of Fifteen Topics, the Four Correct Reliances

Fascicle 45 opens as the second of three installments of the seventeenth chapter, the factors of awakening — the first five topics already completed in fascicle 44, the final topic still to come in fascicle 46. The sixth topic, the four correct reliances, opens by naming its first reliance: relying on meaning rather than on ornamental phrasing, so that a bodhisattva listening to the teaching seeks the substance a teacher is actually conveying rather than the polish of their language, receiving the meaning with genuine reverence even when it arrives through the plainest, most ordinary words.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處菩提分品第十七之二。云何菩薩修正四依？謂諸菩薩為求義故，從他聽法，不為求世藻飾文辭。菩薩求義不為求文而聽法時，雖遇常流言音說法，但依於義，恭敬聽受。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter seventeen, part two: the factors of awakening. How do bodhisattvas correctly cultivate the four reliances? Bodhisattvas who, seeking the meaning of the teaching, listen to the dharma from another, not seeking worldly, ornamental phrasing — when bodhisattvas seeking meaning rather than phrasing listen to the teaching, even when they encounter it delivered in the most ordinary, common speech, they nonetheless rely on its meaning, listening to and receiving it with reverence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi's opening note situates this fascicle within the chapter's own larger architecture: the fifteen topics of the factors of awakening were already named in outline at the very start of chapter seventeen in fascicle 44 — conscience and shame, firm resolve, a tireless mind, skill in treatises, skill in worldly knowledge, and then the nine topics beginning here in fascicle 45, with the fifteenth and final topic, the four dharma-summaries, held over for fascicle 46. "Reliance" itself, he notes, functions here as a corrective principle rather than a merely descriptive category: each of the four names a specific, easily-made mistake and the correct standard that guards against it.

He grounds the first reliance in the treatise's own earlier chapter on the stage of meditative equipoise, which explains that this reliance exists specifically to correct a common failing among ordinary listeners: a fondness for elegant, well-turned phrasing that can eclipse actual comprehension of what that phrasing means, so that beautiful language becomes mistaken for genuine understanding, or plain language is dismissed as though it could carry nothing of value. A bodhisattva relying on meaning listens past the surface texture of any given explanation, plain or polished alike, and receives with reverence whatever substance is actually being conveyed — since the dharma's own value never resided in its literary ornament to begin with. This closes the first reliance; the treatise turns next to the second, relying on the teaching itself rather than on whoever happens to be delivering it.

2. Milestone: Relying on the Teaching, Not on Whoever Happens to Deliver It — Recognizing Provisional and Complete Teaching for What Each Actually Is

The second reliance: correctly recognizing provisional teaching for what it is and complete teaching for what it is, then relying on the underlying principle itself rather than on the reputation of whoever speaks — not simply believing something because an elder, a well-known figure, or even someone attributed to the Buddha or the community said it. Resting on principle this way, a bodhisattva's own confidence in genuine truth remains unshaken, immune to being pulled away by any other influence.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩如實了知闇說、大說；如實知已，以理為依，不由耆長、眾所知識補特伽羅、若佛、若僧所說法故即便信受，是故不依補特伽羅。如是菩薩以理為依，補特伽羅非所依故，於真實義心不動搖，於正法中他緣匪奪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly recognize provisional teaching and complete teaching for what each is; having recognized this correctly, they rely on principle, not believing and accepting something as true merely because an elder, a well-known figure, or the Buddha or the community are said to have taught it — and for this reason they do not rely on the person. In this way, bodhisattvas rely on principle rather than on the person, and their mind remains unshaken regarding genuine truth, never pulled away by some other influence within the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "provisional teaching" and "complete teaching" through two readings preserved in the tradition: read against the two vehicles, provisional teaching names what the Buddha taught to śrāvakas eager only for their own swift release from birth and death — the truth of non-self alone, without yet opening the vaster teaching of emptiness in phenomena — while complete teaching names the great vehicle's own full, unreserved disclosure of every dimension of the path. Read according to Master Kuiji's alternative account, provisional teaching instead names the mistaken and deceptive claims of non-Buddhist teachers generally, while complete teaching names correct, wholesome teaching of every kind found within the Buddhist tradition itself.

He treats this reliance's real target as a specific vulnerability the treatise's own chapter on meditative equipoise names directly: certain flattering, deceptive teachers who rely on personal charisma or borrowed authority rather than genuine soundness of doctrine, counting on a listener's automatic deference to a respected-looking figure to secure belief without actual scrutiny. A bodhisattva relying on principle instead tests any teaching against its own coherence and its correspondence to complete versus provisional truth, refusing to accept a claim on the strength of the speaker's reputation alone — a discipline, he notes, that leaves a bodhisattva's grasp of the actual truth so firmly settled that no rival doctrine can subsequently dislodge it, precisely because that grasp was never borrowed from someone else's authority in the first place. This closes the second reliance; the treatise turns next to the third, relying on scriptures of complete and final meaning rather than those still provisional.

3. Milestone: Relying on Scriptures of Complete and Final Meaning, Never on Those Still Provisional — Why a Provisional Teaching Can Always, in Principle, Be Overturned

The third reliance: a bodhisattva whose faith in the Tathāgata has been deeply, purely planted relies exclusively on scriptures of complete and final meaning, never on those still provisional, so that nothing can shake what the Buddha has genuinely taught. The treatise explains exactly why this distinction matters — provisional scriptures explain the underlying nature of things through varying, situational approaches without yet reaching final settlement, still capable of provoking doubt, while a bodhisattva who fails to make this distinction remains vulnerable to having even genuine teaching later overturned by whatever provisional statement seems, on its own limited terms, to contradict it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於如來所深殖正信、深殖清淨，一向澄清；唯依如來了義經典，非不了義。了義經典為所依故，於佛所說法毗奈耶不可引奪。何以故？以佛所說不了義經，依種種門辯本性義，猶未決定，尚生疑惑，非了義故。若諸菩薩於了義經不決定者，於佛所說法毗奈耶猶可引奪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas who have deeply planted correct faith and deeply planted purity toward the Tathāgata, entirely and thoroughly clarified, rely exclusively on the Tathāgata's scriptures of complete and final meaning, never on those of provisional meaning. Because they rely on scriptures of complete and final meaning, nothing can pull them away from what the Buddha has taught in the dharma and the discipline. Why is this so? Because the provisional scriptures the Buddha spoke explain the underlying nature of things through varying approaches, not yet settled, still capable of provoking doubt, since they are not of complete and final meaning. Should bodhisattvas remain unsettled regarding scriptures of complete and final meaning, they remain vulnerable to being pulled away, even within what the Buddha has taught in the dharma and the discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "deeply planted purity, entirely and thoroughly clarified" as pointing to a bodhisattva who has already attained the four confirmed faiths toward the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and the precepts — a settled confidence, rooted in genuine spiritual attainment, that no future circumstance can uproot — before turning to why exactly a provisional teaching remains structurally vulnerable in a way a complete teaching is not: provisional scriptures explain reality's own nature through a variety of situational lenses — sometimes through the aggregates, sometimes through the sense-bases, sometimes through dependent origination or the four foundations of mindfulness — teaching existence at one moment and emptiness at another according to whichever framing best suits a given audience's own present capacity, and it is precisely this context-dependent variability that leaves such teaching, however genuinely useful in its own setting, still capable of generating doubt when compared against a different provisional statement elsewhere.

He treats the great vehicle's own complete teaching, by contrast, as resolving this instability at its root: the conventionally-labeled nature belonging to the aggregates, sense-bases, dependent origination, and the rest is genuinely without independent existence — neither falsely affirmed as real nor nihilistically denied altogether, since the non-conceptual nature underlying that conventional labeling, and the perfected nature it ultimately discloses, are genuinely present and real. Because this complete teaching commits to neither extreme of existence or nonexistence, it leaves nothing further to reconcile, and hence generates no genuine doubt at all — which is precisely why the treatise insists that a bodhisattva who has not clearly grasped this fourth distinction between provisional and complete meaning remains at risk of having even correctly received teaching subsequently unsettled by some seemingly contradictory provisional statement encountered later. This closes the third reliance; the treatise turns next to the fourth and final reliance — relying on wisdom actually realized through cultivation, never on the mere consciousness that learning and reflection alone can produce.

4. Milestone: Relying on Realized Wisdom, Never on Mere Learning — Closing the Four Reliances, the Four Standards They Establish, and Their Power to Guide Beings Fully Out of Affliction

The fourth reliance closes the set: a bodhisattva regards only wisdom genuinely realized through direct cultivation as true, not the understanding gained merely through learning and reflection, since what learning and reflection deliver still falls short of what cultivation alone can verify directly — and, having correctly recognized this, never disparages even the most profound teaching once actually heard. A closing summary distills all four reliances into four standards — meaning, principle, the genuine teacher, and realized wisdom — and confirms that a bodhisattva grounded in all four can guide beings, without any confusion whatsoever, along the six factors that lead genuinely out of affliction: loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity, signless absorption, and freedom from the conceit of self.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於真證智見為真實，非於聞思但識法義非真證智。是諸菩薩如實了知修所成智所應知者，非唯聞思所成諸識所能了達。如實知已，聞如來說最極甚深所有法義，終不誹毀。是名菩薩修正四依。依正四依善修習故，略顯四量。謂所說義、正理、大師、修所成慧真實證智。又諸菩薩一切四依為所依止，精勤發起正加行故，於出要道明了開示，無有迷惑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas regard wisdom genuinely realized through direct insight as true, not the mere understanding of the teaching's meaning gained only through learning and reflection, which is not genuinely realized wisdom. Such bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly recognize that what should be known through wisdom accomplished by cultivation is not something the consciousnesses accomplished only by learning and reflection are capable of fully comprehending. Having correctly recognized this, upon hearing the Tathāgata teach the most extremely profound dharma of every kind, they never disparage it. This is called a bodhisattva's correct cultivation of the four reliances. Because bodhisattvas skillfully cultivate the four correct reliances, four standards of measure are thereby, in brief, revealed: the meaning that is spoken, correct principle, the genuine teacher, and the true, realized wisdom accomplished by cultivation. Furthermore, bodhisattvas who take all four reliances as their basis, diligently arousing correct practice, are able to clearly explain the essential path leading out of affliction, entirely without confusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this final reliance to the treatise's own diagnosis, again drawn from the chapter on meditative equipoise, of a specific complacency it exists to correct: a listener who treats hearing the teaching as itself the finish line, content with intellectual comprehension alone and never pressing on into direct cultivation — like the poor man in the classical parable who spends all day counting another's treasure without ever gaining a coin of it for himself, comprehending the dharma's words without ever converting that comprehension into the wisdom that actually liberates. Only wisdom born of genuine cultivation, transforming ordinary consciousness into realized understanding, counts here as truly reliable; the treatise's own instruction not to disparage even the most profound teaching guards specifically against the temptation to dismiss as incomprehensible whatever cultivation has not yet caught up to explaining.

He treats the four resulting standards — meaning over phrasing, principle over person, the genuine teacher's complete teaching over provisional statement, and realized wisdom over mere learned consciousness — as together forming a single coherent method of measuring any teaching's own trustworthiness, rather than four unrelated rules. He closes by unpacking the six factors leading out of affliction that a bodhisattva grounded in these four reliances can then teach without confusion: loving-kindness overcoming hostility, compassion overcoming the impulse to harm, sympathetic joy overcoming envious displeasure at others' good fortune, equanimity overcoming both craving and aversion, signless absorption overcoming attachment to fixed characteristics, and freedom from the conceit of self overcoming the underlying view of a fixed personal identity that generates doubt about liberation itself — each addressed, he notes, by cultivating its own specific antidote in turn: loving-kindness for anger, compassion for the wish to harm, sympathetic joy for jealousy, equanimity for craving and aversion together, signless absorption for grasping at characteristics, and the view of no-self for self-conceit. This closes the sixth of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, the four correct reliances, in full; the treatise turns next to the seventh topic, the four unhindered understandings.

5. Milestone: The Four Unhindered Understandings — Teaching, Meaning, Language, and Eloquence, Each Resting on Both the Comprehensive Detail and the Essential Nature of Every Phenomenon

The seventh topic, the four unhindered understandings, names four distinct capacities, each grounded in the identical undefiled, unregressing wisdom accomplished through cultivation, and each spanning both the exhaustive particulars and the underlying essential nature of every phenomenon: unhindered understanding of the teaching itself, mastering every doctrinal approach the Buddha has taught across every audience; unhindered understanding of meaning, penetrating the actual significance beneath any given wording; unhindered understanding of language, commanding the vocabulary and idiom needed to explain any teaching fluently; and unhindered eloquence, delivering all of this to others without the slightest obstruction or hesitation.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩所修菩薩四無礙解？謂諸菩薩，於一切法一切異門，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩法無礙解。又諸菩薩，於一切法一切異相，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩義無礙解。又諸菩薩，於一切法一切釋辭，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩辭無礙解。又諸菩薩，於一切法一切品別，盡所有性、如所有性，依修所成無所滯礙無退轉智，是名菩薩辯無礙解。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the four unhindered understandings bodhisattvas cultivate? Bodhisattvas who, regarding every teaching-approach to every phenomenon, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature, relying on an unhindered, unregressing wisdom accomplished through cultivation — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered understanding of the teaching. Bodhisattvas who, regarding every distinct aspect of every phenomenon, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature through this same wisdom — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered understanding of meaning. Bodhisattvas who, regarding every mode of explanation for every phenomenon, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature through this same wisdom — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered understanding of language. Bodhisattvas who, regarding every category and distinction among phenomena, comprehend both its exhaustive particulars and its essential nature through this same wisdom — this is called a bodhisattva's unhindered eloquence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "exhaustive particulars" and "essential nature," the same technical pair introduced earlier in this chapter's account of skill in worldly knowledge, through the treatise's own later chapter of determinations: exhaustive particulars names the full range of specific, differentiated instances within any given category of phenomena, defiled or pure — the five aggregates, the six inner and outer sense-bases, and every classification the Āgamas apply to form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness across past, future, present, coarse, subtle, superior, inferior, distant, and near; essential nature names the shared, underlying suchness common to all of them, the fact that everything within that same category is equally impermanent, suffering, empty, and without fixed self. The two together give a genuinely complete grasp of any phenomenon — exhaustive particulars supplying the detailed factual picture, essential nature supplying the underlying principle those particulars all instantiate.

He treats "the teaching" as covering the Buddha's own many doctrinal approaches taught to different audiences according to their capacity — sometimes the śrāvaka path, sometimes the solitary-realizer's path, sometimes Madhyamaka, sometimes Yogācāra, sometimes tathāgatagarbha teaching — with unhindered understanding of the teaching meaning genuine mastery across every one of these approaches rather than fluency in only one; he cites the late Elder Miaojing's own observation that a classical commentator's capacity to organize and subdivide an entire scripture into its proper sections and topics is itself concrete proof of unhindered understanding of meaning, since correctly dividing a text into its constituent parts requires first genuinely grasping what each part actually means. Language, he notes, further extends this into command of vocabulary and regional idiom sufficient to explain any teaching fluently across different audiences and dialects, while eloquence gathers everything the first three have established into an actual capacity to deliver it aloud, without hesitation or obstruction, to whoever is listening. He notes the cross-reference the treatise makes to the ninth ground's own account of this fourfold mastery, since it is specifically at that advanced stage that a bodhisattva correctly understands, without exception, every possible variety of defiled and purified states, their causes, and their objects across the entire range of a teacher's own activity. This closes the four unhindered understandings' own intrinsic natures; the treatise turns next to their concrete benefit — the five kinds of skillful mastery they make possible, and the awakening they alone can bring to genuine completion.

6. Milestone: The Four Unhindered Understandings' Benefit — Five Kinds of Skillful Mastery, and the Awakening That Nothing Beyond Them Can Produce

Grounded in the four unhindered understandings, a bodhisattva obtains five kinds of immeasurable, supreme skillful mastery: mastery of the aggregates, correctly seeing that no fixed self can be found apart from the five aggregates' own combination; mastery of the elements, correctly discerning the distinct seeds and functions of the six sense faculties, their objects, and consciousness; mastery of the sense-bases, correctly discerning the three conditions by which perception actually arises; mastery of dependent origination, correctly discerning the twelvefold chain's own impermanent, painful, and selfless nature; and mastery of the possible and impossible, correctly discerning which causes genuinely produce which effects and which do not. The treatise then declares that nothing beyond these four unhindered understandings can bring a bodhisattva to genuine, complete awakening, nor equip them to teach others without distortion.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩依是菩薩四無礙解，應知獲得無量最勝五處善巧。一、蘊善巧，二、界善巧，三、處善巧，四、緣起善巧，五、處非處善巧。菩薩由此四種行相，於一切法自能妙善現正等覺，亦善為他無倒開示。此上無有自能妙善現正等覺，況善為他無倒開示。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who rely on these four unhindered understandings should be understood to obtain immeasurable, supreme skillful mastery in five domains: first, mastery of the aggregates; second, mastery of the elements; third, mastery of the sense-bases; fourth, mastery of dependent origination; fifth, mastery of the possible and the impossible. Through these four modes of understanding, bodhisattvas are themselves able, regarding every phenomenon, to wondrously and skillfully manifest complete, correct awakening, and are also able to skillfully explain it to others without any distortion. Beyond this, there is nothing capable of enabling a bodhisattva to wondrously and skillfully manifest complete, correct awakening themselves — let alone skillfully explain it to others without distortion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all five kinds of skillful mastery, cross-referencing the treatise's own Śrāvaka Stage and later chapters of determination for their full technical definitions: mastery of the aggregates rests on the meaning of "accumulation" itself, correctly seeing that form, sensation, perception, formation, and consciousness are only ever found as mere aggregated phenomena, never as a separate, permanent self standing apart from or within them; mastery of the elements rests on the meaning of "cause," "seed," "inherent nature," "lineage," "subtlety," and "sustaining," correctly discerning the distinct seeds and distinct natures underlying the eighteen elements of the six sense faculties, their objects, and their resulting consciousnesses; mastery of the sense-bases rests on the meaning of "the gateway through which mind and its associates arise," correctly discerning the three conditions — the dominant condition, the object condition, and the immediately preceding condition — that actually govern how the six inner and six outer sense-bases give rise to perception; mastery of dependent origination rests on eight distinct aspects of causal meaning — freedom from a fixed being, impermanence, momentary duration, dependence on other factors, freedom from any independent agent, unbroken causal continuity, causal resemblance across the chain, and being one's own karmic product — correctly discerning through all eight the wisdom of impermanence, of suffering, or of non-self within the twelvefold chain; and mastery of the possible and impossible rests on distinguishing where a given cause and effect genuinely cohere without contradiction from where they do not, correctly discerning the causal principle of equity that never simply happens by chance.

He treats the treatise's own emphatic closing statement — that nothing beyond these four unhindered understandings can bring a bodhisattva to genuine awakening or equip them to teach others without distortion — as the strongest possible claim this topic makes for its own necessity: the Cheng Weishi Lun's own account holds that a bodhisattva above the first ground possesses only a partial measure of these four, the ninth ground's own bodhisattva possesses them in full completion, and only buddhahood itself brings them to their absolute, perfected extent — meaning that even a bodhisattva who has not yet fully awakened to the ultimate truth genuinely cannot teach others without some risk of distortion, since teaching correctly presupposes having first understood correctly, and there is no shortcut around that sequence. This closes the seventh of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics, the four unhindered understandings, in full; the treatise turns next to the eighth topic, the provisions for awakening.

7. Milestone: The Provisions for Awakening Open — Merit and Wisdom, Ripening in Three Tiers Across Three Incalculable Eons

The eighth topic, the provisions for awakening, names two kinds in brief: the provision of merit and the provision of wisdom, cross-referenced to the treatise's own earlier chapter on benefiting self and others, where merit is identified with the three perfections of generosity, discipline, and patience, wisdom with the single perfection of wisdom itself, and diligence and meditative concentration understood as belonging to either category depending on which a given bodhisattva emphasizes. The treatise then ranks both provisions across three tiers matched to the three incalculable great eons of a bodhisattva's own path: what is cultivated during the first such eon counts as the lesser tier, the second as the middling tier, and the third as the supreme tier.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩菩提資糧？當知如是菩提資糧略有二種。一者、福德資糧，二者、智慧資糧。此二資糧廣分別義，如前所說自他利品，應知其相。又此福德、智慧資糧，菩薩於初無數大劫所修習者，應知名下；若於第二無數大劫所修習者，應知名中；若於第三無數大劫所修習者，應知名上。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are a bodhisattva's provisions for awakening? One should understand that these provisions for awakening are, in brief, of two kinds: first, the provision of merit; second, the provision of wisdom. The full, detailed meaning of these two provisions is as already explained in the earlier chapter on benefiting self and others, and their characteristics should be understood accordingly. Furthermore, regarding this provision of merit and provision of wisdom, whatever bodhisattvas cultivate during the first incalculable great eon should be understood as the lesser tier; whatever is cultivated during the second incalculable great eon should be understood as the middling tier; and whatever is cultivated during the third incalculable great eon should be understood as the supreme tier.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own earlier chapter for the precise division of the two provisions among the six perfections: merit is, in brief, the three perfections of generosity, discipline, and patience together, while wisdom is the single perfection of wisdom alone — and diligence and meditative concentration are said to belong to whichever category a given bodhisattva's own practice happens to emphasize, since sustained effort and settled concentration can each be directed either toward accumulating merit, as when diligence supports generosity or meditation supports the four immeasurables, or toward accumulating wisdom, as when diligence and meditation instead support investigation into dependent origination, the four noble truths, or the emptiness of phenomena. Rather than assigning these two perfections a fixed, single category, the treatise treats them as genuinely dual in character, following the actual direction a bodhisattva's own cultivation takes.

He treats the threefold ranking across three incalculable great eons as this topic's own concrete measure of a bodhisattva's own developmental scale: the same two provisions, merit and wisdom, are cultivated throughout the entire length of the path, yet what counts as an accomplished level of practice during the very first of the three incalculable eons is only the beginning relative to what the same provisions look like once matured across the second eon, and lesser again compared to their supreme culmination across the third — a bodhisattva's own progress measured not by achieving some entirely new quality partway through the path, but by the same two provisions of merit and wisdom deepening continuously across an almost unimaginable span of cultivation. This closes the eighth of the seventeenth chapter's fifteen topics' opening statement; the treatise turns next to the ninth topic, the thirty-seven classical factors of awakening themselves, now examined through a distinctly great-vehicle lens.
