Fascicle 91 · 瑜伽師地論卷第九十一 · Freedom from Desire and the Rest Begins

Freedom from Desire and the Rest

Fascicle 91 opens 處擇攝's fourth and final particular verse, freedom from desire and the rest (離欲等攝) — following directly on from fascicle 90's completion of the first three (establishing, wisdom, and sameness and equality). Its own six-item summary verse names: freedom from desire and its absence, questioning, causes and conditions, the path of defilement, preserving life, and attachment to place. This fascicle begins with the difference between a practitioner still bound by the three entanglements of self-grasping, self-possession, and conceit and one genuinely free of them, then turns to the four kinds of rightful questioner within the Buddha's teaching — the person of pure faith, the learned monk, the Buddha's own attendant, and the Buddha himself.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 91 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Sense-Bases Section's Fourth Particular Verse Begins — the Summary Verse

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the sense-bases section, the second, part three. Next, a summary verse says: freedom from desire and its absence; questioning; causes and conditions; the path of defilement; preserving life; and attachment to place — all should be known through broad explanation.

Chinese Source

攝事分中契經事處擇攝第二之三

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the sense-bases section, the second, part three.

Chinese Source

復次，嗢柁南曰：離欲未離欲　問因緣染路　保命著處等　皆廣說應知

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, a summary verse says: freedom from desire and its absence; questioning; causes and conditions; the path of defilement; preserving life; and attachment to place — all should be known through broad explanation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Freedom from Desire and the Rest (離欲等攝), the fourth of 處擇攝's four particular verses under its first overall summary verse. Fascicle 89 covered the first, Establishing (安立攝); fascicle 90 covered the second and third, Wisdom (智攝) and Sameness and Equality (同等攝), both now closed entirely. This fascicle opens the fourth and final one, naming six topics to be explained in turn: freedom from desire and its absence, questioning, causes and conditions, the path of defilement, preserving life, and attachment to place.

2. Section: Freedom from Desire and Its Absence Begins — the Defiled Case, Generally Stated

If there is a monk who, with respect to the desire realm, is either already free of desire or not yet free of desire — with respect to the five subtle sense-desires, or with respect to dharmas known by mind at the concentration-ground level, across the three times — is, through the three entanglements and the root latent tendencies belonging to them, rightly undergoing defilement, then, in the present life, such a one is not capable of advancing toward the attestation of ultimate nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

若有苾芻，於其欲界或已離欲、或未離欲，於五妙欲、意所識法定地三世，由三種纏及彼根本所有隨眠正雜染時，於現法中不任趣證究竟涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is a monk who, with respect to the desire realm, is either already free of desire or not yet free of desire — with respect to the five subtle sense-desires, or with respect to dharmas known by mind at the concentration-ground level, across the three times — is, through the three entanglements and the root latent tendencies belonging to them, rightly undergoing defilement, then, in the present life, such a one is not capable of advancing toward the attestation of ultimate nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of the Matters, fascicle 85

This opens freedom from desire and its absence (離欲未離欲), the first of the six topics — one not yet free of desire takes the five sense-desires as object; one already free of desire, resting in concentration, takes mind-known dharmas as object instead. Across all three times, both kinds of practitioner may still cling to self, to self's possession, or to conceit toward whatever object arises — the three entanglements — with their seeds lying dormant as root latent tendencies. So long as this defilement remains, ultimate nirvāṇa cannot be attested in this very life.

3. Section: Matching the Explanation, Closing the Defiled Case Entirely

One should know that here, through the past, one relies upon that grasping-consciousness; through the future, one belongs to that grasping-consciousness; through the present, one is attached to that grasping-consciousness. Because the root latent tendencies belonging to them fall within the mind's continuum, constantly following along, one grasps at that grasping-consciousness.

Chinese Source

當知此中，由過去世依彼取識，由未來世屬彼取識，由現在世著彼取識。由彼根本所有隨眠墮在相續，常隨逐故，執彼取識。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that here, through the past, one relies upon that grasping-consciousness; through the future, one belongs to that grasping-consciousness; through the present, one is attached to that grasping-consciousness. Because the root latent tendencies belonging to them fall within the mind's continuum, constantly following along, one grasps at that grasping-consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever object is met, across all three times, both kinds of practitioner still lacking full purification think and act from a center of self, self's possession, and conceit — this is called grasping-consciousness, and its root latent tendencies, unbroken, follow the practitioner constantly through the five-aggregate continuum. With this, matching the explanation (配釋) closes entirely, closing bringing forth the defiled case (舉有雜染) entirely.

4. Section: The Undefiled Case by Contrast, Closing "Freedom from Desire and Its Absence" Entirely

Contrary to this, when free of defilement, one is, in the present life, capable of advancing toward the attestation of ultimate nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

與此相違無雜染時，於現法中堪能趣證究竟涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contrary to this, when free of defilement, one is, in the present life, capable of advancing toward the attestation of ultimate nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing three companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras (132-134)

Once self-grasping, self-possession, and conceit are cut off, no attachment, no contact, no grasping remains, and one is fully capable of attesting to ultimate nirvāṇa in this very life. The companion sūtras have the householder Ugra ask the Buddha why some monks see reality and attain nirvāṇa while others do not: those whose consciousness, at contact with any of the six sense-objects, still gives rise to craving, recollection, and attachment remain bound and cannot see the Dharma and attain nirvāṇa in this life, whether or not they have attained concentration; those free of such craving, recollection, and attachment, well guarding the sense-doors, are able to see reality and may attain nirvāṇa even in this very life — the same teaching repeats for Ānanda's own question and for the Buddha's self-posed question to the monks. With this, the undefiled case (例無雜染) closes entirely, closing freedom from desire and its absence (離欲未離欲), the first of the six topics under freedom from desire and the rest, entirely.

5. Section: Questioning Begins — Four Kinds of Rightful Questioner

Next, within the noble teaching, one should know there are four kinds of one who rightly asks a question. First, one of pure faith — whether a householder elder or an elder's child; second, a monk of keen intelligence and broad learning; third, one who personally attends upon and serves the great Teacher; fourth, the great Teacher himself.

Chinese Source

復次，於聖教中，當知有四如理問者。一、有淨信，若諸長者、若長者子；二、具聰慧多聞苾芻；三、是大師親承侍者；四、即大師。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, within the noble teaching, one should know there are four kinds of one who rightly asks a question. First, one of pure faith — whether a householder elder or an elder's child; second, a monk of keen intelligence and broad learning; third, one who personally attends upon and serves the great Teacher; fourth, the great Teacher himself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Questioning (問), the second of the six topics — four kinds of rightful questioner in the Buddha's teaching, the fourth being the Buddha's own self-posed question, explained next.

6. Section: Explaining the Last in Turn — Two Causes for the Buddha's Self-Questioning

There are two causes and conditions by which the Buddha, knowingly, poses a question to his disciples. That is: observing that, although a disciple wishes to ask, he lacks the fearlessness to do so; or observing that, with respect to its meaning, he has no understanding of it at all.

Chinese Source

有二因緣，佛於弟子知而故問。謂觀弟子雖欲請問，而無無畏；或於其義無所了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are two causes and conditions by which the Buddha, knowingly, poses a question to his disciples. That is: observing that, although a disciple wishes to ask, he lacks the fearlessness to do so; or observing that, with respect to its meaning, he has no understanding of it at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining the last of the four in turn (隨釋後一) — the Buddha, all-knowing, asks and answers his own question either because a hesitant disciple fears the question is unfit to ask, or because the disciple cannot yet grasp the matter well enough to frame a question at all.

7. Section: Explaining the Purpose, Closing "Questioning" Entirely

It is in order to forestall faults in the present and the future, and in order to enable the true teaching to abide long in the world.

Chinese Source

為遮現在、未來過故，為令正法得久住故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is in order to forestall faults in the present and the future, and in order to enable the true teaching to abide long in the world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing three companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras (132-134)

A doubt left unspoken becomes a loss for the one who fails to ask; and unless the deeper meaning is drawn out through question and answer, later generations — of duller faculties than those who heard the Buddha directly — will struggle to receive the true teaching purely, so the Buddha's own questioning serves to keep it alive for them. The companion sūtras have the householder Ugra, then Ānanda, then the Buddha himself ask the identical question of why some monks attain nirvāṇa and others do not, each answered with the same teaching on craving, recollection, and attachment at contact with the six sense-objects. With this, explaining the purpose (釋其所為) closes entirely, closing explaining the last of the four in turn (隨釋後一) entirely, and closing Questioning (問), the second of the six topics, entirely.

8. Section: Causes and Conditions Begin — Generally Stated

Next, by two causes and conditions, it is said that the six consciousness-bodies take the six internal bases as their cause and the six external bases as their condition.

Chinese Source

復次，由二因緣，說六識身以內六處為因，以外六處為緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, by two causes and conditions, it is said that the six consciousness-bodies take the six internal bases as their cause and the six external bases as their condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Causes and Conditions (因緣), the third of the six topics — "body" carries the sense of accumulation, since eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness are each moment by moment discriminating and accumulating. The six consciousnesses arise where the six internal bases, as cause, meet the six external bases, as their object-condition.

9. Section: The Internal Six Bases as Cause

That is: the six internal bases are what the seeds belonging to them rely upon and adhere to. Further, the six internal bases, continuing as a single kind, turn without exception exactly as previously attained.

Chinese Source

謂內六處，為彼種子所依附故；又內六處，相續一類，如先所得畢竟轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is: the six internal bases are what the seeds belonging to them rely upon and adhere to. Further, the six internal bases, continuing as a single kind, turn without exception exactly as previously attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Decisions on the Five Consciousness-Groups and the Mind-Ground, fascicle 51

Speaking here in terms proper to the Śrāvaka vehicle, before the doctrine of the store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) was formally established: the sense faculties, their physical bases, and consciousness together are said to hold every dharma's seeds, hence the six internal bases are what those seeds rely upon. Further, the six faculties remain the same single kind of thing continuously from birth to death — the eye is always the eye, never becoming the ear, its capacity to see form persisting in a like continuity throughout — hence "turning exactly as previously attained."

10. Section: The External Six Bases as Condition, Closing "Causes and Conditions" Entirely

Objects are not so — they are not what the seeds belonging to them rely upon and adhere to, nor do they turn continuing as a single kind.

Chinese Source

境界不爾，非彼種子所依附故，又非一類相續轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Objects are not so — they are not what the seeds belonging to them rely upon and adhere to, nor do they turn continuing as a single kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra

Form, sound, and the rest of the six external objects, unlike the faculties, are constantly changing and hold no afflictive seeds of their own, nor persist as one unchanging kind — hence they serve only as condition, not cause. The companion sūtra has a monk ask the Buddha directly what gives rise to each of the six consciousnesses, and the Buddha answer with the identical formula six times over: eye-consciousness arises in dependence on the eye faculty and its condition, form; and so likewise for ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. With this, the external six bases as condition (外六處為緣) closes entirely, closing Causes and Conditions (因緣), the third of the six topics under Freedom from Desire and the Rest, entirely.

11. Section: The Path of Defilement Begins — Briefly Named

Next, by two marks, one should fully know every defilement in general. First, the own-nature of every defilement; second, the path of activity of every defilement.

Chinese Source

復次，由二種相，當總了知一切雜染。一者、一切雜染自性，二者、一切雜染行路。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, by two marks, one should fully know every defilement in general. First, the own-nature of every defilement; second, the path of activity of every defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the Path of Defilement (染路), the fourth of the six topics — its own-nature is its root, its path of activity is where its activity actually occurs.

12. Section: Own-Nature Explained

As for own-nature: it is precisely craving for desire, which serves as the root for every defilement.

Chinese Source

言自性者，所謂欲貪，與諸雜染為根本故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for own-nature: it is precisely craving for desire, which serves as the root for every defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Basic Section's Level of Both Investigation and Analysis

Elsewhere the treatise names ignorance as the root of every defilement across the three realms; here the teaching approaches the same point from a different angle, naming craving for desire as the root instead.

13. Section: The Path of Activity Explained, Closing "The Path of Defilement" Entirely

As for the path of activity: it is precisely the internal and external bases, distinguished as what grasps and what is grasped.

Chinese Source

言行路者，謂內外處；能取所取有差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the path of activity: it is precisely the internal and external bases, distinguished as what grasps and what is grasped.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Decisions on the Bodhisattva Level, fascicle 77, and two companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras

What grasps is the five internal pure form faculties together with mind, mentation, consciousness, and every mental factor — the eight consciousness-kings and their companions in Mahāyāna terms; what is grasped is the six external objects of form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and dharmas — the internal and external bases are where affliction most readily takes hold. The companion sūtras have the Buddha teach unprompted that the "dharmas bound by fetters" are the six internal and six external bases, while the "fettering dharma" itself is craving for desire — and, in a second sūtra, that the "dharmas grasped" are these same twelve bases, while the "grasping" itself is again craving for desire. With this, the path of activity (行路) closes entirely, closing the Path of Defilement (染路), the fourth of the six topics, entirely.

14. Section: Preserving Life Begins — Generally Stated

Next, if monks, with respect to two places, observe equally, or in accordance with either — whether walking or abiding still — taking proper attention as their support, they should free their mind from two kinds of defilement.

Chinese Source

復次，若諸苾芻，於二處所，等隨觀察，若行、若住，如理作意為所依止，於二雜染應脫其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, if monks, with respect to two places, observe equally, or in accordance with either — whether walking or abiding still — taking proper attention as their support, they should free their mind from two kinds of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Preserving Life (保命), the fifth of the six topics. The two places are self-preservation of life and heading toward an evil destiny — the first a cause (sustaining this present life, whether toward an uncontaminated or a defiled end), the second its fruit (the next life, avoided by wholesome cause or reached by unwholesome), the two bound in an unbroken chain of cause and effect carrying the mind's continuum forward moment by moment.

15. Section: The Two Places Named

What is called the two places? That is: self-preservation of life, suddenly cut short by untimely death; and an unwholesome mind's demise, heading toward the evil destinies.

Chinese Source

云何名為於二處所？謂自保命，忽然夭喪；不善心殞，往諸惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the two places? That is: self-preservation of life, suddenly cut short by untimely death; and an unwholesome mind's demise, heading toward the evil destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Even while carefully sustaining this life through proper nourishment, an untimely death may strike without warning; guarding the mind matters all the more, since dying with an unwholesome mind leads to a fall into the evil destinies, from which return to a human rebirth able to practice takes a very long time.

16. Section: Proper Attention and the Rest — Two Questions Posed

What is called taking proper attention as one's support? And with respect to which two kinds of defilement should one free one's mind?

Chinese Source

云何名為如理作意為所依止？復於何等二種雜染應脫其心？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called taking proper attention as one's support? And with respect to which two kinds of defilement should one free one's mind?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the general question (總徵), posing the two matters that the following explanation will answer in turn.

17. Section: Never Heading Toward the Evil Destinies

That is: I would rather endure every manner of beating and injury to this body across its every base than let an unwholesome mind's demise cause me to be born in the evil destinies.

Chinese Source

謂我寧遭種種楚撻損害於己諸處之身，勿復令我不善心殞，生諸惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is: I would rather endure every manner of beating and injury to this body across its every base than let an unwholesome mind's demise cause me to be born in the evil destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens taking proper attention as one's support (如理作意為所依止) — the practitioner's own resolve: better bodily suffering now than an unwholesome mind at the moment of death.

18. Section: Contemplating Impermanence, Closing "Proper Attention" Entirely

Further, I should, together with joy and happiness, truly observe — in order to counter present unwholesome states, earnestly and diligently cultivating the contemplation that all formations are impermanent.

Chinese Source

又我應當與喜樂俱如實觀察，為欲對治現行不善，懇勵修習諸行無常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, I should, together with joy and happiness, truly observe — in order to counter present unwholesome states, earnestly and diligently cultivating the contemplation that all formations are impermanent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Unlike the melancholy an ordinary poet feels before impermanence, this contemplation is joined with genuine joy, seeing impermanence exactly as it is — since no one knows whether the next breath or the next life will come first, the moment any unwholesome thought is noticed calls for immediate, earnest effort to counter it, ceaselessly reinforcing the mindfulness of impermanence. With this, proper attention as one's support (如理作意為所依止) closes entirely.

19. Section: Freeing the Mind from Two Defilements Begins — While Walking

If, while walking in meditation, with respect to objects, one grasps at their marks or grasps at their particulars — every such defilement should be freed from the mind.

Chinese Source

若經行時，於諸境界執取諸相、執取隨好所有雜染令心解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, while walking in meditation, with respect to objects, one grasps at their marks or grasps at their particulars — every such defilement should be freed from the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens freeing the mind from two defilements (於二雜染應解脫其心), beginning with the time of walking — encountering all manner of sights, sounds, and scents along the way, one should take neither their general mark nor their particular details, since an untimely death amid such grasping risks a fall into the evil destinies.

20. Section: While Abiding Still, Closing "Distinguishing Severally" Entirely

While abiding in seclusion, with respect to every kind of unwholesome discursive thought — every such defilement should be freed from the mind.

Chinese Source

遠離住時，於諸不善種種尋思所有雜染令心解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

While abiding in seclusion, with respect to every kind of unwholesome discursive thought — every such defilement should be freed from the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eight kinds of unwholesome discursive thought — of desire, ill will, harm, homeland, kinfolk, immortality, family status, and contempt — should likewise be released the moment they arise, denied any continuity, since defilement without continuity has no power to disturb. With this, distinguishing severally (別辨) — the times of walking and of abiding — closes entirely.

21. Section: Weighing Further, Closing "Discerning the Times" Entirely

One should know that here, the first defilement is a resembling cause, and the second defilement is a resembling fruit.

Chinese Source

當知此中，第一雜染是相似因，第二雜染是相似果。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that here, the first defilement is a resembling cause, and the second defilement is a resembling fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

Grasping at marks and particulars while walking is a resembling cause; the same kind of unwholesome discursive thought recurring while seated afterward is its resembling fruit — not identical, but continuous with it, the way a quarrel's agitation replays itself the moment one sits to meditate. Because the mind's formations are always continuous, cause and effect can be distinguished even within a single continuing kind. With this, weighing further (料簡) closes entirely, closing discerning the times (辨時位) entirely.

22. Section: Clarifying Where One Falls Begins — Arising Through Defilement

Further, when these two defilements are currently active, two places result. That is: self-preservation of life, at that very time suddenly returning to untimely death; and an unwholesome mind's demise, heading toward the evil destinies.

Chinese Source

又二雜染現在轉時，生於二處。謂自保命，即於爾時倏歸夭喪；不善心殞，往諸惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, when these two defilements are currently active, two places result. That is: self-preservation of life, at that very time suddenly returning to untimely death; and an unwholesome mind's demise, heading toward the evil destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens clarifying where one falls (明墮處) — a sudden surge of aversion, for instance, can itself trigger a fatal collapse; either way, affliction's manifestation is what brings about an unwholesome death and a fall into the evil destinies.

23. Section: Concluding That One Should Be Freed, Closing "Preserving Life" Entirely

For this reason, with respect to these two defilements, even to see their danger deeply and give rise to shame for a single moment is already excellent — how much more so if this can be sustained continuously.

Chinese Source

是故於彼二種雜染，一剎那中深見過患、發生慚愧，尚為妙善，況能相續。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, with respect to these two defilements, even to see their danger deeply and give rise to shame for a single moment is already excellent — how much more so if this can be sustained continuously.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra cycle (roughly sūtras 139-179)

A single moment's clear-eyed shame at affliction's danger is already a great good; sustaining that vigilance is better still — one's own store of merit and wisdom, built through proper attention, is the truest refuge against an unwholesome death. The companion sūtras open with the Buddha's own vivid vow: better to sear the eyes with a burning copper plate, pierce the ears with a red-hot awl, sever the nose or tongue with a blade, or pierce the body with a spear, than to grasp at any sense-object's mark or particulars and sink like an iron ball into the evil destinies — better even to sleep one's life away in dull ignorance than to let feeling toward any such object take root and breed quarrels and disputes; the noble disciple instead resolves to see all six faculties, objects, and their feelings as impermanent, illusion-like, dependently arisen, thereby growing weary, unattached, and liberated. A long cycle of companion sūtras follows — twenty-four sūtras on not knowing, not recognizing, not cutting off, and not being free of desire toward each of the six faculties preventing the end of suffering; seven sūtras on Māra's "hook" — craving, delight, praise, attachment, steadfastness, joy, and resentment toward each faculty binding one like a fish hooked through the throat; and a "four-part dharma discourse" distinguishing the monk who reacts with craving or aversion to delightful and undelightful objects (still bound by Māra) from the monk who neither clings nor recoils (free of Māra's hold). With this, concluding that one should be freed (結應脫) closes entirely, closing freeing the mind from two defilements (於二雜染應解脫其心) entirely, and closing Preserving Life (保命), the fifth of the six topics under Freedom from Desire and the Rest, entirely.

24. Section: Attachment to Place and the Rest Begins — Where Defilement Takes Root

There are, further, many demonic forces converging upon every resting-place where defilement takes root — the wise, recognizing this, should keep well away. That is: ordinary beings already free of desire cling, as their resting-place, to the savor of the joy and pleasure born of concentration in the places bound up with it; those not yet free of desire, taking as their support the feeling from the five subtle sense-desires, cling, as their resting-place, to the delight, contention, craving, and defiled attachment that follow; toward beings who have shown kindness or enmity, craving and aversion alike become a resting-place; and vast, superior-grade affliction, able to draw beings toward pleasant or painful objects, becomes a resting-place for what is pursued, dwelt upon, and craved across all three times.

Chinese Source

復有眾多魔所歸向所有雜染著安足處，智者了知應當遠避。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, many demonic forces converging upon every resting-place where defilement takes root — the wise, recognizing this, should keep well away.

Chinese Source

That is: ordinary beings already free of desire cling, as their resting-place, to the savor of the joy and pleasure born of concentration in the places bound up with it; those not yet free of desire, taking as their support the feeling from the five subtle sense-desires, cling, as their resting-place, to the delight, contention, craving, and defiled attachment that follow; toward beings who have shown kindness or enmity, craving and aversion alike become a resting-place; and vast, superior-grade affliction, able to draw beings toward pleasant or painful objects, becomes a resting-place for what is pursued, dwelt upon, and craved across all three times.

Concentration's own joy becomes a snare for the already-free; the five sense-desires' pleasure becomes a snare for the not-yet-free; kindness and enmity alike become snares of craving and aversion; and the full sweep of past, present, and future objects becomes a snare for the mind's pursuit, recollection, and longing.

Further, what is called suffering is precisely displeasure regarding an object. What is called affliction is precisely displeasure regarding feeling. What is called contrariness is displeasure regarding the past. What is called adversity is displeasure regarding the future.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (180)

Nirvāṇa lies entirely beyond the reach of both external and internal defilement, and so beyond Māra's reach as well. The companion sūtra tells of Māra disguising himself as a herdsman to accost the Buddha on his alms round, then, recognized, boasting that the six contact-bases are "his own cattle" — the very internal-feeling defilement just explained — and that since all things belong to him, the Buddha can go nowhere beyond his domain. The Buddha replies that the five aggregates hold no self at all, so Māra's claim of ownership fails at the root; Māra presses further, asking why the Buddha, if free, still teaches others the same path, and the Buddha answers that he teaches precisely so that others too may cross to nirvāṇa's far shore and win lasting freedom from Māra's hold. Māra, comparing himself to a crow pecking hopefully at a stone shaped like meat, only to break its beak and fly off in shame, departs defeated and dejected. With this, weighing further (料簡) closes entirely, closing external-object and internal-feeling defilement (外境內受雜染差別), the second of three matters under Attachment to Place and the Rest, entirely.

36. Section: Entering Non-Self Through Same-Portion Consciousness

What is entering non-self through same-portion consciousness? That is: by directly seeing that the body of the five form-possessing bases, composed of the four great elements, is impermanent by way of increase, decrease, grasping, or releasing, one enters into impermanence with respect to the consciousness taking it as condition. What is impermanent is suffering; what is suffering is without self. By this cause and condition, one accordingly enters into non-self.

Chinese Source

云何因同分識隨入無我？謂由現見五有色處四大種身，若增、若減、若取、若捨無常性故，於緣彼識隨入無常，無常則苦，苦則無我。由是因緣，隨入無我。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is entering non-self through same-portion consciousness? That is: by directly seeing that the body of the five form-possessing bases, composed of the four great elements, is impermanent by way of increase, decrease, grasping, or releasing, one enters into impermanence with respect to the consciousness taking it as condition. What is impermanent is suffering; what is suffering is without self. By this cause and condition, one accordingly enters into non-self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The five form faculties and their objects visibly grow stronger or weaker, are taken up or released — impermanence directly seen in the body carries over into the consciousness that takes it as its condition, which is likewise seen as impermanent; and since what is impermanent is suffering, and suffering, arising from conditions beyond one's mastery, is without any sovereign self, this impermanence-contemplation is itself the gateway into non-self.

38. Section: The First Mark — Cutting Self-View at Its Root

That is: every wrong view, without exception, takes self-view as its root — hence this root must first of all be cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Every one of the outsiders' sixty-two views traces back to this single root — to enter non-self, self-view itself must be the very first thing severed.

39. Section: The Second Mark — Countless Distinctions of Basis and Object

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by right wisdom, one observes that this very consciousness, in dependence on its basis and its object, turns through countless distinctions, and so has countless kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As the basis (the six faculties) and the object (the six kinds of sense-data) each vary endlessly, the mind that depends on them varies endlessly too — no single, fixed consciousness can be found underlying this constant turning.

40. Section: The Third Mark — Not Even a Moment's Abiding, Closing "The Cause of the Same Portion" Entirely

Further, observing this consciousness's differential turning, not even a firm abiding lasting the measure of a single moment can be found — how much less anything lasting forever.

Chinese Source

又觀此識差別轉時，如剎那量安住堅實尚不可得，何況畢竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, observing this consciousness's differential turning, not even a firm abiding lasting the measure of a single moment can be found — how much less anything lasting forever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (196, Ānanda and Mahāpuṇḍa)

Consciousness arises and perishes moment by moment, never pausing even for an instant — a third confirmation, alongside self-view's rootlessness and the countless variety of basis and object, that nothing self-like could ever be found within it. By these three marks, affliction is cut off and non-self entered at any time and place. The companion sūtra has Ānanda question Venerable Mahāpuṇḍa step by step: the eye and its object are impermanent, hence eye-consciousness born from them cannot abide unchanging even a moment, and no noble disciple, seeing this arising-and-perishing, would take it as self, as different from self, as self's possession, or as mutually contained with self — the same for each remaining faculty. Ānanda then likens this to a man felling a banana tree in search of solid timber, stripping it layer by layer only to find the trunk entirely hollow at the core — just as consciousness, examined this way, yields nothing graspable, and, grasping nothing, clings to nothing, and, clinging to nothing, awakens of itself to nirvāṇa. With this citation, the third mark closes entirely, closing rightly observing the various consciousnesses by three marks (由三種相正觀諸識) entirely, closing distinct explanation (別釋) entirely, and closing the Cause of the Same Portion (因同分), the first of the nine topics under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

While the six faculties exist, they are said to exist; once lost, they are said not to exist. Compared to another's faculties — or another species' — a difference is found; compared to one's own faculties over time, the same continuous function is found, hence "non-different." 45. Section: The Six Bases' Cessation, Where None of the Four Apply, Closing "The Four Modes" Entirely

The six bases' permanent cessation is a mark of constant quiescence — hence it is not reasonable to think about and observe it through these four modes bound up with proliferating discourse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, the six bases' cessation (六處滅無) closes entirely, closing the four modes (四行相) entirely.

Next, with respect to the internal and external bases, if craving for desire is present, whether the object is currently present or not present, and the faculties are unable to relinquish it, this is called bondage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Bondage and Liberation (縛解脫), the third of the nine topics — whether an object is actually present or merely recalled in discursive thought, unrelinquished craving for desire is itself the bondage.

50. Section: Liberation Explained, Closing "Bondage and Liberation" Entirely

If craving for desire is absent, then, even supposing an object is presently right before one, the faculties are still able to relinquish it — how much more so when it is not present. Hence this is called liberation.

Chinese Source

若無欲貪，設有境界正現在前，諸根於彼尚能棄捨，況不現前，故名解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

If craving for desire is absent, then, even supposing an object is presently right before one, the faculties are still able to relinquish it — how much more so when it is not present. Hence this is called liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (Mahākoṭṭhita and Śāriputra)

Bondage and liberation both turn entirely on the presence or absence of craving for desire in the mind, not on the object itself. The companion sūtra has Mahākoṭṭhita ask Śāriputra whether the eye binds form or form binds the eye, and so for each remaining faculty — Śāriputra answers that neither binds the other, illustrating with two oxen, one black and one white, yoked together by a single crossbar: it is neither ox that binds the other, but the yoke and harness between them. Just so, it is neither the faculty nor the object that binds, but the craving for desire between them — were it otherwise, the Buddha could never have taught the holy life leading to the very end of suffering. Whether the Buddha himself, seeing any form, good or bad, never gives rise to craving, while other beings do, shows plainly that the deciding factor is never the object encountered but always the mind that meets it — like passing through a field of flowers untouched by a single petal. With this citation, liberation (解脫) closes entirely, closing Bondage and Liberation (縛解脫), the third of the nine topics under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely.

51. Section: Marks Begin — Well Guarding the Faculties

Next, for one who has cultivated the holy life well, who has permanently cut off the view of self and self's possession with respect to the aggregates and bases, even if touched by painful feeling that harms the body or takes life itself, no change of complexion, no change of mind, can ever be found — this is called the coarse mark of well-guarding the faculties.

Chinese Source

復次，善修梵行，於諸蘊處我、我所見已永斷者，若為損身乃至奪命苦受所觸，終無色變、心變可得，如是名麤善守根相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, for one who has cultivated the holy life well, who has permanently cut off the view of self and self's possession with respect to the aggregates and bases, even if touched by painful feeling that harms the body or takes life itself, no change of complexion, no change of mind, can ever be found — this is called the coarse mark of well-guarding the faculties.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Marks (相), the fourth of the nine topics — one who has permanently cut off self-view meets even mortal pain without the slightest visible or inward disturbance.

52. Section: Attaining the Joy of Liberation Begins — Four Kinds Briefly Named

Through well-guarding the faculties in this way, by the empowering force of liberation from four sufferings, one attains four kinds of joy. First, because one is liberated from future externally conditioned suffering; second, because one is liberated from future internally conditioned suffering; third, because, at the time of present-life parinirvāṇa, one is liberated from every suffering produced by the two supports.

Chinese Source

彼由如是善守諸根，四苦解脫增上力故，得四種喜。一、由當來外緣生苦得解脫故；二、由當來內緣生苦得解脫故；三、於現法般涅槃時，由二種依所作眾苦得解脫故；

Ru-Yi Translation

Through well-guarding the faculties in this way, by the empowering force of liberation from four sufferings, one attains four kinds of joy. First, because one is liberated from future externally conditioned suffering; second, because one is liberated from future internally conditioned suffering; third, because, at the time of present-life parinirvāṇa, one is liberated from every suffering produced by the two supports.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

This opens attaining the joy of liberation (得解脫喜) — the "two supports" are body and mind, and the suffering produced by them is fully released at the remainder-bound nirvāṇa's attainment; the first two joys belong properly to the remainderless nirvāṇa, where no future support, internal or external, brings suffering at all. A fourth joy follows.

53. Section: The Fourth Joy — Resembling Grass and Trees

Fourth, once life has ended, one resembles the grass and trees commonly seen in the world, since no suffering continues in any series any longer.

Chinese Source

四、命終已，與世所見草木相似，一切眾苦不相續故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, once life has ended, one resembles the grass and trees commonly seen in the world, since no suffering continues in any series any longer.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

At the noble one's death, the body decays as unmoved as withered grass or fallen wood in the material world, the mind untouched, and upon entering the remainderless nirvāṇa every suffering ceases and continues no further.

54. Section: Explaining What Is Difficult — Two Marks of Resembling Grass and Trees

By two marks, one resembles grass and trees. First, the six bases, free of any notion of a sentient being, resemble the grass and trees commonly seen in the world. Second, the six bases, serving as the support upon which the fires of craving, aversion, and delusion are able to burn, resemble the grass and trees commonly seen in the world.

Chinese Source

由二種相，草木相似。一者、六處離有情想，與世所見草木相似。二者、六處為所依止，貪瞋癡火乃得燒然，與世所見草木相似。

Ru-Yi Translation

By two marks, one resembles grass and trees. First, the six bases, free of any notion of a sentient being, resemble the grass and trees commonly seen in the world. Second, the six bases, serving as the support upon which the fires of craving, aversion, and delusion are able to burn, resemble the grass and trees commonly seen in the world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The noble one regards the six faculties as no more a self or a sentient being than a stand of grass or trees, tended and fed only as any living thing needs tending; and just as a wildfire needs grass and wood to burn, the fires of craving, aversion, and delusion need the six faculties as their fuel — mind is a mere reflection, the body no more than grass or wood, nothing in either worth clinging to.

55. Section: A Further Distinction — the Noble Disciples' Portion

Noble disciples who have cultivated the holy life well, because suffering belonging to future existence does not arise for them, resemble in a small portion the power of knowledge the Tathāgatas have accomplished — though this does not mean that suffering conditioned by the present life never arises for them; rather, should it arise even briefly, it is swiftly cut off.

Chinese Source

善修梵行諸聖弟子，當來後有苦不生故，與諸如來成就明力少分相似。非現法緣苦不生故；設暫生已，速疾斷故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Noble disciples who have cultivated the holy life well, because suffering belonging to future existence does not arise for them, resemble in a small portion the power of knowledge the Tathāgatas have accomplished — though this does not mean that suffering conditioned by the present life never arises for them; rather, should it arise even briefly, it is swiftly cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens a further distinction (簡差別) — the arhat shares only a small portion of the Buddha's own threefold knowledge and tenfold power, since present-life affliction, though quickly cut off, can still briefly arise for the arhat in a way it never does for a Buddha.

56. Section: The Tathāgatas' Own Portion, Closing "Marks" Entirely

The Tathāgatas, however, have fully accomplished both kinds of power of knowledge — hence they are called the unsurpassed holders of knowledge.

Chinese Source

然諸如來二種明力皆悉成就，是故說名無上明持。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Tathāgatas, however, have fully accomplished both kinds of power of knowledge — hence they are called the unsurpassed holders of knowledge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary and two companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras

Only the Buddha's wisdom and severance of affliction are wholly perfect and unsurpassed — the Great Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra itself has the Buddha tell Śāriputra that a bodhisattva's single day of practice outweighs a śrāvaka's entire lifetime, since the bodhisattva vows to benefit beings across all future time, which the śrāvaka does not; still, the śrāvaka's own attainment — regarding the body as grass and the mind as a mere reflection, meeting any object without change of face or heart — remains genuinely remarkable. The first companion sūtra has Mahākoṭṭhita ask Śāriputra what ignorance is, and Śāriputra answer: not truly knowing the eye (and the other faculties) as impermanent and as a phenomenon of arising and perishing is ignorance, a great darkness covering the mind; truly knowing this, by contrast, is knowledge. The second, far longer sūtra tells of Venerable Upasena, bitten by a small venomous snake while meditating in a cave; feeling his body about to crumble like chaff, he calls Śāriputra, who finds his face and faculties utterly unchanged and asks why — Upasena explains that having permanently cut off every notion of self and self's possession regarding the six faculties, the elements, and the five aggregates, his face could show no change at all, "like a palm tree severed at the root, never to sprout again." Carried outside, his body does crumble at the first touch of ground, and Śāriputra, without a trace of grief, offers four verses on the four joys just explained — the fourth comparing the world, seen with wisdom, to filthy weeds one will never again seek out or let continue. The Buddha, hearing of this, teaches Śāriputra the very verses and protective mantra of loving-kindness — toward every being with no legs, two legs, four legs, or many, on land or in water, without limit — that would have spared Upasena the snake's poison entirely, so that future disciples might be protected by it instead. With this citation, the Tathāgatas' own portion (諸如來攝) closes entirely, closing a further distinction (簡差別) entirely, closing explaining what is difficult (隨難釋, together with attaining the joy of liberation) entirely, and closing Marks (相), the fourth of the nine topics under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely.

57. Section: Pervasive Contact Begins — Refuting What Is Wrong, Briefly Marked

Next, there is a certain ascetic or brahmin who transgresses against superior direct perception itself — even the foolish of the world are not confused about this, how much less the wise.

Chinese Source

復次，有一沙門、或婆羅門越勝現量，世間愚夫尚不迷惑，況諸智者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, there is a certain ascetic or brahmin who transgresses against superior direct perception itself — even the foolish of the world are not confused about this, how much less the wise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Pervasive Contact (觸遍), the fifth of the nine topics, explaining that pleasure and pain arise pervasively from contact — beginning with refuting what is wrong (遮非): outsiders who fail to see that pleasure and pain arise from contact instead speculate wildly, positing self-caused, other-caused, and further wrong theories, contrary to what even direct, ordinary experience already makes plain — that contact among faculty, object, and consciousness is what gives rise to feeling.

58. Section: Distinguishing Severally Begins — Four Speculations Marked

The resting-place of every kind of foolish delusion is false speculation taken as one's basis — whether relying on the past or relying on the present, firmly and stubbornly held to, thereby establishing four wrong theories of pleasure and pain.

Chinese Source

一切愚癡所安足處，虛妄推度以為依止，或依前際、或依現法堅固執著，建立四種苦樂邪論。

Ru-Yi Translation

The resting-place of every kind of foolish delusion is false speculation taken as one's basis — whether relying on the past or relying on the present, firmly and stubbornly held to, thereby establishing four wrong theories of pleasure and pain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens distinguishing severally (別辨), beginning with marking the four speculations (標四計), whether grounded in false views about the past or about the present life — their differences explained next.

59. Section: Distinguishing the Differences — Views Based on the Past

That is: relying on the past, falsely speculating that karma done in a former life is the cause, one posits that pleasure and pain are entirely self-made. Falsely speculating that Īśvara's free transformation is the cause, one posits that pleasure and pain are entirely other-made. Falsely speculating that Īśvara's own action came first, followed by karma done in a former life, one posits that pleasure and pain are both self-made and other-made. Falsely speculating that they arise from no cause at all, one posits that pleasure and pain arise from a cause that is neither self nor other.

Chinese Source

謂依前際虛妄計度宿作因故，立諸苦樂一向自作。虛妄計度自在變化以為因故，立諸苦樂一向他作。虛妄計度先自在作，然後宿作因所作故，立諸苦樂自作、他作。虛妄計度無因生故，立諸苦樂非自、非他所作因生。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is: relying on the past, falsely speculating that karma done in a former life is the cause, one posits that pleasure and pain are entirely self-made. Falsely speculating that Īśvara's free transformation is the cause, one posits that pleasure and pain are entirely other-made. Falsely speculating that Īśvara's own action came first, followed by karma done in a former life, one posits that pleasure and pain are both self-made and other-made. Falsely speculating that they arise from no cause at all, one posits that pleasure and pain arise from a cause that is neither self nor other.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens views based on the past (依前際) — four wrong theories, each rooted in a different false speculation about what happened before this life: entirely self-made (past karma alone), entirely other-made (Īśvara's transformation alone), both self- and other-made (Īśvara first, then past karma), and caused by neither (arising from no cause at all). A parallel set of speculations grounded in the present life follows.

60. Section: Views Based on the Present Life

Or, relying on the present life, falsely speculating: if what arises follows one's own desire, produced by one's own effort, one posits it as self-made. If it does not follow desire, is not self-perceived, but is induced by another, one posits it as other-made. If it follows desire, is self-perceived, yet is induced by another, one posits it as both self-made and other-made. If it is not produced first by either's effort at all, but merely because the object is presently before one, and, unable to penetrate the subtle cause, contact, one falsely clings to it, saying it arises from a cause that is neither self nor other, one posits it as arising from no cause.

Chinese Source

或依現法虛妄計度，若隨自欲、自作功用所生起者，立為自作。若不隨欲、不自覺知，他所引者，立為他作。若隨所欲，自所覺知，他所引者，立自他作。若非自他功用為先所生起者，但由境界現在前故，不能了達微細因觸，便起邪執，謂非自他所作因生，立無因生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, relying on the present life, falsely speculating: if what arises follows one's own desire, produced by one's own effort, one posits it as self-made. If it does not follow desire, is not self-perceived, but is induced by another, one posits it as other-made. If it follows desire, is self-perceived, yet is induced by another, one posits it as both self-made and other-made. If it is not produced first by either's effort at all, but merely because the object is presently before one, and, unable to penetrate the subtle cause, contact, one falsely clings to it, saying it arises from a cause that is neither self nor other, one posits it as arising from no cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The same four wrong theories reappear, now grounded in the present life rather than the past: entirely self-made (following one's own desire and effort), entirely other-made (neither desired nor perceived, induced by another), both (desired and perceived, yet induced by another), and caused by neither (mistaking an object's mere presence for an uncaused arising, since the subtle workings of contact go unrecognized).

61. Section: Displaying What Is Right, Closing "Pervasive Contact" Entirely

Here, only the coming together of faculty, object, and consciousness can give rise to pleasure and pain — neither in the past nor in the present is any truly existing self-making or other-making ever to be found. It is only through the coming together of these three things that "self" and "other" are provisionally posited. One should therefore know that it is precisely contact, pervading throughout everything, that is the cause of pleasure and pain.

Chinese Source

此中唯有諸根、境、識和合所生苦樂可得，都無前際、或現法中，若自、若他實有可得。唯即於此三事和合，假立自他。是故當知，唯有其觸遍行一切，為苦樂因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, only the coming together of faculty, object, and consciousness can give rise to pleasure and pain — neither in the past nor in the present is any truly existing self-making or other-making ever to be found. It is only through the coming together of these three things that "self" and "other" are provisionally posited. One should therefore know that it is precisely contact, pervading throughout everything, that is the cause of pleasure and pain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (201, Venerable Udāyī and the brāhmaṇī Verañjā)

Neither "self" nor "other" is ever truly found apart from this threefold coming-together — both are only provisional labels for it. The lengthy companion sūtra tells of Venerable Udāyī, staying near the brāhmaṇī Verañjā's hermitage, whose young pupils bring word of his excellent teaching; invited to a meal, she first receives him with visible disrespect — shod, head covered, seated above him — and he, honoring the Dharma too much to teach under such conditions, declines to answer her question each time, simply saying "now is not the time." Only when her pupils explain why does she correct her manner — unshod, properly dressed, seated below him — and he agrees to answer her question on the four wrong theories of pleasure and pain (self-made, other-made, both, neither), replying that the arhat instead teaches a fifth: that pleasure and pain arise dependent on contact at the six bases, walking her through each faculty in turn to confirm this, and further that when the six faculties cease entirely without remainder, the feelings dependent on their contact cease as well. Hearing this, Verañjā attains the pure Dharma-eye, sees, obtains, knows, and enters the Dharma, crosses beyond doubt, and takes refuge in the Three Jewels for the rest of her life. With this citation, displaying what is right (顯正) closes entirely, closing Pervasive Contact (觸遍), the fifth of the nine topics under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely.

62. Section: Resolute Understanding Begins — Able to Permanently Exhaust Contaminants, Marked

Next, by four marks, rightly generating diligence, one swiftly causes every contaminant to be permanently exhausted without remainder.

Chinese Source

復次，由四種相正發精進，速令諸漏永盡無餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, by four marks, rightly generating diligence, one swiftly causes every contaminant to be permanently exhausted without remainder.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Resolute Understanding (勝解), the sixth of the nine topics — a firm, wisdom-grounded conviction in the Dharma — beginning with its first matter, being able to permanently exhaust the contaminants (能永盡漏), through four marks of rightly generated diligence, to be named next.

63. Section: The Four Marks Begin — Equal Diligence and Freedom from Conceit

What are the four? First, generating equal diligence — that is, not generating diligence so agitated that it exhausts and harms body and mind, nor generating diligence so slack that it squanders life to no purpose. This is the first mark. Further, not, on this account, generating conceit — thinking oneself alone able to generate such diligence, unlike everyone else. This is the second mark.

Chinese Source

何等為四？

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the four?

Chinese Source

一者、發起平等精進。謂不極掉舉發勤精進，令其身心疲倦損惱；亦不極下發起精進，虛棄身命，令無所得。是名初相。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, generating equal diligence — that is, not generating diligence so agitated that it exhausts and harms body and mind, nor generating diligence so slack that it squanders life to no purpose. This is the first mark.

Chinese Source

又不由此而生憍慢，謂我獨能發勤精進，餘則不爾。是第二相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, not, on this account, generating conceit — thinking oneself alone able to generate such diligence, unlike everyone else. This is the second mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligence that agitates the mind toward excessive investigation exhausts body and mind and hinders meditation; diligence too slack sinks into torpor and squanders life. Equal diligence avoids both, and, once attained, should never become an occasion for pride over others less diligent.

64. Section: Freedom from Craving and Skillful Upholding

Further, toward the fruit of rightly generated diligence — the distinct comforts attained through worldly concentration — being without any craving for their savor, and cultivating non-negligence together with this. This is the third mark. Further, skillfully upholding the mark of equal diligence, so that it never regresses in the future. This is the fourth mark.

Chinese Source

又於正發勤精進果，世間安觸所證差別無有愛味，與此俱行修不放逸。是第三相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, toward the fruit of rightly generated diligence — the distinct comforts attained through worldly concentration — being without any craving for their savor, and cultivating non-negligence together with this. This is the third mark.

Chinese Source

又於精進平等之相能善攝受，令於當來無有退失。是第四相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, skillfully upholding the mark of equal diligence, so that it never regresses in the future. This is the fourth mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

Tranquility and freedom from coarseness mark the bodily comfort of worldly concentration; the joy and pleasure of the first dhyāna (born of seclusion), the second (born of concentration), the third (sublime pleasure apart from joy), and the fourth (equanimity's utter purity, unmoved by desire, investigation, joy, or pleasure in turn) are its distinct attainments from shallow to deep — none of which should become an object of craving. Upholding equal diligence well, without either extreme ever creeping back in, secures it against future regression.

65. Section: The Conclusion, Closing "Able to Permanently Exhaust Contaminants" Entirely

Rightly generating diligence in this way, one permanently exhausts every contaminant and becomes an arhat.

Chinese Source

如是正發勤精進故，永盡諸漏，成阿羅漢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Rightly generating diligence in this way, one permanently exhausts every contaminant and becomes an arhat.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All four marks together — equal diligence, freedom from conceit, freedom from craving for concentration's fruit, and skillful, lasting upholding — culminate in the arhat's fruit itself. With this, the conclusion (結) closes entirely, closing being able to permanently exhaust the contaminants (能永盡漏), the first of two matters under Resolute Understanding, entirely.

66. Section: Able to Record What Is Understood Begins

If one wishes, before the great Teacher or before wise fellow practitioners of the holy life, to record and distinguish one's own attainment, only the arhat's resolute understanding at the six bases is able rightly to record and distinguish it — resting on the three trainings and the five kinds of persons.

Chinese Source

若欲於彼大師有智同梵行所，記別自己所證差別，唯阿羅漢六處勝解能正記別。謂依三學及以五種補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one wishes, before the great Teacher or before wise fellow practitioners of the holy life, to record and distinguish one's own attainment, only the arhat's resolute understanding at the six bases is able rightly to record and distinguish it — resting on the three trainings and the five kinds of persons.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

This opens being able to record what is understood (能記所解), the second of two matters under Resolute Understanding — resolute understanding at the six bases serves as the support for the trainings in precept, concentration, and wisdom, by which the five kinds of persons progress along the path; among them, only the arhat's resolute understanding is fully perfected, and so only the arhat can rightly record and distinguish this attainment before the Teacher or fellow practitioners.

67. Section: The Six-Base Resolute Understanding Named

What is called the six-base resolute understanding? First, resolute understanding of renunciation; second, resolute understanding of harmlessness; third, resolute understanding of seclusion; fourth, resolute understanding of craving's exhaustion; fifth, resolute understanding of grasping's exhaustion; sixth, resolute understanding of the mind's never forgetting.

Chinese Source

云何名為六處勝解？一、出離勝解，二、無惱勝解，三、遠離勝解，四、愛盡勝解，五、取盡勝解，六、心無忘失勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the six-base resolute understanding? First, resolute understanding of renunciation; second, resolute understanding of harmlessness; third, resolute understanding of seclusion; fourth, resolute understanding of craving's exhaustion; fifth, resolute understanding of grasping's exhaustion; sixth, resolute understanding of the mind's never forgetting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining the distinctions (釋其差別) — renunciation names leaving home out of decisive faith in the Three Jewels, seeing desire's danger; harmlessness names the joy of upholding precepts, subduing one's own affliction and harming no other; seclusion names freedom from the desire-ill will-harm discursive thoughts through concentration; craving's exhaustion names cutting off the cause of future existence; grasping's exhaustion names cutting off craving for present objects; and the mind's never forgetting names the arhat's constant abiding in right mindfulness and right knowing everywhere, at all times.

68. Section: The Three Trainings Named

What are the three trainings? First, the training in higher precept; second, the training in higher mind; third, the training in higher wisdom.

Chinese Source

云何三學？一、增上戒學，二、增上心學，三、增上慧學。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the three trainings? First, the training in higher precept; second, the training in higher mind; third, the training in higher wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Higher precept means abiding in complete precepts, well guarding the code of individual liberation, perfected in deportment and conduct, seeing great danger in even the slightest fault; higher mind chiefly names the four dhyānas, through which the first direct realization of the noble truths is entered; higher wisdom names genuine knowledge and vision of the four noble truths. "Higher" carries two senses — what one tends toward, and what is most excellent: precept tends toward concentration, concentration toward insight, and insight toward liberation itself, and all three are found only within the noble teaching, shared with no outsider path, hence both "most excellent" and "higher."

69. Section: The Five Kinds of Persons — the Question Posed

What are the five kinds of persons?

Chinese Source

云何五種補特伽羅？

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the five kinds of persons?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the five kinds of persons (五種補特伽羅), distinguished by the depth of their practice in precept, concentration, and wisdom, explained one by one next.

70. Section: The First Two Persons

First, an ordinary being, still dwelling at home, resting solely on faith, gives rise to a joyful resolute understanding of renunciation, seeking, from the bondage of objects, to gain release for the mind — this is the first kind of person. Second, an ordinary being, having already renounced, resting solely on precept, performs, through body, speech, and mind, no harmful act toward any sentient being — this is the second kind of person.

Chinese Source

84. Section: Uniformly Wholesome and Faultless, Closing Resolute Understanding, Weighing Superior and Inferior, and the Fifth Person Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Because seeing has ceased entirely, there is no savoring at all, and the mind is uniformly wholesome and faultless.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (Venerable Śroṇa Koṭikarṇa)

All craving for the sense-realm's fine objects having ceased, the arhat's mind holds only wholesome and indeterminate dharmas, nothing unwholesome ever arising again — internally abiding, not narrow, well liberated, well cultivated, and uniformly wholesome and faultless together describe the arhat's full attainment. The companion sūtra tells of Śroṇa Koṭikarṇa, a former wealthy layman turned monk, sitting in discouragement on Vulture Peak, thinking himself among the most diligent of the Buddha's disciples yet still unliberated, and considering a return to lay life where he could at least practice generosity with his wealth. The Buddha, reading his mind, summons him and asks whether he was skilled at the lute (vīṇā) as a layman — yes — and whether a string pulled too tight, or too loose, produces a fine tone — no, in both cases — only a string tuned neither too tight nor too loose. Just so, the Buddha teaches, diligence too intense breeds agitation and regret, too slack breeds laziness — one should cultivate diligence in balance, without craving, without negligence, without grasping at signs. Remembering this lute simile, Śroṇa Koṭikarṇa soon exhausts every contaminant and attains arhatship, then returns to the Buddha to declare his attainment: liberation from desire, from ill will, through seclusion, through craving's exhaustion, through grasping's exhaustion, and through the mind's never forgetting — the six liberations matching exactly the six resolute understandings explained above — insisting that each is genuine only once craving, aversion, and delusion are truly exhausted, not merely claimed on the strength of a little faith, a little precept-keeping, or a little concentration. He compares the learner to an infant only gradually maturing into an adult's full faculties, and the arhat's unshakable mind to a great rock unmoved by the four winds, whatever object the six faculties meet. His closing verses affirm liberation in all six respects, a mind at rest with nothing further to do, and steadfastness before any of the six objects, pleasant or unpleasant alike. The Buddha, hearing this, praises it to the other monks as the model for how an arhat should rightly record his own attainment — neither through the conceit of one who has not truly attained boasting that he has, a grave and fault-ridden lie, nor through false modesty, but through plain, accurate self-knowledge. With this citation, uniformly wholesome and faultless (一向善而無罪) closes entirely, closing bringing forth different names (出異名) entirely, closing the fifth person (第五補特伽羅) entirely, closing weighing superior and inferior (料簡勝劣) entirely, and closing Resolute Understanding (勝解), the sixth of the nine topics under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely.

Because of not knowing it, one is also unable to fulfill even the learner's own mind- and wisdom-liberation. At that time, not yet taking the power of cultivation as one's support, one is not yet able permanently to destroy the coarseness belonging to the class of affliction; and, not relying on the power of prior skillful discernment, mindfulness not yet accomplished as cause and condition — one should know that such a one is unable to closely guard the sense-doors.

90. Section: One Well Able to Guard the Sense-Doors, Closing "Guarding the Sense-Doors" Entirely

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (Venerable Mahākātyāyana and the brahmin Lohicca)

Possessing both the power of discernment (seeing sense-desire's danger clearly enough that affliction never arises) and the power of cultivation (attaining, in samādhi, the wisdom that cuts affliction off), such a one closely guards every sense-door, never letting affliction take hold, until the path itself uproots it entirely. The companion sūtra tells of young brahmin pupils mocking Mahākātyāyana as an ignorant, shaved-head ascetic unworthy of their teacher Lohicca's reverence; Mahākātyāyana, after being ignored three times, teaches them a verse contrasting the ancient true brahmins — who kept the sense-doors closed, mastered speech, and dwelt in loving-kindness, attaining recollection of past lives through pure precepts — with the false ascetics of the present, who merely wear braided hair, hides, and ash while chasing gain, "guarding no sense-door, like treasure gained only in a dream." The offended pupils report this to Lohicca as slander; Lohicca, unconvinced at first, confronts Mahākātyāyana directly, hears the verse for himself, grows angry, then falls silent and asks what "a door" even means. Mahākātyāyana explains: the eye is a door, since it sees form, and so for each remaining faculty. Delighted, Lohicca asks what "not guarding the door" means — an unlearned ordinary being, seeing form, gives rise to craving or aversion without abiding in mindfulness of the body, fails to truly know mind- and wisdom-liberation, and so remains restless in body and mind. Asked what "well guarding the door" means, Mahākātyāyana explains: a learned noble disciple, seeing form, gives rise to neither craving nor aversion, constantly gathers the mind, and abides in mindfulness of the body, coming truly to know the immeasurable mind- and wisdom-liberation of the noble path; every unwholesome dharma in the six spheres is thereby extinguished without remainder, the arhat's fruit attained, mind- and wisdom-liberation fulfilled, bodily misconduct stilled, and right mindfulness secured — this is called the first door's skillful taming, guarding, and cultivation, and so equally for each remaining faculty. Lohicca, comparing the teaching to seeking poison and finding nectar instead, departs in joy rather than anger. With this citation, one well able to closely guard the sense-doors (善能密護根門) closes entirely, closing Guarding the Sense-Doors (護根門), the seventh of the nine topics under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely.

The Decisions on the Level of What Is Accomplished by Hearing name sixteen such underlying intents; different practitioners may enter the same teaching through entirely different doors, and the Buddha's own teaching, expounded countless ways for countless kinds of hearers, remains rightly recorded so long as it does not contradict the Dharma's true nature. The companion sūtra cycle has a group of monks debate the meaning of a verse from the Pārāyaṇa's Tissametteyya chapter on "the two edges," "the middle," and "the binding" — five monks propose five different readings in turn (the six internal versus six external bases; the past versus the future; pleasant versus painful feeling; existence versus its origin; the body versus the body's origin — each pairing "feeling" or "craving" as the middle and "craving" as the binding), unable to agree, and bring the dispute to the Buddha. 98. Section: Explaining Begins — Guarding the Sense-Doors, Pointed Back

此中密護一切根門，略廣應知如聲聞地。

Here, closely guarding all the sense-doors should be known, briefly and in detail, as explained in the Śrāvaka Level.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Level, fascicle 23

This opens explaining (釋). The Śrāvaka Level's own account of sense-restraint names six elements in brief: closely guarding the sense-doors (since the six faculties are where the mind follows objects into affliction); guarding right mindfulness; constantly attending to right mindfulness (so that it remains continuously, thoroughly present); using mindfulness to protect the intellect (keeping the sixth consciousness constantly attentive); dwelling in equanimity (releasing craving, aversion, and delusion the instant they arise, whether through wise release or neutral non-engagement); and cultivating restraint of the mind faculty itself (taking neither the general mark nor the particulars of any of the six objects, never giving rise to unwholesome discursive thought through them).

Ru-Yi Translation

Through such well-protecting of the body, well-guarding of the faculties, and well-abiding in mindfulness, one is able to reflect on the perception of what should not be done, so that affliction cannot cover the mind, even briefly, with any savor of pleasure. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (Venerable Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja and King Udayana of Vatsa)

Well-protecting, well-guarding, and well-abiding together secure the ground on which the first two antidotes — the perception of what should not be done and the perception of utter impurity — can actually take hold. The companion sūtra has King Udayana ask Piṇḍola Bhāradvāja why young monks, barely gone forth, are so radiantly content and steadfast in the holy life for life. The answer comes in three parts, matching the three antidotes: first, the Buddha's own teaching to regard an old woman as one's mother, a woman of middle years as one's sister, and a young girl as one's daughter; second — when the king presses that affection can still smolder into desire even toward family — the contemplation of the body's thirty-six impure substances, from hair to urine, skull to marrow; and third — when the king presses further that the mind changes swiftly and purity-perception can just as quickly fade — closely guarding the sense-doors, well restraining each faculty so that worldly craving and evil states cannot bind the mind. The king confirms this from his own experience: entering his palace without guarding his faculties, the three poisons blaze up even in solitude; entering with his faculties well guarded, they do not blaze even amid the palace's temptations. With this citation, reflecting on the two perceptions (思惟二想) closes entirely, closing countering lustful craving (對治婬貪), the second of two matters under Marks, entirely, closing Marks (相), the ninth and final topic under the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest, entirely — and, with all nine topics now complete (the cause of the same portion, volition, bondage and liberation, marks, pervasive contact, resolute understanding, guarding the sense-doors, teaching, and marks once more), closing the Cause of the Same Portion and the Rest (因同分等攝), the FIRST particular verse under 處擇攝's SECOND overall summary verse, ENTIRELY.

103. Section: Mere Condition Begins — the Meaning Marked

104. Section: The Analogy Applied, Closing "Mere Condition" Entirely

Just as the karma of grasping and receiving, drawn forth by the function the mind gives rise to, has the hand serving only as an assisting condition for grasping and receiving — one should know that the principle here is just the same.

Chinese Source

如心所起功用所引諸取受業，手唯能作助取受緣，當知此中道理亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the karma of grasping and receiving, drawn forth by the function the mind gives rise to, has the hand serving only as an assisting condition for grasping and receiving — one should know that the principle here is just the same.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (206)

The hand does not decide, of itself, to grasp a cup — the mind decides, and the seed behind that decision is the true cause, the hand only the condition through which it is carried out; the six contact-bases stand in exactly the same relation to feeling. The companion sūtra has the Buddha teach that just as having hands enables grasping and releasing, having feet enables walking, having joints enables bending and stretching, and having a stomach enables hunger and thirst to be known, so having eyes gives rise to eye-contact and the feeling that follows, and likewise for each remaining faculty — while lacking any of these physical means, the corresponding activity or feeling cannot arise at all. Master Maitreya's own commentary draws this further toward Consciousness-Only teaching: bodily movement, hunger, and the six kinds of sense-cognition all trace back most fundamentally to the mind's own seeds, which, upon ripening, manifest as the corresponding faculty and object. With this citation, the analogy applied (喻合) closes entirely, closing Mere Condition (唯緣), the first of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

109. Section: Thoughts Arising from Views, Closing "Discursive Thought" Entirely

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (the tortoise and the jackal)

Outsiders, taking self-view as their root, speculate endlessly — eternalist, annihilationist, finite or infinite, percipient or non-percipient — all born of the same delusive discursive thought a noble disciple has left behind. 115. Section: Explaining the Name, Closing "The Vow" Entirely

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, because both its timing and its difficulty of recognition are subtle, it is called the subtlest of the subtle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (208)

Two reasons together make this bond "the subtlest of the subtle": it forms in a single instant of thought, and its presence is recognized only through especially deep wisdom. The companion sūtra opens with an analogy of parched barley struck by six strong men and then a seventh — the ordinary being, already pounded by the six contact-bases' feelings and afflictions, is pounded finer still by a seventh blow: the wish for future existence. The Buddha then catalogues every kind of clinging that likewise shakes the mind — to the five aggregates as self or as self's possession, to future existence or its negation, to a future body with form or without, with perception, without it, or neither — each producing the same disease-like, boil-like, thorn-like agitation, all traced back to craving as their root, whether self-love, love of future existence, love of a particular rebirth-realm, or love of present enjoyment. The Buddha then recounts a battle between the gods and asuras: the asura king Vemacitrin, captured and bound with five bonds, enjoys heavenly pleasures all the same in the god's palace — but the instant he thinks proudly that only the asuras are truly wise and he will return home, he sees himself bound and the pleasures vanish; the instant he thinks instead that the gods too are wise and he may as well remain, his bonds loosen and the pleasures return. Even the asura king's bondage, the Buddha teaches, is this subtle — how much more so the bondage of Māra's own affliction, which forms and dissolves with a single change of mind. With this citation, explaining the name (釋其得名) closes entirely, closing applying the analogy (合法) entirely, closing explaining (釋) entirely, closing displaying the subtlest of bonds (顯縛極細) entirely, and closing the Vow (願), the third of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Basic Section's Level of Even Attainment, fascicles 11-12

By antidote-method: with-investigation-and-analysis is the first dhyāna, still relying on coarse and subtle discursive thought to leave the desire realm; without-investigation-with-analysis-alone is the intermediate concentration between the first and second dhyānas; without-investigation-and-without-analysis spans the second dhyāna and above. By accompanying feeling: joy-accompanied is the first and second dhyānas; pleasure-accompanied is the third; equanimity-accompanied is the fourth and above. By the practitioner's own aptitude: the portion of decline belongs to the dull-facultied, whose shallow resolve cannot bear concentration's own joy and pleasure, so that elation itself causes a fall from it; the portion of abiding belongs to the middling- or sharp-facultied who, hearing of concentration's virtues, savor what they have attained without falling back but also without advancing; the portion of ascent belongs to those who, having also heard how to transcend attachment to concentration, no longer savor it and press on toward a higher attainment; and the portion conforming to discernment belongs to those who, seeing deeply the danger in self-view throughout, are thereby able to enter the undefiled path itself — the undefiled being, in the most ultimate sense, discernment's own portion.

Beyond this, there is no restraint whatsoever, whether excessive or more supreme, remaining still to be attained.

Chinese Source

彼即修習有學律儀，復能永斷妄執我慢以為前行一切修道所斷煩惱，究竟證得無學律儀。此上更無若過若勝所餘律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cultivating the learner's restraint further, one is able permanently to cut off every affliction cut off by the path of cultivation that takes the false grasping of conceit as its forerunner, ultimately attaining the unlearner's restraint. Beyond this, there is no restraint whatsoever, whether excessive or more supreme, remaining still to be attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing two extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras (209)

Innate self-grasping and conceit, still present even after the learner's discriminated afflictions are cut off, are finally exhausted only at the arhat's own unlearner restraint — for the Śrāvaka vehicle, the highest restraint attainable, with nothing beyond it. The first companion sūtra returns to the farmer-and-ox parable in full: a negligent field-watcher lets the ox of craving, aversion, and delusion trample the wholesome sprouts, while a diligent one drives it off with a firm hand on its nose and a switch to its flanks until, remembering the beating, it never returns — just so should a noble disciple, feeling affliction stir at contact with any of the six sense-objects, generate deep aversion and fear, settling the mind resolutely on a single object. The second tells of an ancient king so enchanted by the sound of a lute that he demands the sound itself be brought to him; told this is impossible, since sound depends on the instrument's parts and a skilled player working together, and that the sound already heard has long since vanished beyond recall, the king has the lute smashed into a hundred pieces and scattered — form, feeling, perception, volition, and desire, the Buddha teaches, are exactly the same: conditioned, mind-dependent, and, once their conditions pass, utterly ungraspable, however much they are called "I" or "mine" in the moment. With this citation, the unlearner's restraint (無學律儀) closes entirely, closing explaining the marks (釋相), all five matters (restraint, non-restraint, the three grades, the learner's restraint, and the unlearner's restraint) complete, entirely, and closing Restraint of Every Kind (一切種律儀), the fourth of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Level, fascicle 28, and two extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras

Mindfulness of the body — one of the four foundations of mindfulness — brought to its fullest, cultivation-wisdom's own power, is what finally masters the six restless faculties completely, perfecting the superior grade of restraint explained earlier. The first companion sūtra returns to the leper-in-the-reeds image, contrasting non-restraint (craving toward the desirable, aversion toward the undesirable, feeding a chain of thoughts, blind to or unable to remove the danger) with restraint (neither craving nor aversion arising, the danger seen and released at once). The second, more elaborate sūtra tells of a man who captures six different creatures — a dog, a bird, a snake, a jackal, a crocodile, and a monkey — each pulling toward its own favored territory (village, sky, burrow, graveyard, marsh, forest), and binds them all to a single sturdy post; however hard each strains toward what it desires, none can escape the post itself. Just so, the Buddha explains, each of the six faculties pulls toward its own preferred object, and the "post" that holds them all is mindfulness of the body — well cultivated, it keeps the mind from either craving the desirable or recoiling from the undesirable, across all six senses at once, and the Buddha urges the monks to dwell constantly in this very mindfulness. With this citation, through cultivation-wisdom (由修慧), the third and final cause, closes entirely, closing explaining the three causes (釋) entirely, closing displaying perfection (明圓滿) entirely, closing broadly explaining the mark of restraint (廣律儀相) entirely, closing explaining protection and the rest (釋不護等) entirely, and closing Entering the Noble Teaching Without Protection (入聖教不護), the fifth of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (the four vipers, five enemies, six thieves, and the crossing of the river)

This closes the analogy at its culmination (喻究竟) — the raft carries one only as far as the remainder-bound nirvāṇa; the far shore itself is the remainderless nirvāṇa, ultimate release. The companion sūtra tells of a man fleeing, in turn: a box of four venomous vipers (the four great elements composing the body, capable of killing their own "owner" through imbalance); five enemies with drawn swords (the five aggregates); six thieves hiding in ambush (the six faculties' own craving for their objects); an empty, decaying village they finally flee into (the six faculties themselves, fragile and crumbling at a touch); and a final band of robbers even there — until, blocked by a deep, swift river (the four floods of desire, existence, views, and ignorance, and the three cravings for desire, form, and formlessness), he gathers grass and branches, binds them into a raft, and paddles across by hand and foot to the safety of the far shore. The Buddha explains each image in turn — self-view as the terror of this shore, the raft as the eightfold path, effort as the paddling itself, and the brahmin dwelling on the far side as the Tathāgata — closing with his own compassionate exhortation to the monks to strive just as diligently, in solitude, without laziness, lest regret come too late. With this citation, the analogy's culmination (喻究竟) closes entirely, closing the analogy of gradual progress (喻漸次) entirely, closing diligent practice (精進修行) entirely, and closing Well-Prepared Supreme Provisions (勝資糧善備), the sixth of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

180. Section: The White Class by Contrast, Closing "Abandoning the Training" Entirely

Contrary to this, possessing all seven causes and conditions of the white class, within a well-taught teaching and discipline, having gone forth, one never retreats and abandons what one is rightly cultivating.

Chinese Source

與此相違，所有白品七因緣故，於善說法毗奈耶中，既出家已，終不退捨正所修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contrary to this, possessing all seven causes and conditions of the white class, within a well-taught teaching and discipline, having gone forth, one never retreats and abandons what one is rightly cultivating.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra

The seven bright counterparts — right knowledge, diligent non-negligence, fearlessness, freedom from attachment to kin, freedom from affliction's entanglement, a mind not bound, and superior resolute understanding — together secure a monk against ever abandoning the training. The companion sūtra has the Buddha teach that a learned noble disciple who truly knows the five aggregates' five modes regards the five sense-desires as a deep pit of blazing coals — approached only by a fool, since anyone wise and fond of life would recognize it means certain death — and so is never covered over by worldly craving and sorrow; likened also to a man working carefully amid a thorny grove, eyes wide and limbs drawn in to avoid every thorn, such a disciple keeps right mindfulness in every activity, refusing to let a desirable form pierce the precepts. Even a dull-facultied disciple, momentarily forgetting mindfulness and letting desire flare hot as a glowing iron ball, extinguishes it swiftly with a drop of mindfulness the moment it is noticed. The Buddha then poses a striking question: would such a disciple, offered wealth, rank, and every sensual pleasure by a king or minister in exchange for returning to lay life, ever accept? The monks answer no, and the Buddha affirms it, comparing such a settled mind to the Ganges' own current — flowing east through the long night without fail, no crowd of laborers however great could ever force it to flow west instead, and just as futile would be any attempt to turn such a disciple's mind back toward worldly life. With this citation, the white class (翻白品) closes entirely, closing Abandoning the Training (捨所學), the seventh of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

Chinese Source

又復於先所捨外事有所顧戀，由彼著處增上力故，能令退捨正所修學，如是名為第三邪行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, harboring longing for outer matters previously renounced, through the empowering force of this attachment, one is caused to retreat and abandon what one is rightly cultivating — this is called the third wrong conduct.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, in the present life seeking after gain and veneration, and, toward whatever gain and veneration is obtained, clinging to it without relinquishing — through the empowering force of this attachment, one transgresses the precepts, extending even, broadly speaking, to conch-voice-and-dog-conduct.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Cherishing gain and veneration, one abandons the training, unable to see it as a fault, openly violating the precepts. This is called the fourth wrong conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Different Topics and an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra

"Conch-voice-and-dog-conduct" names a monk who, like a dog whose bark resembles a resonant conch shell, sounds impressively pious while behaving like an animal underneath — declaring seniority to claim the best robes and lodging, speaking emptiness while living entangled in worldly things. The companion sūtra has the Buddha point to a great log floating down the Ganges and ask a monk whether, staying clear of both banks, not sinking, not caught on a sandbar, not seized by anyone, and not rotting, it would eventually reach the sea — then likens a genuinely practicing monk to that very log: the near bank is the six internal bases, the far bank the six external; being "seized by a person" is delighting in mixed company (the first wrong conduct); being "seized by a non-human" is a wrong vow for heavenly rebirth (the second); being "caught in an eddy" is retreating and returning to lay life (the third); and "rotting" is openly violating the precepts while still sounding pious (the fourth) — a monk free of all these flows on, like the log, straight to nirvāṇa. The sūtra closes with the herdsman Nanda, so struck by this teaching that he asks to go forth at once; the Buddha, with characteristic care, has him first properly return the cattle and formally take leave of his employer, precisely so that no unfinished worldly attachment could later pull him back — Nanda does so, returns, and goes on to attain arhatship. With this citation, the fourth wrong conduct closes entirely, closing the four kinds of wrong conduct (四邪行) entirely, closing distinct explanation (別釋) entirely, and closing Attachment to Place (著處), the eighth of the twelve topics under Mere Action and the Rest, entirely.

189. Section: Unwholesome Meaning Begins — Bringing Forth Doubt

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing two extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtras

The four abodes of consciousness — in form, feeling, perception, and formations — are each cultivated through mindfulness of body, feeling, mind, and dharmas in turn, each passing through the same four stages toward the exhaustion of contaminants. The first companion sūtra has a puzzled monk receive three different, equally valid descriptions of how to purify one's view — through the six contact-bases, the six elements, or the five grasping aggregates — and, still troubled by the apparent disagreement, bring his confusion to the Buddha. The Buddha answers with the parable of the kiṃśuka tree: four men who each saw the tree at a different season describe it differently — black like a burnt post, red and blossoming like a piece of flesh, hanging with long tendrils like a śirīṣa fruit, or broad-leaved and smooth like a banyan — yet all describe the same tree truly, each from what he actually saw. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive companion Saṃyukta Āgama sūtra (Venerable Mahāmaudgalyāyana)

Genuine sense-restraint and antidote power together keep the mind from ever drifting into that same downstream current. The companion sūtra has the Buddha, resting from back pain after teaching the assembled Śākyans, ask Mahāmaudgalyāyana to continue teaching in his place. Mahāmaudgalyāyana distinguishes the "contaminated dharma" — an unlearned ordinary being who craves or resents whatever the six faculties meet, lacking mindfulness of the body and any trace of mind- or wisdom-liberating wisdom, thus unable to exhaust unwholesome states — from the "uncontaminated dharma": a learned noble disciple whose constant mindfulness neither craves the desirable nor resents the undesirable, permanently exhausting every unwholesome state as it arises. Māra, he teaches, searches for an opening at each of the six sense-doors precisely as fire seeks dry grass to burn — succeeding against the first kind of person, whose body and mind are as combustible as a hut of dried thatch, but failing utterly against the second, whose firm mindfulness is like a well-sealed upper chamber, its walls solid and its openings thickly plastered, immune to fire from any direction. The Buddha praises this teaching highly and instructs all the monks to receive, uphold, and widely teach it in turn, since it is complete in genuine meaning, complete in the true teaching, complete in the holy life, and opens the way directly toward nirvāṇa. 又即成就此哀愍者，或生王家、或帝師家，雖未出家，內興勇悍：我今定當通達妙跡，歸修梵行，終無退轉。如是名為內興勇悍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, one who has accomplished this very compassion, whether born into a royal family or a royal preceptor's family, though not yet gone forth, inwardly arouses courage and valor: "I shall now certainly penetrate the sublime track, return to and cultivate the holy life, and never turn back." This is called inwardly arousing courage and valor.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Just as Śākyamuni himself was born a royal prince, the bodhisattva's compassion, even before formally renouncing the world, already carries an unshakable inner resolve toward the holy life. With this, inner courage and valor (內勇悍), the second of the bodhisattva's eight marks, closes entirely.

210. Section: The Forbearance That Truly Examines Dharmas, Closing It Entirely

Further, that very one, while not yet gone forth, dwelling in this Jambudvīpa-supported body as if it were a mere reflection, sitting alone in contemplation, is able to enter the very first dhyāna. Afterward, rightly and carefully observing the dharma of old age, sickness, and death in both oneself and others, one is able to attain decisive forbearance. This is called the forbearance that truly examines dharmas, manifesting from within.

Chinese Source

又彼即於未出家位，居贍部影，獨坐思惟，便能證入最初靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, that very one, while not yet gone forth, dwelling in this Jambudvīpa-supported body as if it were a mere reflection, sitting alone in contemplation, is able to enter the very first dhyāna.

Chinese Source

後於自他老病死法正審觀察，能定忍可。如是名為諦察法忍內自現前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Afterward, rightly and carefully observing the dharma of old age, sickness, and death in both oneself and others, one is able to attain decisive forbearance. This is called the forbearance that truly examines dharmas, manifesting from within.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Dwelling in Jambudvīpa as if it were a mere reflection" likens the body supported on this southern continent to an illusion-like appearance. Recalling the young Śākyamuni's own contemplative absorption beneath the rose-apple tree, and his confrontation with old age, sickness, and death, this forbearance is the decisive wisdom that truly penetrates their reality. With this, the forbearance that truly examines dharmas (諦察法忍), the third of the bodhisattva's eight marks, closes entirely.

211. Section: The Ability to Renounce Begins — Able to Go Forth

Further, that one's wholesome roots cultivated in former lives, awakened by every wholesome conduct, and, further, through the empowering force of courage, valor, and the forbearance that truly examines dharmas, one is able to relinquish vast, sublime sense-pleasures and, with pure faith, go forth.

Chinese Source

又彼宿世所習善根，一切善行之所覺發，復由勇悍諦察法忍增上力故，便能棄捨廣大妙欲，淨信出家。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, that one's wholesome roots cultivated in former lives, awakened by every wholesome conduct, and, further, through the empowering force of courage, valor, and the forbearance that truly examines dharmas, one is able to relinquish vast, sublime sense-pleasures and, with pure faith, go forth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the Ability to Renounce (由能出離), the fourth of the bodhisattva's eight marks, beginning with distinguishing (辨), and, within that, being able to go forth (能出家) — the same wholesome roots, courage, and forbearance already explained now converge to make genuine renunciation possible.

212. Section: Able to Receive Precepts and Attain Concentration, Closing "The Ability to Renounce" Entirely

Though there was no one to formally establish the true holy life for him, he was nonetheless able to receive and uphold the precepts spontaneously. Resting on these precepts, he progressively attained concentration up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. Thus is it called rightly renouncing.

Chinese Source

雖無施設正梵行者，而能自然受持禁戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though there was no one to formally establish the true holy life for him, he was nonetheless able to receive and uphold the precepts spontaneously.

Chinese Source

由此禁戒為依止故，漸次能證乃至非想非非想處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on these precepts, he progressively attained concentration up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

Chinese Source

如是名為能正出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus is it called rightly renouncing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With no outsider teacher having established the true holy life before him, the bodhisattva-to-be spontaneously upheld pure precepts and progressively mastered every worldly concentration up to its very summit. With this, being able to receive precepts (能受戒) and being able to attest to concentration (能證定) close entirely, closing the Ability to Renounce (由能出離), the fourth of the bodhisattva's eight marks, entirely.

213. Section: Personally Arousing Contemplation of the Truths Begins — Giving Rise to Observation

Further, wishing to abandon the worldly path and rightly seek renunciation — because, through the empowering force of the unsurpassed, ultimate resolve toward renunciation, accumulated and cultivated across the continuity of many bodies in the presence of past perfectly awakened ones through hearing the true teaching, he had no faith or joy at all in the worldly path — by this cause and condition, he went to the bodhi tree and, resting on the very path of contemplating old age, sickness, and death used before renouncing, observed the marks of the truths in sequence.

Chinese Source

又彼為欲棄世間道，正求出離，由於先世正等覺所，獲得無上究竟出離正聞勝解積集熏修身相續故，於世間道都無信樂。由是因緣，往菩提樹，即依先時觀老病死假想之道，於諸諦相次第觀察。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, wishing to abandon the worldly path and rightly seek renunciation — because, through the empowering force of the unsurpassed, ultimate resolve toward renunciation, accumulated and cultivated across the continuity of many bodies in the presence of past perfectly awakened ones through hearing the true teaching, he had no faith or joy at all in the worldly path — by this cause and condition, he went to the bodhi tree and, resting on the very path of contemplating old age, sickness, and death used before renouncing, observed the marks of the truths in sequence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Personally Arousing, from Within, Contemplation of the Truths (由內觀諦), the fifth of the bodhisattva's eight marks — having mastered every worldly concentration under outsider teachers yet still not free of birth-and-death, he turned instead beneath the bodhi tree to the very path of contemplation he had begun before renouncing, observing old age and death, its origin, its cessation, and the path to its cessation — the four truths in their own order.

214. Section: Attentive Reflection

He reflected thus: all these kinds of worldly sentient beings, fallen into manifold peril and suffering, have birth, have old age, have sickness, have death — yet are unable truly to know how to be ultimately free of old age, sickness, and death.

Chinese Source

作是思惟：是諸世間有情之類，墮在種種艱險眾苦，有生、有老、有病、有死，然其不能於老病死究竟出離如實了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

He reflected thus: all these kinds of worldly sentient beings, fallen into manifold peril and suffering, have birth, have old age, have sickness, have death — yet are unable truly to know how to be ultimately free of old age, sickness, and death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Birth risks the infant and the mother alike; old age brings failing sight, hearing, and mind; illness spares neither young nor old; and death, however lightly regarded from a distance, proves unbearable when it finally arrives — yet beings remain unable to see how to escape any of it.

215. Section: Awakening to the Nature of Dharmas, Closing "Personally Arousing Contemplation of the Truths" Entirely

Observing old age and death in this sequence — observing old age and death, observing its origin, observing its cessation, observing the conduct able to reach the attestation of its cessation — resting on proper attention as support, and, having long accumulated vast provisions, through innate wisdom, one is thereby able to awaken to the nature of all dharmas, abiding in the dharma-abiding and dharma-realm of all dharmas. Thus is it called personally arousing, from within, the contemplation of the truths.

Chinese Source

如是次第觀於老死、觀老死集、觀老死滅、觀能趣證老死滅行，如理作意為依止故，久已積集大資糧故，以俱生慧，便能覺悟一切法性，安住諸法法住、法界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Observing old age and death in this sequence — observing old age and death, observing its origin, observing its cessation, observing the conduct able to reach the attestation of its cessation — resting on proper attention as support, and, having long accumulated vast provisions, through innate wisdom, one is thereby able to awaken to the nature of all dharmas, abiding in the dharma-abiding and dharma-realm of all dharmas.

Chinese Source

如是名為自內發起觀察諦行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus is it called personally arousing, from within, the contemplation of the truths.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Contemplating old age and death through this same fourfold structure as the four noble truths (suffering, origin, cessation, path) across three incalculable eons of accumulated merit and wisdom, innate wisdom finally awakens, unaided, to the emptiness underlying all dharmas — dharma-nature, dharma-abiding, and dharma-realm all naming this same reality from different angles. With this, awakening to the nature of dharmas (覺悟法性) closes entirely, closing Personally Arousing, from Within, Contemplation of the Truths (由內觀諦), the fifth of the bodhisattva's eight marks, entirely.

216. Section: Right View Manifest Before One Begins — Its Skillful Arousal and Ultimate Support

Further, wishing further to seek the exhaustion of contaminants above, through the skillful means of arousing the wisdom of recollecting past abodes, remembering former lives, from the presence of every Tathāgata, perfectly awakened one, accumulating the practice of hearing and reflection on the path to the exhaustion of contaminants — by this, one arouses the worldly right view accumulated over a long time, making it present before oneself. Yet this right view, as taught by these teachers, taking this as support, is able to enable the bodhisattva to settle into a single sitting, up through attesting to the ultimate exhaustion of contaminants.

Chinese Source

又彼復欲求上漏盡，方便發起宿住念智，憶念先世，從諸如來正等覺所，於漏盡道積習聞思，由是發起長時積集世間正見令現在前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, wishing further to seek the exhaustion of contaminants above, through the skillful means of arousing the wisdom of recollecting past abodes, remembering former lives, from the presence of every Tathāgata, perfectly awakened one, accumulating the practice of hearing and reflection on the path to the exhaustion of contaminants — by this, one arouses the worldly right view accumulated over a long time, making it present before oneself.

Chinese Source

然此正見如教授者，以此為依，能令菩薩安處一坐，乃至證得究竟漏盡。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Broadly and Skillfully Cultivating Worldly Right View Manifest Before One (由見現前), the sixth of the bodhisattva's eight marks — recollecting former lives spent hearing and reflecting under past Buddhas brings this accumulated right view fully to bear in this single, final sitting beneath the bodhi tree.

217. Section: The Conclusion, Closing "Right View Manifest Before One" Entirely

Thus is it called broadly and skillfully cultivating right view manifest before one.

Chinese Source

如是名為廣大善修正見現前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus is it called broadly and skillfully cultivating right view manifest before one.

With this, the conclusion (結) closes entirely, closing Right View Manifest Before One (由見現前), the sixth of the bodhisattva's eight marks, entirely.

218. Section: Purity of View Begins — Attesting to the Uncontaminated Dharmas

Further, through that very right view taught by these teachers, progressively advancing, having already become free of the lower grounds' desires, up to the very peak, the sphere of nothingness — when one attains direct realization within the noble truths, one then attests to the uncontaminated four foundations of mindfulness and the rest, up through, last, the eightfold noble path — every factor of awakening there is. Naming only the last, one should know this also subsumes every prior stage.

Chinese Source

又即由彼如教授者所有正見漸次勝進，先已遠離下地諸欲，乃至上極無所有處，當於聖諦得現觀時，便證無漏四念住等，乃至最後八聖支道，所有一切菩提分法。舉其最後，當知亦攝前位一切。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through that very right view taught by these teachers, progressively advancing, having already become free of the lower grounds' desires, up to the very peak, the sphere of nothingness — when one attains direct realization within the noble truths, one then attests to the uncontaminated four foundations of mindfulness and the rest, up through, last, the eightfold noble path — every factor of awakening there is. Naming only the last, one should know this also subsumes every prior stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens Purity Attained Through the Uncontaminated Factors of Awakening (由見清淨), the seventh of the bodhisattva's eight marks, beginning with attesting to the uncontaminated dharmas (證無漏法) — resting on the sphere of nothingness, the peak of worldly concentration where the noble path can still be cultivated (since the very summit, neither-perception-nor-non-perception, is too subtle to support it), the full thirty-seven factors of awakening are attested in direct realization of the four truths.

219. Section: Explaining the Fruit's Benefit Begins — Becoming a Non-Returner, and Attaining Purity

Through attaining this, one accomplishes the fruit of non-returning. Through attaining the uncontaminated factors of awakening, this is therefore called attaining coolness.

Chinese Source

由得彼故，成不還果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through attaining this, one accomplishes the fruit of non-returning.

Chinese Source

以得無漏菩提分法，是故說名獲得清涼。

This opens explaining the fruit's benefit (釋其果利) — speaking provisionally, in terms accommodated to the Śrāvaka framework rather than the ultimate Mahāyāna one, this attainment of freedom from desire is described as the non-returner's fruit, and, through the uncontaminated factors of awakening, as coolness itself.

221. Section: The Six Perfections Begin — Cultivation's Perfection, Briefly Listed

In order to permanently and completely cut off the affliction bound to the peak of existence, one further diligently cultivates the purely uncontaminated path — that is, cultivating the unsurpassed factor of awakening. This is called advancing in the cultivation of the unsurpassed path. Through this cultivation, in the unlearner's ground, six kinds of cultivation reach ultimate perfection. First, cultivating the noble supernatural powers to ultimate perfection; second, cultivating the pure five faculties to ultimate perfection; third, attaining the complete, remainderless release from every affliction together with its habitual residue, to ultimate perfection; fourth, attaining the four kinds of present-life pleasant abiding to ultimate perfection; fifth, attaining worldly meditative absorption, liberation, concentration, and attainment, to ultimate perfection; sixth, attaining the body of names, the body of phrases, and the body of letters, gaining the ability to teach the true Dharma at will and without difficulty, to ultimate perfection.

Chinese Source

為欲無餘永斷有頂所繫煩惱，故復勤修純無漏道，所謂修習無上覺支，是名進修無上修道。由此修故，無學地中六種修法究竟圓滿。一者、修聖神通究竟圓滿；二者、修淨五根究竟圓滿；三者、證得煩惱并諸習氣無餘離繫究竟圓滿；四者、證得四種現法樂住究竟圓滿；五者、證得世間靜慮、解脫、等持、等至究竟圓滿；六者、證得名身、句身、文身，得隨所欲、得無艱難宣說正法究竟圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to permanently and completely cut off the affliction bound to the peak of existence, one further diligently cultivates the purely uncontaminated path — that is, cultivating the unsurpassed factor of awakening. This is called advancing in the cultivation of the unsurpassed path. Through this cultivation, in the unlearner's ground, six kinds of cultivation reach ultimate perfection. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the Six Perfections (由六圓滿), the eighth and final mark of the bodhisattva's supremacy — even the subtlest remaining affliction, bound to the very peak of existence, is now permanently uprooted through cultivating the unsurpassed factor of awakening, yielding six kinds of perfection in the unlearner's own ground: supernatural power, the five faculties purified, complete release from affliction and its habitual residue, the four dhyānas' present-life ease, worldly meditative mastery in full, and unhindered eloquence in teaching the Dharma. Each is explained individually next.
