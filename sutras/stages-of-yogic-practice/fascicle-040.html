Fascicle 40 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十 · The Chapter on the Perfection of Discipline Opens

The Perfection of Discipline

With the perfection of giving now fully described, the treatise turns to the second of the six perfections — the bodhisattva precepts themselves, foundation of the entire noble path, unpacked through the same nine classical aspects already used for giving. This fascicle opens with discipline's own intrinsic nature: the four defining qualities that make the bodhisattva precepts what they genuinely are.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 40 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Chapter on Discipline Opens — Nine Aspects, the Same Framework as Giving

With the entire chapter on giving now complete, the treatise turns to the second of the six perfections: the bodhisattva precepts, foundation of every stage of the noble path, a topic so extensive it spans this fascicle and the two that follow. Just as giving was unpacked through nine classical aspects, discipline is unpacked through the very same nine, set out in an identical summarizing verse.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩戒波羅蜜多？嗢柁南曰：自性一切難，一切門善士，一切種遂求，二世樂清淨。如是九種相，是名略說戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's perfection of discipline? The summarizing verse states: intrinsic nature, comprehensiveness, difficulty, universal scope, purity of motive, completeness across every circumstance, responsiveness to what is asked for, benefit in both this life and the next, and freedom from taint — these nine aspects are, in brief, called discipline.

Chinese Source

謂九種相戒，名為菩薩戒波羅蜜多。一、自性戒，二、一切戒，三、難行戒，四、一切門戒，五、善士戒，六、一切種戒，七、遂求戒，八、此世他世樂戒，九、清淨戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

These nine aspects of discipline are called a bodhisattva's perfection of discipline: first, discipline in its intrinsic nature; second, comprehensive discipline; third, difficult discipline; fourth, discipline through every gateway; fifth, discipline of a genuinely good person; sixth, discipline complete in every kind; seventh, discipline that fulfills what is asked; eighth, discipline that brings joy in this life and the next; ninth, pure discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi situates this chapter within the treatise's own larger architecture: discipline is the tenth of eighteen sections comprising the treatise's foundational account of a bodhisattva's causal path toward buddhahood, and, following giving, the second of the six perfections cultivated under genuine learning's own broader structure. He notes discipline's own scope is vast enough to occupy this entire fascicle and roughly a third of the next two combined — a reflection of how foundational right conduct is to every later stage of practice, since without it, meditative concentration and wisdom alike lack the stable ground they need to actually develop. He previews each of the nine aspects briefly: intrinsic nature names discipline's own defining character; comprehensiveness distinguishes its many forms, for both householders and renunciants, organized around restraint, cultivating wholesome practice, and benefiting beings; difficulty names three specific ways discipline is hard-won; universal gateway names four channels through which discipline is received and practiced; a good person's discipline names conduct arising from a genuinely wholesome character; complete discipline organizes its content into six or seven further categories; discipline that fulfills what is asked addresses both the object of practice and its actual undertaking; discipline bringing joy in both lives names its benefit across time; and pure discipline names conduct entirely free of fault. This closes the ninefold overview; discipline's own intrinsic nature, unpacked first, follows next.

2. Milestone: Discipline's Intrinsic Nature Opens — Four Defining Qualities

The bodhisattva precepts, in their own intrinsic nature, rest on four defining qualities: properly received from a qualified preceptor, held with a genuinely good and pure aspiration, purified again through confession whenever a lapse occurs, and, in the rare and ideal case, held with such deep reverence and focused mindfulness that no violation ever occurs at all.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩自性戒？謂若略說具四功德，當知是名菩薩自性戒。何等為四？一、從他正受，二、善淨意樂，三、犯已還淨，四、深敬專念無有違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's discipline in its intrinsic nature? In brief, possessing four qualities is what should be understood as a bodhisattva's discipline in its intrinsic nature. What are the four? First, properly received from another; second, a genuinely good and pure aspiration; third, purified again after a lapse; fourth, deep reverence and focused mindfulness with no violation at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these four qualities as together defining what genuinely makes a set of precepts "bodhisattva discipline" rather than mere personal resolve — the precepts must actually be received through a proper transmission, held with the right underlying motivation, kept clean through honest correction when broken, and, ideally, kept with a devotion so complete that correction never even becomes necessary. He notes the first quality requires a qualified preceptor — someone with the wisdom and ability to genuinely teach and clarify the precepts, who has already made the great vow toward awakening, and who already holds the bodhisattva precepts themselves — since transmission from someone lacking these qualifications would not genuinely convey the precepts at all. This closes the overview of all four qualities; each is unpacked in turn, beginning with proper reception, next.

3. Milestone: Properly Received, and Held With a Genuinely Good Aspiration

The first quality, proper reception, requires the precepts be formally requested and received from a qualified preceptor — one with genuine wisdom and the ability to teach the precepts clearly, who has already made the great vow toward awakening and already holds the bodhisattva precepts themselves; and the second, a genuinely good and pure aspiration, requires the very same aspiration toward buddhahood above and toward saving beings below that continues, in ever-deepening form, all the way through every stage of the path to its final completion.

Chinese Source

從他正受者：他謂有智有力於語表義能授能開、已發大願同法菩薩。於如是等功德具足勝菩薩所，如法求請授與淨戒，乃至廣說，是名正受。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Received from another" means: another who has the wisdom and ability to convey and clarify the meaning of the precepts through language, who has made the great vow and holds the same teaching as a fellow bodhisattva. Toward such a person, fully possessing these qualities, one properly requests and receives the pure precepts in accordance with the teaching, as explained in full detail elsewhere — this is called proper reception.

Chinese Source

善淨意樂者：增上意樂品說：菩薩到究竟地所有一切增上意樂，是名菩薩善淨意樂。由願當證而作是說，非已證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"A genuinely good and pure aspiration" means: as the chapter on elevated aspiration explains, the entirety of the elevated aspiration a bodhisattva holds all the way through the final ground is called a bodhisattva's genuinely good and pure aspiration. This is spoken of in terms of the aspiration toward what is yet to be realized, not what has already been realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains proper reception's own careful requirements: a genuine preceptor must be capable of actually explaining the precepts' meaning through language, not merely reciting them, must have already made the great bodhisattva vow, and must already hold the precepts personally — someone lacking any of these cannot genuinely transmit them. A prospective student, not yet having received the precepts, formally requests them three times from such a qualified, more advanced bodhisattva, following the proper procedure described in detail elsewhere in the treatise, and only once genuinely granted are the precepts considered properly received; he notes this transmission, once made, endures across all future lives unless deliberately relinquished, a mark of how remarkable the bodhisattva precepts genuinely are.

He explains the second quality by tying it to the treatise's own account of "elevated aspiration" — an aspiration toward both buddhahood above and benefiting beings below, present already in a beginning bodhisattva who has not yet reached any noble stage, yet continuing, in ever-maturing form, all the way through to the tenth and final ground; the phrase describes an aspiration still oriented toward what is yet to be realized, not a claim that realization has already occurred. He notes this same aspiration, understood as seven distinct qualities of genuine compassion — fearless, principled, tireless, seeking nothing in return, untainted, expansive, and impartial — runs continuously beneath a bodhisattva's practice from the very first genuine resolve onward, which is precisely why even an ordinary, still-imperfect practitioner can meaningfully be said to hold this same "elevated aspiration" the moment their motivation is genuine. This closes the first two qualities; being purified again through confession after an unintended lapse follows next.

4. Milestone: Purified Again After a Lapse — Five Kinds of Wholesome Remorse

The third quality allows for genuine human imperfection: having received the precepts, a practitioner may still, through a lapse of mindfulness, commit an infraction — but any such infraction, so long as it remains a minor rather than a fundamental breach, is fully purified the moment it is honestly confessed to another qualified holder of the precepts, dissolved through any of five specific, wholesome forms of remorse rather than left to fester unaddressed.

Chinese Source

犯已還淨者：謂於所受淨戒若有毀犯，當知一切皆是惡作所攝。應對於他補特伽羅發露悔滅，由是除遣所生惡作。惡作有五，攝事分中別釋其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Purified again after a lapse" means: regarding the pure precepts received, should any violation occur, it should be understood as belonging entirely to the category of minor wrongdoing. One should confess this openly to another qualified person and dissolve it through repentance, thereby removing the resulting remorse. This minor wrongdoing has five forms, each explained separately elsewhere in the treatise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes minor infractions, fully purified through honest confession, from a fundamental root violation, which cannot be resolved this way and is addressed separately later in the treatise. He lists the five forms of wholesome remorse that can arise from even a minor lapse, each worth naming individually: remorse at the thought that this infraction will certainly bring one's own future regret; remorse at the thought that even heavenly beings would rightly reproach such conduct; remorse at the thought that wise fellow practitioners would rightly reprove it; remorse at the thought that word of such conduct would spread and tarnish one's own reputation; and, most serious of all, remorse at the thought that dying with this unresolved could lead to rebirth in the miserable realms.

He explains that confession to another qualified person — disclosing exactly what happened rather than quietly hoping it goes unnoticed — is what actually dissolves this remorse and restores the precepts to their original purity. This closes the third quality; the fourth and rarest quality, deep reverence sustained without any violation ever occurring at all, follows next.

5. Milestone: Deep Reverence Without Violation — The Rare, Ideal Case

The fourth quality describes a rarer, more complete devotion: a practitioner who holds the precepts in such profound reverence, and maintains such focused, unbroken mindfulness of them, that no violation occurs at all from the very moment of first receiving them — not because correction is unavailable, but because the underlying commitment is simply that thorough and sustained.

Chinese Source

深敬專念無有違犯者：謂由善淨意樂，於所受戒生起最極尊重恭敬。從初專精，不失正念，故無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Deep reverence and focused mindfulness with no violation" means: through a genuinely good and pure aspiration, giving rise to the utmost reverence and respect toward the precepts received. From the very outset, sustaining focused diligence and never losing mindfulness, no violation occurs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes this fourth quality as belonging to a practitioner for whom receiving the bodhisattva precepts feels genuinely rare and precious — reverence deep enough that the precepts are studied, recited, and held in mind constantly, and sustained mindfulness thorough enough that no lapse ever actually occurs. He notes this differs from the third quality not in the precepts' own content but in the practitioner's own sustained attentiveness — someone who never truly learns the precepts' own detailed content in the first place will inevitably violate them without even realizing it, while someone who studies them closely and never lets that attention lapse achieves this rarer, complete standard instead. He stresses the treatise itself never demands this fourth quality as the only acceptable path — purification through honest confession remains a fully legitimate, encouraged practice for ordinary, imperfect practitioners — but names this fourth quality as the ideal a bodhisattva continues aspiring toward regardless. This closes all four qualities individually; how they can be further grouped into three, and then into two, follows next.

6. Milestone: Four Qualities Regrouped — Shame Before Others, Shame Before Oneself, and Freedom From Lingering Remorse

The same four qualities regroup naturally into three: proper reception from a preceptor grounds an outward-facing sense of shame, since a lapse now means facing someone who transmitted the precepts in good faith; a genuinely good aspiration grounds an inward-facing sense of shame, since a lapse now means confronting one's own conscience directly; and purification through confession together with unbroken reverence, taken together, free a bodhisattva from lingering remorse altogether — the corrosive regret that, left unresolved, can quietly undermine meditative stability itself.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩從他正受故，於所學戒若有違犯，即外觀他深生愧恥；由諸菩薩善淨意樂故，於所學戒若有違犯，即內自顧深起慚羞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas properly receive the precepts from another, should they violate what they have learned, they look outward toward that other and feel deep shame. Because bodhisattvas hold a genuinely good and pure aspiration, should they violate what they have learned, they look inward at themselves and feel deep shame.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩於諸學處犯已還淨、深敬專念初無違犯二因緣故，離諸惡作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas, regarding the precepts, either purify a lapse through confession or, from the very start, hold such deep reverence and focused mindfulness that no violation ever occurs, through these two causes they are freed of lingering remorse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two forms of shame with real precision: the outward-facing kind arises from genuine awareness of wrongdoing paired with respectful concern for how a teacher or respected community would regard it, while the inward-facing kind arises from that same awareness paired with a private reckoning against one's own conscience, needing no witness at all. He treats both as healthy, functional responses to an actual lapse — not crippling guilt, but the ordinary moral sensitivity that keeps a practitioner honest with themselves and accountable to their community.

He then explains freedom from lingering remorse by citing a classical treatise's own definition: remorse is a state of anxious, regretful preoccupation over something done or left undone, whether that original act was itself wholesome or not, and its defining harm is that it directly obstructs the development of calm, settled meditation — a mind chewing over old regret cannot easily become still. He draws out the treatise's own quietly important point here: a bodhisattva who has genuinely confessed and resolved a lapse should not continue dwelling on it afterward, since re-opening settled regret serves no purpose and is, in its own way, simply another kind of confusion — confession clears the matter; continuing to relive it afterward does not deepen sincerity, it only compounds unnecessary suffering. This closes the regrouping into three; the further, final regrouping into just two follows next.

7. Milestone: Four Qualities Regroup Into Two, Through Three Different Lenses

The same four qualities admit a further, twofold reduction, viewed through three distinct lenses: viewed subjectively, proper reception and pure aspiration together produce the shame that enables the other two; viewed through the underlying mental factors involved, proper reception and pure aspiration are themselves the precepts' own defining substance, while the other two simply follow from them; and viewed through the presence or absence of an actual violation, three of the four qualities together describe the condition of never having violated at all, while the fourth alone describes the condition of having violated and then been restored.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩從他正受、善淨意樂為依止故，生起慚愧；由慚愧故，能善防護所受尸羅；由善防護所受戒故，離諸惡作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, resting on proper reception and pure aspiration, give rise to a sense of conscience; through that conscience, they are able to skillfully guard the precepts received; and through skillfully guarding the precepts received, they are freed of lingering remorse.

Chinese Source

又於是中，從他正受、善淨意樂，此二是法；犯已還淨、深敬專念無有違犯，此二是前二法所引。又於是中，從他正受、善淨意樂、深敬專念無有違犯，由此三法，應知能令不毀菩薩所受淨戒；犯已還淨，由此一法，應知能令犯已還出。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, among these: proper reception and pure aspiration are themselves the underlying substance; purification through confession and unbroken reverence are what these two produce. And further: proper reception, pure aspiration, and unbroken reverence, these three together, should be understood as what keeps the received precepts from ever being violated at all; purification through confession alone should be understood as what restores them once violated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first lens through simple lived experience: receiving the precepts properly and holding a genuinely good aspiration together produce real conscience, both the outward shame felt before a teacher and the inward shame felt before oneself, and this conscience is precisely what makes actually guarding the precepts psychologically possible, which in turn is what keeps a practitioner free of lingering remorse.

He explains the second lens more technically: proper reception corresponds to the mental factor of reception itself, the act of taking the precepts into one's own mind-stream at the moment of ordination, while pure aspiration corresponds to the mental factor of aspiration, the genuine wish underlying that reception — these two mental factors are, in a real sense, discipline's own defining substance, while purification through confession follows directly from having received the precepts in the first place (without reception, there would be nothing to violate), and unbroken reverence follows directly from the depth of that original aspiration. He explains the third lens as simply distinguishing the condition of never having violated the precepts, which draws on three of the four qualities together, from the condition of having violated and then been restored, which rests on the fourth quality alone. This closes the fourfold-to-twofold reduction across all three lenses; the treatise's own account of discipline's three great storehouses — restraint, wholesome practice, and benefiting beings — follows next.

8. Milestone: The Three Great Storehouses of Discipline — Restraint, Wholesome Practice, Benefiting Beings

Discipline's own intrinsic nature, held with all four qualities intact, unfolds into three great storehouses of boundless merit — restraint from wrongdoing, gathering wholesome practice, and benefiting beings directly — each one capable, on its own, of bringing genuine benefit and happiness to countless beings, and together forming the very cause that draws a bodhisattva toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening itself.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩具四功德自性尸羅，應知即是妙善淨戒，正受隨學，能利自他，利益安樂無量眾生，哀愍世間諸天人等，令得義利利益安樂故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who possess these four qualities in their discipline's own intrinsic nature should be understood to hold a discipline both excellent and pure — properly received and then followed through in practice, able to benefit both self and others, bringing benefit and happiness to countless beings, out of compassion for gods and humans throughout the world, so that they attain genuine benefit and happiness.

Chinese Source

應知即是無量淨戒，攝受無量菩薩所學故。應知即是饒益一切有情淨戒，現前能作一切有情利益安樂故。應知即是能獲大果勝利淨戒，攝受隨與無上正等菩提果故。是名菩薩自性戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood as a boundless pure discipline, gathering in everything countless bodhisattvas must learn. It should be understood as a pure discipline benefiting all beings, directly able to bring benefit and happiness to all beings. It should be understood as a pure discipline able to secure a great, supreme fruit, drawing one toward the fruit of unsurpassed, perfect awakening. This is called a bodhisattva's discipline in its intrinsic nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces here what becomes one of Mahāyāna Buddhism's most foundational ethical frameworks: the three collections of pure precepts. Restraint from wrongdoing is discipline in its most familiar sense, refraining from harm; gathering wholesome practice extends far beyond mere restraint to actively cultivating every wholesome quality that leads toward awakening — the six perfections and beyond; and benefiting beings directly turns discipline outward, toward concrete, active service to others' genuine welfare. He stresses none of the three stands alone — a bodhisattva who only refrains from harm but never actively cultivates virtue, or who cultivates virtue privately but never turns to serve others, has not yet completed the fullness of what discipline actually asks.

He reads the classical etymology behind "excellent and pure" as deliberately paired in reverse order — the text names "excellent" before explaining benefit, and "pure" before explaining happiness, a rhetorical inversion common in the treatise's own commentarial style, where genuine benefit for others is what makes conduct "excellent," and freedom from harming anyone is what makes it "happy" and thus "pure." He notes gods and humans specifically are named as the recipients able to genuinely take up and practice these precepts, since their circumstances allow for the sustained reflection and diligent practice the path requires, unlike beings in the lower realms whose suffering leaves little room for it. He closes by explaining the final storehouse of merit directly: realizing the unsurpassed, non-abiding nirvana that only buddhahood secures is called "genuine benefit," since it is entirely free of fault and universally desirable, while realizing the countless virtuous qualities that accompany it is called "advantage," the ultimate, irreversible gain the noble path alone provides. This closes discipline's intrinsic nature, the first of the nine aspects, in its entirety; comprehensive discipline, unfolding first into lay and monastic forms and then into the three storehouses just named, follows next.

9. Milestone: Comprehensive Discipline Opens — Lay and Monastic, and Restraint's Seven Assemblies

Comprehensive discipline first divides simply into precepts for householders and precepts for renunciants, and then, cutting across that same division, into the three storehouses already named; restraint itself, the first storehouse, is identified with the seven classical assemblies' own individually liberating precepts — monastic and lay alike — reflecting the Buddha's own compassionate accommodation of beings at very different points of readiness for renunciation.

Chinese Source

謂菩薩戒略有二種。一、在家分戒，二、出家分戒。是名一切戒。又即依此在家、出家二分淨戒，略說三種。一、律儀戒，二、攝善法戒，三、饒益有情戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva discipline is, in brief, of two kinds: precepts for the householder portion, and precepts for the renunciant portion. This is called comprehensive discipline. And, resting on these same two portions of pure discipline, it may further, in brief, be described as three kinds: restraint discipline, discipline gathering wholesome practice, and discipline benefiting beings.

Chinese Source

律儀戒者，謂諸菩薩所受七眾別解脫律儀。即是苾芻戒、苾芻尼戒、正學戒、勤策男戒、勤策女戒、近事男戒、近事女戒。如是七種，依止在家、出家二分，如應當知。是名菩薩律儀戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Restraint discipline means the individually liberating precepts of the seven assemblies that bodhisattvas receive: the monk's precepts, the nun's precepts, the female probationer's precepts, the male novice's precepts, the female novice's precepts, the male lay follower's precepts, and the female lay follower's precepts. These seven, resting respectively on the householder and renunciant portions, should be understood accordingly. This is called a bodhisattva's restraint discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the Buddha's own reasoning for establishing exactly these seven categories, tied to three distinct kinds of practitioner: those genuinely able to renounce both wrongdoing and worldly desire altogether receive one of the five monastic precepts — monk, nun, female probationer, or novice of either gender; those able to avoid wrongdoing but not yet able to leave household life receive the lay precepts instead; and those unable to sustain either commitment for long receive the eight precepts of a single day and night's retreat, a distinct, temporary accommodation the Buddha established precisely for those not otherwise able to commit further, though not formally counted among the seven assemblies since it is held only briefly rather than for life.

He notes bodhisattva restraint precepts, though identical in content to the corresponding non-Mahāyāna precepts, are never called by that other name — the same code of conduct, undertaken with the motivation of universal awakening rather than personal liberation alone, is properly called bodhisattva discipline throughout. He highlights three further important clarifications: bodhisattva precepts are shared in content with these other traditions but not restricted only to those already ordained within them, not universally required of every member of the seven assemblies, and not received in any fixed sequence — unlike monastic ordination's own strict progression, a bodhisattva may receive these precepts in any order, and, strikingly, even someone who later disrobes from monastic life and returns to lay status retains the bodhisattva precepts fully intact regardless, since that vow was never tied to formal monastic status in the first place. This closes restraint discipline; gathering wholesome practice, the second storehouse, follows next.

10. Milestone: Wholesome-Practice Precepts — Everything Done for Great Awakening, Rooted in Restraint

Gathering wholesome practice encompasses everything a bodhisattva does with body, speech, and mind, once restraint discipline has already been received, in pursuit of great awakening — the entire range of the six perfections and beyond — resting on restraint as its own necessary foundation, since a bodhisattva unable to guard restraint discipline cannot genuinely guard the other two storehouses either, even though, in actual practice, all three storehouses are received together as a single act at ordination rather than one after another.

Chinese Source

攝善法戒者，謂諸菩薩受律儀戒後，所有一切為大菩提，由身語意積集諸善，總說名為攝善法戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Discipline gathering wholesome practice means: bodhisattvas, having received restraint discipline, gather every wholesome quality of body, speech, and mind undertaken for the sake of great awakening — all of this together is called discipline gathering wholesome practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why restraint is named as the foundation the other two storehouses depend on: restraint's own defining character is guarding against harm, and a bodhisattva unable to guard even this basic restraint has, in effect, already broken all three storehouses at once, since gathering wholesome practice and benefiting beings both presuppose the same underlying stability restraint alone establishes — a bodhisattva who violates restraint discipline is described as having violated the entirety of bodhisattva discipline, not merely one third of it.

He is careful to clarify, however, that this dependency describes a logical and not a chronological relationship: a bodhisattva does not first receive restraint discipline alone, practice it to some degree of mastery, and only afterward separately request the remaining two storehouses — all three are, in actual ordination practice, received together in a single ceremony, restraint discipline simply serving as their shared foundation rather than a separate, earlier stage. He adds one further clarification: a person of genuine bodhisattva lineage who has newly given rise to the great resolve is not required to first separately receive the individually liberating precepts of another vehicle before being permitted to receive the remaining two bodhisattva storehouses — the three storehouses of bodhisattva discipline form their own complete and sufficient whole. This closes discipline gathering wholesome practice in overview; how such wholesome qualities are actually generated, beginning with what a bodhisattva cultivates through their own sustained practice, follows next.

11. Milestone: Self-Generated Wholesome Practice — Hearing, Reflecting, and the Four Stages of Meditation

The first source of wholesome practice a bodhisattva gathers is entirely self-generated: resting on restraint discipline already received, diligently devoting oneself to hearing the teaching without fatigue, to reflecting on it through eight distinct facets, and to cultivating calm-abiding and insight through the same four progressive stages already described at length elsewhere in the treatise — hearing, reflection, and meditation together forming the genuine root from which all further wholesome practice actually grows.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩依戒、住戒，於聞、於思、於修止觀、於樂獨處精勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, resting on and abiding in discipline, diligently devote themselves to hearing the teaching, to reflecting on it, to cultivating calm-abiding and insight, and to delighting in solitude.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this passage directly back to the treatise's own extensive earlier account, in the chapter on the lineage of the powers, of exactly what genuine hearing, reflection, and cultivation look like — deep reverence for well-spoken teaching, never growing weary or satisfied in listening to it, is hearing diligently pursued; the eight facets of correct reflection already detailed there, from avoiding unfruitful speculation through the first breakthrough into genuine understanding, is reflection diligently pursued; and the four progressive stages of cultivation already detailed there — calm-abiding, insight, their union, and the eventual ease of their joint mastery — is meditation diligently pursued.

He adds a practical caution of his own: a beginning practitioner eager to rush straight into helping others, without first genuinely establishing settled understanding through sustained hearing, reflection, and practice, risks drifting into confused or even mistaken views for lack of a stable foundation — which is precisely why delighting in solitude, avoiding the company of those whose views diverge and instead seeking out fellow practitioners genuinely devoted to the same calm, reflective cultivation, belongs here as an essential condition for wholesome practice to actually take root, rather than being abandoned prematurely the moment some small progress has been made. This closes self-generated wholesome practice; wholesome practice generated through others, beginning with respect shown toward one's elders, follows next.

12. Wholesome Practice Generated Through Others Begins — Respecting One's Elders

A further source of wholesome practice arises through a bodhisattva's own conduct toward others, beginning with genuine respect shown to elders — those senior in both age and virtue — expressed through concrete, repeated acts of courtesy: joining the palms in greeting, rising to receive them, inquiring after their wellbeing, and bowing in reverence whenever the occasion calls for it.

Chinese Source

如是時時於諸尊長精勤修習合掌、起迎、問訊、禮拜、恭敬之業，即於尊長勤修敬事。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, again and again, one diligently practices, toward one's elders, joining the palms, rising to greet them, inquiring after them, bowing, and other acts of respect — this is diligently cultivating reverent service toward one's elders.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi defines an "elder" here as someone senior in both age and genuine virtue together, not age alone, and walks through each specific gesture named: joining the palms when welcoming or formally requesting something of an elder; rising to one's feet the moment they arrive; asking after their health and wellbeing with genuine concern; and bowing at their feet when making some formal request. He notes this first category of "other-generated" wholesome practice is deliberately concrete and behavioral, since genuine reverence, however sincerely felt inwardly, is meant to actually show outwardly as well — this closes respecting one's elders; the remaining categories of wholesome practice generated through one's relationships with others follow next.

13. Caring for the Sick, and Praising Genuinely Correct Teaching

Two further categories of other-generated wholesome practice: compassionate, attentive care for anyone ill, checking on them, tending to their needs, and providing medicine without the slightest carelessness or delay; and genuinely praising anyone who teaches correctly, whether pointing toward a favorable rebirth through giving and discipline, or pointing all the way toward liberation through the four noble truths.

Chinese Source

於疾病者，悲愍殷重瞻侍供給。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward the sick, one attends and provides for them with compassion and earnest care.

Chinese Source

於諸妙說，施以善哉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward genuinely correct teaching, one offers genuine praise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains genuine care for the sick as combining real compassion — the sincere wish to relieve suffering — with actual attentive follow-through, checking in and providing without any trace of carelessness or hurry. He explains genuinely correct teaching as covering both the worldly register — discourses on giving, discipline, and rebirth in the heavens — and the transcendent register — the four noble truths' own account of suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path — and stresses that praising such teaching sincerely whenever one hears it, rather than staying silent out of indifference, is itself a form of wholesome practice worth actively cultivating. This closes these two categories; genuine praise for another's virtue, and rejoicing aloud in the merit of beings everywhere, follow next.

14. Genuine Praise for Virtue, and Rejoicing Aloud in Everyone's Merit

Two further categories: sincerely praising anyone who genuinely holds even one of five qualities — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, or wisdom — never as flattery, but as honest recognition; and rejoicing, with settled conviction and genuine joy, in every wholesome deed performed by any being anywhere across the ten directions, expressing that rejoicing out loud rather than leaving it only as a private, silent feeling.

Chinese Source

於有功德補特伽羅，真誠讚美。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward anyone possessing genuine virtue, one offers sincere, genuine praise.

Chinese Source

於十方界一切有情一切福業，以勝意樂起淨信心發言隨喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward every wholesome deed performed by any being throughout the ten directions, one gives rise to pure faith through an elevated intention, and expresses that rejoicing aloud.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the five qualities worth praising wherever genuinely found — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, wisdom — and stresses genuine sincerity as the test distinguishing honest praise from flattery. He explains "elevated intention" as an aspiration running continuously from an ordinary beginner all the way through the tenth ground, rooted in genuine faith and reasoned conviction rather than mere sentiment, settled and unwavering the way a formal seal settles a document — once genuinely convinced of a truth, a bodhisattva's confidence in it no longer wavers. He notes that expressing rejoicing out loud, rather than merely feeling it privately, matters precisely because ordinary beings tend toward quiet envy at others' success, silently wishing they had accomplished it themselves instead — actively voicing genuine joy at another's merit is itself a direct antidote to that very envy. This closes these two categories; bearing others' wrongs through five specific contemplations, closing all six categories of other-generated practice, follows next.

15. Milestone: Bearing Others' Wrongs — Five Contemplations That Make Genuine Patience Possible

The sixth and final category of other-generated wholesome practice addresses being struck, cursed, mocked, or provoked by another — met not with retaliation but with genuine, considered patience, made possible through five specific contemplations: seeing the whole exchange as merely impersonal phenomena with no fixed self on either side; recalling that every being has, across countless past lives, once been a beloved parent or sibling; observing that the offense itself is already gone the instant it occurred; recognizing that the aggressor's own affliction is already a form of suffering that retaliation would only deepen; and treating the encounter as a genuine opportunity to win the aggressor over as a friend.

Chinese Source

於他所作一切違犯，思擇安忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward whatever offense another commits, one carefully reflects and patiently endures.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the five contemplations in turn. The first, seeing the exchange as mere impersonal phenomena, recognizes that what feels like "someone cursing me" is, examined closely, only mind-generated appearances arising and passing — no fixed self doing the cursing, no fixed self being cursed, both sides equally without solid, independent existence, which is precisely what allows the whole encounter to simply be released rather than gripped tightly. The second, recalling countless past lives of kinship, draws on the classical teaching that every being has, across beginningless time, at some point been one's own parent or sibling — regarding the person now provoking anger as a long-lost family member makes the anger itself far harder to sustain.

The third, observing the offense's own instantaneous passing, notes that a harsh word exists only for the single moment it is spoken; refusing to mentally string that instant together with a lingering grievance is what actually keeps the insult from taking root at all. The fourth, recognizing suffering already present in the aggressor, observes that anger and hostility burn the person feeling them first and worst, so retaliating simply piles more suffering onto someone already suffering, while patience alone breaks that cycle. And the fifth, treating the moment as an opportunity, reframes an insult as an unexpected chance to demonstrate genuine magnanimity, potentially winning over as a friend someone who approached as an adversary. He notes patience cultivated this way holds firm without either wavering resolve to benefit the other person or any trace of resentment creeping back in — genuine equanimity, not suppressed anger. This closes all six categories of wholesome practice generated through relationships with others; wholesome practice generated through especially elevated objects — dedicating merit to awakening, offering to the three jewels, and diligent cultivation of every wholesome practice — opens next.

16. Milestone: Dedicating Every Wholesome Root to Awakening Itself

A further, especially elevated source of wholesome practice is dedicating every wholesome root already accomplished or still to be accomplished, through body, speech, and mind alike, directly toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening — releasing any lingering attachment to worldly wealth or comfort as the aim — together with repeatedly giving rise to the specific vows that carry a bodhisattva's own aspiration forward at every stage of the path.

Chinese Source

以身語意已作、未作一切善根，迴向無上正等菩提，時時發起種種正願。

Ru-Yi Translation

One dedicates every wholesome root of body, speech, and mind, whether already accomplished or not yet accomplished, toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening, and again and again gives rise to various genuine vows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three classical modes of dedication at work here: dedicating one's own merit toward others, wishing every being might likewise reach awakening; dedicating a present cause toward its ultimate fruit, so that whatever wholesome root is cultivated now is aimed unwaveringly at buddhahood itself; and dedicating an ordinary act toward its ultimate underlying principle, so that even a small, everyday deed is understood as already connected to the truth awakening itself reveals. He notes genuine dedication is marked by releasing any lingering wish for wealth or comfortable rebirth as the goal — small in outward scale, the resulting merit remains boundless precisely because the intention behind it is boundless.

He names the five classical vows that continue to arise throughout a bodhisattva's path: the vow of first arousing the aspiration toward awakening; the vow shaping where and how one is reborn to best help beings; the vow to actually practice the six perfections wherever reborn; further vows gathering countless additional virtuous qualities; and the great vows, ten in number, elaborated elsewhere in the treatise. He recommends the Flower Ornament Sūtra's own chapter on pure conduct as a model worth memorizing outright, since it shows how literally any ordinary daily circumstance — waking, washing, eating, walking — can become the occasion for a genuine vow toward awakening, precisely the practice new novices are traditionally introduced to at the very start of monastic training. This closes dedication and vow-making; offering to the three jewels with six kinds of elevated intention follows next.

17. Offering to the Three Jewels With Six Kinds of Elevated Intention

Offering every kind of fine, precious object to the Buddha, the teaching, and the noble community is a further source of wholesome practice, made genuine through six specific qualities of intention behind it: broad in scope, sustained over time, genuinely joyful, motivated by gratitude for an immeasurable debt, shaped by a great aspiration to one day embody the very qualities being honored, and purely wholesome, entirely free of any desire for a resulting personal reward.

Chinese Source

以一切種上妙供具，供佛法僧。

Ru-Yi Translation

One offers every kind of fine, precious object in devotion to the Buddha, the teaching, and the community.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the six qualities directly: breadth, offering many kinds of fine objects rather than a single token gesture; sustained duration, offering repeatedly over time rather than only once; genuine joy, offering with a glad rather than dutiful heart; gratitude, recognizing how immeasurable a debt is owed to the three jewels in the first place; great aspiration, offering now while aspiring to one day embody these same qualities oneself; and purity, offering with a mind entirely free of any wish for personal gain in return. He notes such offerings, made with all six qualities present, become their own genuine source of merit regardless of the objects' worldly value, since the underlying intention is what actually does the work. This closes offering to the three jewels; diligent cultivation of every wholesome practice, closing the elevated sources of wholesome practice, follows next.

18. Diligent Cultivation of Every Wholesome Practice

The final elevated source of wholesome practice is simply sustained, courageous diligence applied to every wholesome quality that supports meditative concentration — approached not as a brief burst of effort but as a long, uninterrupted commitment, held without discouragement or retreat, until genuine mastery and ease in cultivating these qualities is actually reached.

Chinese Source

於諸善品，恆常勇猛精進修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward every wholesome quality, one constantly and courageously cultivates with diligence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "wholesome qualities" here as everything belonging to the broad support system for meditative concentration — a wide-ranging category detailed at length elsewhere in the treatise, covering everything from the basic conditions that sustain practice to the specific forms of attention that develop it. He reads "constant" as sustained, uninterrupted effort over a long stretch of time rather than sporadic bursts, and "courageous" as a mind that never grows fearful or gives up partway through, however difficult the practice proves — together describing the same unwavering diligence already emphasized many times throughout this treatise. This closes the third and final elevated source of wholesome practice, completing the entire threefold account of how wholesome practice is generated — through one's own sustained effort, through relationships with others, and through especially elevated objects of devotion; how such wholesome practice is actively protected once gathered, unfolding into five supports, follows next.

19. Milestone: Protecting Wholesome Practice — Five Supports From Vigilance to Nightly Cultivation

Once gathered, wholesome practice must also be actively protected, through five specific supports: genuine vigilance across body, speech, and mind; clear, undistracted mindfulness of what one has actually studied; carefully guarding the sense doors so they do not carelessly latch onto attractive or aversive appearances; moderation in eating, taken only to sustain the body for practice rather than for indulgence or vanity; and sustained wakeful practice during the first and last watches of the night rather than sleeping straight through them.

Chinese Source

於身語意住不放逸。於諸學處，正念正知。正行防守，密護根門。

Ru-Yi Translation

One abides in vigilance over body, speech, and mind. Regarding what one has studied, one holds clear mindfulness and awareness. Acting rightly, one guards and carefully protects the sense doors.

Chinese Source

於食知量。初夜後夜常修覺悟。

Ru-Yi Translation

One knows the right measure in eating. In the first and last watches of the night, one constantly cultivates wakeful practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains vigilance as combining two active efforts at once — diligently cultivating what is wholesome while simultaneously guarding against what is not, both undertaken together rather than either alone. He explains clear mindfulness and awareness as arising directly from sustained study of the Mahāyāna scriptures and their commentaries, letting genuine understanding settle so firmly in mind that a practitioner can readily discern, in any given moment, what a bodhisattva genuinely should or should not do. He explains guarding the sense doors as centered mainly on the sixth, mental faculty, since the first five senses generally follow wherever the mind itself directs them — actively engaging attention with wholesome objects while refusing to let it wander toward alluring or aversive appearances that would only stir up affliction.

He details moderation in eating through its own careful reasoning: neither eating so much that the body grows sluggish and unfit for practice, nor so little that it lacks the strength practice requires, and never for indulgence, vanity, or physical display, but strictly to sustain the body, ease hunger and thirst, support the practice of pure conduct, and prevent illness from arising or worsening — food obtained through entirely honest means, eaten with genuine reflection rather than mindless habit. And he explains wakeful nightly practice through the traditional division of night into four three-hour watches, the first and the last of which are set aside for walking meditation and seated practice rather than sleep, specifically to counter the five hindrances — sensory craving, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and worry, and doubt — that most easily creep in during quiet, unoccupied hours. This closes all five supports for protecting wholesome practice; how wholesome practice is further increased, through association with genuinely good friends, follows next.

20. Increasing Wholesome Practice — Association With Genuinely Good Friends

Wholesome practice is further increased through drawing close to genuinely virtuous people and relying on good friends — a good person being one who has thoroughly abandoned unwholesome conduct and cultivated a hundred distinct forms of wholesome practice in its place, and a good friend being one who embodies eight specific qualities, though even partial embodiment of these qualities in the present, more difficult age still makes someone genuinely worth learning from.

Chinese Source

親近善士，依止善友。

Ru-Yi Translation

One draws close to genuinely good people, and relies on good friends.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the "hundred forms of wholesome practice" as the ten wholesome courses of action, each considered across degrees of completeness, duration of practice, and whether undertaken personally, encouraged in others, praised, or rejoiced in — ten categories multiplying into a hundred distinct expressions of the same underlying wholesome conduct. He names the eight qualities of a genuine good friend directly: upholding discipline, extensive learning, genuine realization, compassion, fearlessness in teaching, patience, tirelessness, and skillful speech — acknowledging that finding all eight fully present in any one teacher grows harder in a later, more difficult age, but stressing that even one genuine quality present is reason enough to learn from that person, since witnessing wholesome conduct in someone else naturally encourages the same growth in oneself. He notes the deepest value of such association is protection from wrong view above all — the other nine wholesome courses of conduct are comparatively straightforward, but freedom from delusion and distorted view is genuinely difficult to sustain alone, which is precisely what a genuine teacher's presence most reliably safeguards. This closes how wholesome practice is gathered, protected, and increased in its entirety — the complete account of discipline gathering wholesome practice, the second storehouse; how all of this is properly named as discipline follows next.

21. Milestone: Gathering Wholesome Practice Concludes

A closing statement gathers everything just described: honestly recognizing one's own faults and genuinely seeing their gravity; for whatever remains unbroken, guarding it with focused intent; and for whatever has already been broken, sincerely confessing before the Buddha, before fellow bodhisattvas, or before fellow practitioners generally, and repenting in accordance with the teaching — closing the entire account of how wholesome practice is gathered, protected, and increased as the complete definition of discipline gathering wholesome practice, the second of the three great storehouses.

Chinese Source

於自愆犯審諦了知，深見過失。既審了知深見過已，其未犯者，專意護持；其已犯者，於佛菩薩同法者所，至心發露如法悔除。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding one's own faults, one honestly recognizes them, genuinely seeing their gravity. Having honestly recognized and genuinely seen this, whatever remains unbroken one guards with focused intent; whatever has already been broken, one sincerely confesses before the Buddha, before bodhisattvas, or before fellow practitioners, and repents in accordance with the teaching.

Chinese Source

如是等類所有引攝護持增長諸善法戒，是名菩薩攝善法戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

All such wholesome practices gathered, protected, and increased in this way are called a bodhisattva's discipline gathering wholesome practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi clarifies that "fault" here specifically concerns failures within gathering wholesome practice itself — neglecting to actually do something genuinely wholesome, such as failing to respect an elder or visit someone ill — distinct from actively committing a genuine wrongdoing, addressed under restraint discipline instead. He notes confession may be made before any of three kinds of witness depending on what fits the situation — before the Buddha directly, before a fellow bodhisattva specifically for matters unique to the Mahāyāna path, or before any fellow practitioner more broadly, whether following the bodhisattva path or the path of individual liberation — the specific choice mattering less than the sincerity of the disclosure itself. This closes discipline gathering wholesome practice in its entirety, the second of the three great storehouses; discipline benefiting beings directly, the third and final storehouse, opens next.

22. Milestone: Discipline Benefiting Beings Opens — Eleven Aspects, and What Counts as Genuine Benefit

The third storehouse, benefiting beings directly, unfolds into eleven distinct aspects — but first requires the same careful discernment already established for giving: only what brings genuine benefit, free of fault, whether or not it also brings immediate pleasure, is worth providing; whatever brings pleasure alone without real benefit, or claims benefit without any actual pleasure resulting, calls for careful further judgment rather than automatic action.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩饒益有情戒？當知此戒略有十一相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's discipline benefiting beings? This discipline should be understood as, in brief, eleven aspects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this opening discernment directly back to the identical principle already established in the chapter on giving: genuine benefit means whatever brings a being toward a favorable circumstance now or a wholesome result later, eases suffering, or relieves harm, always free of fault — this is what should genuinely be provided. Anything falling short of this standard, bringing pleasure alone without real benefit, or claiming benefit without any real pleasure resulting, calls for the same careful, case-by-case discernment already emphasized throughout this treatise, rather than being given or withheld reflexively. This closes the opening discernment; the eleven specific aspects, beginning with being a genuine companion in both worldly tasks and illness, follow next.

23. Milestone: Being a Genuine Companion — In Worldly Tasks and in Illness

The first aspect of benefiting beings is simply becoming a genuine companion: joining in whatever worldly or spiritual endeavor genuinely brings a being benefit, and personally attending to anyone suffering from illness, tending to their needs exactly as their condition requires.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，於諸有情能引義利彼彼事業，與作助伴。於諸有情隨所生起疾病等苦瞻侍病等，亦作助伴。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas become a genuine companion in whatever endeavor genuinely brings beings benefit. They likewise become a genuine companion in attending to any being suffering the pain of illness, tending to them accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes worldly benefit — a favorable circumstance or rebirth — from transcendent benefit — genuine progress along the noble path — noting a bodhisattva joins in whatever endeavor, of either kind, a specific being is actually engaged in pursuing, since genuine help requires actually thinking through and then acting on what a situation calls for, not merely wishing someone well from a distance. He treats attending to the sick as this same principle applied to a uniquely vulnerable circumstance, where practical, hands-on presence often matters more than any amount of good intention alone. This closes companionship in worldly matters and illness; teaching the essential dharma, unfolding into its own eight qualities of skillful speech, follows next.

24. Milestone: Teaching the Essential Dharma — Five Skillful Qualities, Three Principled Ones

The second aspect is teaching the essential dharma itself, resting on both worldly and transcendent benefit, first easing a listener in through accommodating, skillful speech, and then teaching in accordance with genuine principle — skillful speech marked by five qualities and principled speech by three, together ensuring the teaching actually reaches and benefits whoever hears it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，依世出世種種義利，能為有情說諸法要；先方便說，先如理說，後令獲得彼彼義利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, resting on every kind of worldly and transcendent benefit, are able to teach beings the essential dharma — first speaking accommodatingly, then speaking in accordance with genuine principle, so that the listener comes to attain that very benefit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the five qualities of accommodating, skillful speech directly: easy to understand rather than needlessly obscure; genuinely worth the listener's effort, actually producing some real benefit rather than being wasted words; free of any attachment to fame or personal gain in the speaker; never delivered so excessively that it becomes tiresome, even the Lotus Sūtra itself cautioning against over-explaining even to an eager listener; and inexhaustible in its capacity to expand or condense as the occasion requires. He lists the three qualities of principled speech: oriented always toward nirvana as its ultimate aim, delivered in genuinely pleasing, well-chosen language, and organized clearly, moving from simple to profound in an order the listener can actually follow.

He draws out one especially rich philosophical distinction the treatise itself develops later in this same aspect — teaching impermanence and selflessness differently depending on whether the listener follows the Great Vehicle or the vehicle of individual liberation. For the Great Vehicle, impermanence means that the very notion of a fixed characteristic was never genuinely present at all, since all apparent phenomena are themselves nothing more than the mind's own projections; for the vehicle of individual liberation, impermanence means that real characteristics genuinely arise and then genuinely perish. Correspondingly, selflessness for the Great Vehicle means phenomena themselves lack any fixed, independent nature, while for the other vehicle it means only the person lacks a fixed, independent self, phenomena themselves being taken more literally. A skillful teacher, he stresses, teaches each listener within their own vehicle's own framework rather than imposing one framework indiscriminately on everyone. This closes teaching the essential dharma; remembering and repaying kindness, rescuing beings from fear, and resolving grief follow next.

25. Remembering and Repaying Kindness, Rescuing From Fear, Resolving Grief

Three further aspects: genuinely remembering and repaying every kindness ever received, by whatever means actually fits the benefactor and circumstance, sometimes gentle and sometimes forceful; rescuing beings caught in genuine fear, whether of predatory beasts, malevolent spirits, oppressive rulers or bandits, or natural disaster; and skillfully helping those grieving the loss of wealth or family find genuine relief from their sorrow.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於先有恩諸有情所，善守知恩，隨其所應現前酬報。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward any being who has shown them kindness in the past, genuinely hold and recognize that kindness, repaying it directly in whatever manner actually fits.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於墮種種師子、虎狼、鬼魅、王賊、水火等畏諸有情類，皆能救護，令離如是諸怖畏處。又諸菩薩，於諸喪失財寶親屬諸有情類，善為開解，令離愁憂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward beings caught in fear of lions, wolves, malevolent spirits, oppressive rulers or bandits, flood or fire, are able to rescue them, freeing them from these fearful circumstances. Bodhisattvas, toward beings who have lost wealth or family, skillfully help them find relief, freeing them from grief.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes repaying kindness may take whatever form genuinely serves the situation — becoming a companion in some task, or, in more striking cases, even manifesting a wrathful, startling display specifically to shock a benefactor who has themselves gone astray back onto a wholesome path, since genuine repayment of kindness sometimes looks stern rather than gentle. He reads rescue from fear and relief from grief as extensions of the same compassionate responsiveness already developed at length in the chapter on giving, now folded directly into discipline itself. This closes these three aspects; giving away life's genuine necessities, with one important exception, follows next.

26. Milestone: Giving Life's Necessities — Except What Merely Feeds Indulgence

The sixth aspect is giving whatever is genuinely lacking from among life's basic necessities — but pointedly excluding two specific categories from this otherwise generous list: entertainment such as song, laughter, and dance, and anything pertaining to sexual activity between men and women, since neither genuinely counts as a necessity a bodhisattva should be supplying.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於有匱乏資生眾具諸有情類，施與一切資生眾具。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward beings genuinely lacking life's necessities, give every genuine necessity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the ten classical categories of life's necessities named elsewhere in the treatise — food, drink, transport, clothing, ornaments, entertainment, perfume and garlands, food-related utensils, lighting, and matters between men and women — and notes that of these ten, entertainment and sexual matters are pointedly excluded here, exactly paralleling the same exclusion already established at length in the chapter on giving: these two categories serve indulgence rather than genuine need, and a bodhisattva's generosity, however expansive, is never extended to actively supplying them. This closes giving life's necessities; leading a community rightly, unfolding into a striking distinction between how the two vehicles frame the same teaching, follows next.

27. Milestone: Leading a Community Rightly — Teaching Each According to Their Own Vehicle

The seventh aspect is rightly guiding a community entrusted to one's care, teaching correctly and without distortion according to whichever vehicle — Great or lesser — genuinely fits each follower, and providing for them through both material and teaching-based support, entirely free of any craving for their service or devotion in return.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，隨順道理，正與依止，如法御眾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in accordance with genuine principle, rightly provide support, leading their community according to the teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the treatise's own striking philosophical care here: teaching the noble truth of suffering's impermanence differently depending on the listener's own vehicle — for the Great Vehicle, impermanence means that the very notion of a fixed characteristic in what is perceived was never genuinely present to begin with, since the five aggregates a being clings to are themselves nothing more than the mind's own conceptual construction; for the vehicle of individual liberation, impermanence instead means those aggregates, taken as genuinely existing entities, truly arise and truly perish. The same care extends to selflessness — the Great Vehicle teaches that phenomena themselves lack any fixed, independent nature, while the other vehicle teaches specifically that no fixed, independent person can be found within the ever-changing aggregates. A skillful leader teaches whichever framework genuinely matches a given follower's own path, rather than imposing one uniformly.

He explains providing support as taking two forms — material support, supplying clothing, food, bedding, and medicine so a follower's basic needs are met while practicing, and teaching-based support, instructing them correctly in view and conduct — both offered entirely free of any wish for the follower's own gratitude, service, or devotion in return, since a genuine leader's motivation rests purely in the follower's own genuine benefit. This closes leading a community rightly; going along with ordinary worldly customs, except where genuine harm would result, follows next.

28. Going Along With Worldly Customs — Except Where Genuine Harm Would Result

The eighth aspect is simply participating naturally in ordinary social life — greetings, conversation, celebrations, shared meals, mutual assistance with everyday tasks — matching the customs of those around oneself rather than standing needlessly apart, with a single, clear exception: whatever would genuinely harm someone or go directly against their own wishes is always set aside instead.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，隨順世間事務言說，呼召去來，談論慶慰，隨時往赴，從他受取飲食等事。以要言之，遠離一切能引無義、違意現行，於所餘事心皆隨轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas go along with ordinary worldly customs and speech — greetings and farewells, conversation, celebration and comfort, attending gatherings when the occasion calls, sharing food with others. Stated concisely: whatever would bring about something genuinely harmful or run against another's own wishes is set aside; in everything else, one simply goes along.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three categories of ordinary social custom named here — everyday conversation and mutual well-wishing, participation in life events like weddings and shared meals as guest or host, and mutual practical assistance with tasks like harvest or construction — and stresses a bodhisattva participates in all of these naturally rather than affecting an aloof, separate manner that would only alienate the very people a bodhisattva hopes to eventually benefit. The single governing exception remains simple: anything that would genuinely harm a participant, or anything a specific person clearly does not wish to happen, is where a bodhisattva quietly declines rather than going along regardless. This closes going along with worldly custom; revealing another's genuine virtue, sometimes openly and sometimes discreetly, follows next.

29. Revealing Genuine Virtue — Sometimes Openly, Sometimes Discreetly

The ninth aspect is making known a being's own genuine virtue — whichever of faith, discipline, learning, generosity, or wisdom they genuinely possess — sometimes praising it openly before others and sometimes acknowledging it more discreetly, choosing whichever approach will actually encourage that person's continued, joyful progress rather than backfiring into pride or discomfort.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若隱若露顯示所有真實功德，令諸有情歡喜進學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, whether discreetly or openly, make known a being's genuine virtue, so that they take joy in continuing to practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the choice between open and discreet acknowledgment as depending entirely on what will genuinely help the specific person in question — someone who welcomes public recognition and is spurred on by it should be praised openly, while someone who might grow prideful or self-conscious from public praise is better encouraged privately instead, the underlying goal in either case being the same: helping that person's own wholesome qualities continue to grow rather than stagnate. This closes revealing genuine virtue; correcting a being who has genuinely erred, carried out with real underlying affection even when the method itself must be firm, follows next.

30. Milestone: Correcting the Erring — Genuine Affection Beneath a Firm Method

The tenth aspect addresses someone who has genuinely erred: a bodhisattva approaches them with an inner warmth much like a parent's own love for a child, motivated purely by wanting their genuine benefit and peace, yet may still need to express that same underlying care through outwardly firm means — reproach, discipline, or, in serious cases, formal exclusion — precisely in order to draw that person out of harmful conduct and back onto wholesome ground, sparing them the far greater suffering that conduct would otherwise bring.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於有過者，內懷親昵利益安樂增上意樂，調伏、訶責、治罰、驅擯；為欲令其出不善處，安置善處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward one who has erred, hold inwardly a warm affection and an elevated wish for their benefit and peace, while outwardly taming, reproaching, disciplining, or excluding them — precisely in order to bring them out of an unwholesome state and settle them in a wholesome one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses the treatise's own insistence that inner motivation and outer method can diverge entirely without contradiction — a bodhisattva may present a stern, even wrathful face while the heart underneath remains as warm as a parent's toward a wayward child, precisely because genuine love for someone sometimes requires firmness rather than gentle accommodation, especially when gentler methods alone have already failed to redirect harmful conduct. He notes reproach operates through speech, discipline through more direct action, and exclusion through formally withdrawing a community's embrace altogether — each calibrated in severity to the gravity of the actual offense, and each aimed, without exception, at the same underlying goal: sparing that person from a much greater harm down the road by correcting course now, however uncomfortable the correction feels in the moment. This closes correcting the erring; the eleventh and final aspect of benefiting beings follows next.

31. Milestone: Demonstrating Supernatural Power to Benefit Beings — Comprehensive Discipline Concludes

The eleventh and final aspect of benefiting beings is skillfully demonstrating supernatural power for two distinct purposes: showing beings drawn toward wrongdoing a vivid glimpse of the lower realms' own suffering, so that fear itself turns them away from harm, and manifesting wondrous displays for those without faith, so that wonder itself draws them toward genuine joy and diligent practice — closing, with this, the entire eleven-aspect account of benefiting beings, the third storehouse, and with it the complete treatment of comprehensive discipline, the second of the nine aspects.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，以神通力方便示現那落迦等諸趣等相，令諸有情厭離不善；方便引令入佛聖教，歡喜信樂，生希有心，勤修正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, through the skillful means of supernatural power, display the appearance of hell and the other lower realms, causing beings to turn away from wrongdoing in disgust; and, through skillful means, draw others into the Buddha's noble teaching, so that they feel genuine joy and faith, a sense of wonder, and diligently cultivate genuine practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains this power accomplishes two distinct tasks: through fear, vividly manifesting the ripened suffering of the lower realms for beings inclined toward wrongdoing, or manifesting imposing, fearsome guardian spirits for those without any faith at all, turning them from wrongdoing and arrogance toward genuine faith instead; and through positive attraction, manifesting genuinely wondrous displays — water flowing from the upper body, fire from the lower, passing unimpeded through solid walls — for those already receptive, so that witnessing such rare and marvelous ability moves them to genuine, joyful reverence for the teaching and eager, sustained practice of it.

He closes by noting this completes the treatise's entire account of comprehensive discipline — first the intrinsic nature of restraint, wholesome practice, and benefiting beings established and named in overview, now fully unpacked in exhaustive detail across all eleven aspects of the third storehouse alone. The treatise turns next from simply naming what these three storehouses consist of to the more practical question of how a bodhisattva actually dwells in and lives out each one day to day.

32. Milestone: From Naming the Precepts to Actually Abiding in Them

Having named what the three storehouses of discipline consist of, the treatise turns to how a bodhisattva actually abides in each — genuinely dwelling in a precept means never abandoning the great vow toward awakening and never allowing severe, deeply rooted affliction to actually manifest — with restraint serving as the foundation that makes guarding the other two possible, wholesome practice cultivated through sustained effort, and benefiting beings carried out through the actual, ongoing practice of the four means of embrace.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩住律儀戒，住攝善法戒，住饒益有情戒？善護律儀戒，善修攝善法戒，善行一切種饒益有情戒？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does a bodhisattva abide in restraint discipline, abide in discipline gathering wholesome practice, and abide in discipline benefiting beings? How does one skillfully guard restraint discipline, skillfully cultivate discipline gathering wholesome practice, and skillfully practice discipline benefiting beings in its every kind?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "abiding" as resting on never relinquishing two specific things: the original great vow toward unsurpassed awakening, kept alive through continuous, renewed intention rather than a single, one-time resolution; and freedom from what the treatise elsewhere names severe affliction — marked by four telltale signs discussed later in this very fascicle: repeated indulgence in a fundamental violation, complete absence of any shame about it, genuine delight in that violation, and, worst of all, actually regarding the violation as a kind of accomplishment. A bodhisattva free of these two failures is said to genuinely abide in the precepts, regardless of the ordinary, correctable lapses discussed earlier.

He explains why restraint is described as the foundation for guarding the other two: restraint discipline overlaps directly with the individually liberating precepts already familiar from non-Mahāyāna practice, and diligently guarding this shared foundation is precisely what makes it possible to guard the two further storehouses built upon it. He explains cultivating wholesome practice as sustained, repeated effort at generating wholesome qualities, which draws a bodhisattva toward the fruit of unsurpassed awakening; and practicing benefiting beings as the actual, ongoing exercise of the four means of embrace — giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and shared work — genuinely carried out through body, speech, and mind rather than merely understood in the abstract. This closes the overview of what abiding in each storehouse actually means; the detailed unpacking of abiding in restraint discipline specifically, beginning with a striking image of renouncing even the greatest worldly power, follows next.

33. Milestone: Abiding in Restraint Begins — Renouncing a Universal Monarch's Throne Like Discarding Garbage

Abiding in restraint discipline opens with a renunciant bodhisattva's own complete release of past worldly pleasure — illustrated through the striking image of someone who, having actually ruled as a universal monarch over the world, renounces that very throne to become a monk without the slightest backward glance, discarding it as easily as one discards rotting garbage, a release the treatise carefully notes happens before, not after, genuine purified intention has actually been reached, precisely because it is undertaken in pursuit of that purification rather than as its result.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，住別解脫律儀戒時，捨轉輪王而出家已，不顧王位，如棄草穢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, abiding in individually liberating restraint discipline, having renounced a universal monarch's throne to become a monk, do not look back on that throne, discarding it as one discards rotting garbage.

Chinese Source

如有貧庶為活命故，棄下劣欲而出家已，不顧劣欲，不如菩薩清淨意樂，捨輪王位而出家已，不顧一切人中最勝轉輪王位。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as someone genuinely poor, for the sake of mere survival, renounces base pleasures to become a monk and never looks back on them — a bodhisattva's own pure aspiration goes further still, renouncing even a universal monarch's throne, the very greatest position among all humankind, without the slightest backward glance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own careful clarification here, easily missed: this striking renunciation happens before a bodhisattva has actually reached the purified intention of the first ground, not after — the renunciation is undertaken precisely in order to reach that purification, not as evidence it has already been reached, since otherwise the comparison to an ordinary poor person renouncing mere survival-level pleasure would make no logical sense at all. He reads the deliberate contrast as the whole point: if even someone driven only by bare necessity can fully release base pleasures without regret, how much more completely should a bodhisattva release the very highest worldly power available to any human being, motivated by something far greater than survival. This closes the first mark of restraint; releasing any hope for the exquisite pleasures of a future heavenly rebirth follows next.

34. Milestone: Not Hoping for Future Heavenly Pleasures, Not Clinging to Present Honor — Like Food Already Vomited

Two further marks: a bodhisattva takes no delight even in the prospect of the most exquisite heavenly pleasures their own pure discipline could eventually earn, seeing such pleasure as no different from walking straight into a vast, dense forest crawling with predators; and a bodhisattva, having renounced worldly life, feels no attachment even to the finest honor and support offered by society's most exalted figures, regarding it exactly as one regards food already vomited — once expelled, no longer food at all, and never something one would wish to taste again.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，住律儀戒，於未來世天魔王宮所有妙欲，不生喜樂。……於彼妙欲尚如實觀猶如趣入廣大種種恐畏稠林，況餘諸欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, abiding in restraint discipline, take no delight even in the exquisite pleasures of a future heavenly or demonic palace. ... They genuinely observe even these pleasures as like entering a vast, dense forest full of terrifying dangers — how much more so, then, any lesser pleasure.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，既出家已，於現在世尊貴有情種種上妙利養恭敬，正慧審觀尚如變吐曾不味著，何況於餘卑賤有情所有下劣利養恭敬。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, having renounced worldly life, examine with genuine wisdom even the finest support and honor offered by society's most exalted figures, and find it exactly like food already vomited, never again to be savored — how much more so, then, the lesser honor offered by the humble.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the forest-of-predators image directly: heavenly pleasure, however exquisite in the moment, functions exactly like a predator lying in wait, since indulging in it only deepens the very craving that keeps a being bound within samsara, however pleasant that binding may feel while it lasts — recognizing this clearly, a bodhisattva sees no genuine appeal in it at all, whatever merit might eventually earn it as a reward. He reads the vomited-food image just as directly: honor and support, once genuinely seen through, hold no more appeal than food already expelled from the body — the very thought becomes not merely unappealing but actively repellent, which is precisely the strength of aversion the treatise intends a bodhisattva to develop toward worldly recognition of any kind, whether offered by the powerful or the humble alike. This closes both marks; delighting in genuine solitude and never resting content with restraint alone follow next.

35. Milestone: Delighting in Solitude, Never Resting Content With Restraint Alone

A bodhisattva constantly delights in genuine solitude — both of body, physically apart from worldly and even communal distraction, and of mind, kept still and undisturbed whether alone or within a crowd — and, crucially, never rests satisfied with restraint discipline alone as though it were the whole of the path, continuing on with real diligence to cultivate the boundless meditative states that lead toward genuine mastery and freedom.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，常樂遠離，若獨靜處，若在眾中，於一切時心專遠離寂靜而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas constantly delight in solitude — whether alone in a quiet place or amid a crowd, at every moment the mind remains focused on genuine seclusion, abiding in stillness.

Chinese Source

不唯於是尸羅律儀而生喜足，依戒、住戒，勤修無量菩薩等持，為欲引發證得自在。

Ru-Yi Translation

They are not satisfied with restraint discipline alone; resting on and abiding in discipline, they diligently cultivate the boundless meditative states of a bodhisattva, in order to bring forth and realize genuine mastery.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes physical solitude, actually being apart from others, from mental solitude, the deeper and more essential quality of keeping the mind free of afflicted or even neutral distraction regardless of one's physical surroundings — a bodhisattva cultivates the mental kind at every moment, whether or not physical solitude happens to be available in a given circumstance. He reads never resting content with restraint alone as an important corrective against a subtle complacency: keeping the precepts cleanly is genuinely necessary but not sufficient on its own, since restraint alone does not yet deliver the mastery over one's own mind that meditative concentration actually provides — genuine freedom requires going on to develop that same settled, masterful concentration, not stopping at restraint as though the work were already complete. This closes solitude and continued diligence; clearing away improper talk and unwholesome thought, with its striking insight into redirecting rather than merely suppressing old habituated cravings, follows next.

36. Milestone: Clearing Away Improper Talk — How the Same Craving Can Be Redirected, Not Merely Suppressed

A bodhisattva actively clears away idle, improper talk and unwholesome trains of thought, and even when a lapse briefly occurs, responds with genuine, immediate remorse rather than indifference — and, through sustained practice of this same self-correction, gradually discovers something remarkable: the very same intensity of delight once invested in indulging affliction can, with enough patient habituation, be redirected instead toward delighting in purity itself, not merely suppressed but genuinely transformed.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，雖處雜眾，而不樂為乃至少分不正言論；居遠離處，不起少分諸惡尋思。或時失念暫爾現行，尋便發起猛利悔愧，深見其過。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, even amid a mixed crowd, take no delight in even the slightest improper talk; dwelling in seclusion, they do not give rise to even the slightest unwholesome thought. Should a lapse of mindfulness briefly occur, they immediately give rise to intense remorse and shame, genuinely seeing the fault in it.

Chinese Source

習拘檢故，漸能如昔於彼現行深生喜樂，於今安住彼不現行喜樂亦爾。又能違逆，令不現起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through habituated self-restraint, one gradually comes to feel, toward abiding in the non-arising of affliction now, the very same depth of delight once felt indulging in its arising before — and is thereby able to actively counter it, preventing it from arising at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the classical eight kinds of unwholesome thought this practice guards against — desire, ill will, harm, attachment to homeland, attachment to family, denial of mortality, concern for social standing, and contempt for others — all of which scatter the mind and give rise to further unwholesome states if left unchecked. He reads the treatise's own psychological insight here as genuinely striking: an ordinary practitioner, newly restraining old habits, typically finds the restrained state merely tolerable at best, still quietly missing the old indulgence underneath; but through sustained, patient habituation, that same emotional intensity once invested in craving actually migrates, over time, into a comparably strong delight in purity itself — not white-knuckled suppression maintained by willpower alone, but a genuine redirection of the heart's own underlying energy, exactly as strong in its new direction as it once was in the old. He compares this to how a genuinely committed practitioner comes to pursue understanding the teaching with the same driving intensity an ambitious merchant pursues profit — once that redirection has genuinely taken hold, resisting affliction no longer feels like a sacrifice at all. This closes clearing away improper talk and thought; not belittling oneself before the vast scope of the path still ahead follows next.

37. Milestone: Not Belittling Oneself — They Were Once Human Too, and So Am I

The sixth mark closes with a genuinely encouraging reflection: rather than despairing at the vast, seemingly unreachable accomplishments of bodhisattvas already established on the noble grounds, a practitioner reflects simply that those very bodhisattvas were themselves once ordinary human beings, who reached their present perfection only through the same gradual, step-by-step cultivation available to anyone — and that one's own gradual practice, followed with equal sincerity, will just as surely arrive at that same complete purity in time.

Chinese Source

唯作是念：彼既是人，漸次修學，於諸菩薩一切學處廣大無量不可思議淨身語等諸律儀戒成就圓滿；我亦是人，漸次修學，決定無疑，當得如彼淨身語等諸律儀戒成就圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

One simply reflects: those bodhisattvas, being human just as I am, gradually trained themselves, and thereby perfected the vast, boundless, inconceivable purity of body, speech, and restraint discipline across every bodhisattva practice. I too am human, and gradually training myself, will without any doubt likewise come to perfect that same pure discipline of body, speech, and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this reflection as directly countering a discouragement that could otherwise derail sincere practice before it even properly begins — hearing of the almost unimaginable scope of an advanced bodhisattva's accomplishments, a beginning practitioner might reasonably wonder whether such attainment is even possible for someone ordinary. The treatise's own answer is refreshingly plain: every advanced bodhisattva was once exactly where the beginner now stands, progressing entirely through patient, gradual cultivation rather than some special starting advantage — the same path remains fully open to anyone willing to walk it with equal sincerity, human capacity for genuine practice being the one thing every bodhisattva, advanced or beginning, genuinely shares. This closes not belittling oneself before the vastness of the path; a naturally gentle disposition, even toward those who would harm oneself, follows next.

38. Milestone: A Naturally Gentle Disposition — Even Toward the Violent and the Precept-Breaking

The seventh mark is a genuinely gentle disposition, constantly examining one's own faults rather than searching out others' failings, and holding no trace of harm or resentment even toward beings actively violent or careless of precepts — sustained through recognizing one's own past karma as the true source of present suffering, through the five contemplations already described, and through a compassion so deep it resembles a parent's own greatest worry over their most troubled child.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，住律儀戒，常察己過，不伺他非。普於一切凶暴犯戒諸有情所，無損害心，無瞋恚心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, abiding in restraint discipline, constantly examine their own faults, never searching out others' failings. Toward every violent, precept-breaking being universally, they hold no mind to harm, no mind of resentment.

Chinese Source

菩薩於彼由懷上品法大悲故，現前發起深憐愍心、欲饒益心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, holding a great compassion of the highest order toward that being, directly give rise to a deep sense of pity and a genuine wish to benefit them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the reasoning that makes this remarkable equanimity genuinely possible rather than merely aspirational. First, a bodhisattva facing harm from another reflects that the suffering itself is simply the ripening of their own past unwholesome karma, so retaliating now would only plant the seed of even greater suffering later — this is examining one's own fault rather than blaming the aggressor. Second, reflecting further that yielding to anger only harms oneself three ways at once — generating more unwanted suffering, binding oneself tighter in affliction, and creating fresh enmity — makes clear the aggressor was never really the source of the problem to begin with. Third, recognizing that both one's own aggregates and the aggressor's own aggregates share the identical nature of suffering at their core, a bodhisattva reasons: the aggressor, lacking wisdom, merely adds more suffering onto an already-suffering existence; having wisdom myself, why would I do the very same thing?

He recalls the five contemplations already introduced earlier in this fascicle — recalling past-life kinship, seeing the exchange as impersonal phenomena alone, impermanence, shared suffering, and treating the moment as an opportunity — as the practical tools that make this reasoning actually sustainable in the heat of the moment rather than merely comforting in the abstract. And he compares the resulting compassion to a parent whose greatest concern always falls on whichever child struggles most, however capable or comfortable the other children may be — a bodhisattva's deepest compassion likewise falls hardest precisely on beings behaving worst, since they are, in truth, the ones most in need of it. This closes gentle disposition; enduring actual physical harm without the slightest resentment follows next.

39. Milestone: Enduring Physical Attack Without the Slightest Resentment

The eighth mark carries genuine patience into its most demanding test: even when actually struck by hand, foot, thrown stone, or weapon, and even facing extremes of cold, heat, hunger, or thirst inflicted by another, a bodhisattva holds not the slightest trace of resentment, and never retaliates in speech through insult or reproach, or in action through any form of harm in return.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，住律儀戒，雖復遭他手足、塊石、刀杖等觸之所加害，於彼尚無少恚恨心，況當於彼欲出惡言、欲行加害，況復發言毀辱訶責，以少苦觸作不饒益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, abiding in restraint discipline, even when struck by another's hand, foot, thrown stone, or weapon, hold not the slightest resentment toward them — how much less, then, would they wish to speak harshly or act to harm in return, let alone utter words of insult or reproach, or inflict even the smallest hurt upon them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this mark deliberately escalates beyond the previous one's inner attitude toward an actual, physical test of that same equanimity — genuine patience proves itself precisely under the pressure of real harm, not merely in comfortable theory. He stresses that neither verbal retaliation nor physical retaliation is entertained even briefly, the same reasoning already established — recognizing shared suffering, past karma, and the five contemplations — carrying a bodhisattva through even this most demanding circumstance intact. This closes patience under actual harm; vigilance sustained across past, present, and future, and before any action even begins, follows next.

40. Milestone: Vigilance Across Past, Present, Future — And Before Any Action Even Begins

The ninth mark is sustained vigilance expressed across five distinct dimensions of time: confessing and purifying any past violation, resolving in advance to confess and purify any future violation, immediately confessing and purifying any present violation, forming a resolute vow before any action even begins to act and dwell exactly as the teaching requires, and then actually carrying that same vow through once the action is genuinely underway.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，住律儀戒，具足成就五支所攝不放逸行。一、前際俱行不放逸行，二、後際俱行不放逸行，三、中際俱行不放逸行，四、先時所作不放逸行，五、俱時隨行不放逸行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, abiding in restraint discipline, fully accomplish vigilance comprised of five aspects: vigilance regarding the past, vigilance regarding the future, vigilance regarding the present, vigilance formed in advance of action, and vigilance sustained throughout action.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，先於後時當所違犯，發起猛利自誓欲樂：謂我定當如如所應行，如如所應住，如是如是行，如是如是住，令無所犯。是名菩薩先時所作不放逸行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, before any potential future violation, give rise to an intense, self-made vow of aspiration: I will surely act and dwell exactly as required, precisely in this way, precisely in that way, so that no violation occurs at all. This is called a bodhisattva's vigilance formed in advance of action.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first three temporal aspects straightforwardly: whatever violation has already occurred, whatever might occur in the future, and whatever is occurring right now are each met identically, through honest confession and purification, rather than the past being buried, the future ignored, or the present excused. He explains the fourth and fifth aspects as a matched pair — before undertaking any activity at all, a bodhisattva first forms a resolute inner vow to carry it out exactly as the teaching specifies, and then, once actually engaged in that activity, carries the vow through moment by moment with sustained mindful awareness, drawing on the same detailed, everyday mindfulness practices described at length elsewhere in the treatise — clear awareness while walking, returning, looking near or far, bending or stretching a limb, handling one's robes and bowl, eating, drinking, and resting, all governed by the same underlying question of whether affliction or clarity currently governs the mind. He notes bodhisattva restraint discipline shares this same careful, moment-to-moment attentiveness with monastic practice generally, applied now within the wider frame of the bodhisattva vow. This closes vigilance across all five temporal dimensions; proper conduct and pure livelihood, the tenth and final mark, follows next.

41. Milestone: Proper Conduct and Pure Livelihood — Ten Marks of Restraint Conclude

The tenth and final mark gathers several qualities into a single picture of genuine integrity: concealing one's own virtues rather than advertising them, openly confessing one's own faults rather than hiding them, remaining content with whatever is actually received, enduring hardship of every kind without complaint, maintaining a naturally cheerful rather than gloomy disposition, staying free of restlessness, carrying oneself with calm, dignified composure, and remaining entirely free of any deceptive means of securing one's own livelihood — closing, with this tenth mark, the complete account of how a bodhisattva actually abides in and guards restraint discipline.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，住律儀戒，覆藏自善，發露己惡，少欲喜足，堪忍眾苦，性無憂慼，不掉不躁，威儀寂靜，離矯詐等一切能起邪命之法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, abiding in restraint discipline, conceal their own virtues, openly confess their own faults, remain content with little, endure every hardship, are naturally free of gloom, are neither restless nor agitated, carry themselves with calm dignity, and are entirely free of deception or any other means that would give rise to improper livelihood.

Chinese Source

菩薩成就如是十支，名住律儀戒，善護律儀戒。謂不顧戀過去諸欲，又不希求未來諸欲，又不耽著現在諸欲，又樂遠離不生喜足，又能掃滌不正言論諸惡尋思，又能於己不自輕懱，又性柔和，又能堪忍，又不放逸，又能具足軌則淨命。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who accomplish these ten marks are said to abide in and skillfully guard restraint discipline: not clinging to past desires, not hoping for future desires, not attached to present desires, delighting in solitude without resting content, clearing away improper talk and unwholesome thought, never belittling themselves, holding a gentle disposition, capable of genuine patience, never negligent, and fully accomplishing proper conduct and pure livelihood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the specific behaviors this tenth mark covers: never boasting of one's own accomplishments in practice, whether in daily conduct or in cultivating wholesome qualities; immediately and openly disclosing any genuine lapse rather than concealing it; feeling genuine contentment with whatever food, clothing, or bedding circumstance provides, however modest; withstanding cold, heat, hunger, thirst, insects, and even severe bodily pain without complaint; maintaining a naturally settled, unclouded temperament rather than a habitually gloomy one; keeping the senses calm rather than restless or grasping; carrying oneself with the composed bearing traditional monastic conduct describes — moving like the wind, standing like a pine, sitting like a bell, resting like a drawn bow; and never resorting to deceptive displays or contrived behavior designed to attract donations or support under false pretenses.

He closes by tying the entire ten-mark structure together as a coherent whole — freedom from attachment to past, future, and present desire; genuine solitude paired with continued diligence; a mind cleared of improper thought; freedom from self-doubt; gentleness and patience even under real provocation; sustained vigilance across every dimension of time; and, finally, integrity of conduct and livelihood — together constituting the complete, practical answer to how a bodhisattva actually abides in and guards restraint discipline day to day. This closes restraint discipline in its entirety; how a bodhisattva abides in and cultivates discipline gathering wholesome practice, opening with progressive practice in giving, follows next.

42. Milestone: Abiding in Wholesome Practice Opens — Progress Through Giving, Discipline, and Patience

Abiding in discipline gathering wholesome practice unfolds through progressive mastery of each perfection in turn — beginning with giving, where even the slightest lingering attachment to body or wealth becomes genuinely intolerable, let alone a greater one; continuing through discipline, where even the smallest trace of a root affliction or its lesser offshoots becomes intolerable the instant it arises; and through patience, where any flicker of hostility, harmful intent, or lingering resentment toward another likewise becomes impossible to simply let stand.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，已能安住攝善法戒，若於身財少生顧戀尚不忍受，何況其多。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, already able to abide in discipline gathering wholesome practice, find even the slightest lingering attachment to body or wealth intolerable — how much more so, then, a greater attachment.

Chinese Source

又於一切犯戒因緣根本煩惱、少分煩惱忿恨等生，亦不忍受。又於他所發生恚、害、怨恨等心，亦不忍受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding every root affliction that gives rise to violating discipline, or even a lesser affliction such as indignation or lingering resentment, none of it is tolerated the moment it arises. Regarding any hostility, intent to harm, or lingering resentment toward another, none of it is tolerated either.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "intolerable" here as an active, immediate response rather than passive endurance — the moment even a trace of attachment, affliction, or resentment is noticed arising, a bodhisattva refuses to let it settle in and grow, addressing it at its very first appearance rather than waiting until it has already taken root. He distinguishes attachment to one's own body as a subtler form of self-clinging from attachment to possessions as clinging to what belongs to the self, and lists the specific root afflictions — craving, aversion, delusion, pride, and doubt — together with their common offshoots, indignation, spite, concealment, and envy among others, all equally intolerable the instant they surface. He reads hostility as active ill will toward a being, harmful intent as a colder indifference to their wellbeing, and lingering resentment as an old grievance nursed rather than released — genuine patience refuses to let any of the three quietly persist. This closes progressive mastery in giving, discipline, and patience; progress through diligence and meditative concentration follows next.

43. Progress Through Diligence and Meditative Concentration

Progressive mastery continues through diligence, where any laziness or sluggishness that would let a wholesome quality stagnate becomes intolerable; and through meditative concentration, where even a subtle craving for the pleasure of absorption itself, or the pride, wrong view, and doubt that can quietly accompany it, becomes something a bodhisattva refuses to let stand unaddressed.

Chinese Source

又於所起懈怠、懶惰，亦不忍受。又於所起等至味著、等至煩惱，亦不忍受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding any laziness or sluggishness that arises, none of it is tolerated. Regarding any craving for the pleasure of meditative attainment, or any affliction accompanying that attainment, none of it is tolerated either.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes laziness, an unwillingness to push a wholesome practice forward toward completion, from sluggishness, a simple preference for sleep and reclining comfort over sustained, upright practice. He explains craving for meditative attainment as clinging to the genuine joy, bliss, or equanimity that even a first, still-worldly level of absorption can bring, and the accompanying afflictions as pride at having attained it, distorted views arising within that state, and lingering doubt about the three jewels that can still surface even amid otherwise settled concentration — a bodhisattva recognizes all of these as genuine obstacles to true mastery over meditative absorption, refusing to let any of them pass unaddressed simply because the surrounding state feels pleasant or accomplished. This closes progress through diligence and meditative concentration; the five kinds of wisdom completing this progressive structure follow next.

44. Milestone: Five Kinds of Wisdom — Seeing Cause, Effect, and Their Distortion Clearly

The final stage of progress rests on five kinds of genuine wisdom: truly knowing the supreme benefit awakening's fruit actually holds; truly knowing the genuine cause that produces it — the great vow and the precepts received in support of it; truly discerning whether one's own understanding of cause and effect is correct or distorted; and, resting on that same clarity, truly recognizing and swiftly overcoming the deepest obstacle to gathering wholesome practice at all — mistaking what is impermanent, suffering, impure, and without a fixed self for their exact opposites.

Chinese Source

又於五處如實了知。謂如實知善果勝利，又能如實了知善因，又能如實知善因果倒與無倒，又如實知攝善法障。

Ru-Yi Translation

One truly knows five things: truly knowing the supreme benefit of a wholesome fruit; truly knowing its wholesome cause; truly knowing whether one's understanding of that wholesome cause and effect is correct or distorted; and truly knowing what obstructs gathering wholesome practice.

Chinese Source

不於無常妄見為常，不於其苦妄見為樂，不於不淨妄見為淨，不於無我妄見為我，如實了知攝善法障；為攝善故，速疾遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not mistaking what is impermanent for permanent, not mistaking what is suffering for pleasurable, not mistaking what is impure for pure, not mistaking what is without a fixed self for a self — truly recognizing what obstructs gathering wholesome practice this way, and for the sake of gathering it, swiftly overcoming that obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the five wisdoms in turn. The first recognizes unsurpassed awakening's own fruit — the buddha's complete knowledge, the thirty-two excellent marks, and the full range of qualities unique to a buddha — as the supreme benefit worth every effort. The second recognizes the great vow toward awakening and the precepts received in its support as the genuine cause that actually produces this fruit, nothing else. The third and fourth together discern whether one's grasp of that cause-and-effect relationship is genuinely correct — wholesome cause truly yielding pleasant fruit, unwholesome cause truly yielding painful fruit, without contradiction — or has become subtly distorted, mistaking one for the other.

The fifth, and philosophically richest, identifies the deepest obstacle blocking wholesome practice altogether: the four classical distortions of view — treating what is genuinely impermanent as though it endured unchanged, treating what is genuinely painful as though it were pleasurable, treating what is genuinely impure as though it were pure, and treating what genuinely lacks any fixed, independent self as though a fixed self were truly present. He explains the Great Vehicle's own distinctive account of each: impermanence means the fixed characteristics ordinary perception projects onto experience were never genuinely present at all, since they are simply the mind's own conceptual construction; suffering means the deep-seated habitual tendency to take that same projection as real is itself what renders body and mind unfit and burdened; impurity refers specifically to the more subtle obstruction to complete knowledge that still clouds even a bodhisattva's own understanding, distinct from the affliction a solitary path toward liberation alone might already have cleared; and selflessness means not merely that no fixed person can be found, as a more limited path might conclude, but that no phenomenon whatsoever carries any fixed, independent nature of its own. Genuinely seeing through all four distortions is precisely what allows a bodhisattva to remain in the midst of birth and death without being stained by it and without fleeing into a merely personal peace, continuing instead to gather every wholesome practice the path toward complete awakening actually requires. This closes the five kinds of wisdom, completing progress through discipline gathering wholesome practice entirely; a closing summary of all ten marks follows next.

45. Milestone: Abiding in Wholesome Practice Concludes — Ten Marks Named Together

A closing summary gathers all ten marks together: progress through giving, discipline, patience, diligence, and meditative concentration, together with the five kinds of wisdom — this complete structure is what allows a bodhisattva to swiftly and thoroughly gather every kind of wholesome practice, closing the entire account of how discipline gathering wholesome practice is actually lived out day to day.

Chinese Source

菩薩由此十種相故，名住攝善法戒。速能攝善一切種相，謂施漸次、若戒漸次、若忍漸次、若精進漸次、若靜慮漸次，及五種慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, through these ten marks, are said to abide in discipline gathering wholesome practice, swiftly gathering wholesome practice of every kind: progress through giving, progress through discipline, progress through patience, progress through diligence, progress through meditative concentration, and the five kinds of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties each progressive stage back to the specific affliction it directly uproots: giving progressively dissolves stinginess, discipline progressively dissolves the root and secondary afflictions that lead to violation, patience progressively dissolves hostility, diligence progressively dissolves laziness and sluggishness, meditative concentration progressively dissolves craving and pride even within pleasant absorption, and the five wisdoms together dissolve confusion about genuine cause and effect and the four fundamental distortions of view that would otherwise obstruct gathering wholesome practice at its very root. This closes discipline gathering wholesome practice in its entirety, completing all three storehouses' own detailed practice, restraint already covered and this the second; how a bodhisattva abides in and lives out discipline benefiting beings, unfolding again into eleven aspects, opens next.

46. Milestone: Abiding in Benefiting Beings Opens — Being a Genuine Companion

Abiding in discipline benefiting beings unfolds into the same eleven aspects already named, now detailed in actual practice, beginning with being a genuine companion in whatever task a being is engaged in — whether accompanying them on a journey, correctly guiding them when they misunderstand something, protecting their property from theft or disaster, reconciling a broken relationship, joining a genuinely meaningful gathering, or supporting any of the three kinds of merit-making.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩由十一相，名住一切種饒益有情戒。謂諸菩薩，於諸有情彼彼事業，皆為助伴。謂於思量所作事業，及於功用所作事業，悉能與彼而作助伴。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, through eleven aspects, are said to abide in discipline benefiting beings in its every kind. Bodhisattvas become a genuine companion in whatever endeavor beings are engaged in — whether an endeavor arising from careful deliberation or one carried out through active effort, they are able to become a companion in either.

Chinese Source

或於道路若往、若來，或於無倒事業加行，或於守護所有財物，或於和合展轉乖離，或於義會，或於修福，皆為助伴。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether accompanying them on a journey, correcting a distorted approach to some task, protecting their property, reconciling a broken relationship, joining a genuinely meaningful gathering, or supporting an act of merit — one becomes a companion in all of these.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes deliberated action, arising from a being's own careful weighing of what they wish to accomplish, from effortful action, its actual physical execution — a bodhisattva joins in genuinely as companion at either stage, whichever is called for. He lists the concrete circumstances this covers: traveling alongside someone at their own request; correcting a misunderstanding when someone pursues a task in a mistaken way, so that what they actually set out to do is genuinely accomplished; safeguarding hard-won property from theft, fire, or flood; mending a relationship strained by another's divisive words or by the two parties' own drifting apart, offering genuinely warm, reconciling words to each side; joining gatherings genuinely oriented toward worldly or spiritual benefit, such as hearing the teaching together; and supporting any of the three classical forms of merit-making — through giving, through discipline, or through meditative cultivation. This closes the first of the eleven aspects of benefiting beings in actual practice; the remaining ten aspects, each detailed with the same practical care, continue in the treatise's own extensive account still ahead.

47. Milestone: Rescuing From Every Kind of Affliction — Disaster and the Five Hindrances

Being a genuine companion extends further still, into actively rescuing beings from suffering of every kind — beginning with concrete, practical disaster and hardship: attending the sick, guiding the blind, communicating through gesture with the deaf, orienting the lost traveler, providing transport for the disabled, and patiently teaching genuine understanding to the simple-minded — and continuing into the subtler suffering of the five hindrances that block genuine meditative progress, each addressed through its own specific, fitting antidote.

Chinese Source

於諸救苦亦為助伴。謂於遭遇疾疫有情瞻侍供給，盲者啟導，聾者撝義，手代言者曉以想像，迷方路者示以隅途，支不具者惠以荷乘，其愚騃者誨以勝慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

One also becomes a companion in rescuing beings from suffering: attending and providing for those afflicted by illness or epidemic; guiding the blind; communicating meaning to the deaf; making oneself understood to those who speak by hand-gesture; showing the way to a traveler lost in an unfamiliar place; providing transport for those with a physical disability; and teaching genuine wisdom to those of simple understanding.

Chinese Source

為貪欲纏所苦有情，開解令離貪欲纏苦。如是若為瞋恚、惛沈睡眠、掉舉惡作、疑纏所苦有情，開解令離疑纏等苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward beings suffering under the grip of craving, one explains and resolves it, freeing them from that suffering. In the same way, toward those suffering under aversion, torpor and drowsiness, restlessness and worry, or doubt, one explains and resolves each, freeing them from that suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the disaster list as covering exactly the practical, unglamorous forms of help that genuinely matter most in daily life — patient companionship for the sick, physical guidance for the blind, hand-signed communication for the deaf, simple direction for someone lost, and, for those slower to grasp new ideas, patient, repeated teaching rather than impatience, since being of limited understanding is never a moral failing needing correction so much as a circumstance calling for extra care.

He explains the five hindrances through their own classical causes and antidotes: craving arises from dwelling on an attractive appearance without examining it critically, and is countered by contemplating the body's own actual impurity; aversion arises from dwelling on an unwelcome appearance, and is countered by cultivating genuine loving-kindness; torpor and drowsiness arise from a habitually dull, unlit state of mind, and are countered by contemplating actual and metaphorical light; restlessness and worry arise from replaying old memories of pleasure or distraction, and are countered by the nine mental abidings already described elsewhere in the treatise; and doubt arises from a lingering, unresolved grip of self-clinging and conceptual clinging alike, countered by contemplating dependent origination and the emptiness both of self and of phenomena. A bodhisattva, understanding all five clearly, is able to diagnose precisely which hindrance actually troubles a given practitioner and offer exactly the teaching that resolves it, rather than a generic response. This closes rescue from disaster and the five hindrances; rescue from unwholesome discursive thought, from the sting of comparison, and from simple travel fatigue follow next.

48. Eight Kinds of Unwholesome Thought, the Sting of Comparison, and Simple Travel Fatigue

Rescue from suffering continues through resolving eight classical kinds of restless, unwholesome discursive thought — desire, hostility, harm, homeland, family, denial of mortality, comparison, and social standing — through easing the sting felt when belittled or outmatched by another, and through the simplest, most concrete relief of all: offering a weary traveler a seat, a place to rest, and a gentle massage.

Chinese Source

欲尋思纏所苦有情，開解令離欲尋思苦。如欲尋思，恚、害、親里、國土、不死、輕侮相應、族姓相應所有尋思，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward beings troubled by desire-oriented discursive thought, one explains and resolves it, freeing them from that suffering. As with desire-oriented thought, so too with thoughts of hostility, harm, homeland, family, denial of mortality, comparison, and social standing.

Chinese Source

他懱、他勝所苦有情，開解令離被懱、勝苦。行路疲乏所苦有情，施座施處，調身按摩，令其止息勞倦眾苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward beings suffering from being belittled or overpowered by another, one explains and resolves it, freeing them from that suffering. Toward beings suffering exhaustion from travel, one offers a seat and a place to rest, adjusting and massaging the body, so that their weariness is genuinely eased.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the eight kinds of discursive thought directly: dwelling on sensual craving, on ill will toward another, on causing harm, on one's old home and community, on family ties, on postponing genuine practice under the comforting illusion that death remains distant, on comparing one's own status against others', and on cultivating connections with wealthy patrons — all eight scattering the mind and pulling it away from settled practice, each requiring its own patient, individually tailored explanation to actually resolve. He treats the sting of being belittled or outmatched as a genuinely common, ordinary suffering worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, since comparison and its resulting wounded pride trouble beginning practitioners constantly, and reads simple physical fatigue as needing no elaborate philosophy at all — sometimes a weary traveler's real need is exactly what it appears to be, a seat, a rest, and gentle care for an aching body. This closes rescue from suffering in all its forms; teaching the true dharma appropriately, matched to a listener's actual disposition, follows next.

49. Milestone: Teaching Each According to Need — Principled and Accommodating Speech

The second major aspect of benefiting beings is teaching the true dharma itself, delivered in one of two registers depending entirely on the listener's own readiness: principled teaching, pointing all the way toward liberation through coherent, well-ordered, genuinely useful, timely language matched to the four modes of genuine reasoning, offered to beings genuinely ready for it regardless of whether they are presently inclined toward wrongdoing, stinginess, or even open hostility toward the teaching itself; and accommodating teaching, offered instead when a listener is not yet ready for anything so direct.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，為諸有情如理宣說。謂於樂行惡行有情，為欲令斷諸惡行故，以相應文句、助伴、隨順、清亮、有用、相稱、應順、常委分資糧法而為宣說。或復方便善巧宣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas teach beings in accordance with genuine principle. Toward beings inclined to wrongdoing, in order to bring that wrongdoing to an end, they teach using coherent language, mutually supporting points, a natural progression, clarity, genuine usefulness, fitting relevance, timeliness, and the sustained, thorough presentation of the very qualities that support the path — or, alternatively, they teach through skillful accommodation instead.

Chinese Source

如於樂行惡行有情，為欲令斷諸惡行故；如是於行慳行有情，為欲令彼斷慳行故；於現法中求財寶者，為欲令彼正少功力，集多財寶，守護無失；於佛聖教懷憎嫉者，為欲令彼得清淨信，證清淨見，超諸惡趣，盡一切結，越一切苦；應知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as with beings inclined to wrongdoing, so too with beings inclined to stinginess, taught so as to end that stinginess; with beings presently seeking wealth, taught so that they gather it rightly with little effort and keep it safe; and with beings who resent or feel hostility toward the Buddha's teaching, taught so that they attain pure faith, realize a pure view, rise above the miserable realms, and ultimately exhaust every fetter and cross beyond all suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes principled teaching, pointing directly and ultimately toward liberation, from accommodating teaching, meeting a listener not yet ready for anything so direct with simpler discourse on giving, discipline, or a favorable rebirth instead — both are genuinely valuable, matched to where a given listener actually stands rather than imposing one register indiscriminately on everyone. He unpacks the many qualities principled teaching should embody: language whose terms are carefully, logically ordered and consistent with the four modes of genuine reasoning; content that mutually reinforces its own overall point; an order that unfolds naturally from one idea to the next; clarity that leaves nothing obscure; genuine, practical usefulness rather than empty erudition; relevance to the specific audience gathered; timeliness matched to the actual occasion; and a thorough, sustained presentation of exactly the supporting qualities — right view chief among them — that genuinely carry a listener toward the noble path.

He walks through the same principled approach matched to four specific dispositions: teaching on discipline directly counters a tendency toward wrongdoing; teaching on giving, through the nine aspects already covered at length in the previous chapter, directly counters stinginess; teaching on honest livelihood and practical skill helps a wealth-seeker gather resources rightly and keep them secure; and teaching the four noble truths, patiently offered even to someone presently hostile toward the tradition, can gradually kindle genuine faith, then a clear, correct view free of distorted conceptualization — marked, he notes, by visible signs like the hair standing on end or tears of genuine emotion — carrying that listener step by step toward stream-entry, freedom from rebirth in the miserable realms, and ultimately, cutting through every one of the five lower and five higher fetters, complete liberation from all suffering whatsoever. This closes teaching matched to a listener's disposition; knowing and repaying kindness, unfolding into its own concrete gestures of warmth and generosity, follows next.

50. Milestone: Knowing and Repaying Kindness — Warm Welcome, Generous Repayment, Unbidden Help

The third aspect is genuine gratitude toward anyone who has ever shown kindness, expressed through concrete, warm gestures rather than mere private feeling: greeting a benefactor with genuine delight and a cheerful welcome, offering them the best seat with real courtesy, repaying their past generosity with something equal or greater rather than something lesser, and helping with their own endeavors even when they have not asked — let alone when they actually have.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於其有恩諸有情所深知恩惠，常思酬報。……暫見申敬，讚言善來，怡顏歡慰，吐誠談謔；祥處設座，正筵令坐。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward anyone who has shown them kindness, genuinely recognize that debt and constantly consider how to repay it. ... The moment they see such a person, they show genuine respect, exclaiming warmly, "how wonderful that you have come," greeting them with a cheerful expression and sincere, easy conversation, offering a fitting place and a properly arranged seat.

Chinese Source

若等若增財利供養現前酬答，非以下劣。於彼事業雖不求請，尚應伴助，況乎有命。

Ru-Yi Translation

They repay with material support equal to or greater than what was given, never with something lesser. Toward that benefactor's own endeavors, even without being asked, they should still help — how much more so, then, when actually asked.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this aspect as insisting genuine gratitude always show itself concretely rather than remaining a private sentiment never actually expressed — a warm greeting, an honest smile, offering the better seat, are small gestures that nonetheless make gratitude real and felt by the person receiving it. He notes repaying with something equal or greater, never less, reflects the treatise's own consistent standard that a bodhisattva's generosity should never appear to shrink over time, and that helping a benefactor's own endeavors proactively, before being asked at all, demonstrates a depth of attentiveness that waiting to be asked never quite matches. This closes the specific gestures of gratitude; how this same principle extends further, to benefactors in more difficult or complicated circumstances, continues in the treatise's own account still ahead.

51. Milestone: Rescuing From Every Kind of Fear

Gratitude's same careful attention extends across every remaining aspect of benefiting beings, and rescuing from fear opens next, covering a strikingly comprehensive range of what genuinely frightens beings: predatory animals, flood and fire, oppressive rulers and bandits, personal enemies, domineering household heads, corrupt officials, the fear of simply not surviving, the fear of a ruined reputation, unease before a crowd, being overwhelmed by another's sheer authority, and encounters with non-human beings, reanimated corpses, or malevolent spirits — every one of them met with genuine rescue and restored peace of mind.

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas are able to rescue beings caught in fear — fear of predatory animals, of flood and fire, of oppressive rulers and bandits, of enemies, of a domineering household head, of corrupt officials, fear of not surviving, of a ruined reputation, of a crowd, of being overwhelmed by authority, and fear of non-human beings, reanimated corpses, or malevolent spirits — rescuing beings from all of these, restoring their peace of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the fear of not surviving as arising from genuine lack of the resources needed to live, the fear of a ruined reputation as arising from craving others' good opinion strongly enough that its loss becomes terrifying, unease before a crowd as arising from a lack of inner confidence when surrounded by many people at once, and being overwhelmed by authority as arising from encountering someone or something whose sheer power leaves one feeling powerless by comparison. He notes the sheer range of this list — from wild animals to social anxiety to supernatural dread — reflects genuine attentiveness to how varied human fear actually is, none of it dismissed as too mundane or too strange to warrant a bodhisattva's genuine concern and rescue. This closes rescue from fear; resolving grief over lost family and lost wealth follows next.

52. Milestone: Resolving Grief Over Lost Family and Lost Wealth

Resolving grief covers two major sources of genuine sorrow: the loss of family, whether by blood or by spiritual bond — parents, siblings, spouse, household staff, elders, friends, extended relatives, or one's own preceptor and teachers — and the loss of wealth, through robbery, fire, flood, fraud, failed enterprise, a predatory relative, or a household's own internal conflict and waste — met, whatever its severity, with genuine, skillful comfort.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於處衰惱諸有情類，能善開解，令離愁憂。或依親屬有所衰亡。所謂父母、兄弟、妻子、奴婢、僮僕、宗長、朋友、內外族因、親教、軌範及餘尊重，時有喪亡，善為開解，令離憂惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas are able to skillfully comfort beings genuinely grieving, freeing them from sorrow. Whether grieving the loss of family — parents, siblings, spouse, household staff, elders, friends, extended relatives, one's own preceptor, instructing teacher, or other respected figures — they skillfully comfort them, freeing them from grief.

Chinese Source

Whatever the resulting grief's degree, mild, moderate, or severe, bodhisattvas are able to rightly comfort it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes family lost by blood relation from family lost by spiritual bond — one's own preceptor or teacher, and fellow practitioners on the same path, grieved with the same genuine depth as a birth relative — since both kinds of relationship carry real, felt significance worth honoring in loss. He treats the specific causes of lost wealth as covering the full realistic range a household might actually face, from outright theft or natural disaster through more insidious losses like fraud or a family's own internal conflict quietly draining its resources — noting that regardless of cause or severity, a bodhisattva's response remains the same skillful, attentive comfort, adjusted in intensity to match how deeply the grief actually runs. This closes resolving grief; giving whatever is genuinely needed follows next.

53. Giving Whatever Is Genuinely Needed

The sixth aspect is simple and direct: keeping life's necessities on hand and giving exactly what is asked for — food, drink, transport, clothing, ornaments, everyday goods, perfume and garlands, lodging, or light — matched precisely to the request.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，備資生具，隨有來求，即皆施與。謂諸有情求食與食，求飲與飲，求乘與乘，求衣與衣，求莊嚴具施莊嚴具，求諸什物施以什物，求鬘塗香施鬘塗香，求止憩處施止憩處，求諸光明施以光明。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas keep life's necessities ready, giving them the moment they are requested: food for one seeking food, drink for one seeking drink, transport, clothing, ornaments, everyday goods, perfume and garlands, lodging, or light, each given exactly as requested.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this aspect simply restates, within the frame of actual practice, the identical principle already established at length in the previous chapter on giving — practical readiness matched to a genuine, specific request, given promptly rather than delayed. This closes giving life's necessities; leading a community rightly, unfolding into both material and teaching-based support, follows next.

54. Milestone: Leading a Community Rightly — Material Support Given Without Hidden Motive

The seventh aspect is genuinely leading a community entrusted to one's care, teaching each of five kinds of follower — householders, renunciants, the already faithful, the hostile, and those in between — according to what actually fits them, and providing material support entirely free of any hidden motive: never taking someone in merely to secure their service, never staging a rescue purely to bind them into obligation, and, once resources are gathered — whether from one's own means or from a generous, faithful patron — sharing them equally with the whole community rather than hoarding any part privately.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，性好攝受諸有情類，如法御眾，方便饒益。……以無染心先與依止，以憐愍心現作饒益，然後給施如法衣服、飲食、臥具、病緣醫藥資身什物。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, by nature delighting in embracing beings, rightly lead their community according to the teaching, benefiting them through skillful means. ... With a mind entirely free of any hidden motive, they first provide support, acting for genuine benefit out of real compassion, and only then give lawfully obtained clothing, food, bedding, medicine, and other necessities.

Chinese Source

若自無有，應從淨信長者、居士、婆羅門等求索與之。於己以法所獲如法衣服、飲食、諸坐臥具、病緣醫藥資身什物，與眾同用，自無隱費。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one has nothing oneself, one should seek it from a faithful patron of means. Whatever is lawfully obtained in this way — clothing, food, bedding, medicine, and other necessities — is shared equally with the whole community; none of it is quietly kept back for private use.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the five kinds of follower a genuine leader adjusts their teaching for: householders, taught through praising wholesome and condemning unwholesome worldly conduct; renunciants, taught the three trainings directly, since their concern is liberation rather than worldly comfort; the already faithful, taught the teaching's own grandeur to deepen that faith further; those hostile to the teaching or genuinely in between, each led gradually toward greater confidence through whatever approach actually reaches them.

He stresses the treatise's own pointed warning against a subtler corruption possible even in apparently generous leadership: taking in a follower purely to secure their labor, or even staging what looks like a rescue from difficult circumstances elsewhere purely to bind that person into personal service afterward — neither counts as genuine compassion or genuine benefit at all, however charitable it might appear from outside. Genuine material support, whether from a leader's own resources or gathered by asking a generous, faithful patron for help, is then shared equally among the whole community rather than any portion being secretly reserved for private use — communal integrity, he notes, is itself part of what makes support genuinely lawful and clean. This closes material support; teaching-based support, tied directly to cultivating the seeds of the buddha's own powers, follows next.

55. Milestone: Teaching-Based Support — Cultivating the Buddha's Own Powers

Leading a community rightly also means providing teaching-based support at every opportunity, through the eight kinds of instruction and five kinds of admonishment already detailed at length in the chapter on the lineage of the powers — practices that, the treatise reminds its reader, are never merely pedagogical technique, but the very means by which a bodhisattva cultivates the seeds of the buddha's own ten powers, applied here specifically toward followers of genuine bodhisattva disposition, though a bodhisattva's compassion in fact embraces every being without exception.

Chinese Source

於時時間，以其隨順八種教授而正教授，五種教誡而正教誡。此中所說教授、教誡，當知如前力種性品已廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

At every fitting opportunity, one rightly instructs through the eight kinds of instruction, and rightly admonishes through the five kinds of admonishment. This instruction and admonishment should be understood exactly as already explained at length in the chapter on the lineage of the powers.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recalls the eight kinds of instruction directly: the five gateways of practice matched to a student's dominant affliction, the two correctives against eternalism and nihilism, and removing the presumption of false attainment — and the five kinds of admonishment: prohibiting, permitting, gentle correction, firm reproof, and praise. He stresses the treatise's own explicit point that engaging in this teaching is precisely what cultivates a bodhisattva's own seeds of the buddha's remaining eight powers, exactly as explained when these practices were first introduced — teaching others is never merely a service rendered outward, but simultaneously the very practice that ripens the teacher's own path toward complete awakening.

He clarifies one important nuance in how this aspect is framed here: while a bodhisattva's compassion and willingness to embrace beings extends to absolutely everyone, without exception, the specific instruction and admonishment named here is described in terms suited to followers of genuine bodhisattva disposition in particular, since the framework directly connects to cultivating the bodhisattva's own path toward buddhahood — this is simply a matter of the passage's own particular emphasis, not a limit on who a bodhisattva actually cares for. This closes both material and teaching-based support, completing leading a community rightly in its entirety; going along with a follower's own genuine nature, unfolding into knowing their disposition and then actually dwelling and practicing alongside them, follows next.

56. Milestone: Going Along With a Follower's Own Nature — Knowing Their Disposition, Then Practicing Alongside Them

The eighth aspect is genuinely accommodating a follower's own disposition: first coming to know their underlying nature — whether inclined toward the path of individual liberation, solitary realization, or the bodhisattva path itself, and whether sharp or dull in faculty, dominated by one affliction or another — and then, once genuinely understood, actually dwelling together and practicing together in whatever manner truly fits that disposition, rather than imposing one uniform approach on everyone alike.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於有情心性好隨轉。隨心轉時，先知有情若體若性。知體性已，隨諸有情所應共住，即應如是與其共住；隨諸有情所應同行，即應如是與彼同行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in their disposition, delight in going along with beings' own hearts. In going along, they first come to know a being's own underlying nature and character. Having known it, they dwell together in whatever way genuinely fits that being, and practice together in whatever way genuinely fits them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains a being's underlying nature as covering whether they carry the potential for genuine liberation at all, and if so, along which of the three paths — individual liberation, solitary realization, or the bodhisattva path — while their character covers finer distinctions like sharpness of faculty and which affliction dominates. Knowing this clearly, a bodhisattva adjusts both companionship and practice accordingly: someone drawn toward worldly merit is joined in cultivating merit, someone drawn toward quiet, still practice is joined in solitude and calm, and someone drawn toward actively benefiting others is joined in exactly that active service — dwelling together means genuinely sharing resources and the six forms of harmonious community life, while practicing together means jointly undertaking the specific activity and encouraging one another in it. This closes knowing a follower's nature and practicing accordingly; a subtler further consideration — carefully weighing one's own words and actions so correction never causes needless distress — follows next.

57. Milestone: Weighing Whether Correction Genuinely Helps — And a Striking Exception When Compassion Requires Breaking the Rule

Correcting an offender always rests on a careful, three-part weighing: first, whether the correction will actually help this particular offender change, withheld if it would only cause distress without any real hope of improvement, carried out with genuine compassion if it would; second, whether it might needlessly distress bystanders witnessing it, weighed the same way; and third, whether the corrective act itself would violate a bodhisattva's own proper conduct — with one striking exception the treatise itself allows: when breaking even a grave precept becomes the only way to prevent a far greater harm, compassion alone determines the act is blameless.

Chinese Source

如是憂苦若能令其出不善處安立善處，菩薩爾時於如是事現行身語，住哀愍心，不隨如是有情心轉，方便思擇勵力策發要令現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

If such distress is genuinely able to draw the offender out of an unwholesome state and settle them in a wholesome one, the bodhisattva does carry out the correction — abiding in a mind of genuine pity, not swayed by the offender's own reaction, deliberately and resolutely bringing the correction about.

Chinese Source

若於如是他有情事現行身語令餘有情發生憂苦，……菩薩爾時於如是事現行身語，護餘心故，方便思擇勵力遮止令不現行。……謂諸菩薩為利他故，於諸性罪少分現行，由是因緣，於菩薩戒無所違犯，生多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should carrying out that same correction cause distress to onlookers as well, ... the bodhisattva, in order to protect their minds, deliberately withholds it instead. ... Bodhisattvas, for the sake of benefiting others, may in specific circumstances even engage in a small portion of what would ordinarily be a genuinely wrong act — through this very cause, they do not violate the bodhisattva precepts at all, and generate great merit instead.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the layered decision carefully: correcting someone genuinely capable of learning from it, even if it causes them real distress in the moment, is worth doing precisely because it draws them toward something better; but correcting someone who simply cannot benefit from it in the moment only adds pain without any redemptive purpose, and a bodhisattva withholds the correction instead, choosing patient silence over pointless harshness. He extends the same weighing to bystanders — a correction that helps the offender but genuinely distresses onlookers without benefiting anyone requires the same restraint, protecting their minds too.

He recounts the striking classical account the treatise itself offers to illustrate the third and final consideration: a bodhisattva traveling among five hundred merchants by ship discovers one of them secretly plotting to murder the rest for their wealth. With no other way to prevent the massacre, the bodhisattva kills that single plotter first — an act that would ordinarily constitute the gravest possible violation, taking a life — yet the treatise holds this incurs no violation of the bodhisattva precepts whatsoever, since it was undertaken purely to save five hundred lives from a certain murderer, out of genuine compassion rather than personal gain or malice. He stresses this exception is narrow and deliberate, never a general license for convenient rule-breaking — it applies only where the compassionate motive is entirely pure and no other means exists to prevent a clearly greater harm. This closes weighing correction that causes distress; the identical weighing applied to bringing about joy rather than sorrow follows next.

58. The Same Careful Weighing Applies to Bringing Joy, Not Just Sorrow

The identical three-part weighing applies symmetrically when an action would bring someone joy rather than distress: a bodhisattva acts only if that joy genuinely helps draw the person toward a wholesome state, never simply because pleasing someone feels agreeable in the moment, applying the same care toward the person directly involved, toward bystanders, and toward the bodhisattva's own proper conduct throughout.

Chinese Source

如生憂苦，如是廣說生於喜樂，隨其所應，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Exactly as explained regarding causing distress, so too, in full detail, regarding bringing about joy — it should be understood as governed by the very same considerations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this symmetry closes an easy loophole: a bodhisattva might otherwise assume that anything making someone happy is automatically fine to do, when in fact the same underlying standard applies — pleasure offered without any genuine benefit accomplishes nothing worthwhile, however agreeable it feels in the moment, while pleasure that genuinely helps someone move toward a wholesome state is worth bringing about deliberately. This closes the weighing framework in both directions; going along with every being more broadly, beginning with never provoking someone already caught in anger, follows next.

59. Milestone: Going Along With Every Being — Never Provoking Anger, Always Reciprocating Celebration

Accommodating beings more broadly opens with two specific practices: never praising or criticizing anyone in front of a person currently caught in the grip of anger, since nothing said in that moment could possibly land well; and actively initiating warm conversation and genuine celebration toward others even when they themselves never think to offer it first, let alone failing to reciprocate when they do.

Chinese Source

又隨他心而轉菩薩，知他有情忿纏所纏，現前忿纏難可捨離，尚不讚歎，何況毀訾；即於爾時亦不諫誨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas going along with beings, knowing that someone is presently gripped by anger not easily released in the moment, do not even offer praise, let alone criticism; nor, at that same moment, do they attempt to counsel or correct them.

Chinese Source

又隨他心而轉菩薩，他雖不來談論慶慰，尚應自往談論慶慰，何況彼來而不酬報。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas going along with beings, even when another does not initiate conversation or celebration, should still initiate it themselves — how much more so, then, reciprocating when the other person does come to them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first point through simple realism about anger's own nature: a mind gripped by fury cannot absorb praise or correction in that moment, however true or well-intentioned, since the anger itself acts as a wall nothing else can get through — patient silence, waiting until the anger has genuinely passed, accomplishes far more than words spoken directly into it. He reads the second point as an expression of a bodhisattva's own natural warmth toward others, actively reaching out with genuine, friendly interest and celebrating others' good fortune without waiting to be prompted, and certainly reciprocating warmly whenever someone reaches out first. This closes these two practices; never deliberately provoking distress except in genuine correction, never humiliating another, and never growing prideful from someone's own deference follow next.

60. Milestone: Never Deliberately Provoking Distress, Never Humiliating Another, Never Growing Prideful

Three further practices: never deliberately causing another distress except when actually correcting a genuine wrongdoing, and even then doing so calmly, with a settled, compassionate mind rather than reactive irritation; never humiliating someone even after genuinely besting them in debate, allowing them to save face rather than rubbing in the defeat; and never growing prideful simply because someone treats oneself with great deference or humility.

Chinese Source

又隨他心而轉菩薩，終不故意惱觸於他，唯除訶責諸犯過者，起慈悲心，諸根寂靜，如應訶責令其調伏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas going along with beings never deliberately provoke distress in another, except when reproaching someone who has genuinely erred — and even then, giving rise to compassion, the senses remaining calm, reproaching only as fitting, so as to help them settle their own conduct.

Chinese Source

又隨他心而轉菩薩，終不嗤誚輕弄於他，令其赧愧不安隱住，亦不令其心生憂悔。雖能摧伏得勝於彼，而不彰其墮在負處。彼雖淨信，生於謙下，終不現相而起自高。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas going along with beings never mock or belittle another, causing them shame or lingering regret. Even having genuinely bested another in debate, they never make a show of that person's defeat. And even when another, out of genuine faith, treats them with great humility, they never let that go to their head.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the calm, compassionate manner required even in legitimate reproach as distinguishing genuine correction from mere venting of one's own irritation — the senses staying settled means a bodhisattva never lets even justified reproach curdle into contempt for the person being corrected, addressing only the actual fault rather than piling on. He reads sparing someone the humiliation of open defeat as basic dignity extended even to an opponent one has genuinely outargued, since winning a point is not the same as needing to grind someone's face in having lost it. And he reads guarding against pride at another's deference as a caution against a subtle trap — genuine humility shown by others can easily curdle into a bodhisattva's own quiet vanity if not actively watched for, however unintentionally that deference was ever meant to inflate anyone's ego. This closes these three practices; appropriate closeness and appropriate, graceful speech, closing all six practices of accommodating every being, follow next.

61. Milestone: Appropriate Closeness, Careful Speech, and Honoring Commitments With Grace

The final two practices close this account: staying neither aloof nor overly clingy with any being, and drawing close only at genuinely fitting moments; and speaking with real care — never criticizing what someone loves, never praising what they resent, staying appropriately reserved with those not yet close, never overburdening a casual acquaintance with repeated requests, and, having once promised to share a meal or keep an appointment, never quietly abandoning it, offering instead a warm, honest, graceful explanation whenever circumstances genuinely prevent it.

Chinese Source

又隨他心而轉菩薩，於諸有情非不親近、不極親近，亦不非時而相親近。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas going along with beings are never aloof, never excessively clingy, and never draw close to anyone at an unfitting moment.

Chinese Source

又隨他心而轉菩薩，終不現前毀他所愛，亦不現前讚他非愛；非情交者不吐實誠，不屢希望，知量而受。若先許應他飲食等，終無假託不赴先祈，為性謙沖如法曉喻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas going along with beings never criticize, in front of someone, what that person loves, nor praise, in front of them, what they resent; with those not yet close, they do not speak with excessive intimacy, do not make repeated requests, and accept what is offered only in fitting measure. Having once promised to share a meal or similar occasion, they never quietly excuse themselves from it under some pretext, always explaining themselves humbly and honestly if genuinely unable to attend.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads appropriate closeness as a matter of genuine social sensitivity — a bodhisattva participates in ordinary life freely, but never forces intimacy on someone not ready for it, and never withdraws so completely as to seem cold or unreachable, adjusting to the actual moment rather than a fixed rule. He reads careful speech as protecting relationships from needless strain — criticizing a person's own loved one in front of them, or praising their known enemy, serves no purpose but to wound, and a bodhisattva simply avoids it; with someone not yet a close friend, oversharing intimate matters or leaning on them too often for favors risks straining a bond not yet strong enough to bear it.

He treats honoring commitments as a small but telling test of genuine integrity, offering the treatise's own graceful script for the rare occasion an obligation truly cannot be kept: it is not that I did not wish to come, only that circumstances genuinely prevented it — please do not hold this against me. This closes all six practices of accommodating every being, completing the eighth aspect of benefiting beings in its full detail; praising another's genuine virtue, unfolding into the five spiritual treasures and a striking true account of unwavering faith, follows next.

62. Milestone: Praising Genuine Virtue — The Five Spiritual Treasures, and a Lay Scholar's Unshakeable Bows

The ninth aspect is a bodhisattva's own natural delight in praising whatever genuine virtue is actually present — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, or wisdom — each praised in whoever genuinely embodies it, so that the person recognized feels real encouragement to continue growing in exactly that quality, illustrated through a striking account of a modern lay Buddhist scholar whose faith in the monastic community proved so unwavering that not even open mockery could shake it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩性好讚揚真實功德，令他歡喜。於信功德具足者前，讚揚信德令其歡喜；於戒功德具足者前，讚揚戒德令其歡喜；於聞功德具足者前，讚揚聞德令其歡喜；於捨功德具足者前，讚揚捨德令其歡喜；於慧功德具足者前，讚揚慧德令其歡喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, by nature, delight in praising genuine virtue, bringing others joy. Before one genuinely possessing the virtue of faith, they praise that virtue of faith, bringing joy; before one possessing discipline, they praise discipline; before one possessing learning, they praise learning; before one possessing generosity, they praise generosity; before one possessing wisdom, they praise wisdom — each bringing joy in turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names these five together, with shame and conscience, as the seven classical spiritual treasures, each defined by the specific affliction it overcomes and the specific genuine joy it brings — faith overcoming hostility toward the teaching and bringing the settled joy of genuine confidence; discipline overcoming a tendency toward the lower realms and bringing the joy of favorable circumstance; learning overcoming confusion about dependent origination and bringing the joy of genuine understanding; generosity overcoming stinginess and bringing the assurance of future sufficiency; and wisdom overcoming narrow, limited understanding and bringing the profound joy of genuinely realizing how things actually are.

He illustrates the virtue of faith through a striking twentieth-century account of the lay Buddhist scholar Zhou Shujia, a well-regarded author of works on consciousness-only philosophy, whose reverence for the monastic community was so complete that he bowed to every monk or nun he encountered without exception, whether senior and celebrated or utterly unknown, whether outwardly refined or shabbily dressed — treating the robe itself, not the individual's personal reputation, as what genuinely deserved reverence. After selling his own family estate to fund two Buddhist academies following wartime upheaval, he continued bowing to the monastic teachers even after becoming a respected instructor there himself; monks who knew him would sometimes mock him from a balcony as "muddled Zhou" as he arrived, and he would simply bow to them anyway, entirely unmoved by the ridicule. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this kind of faith — untouched by whether it is met with respect or mockery, unconcerned with the personal merit of whoever happens to wear the robe — is genuinely rare even among learned, accomplished lay practitioners, most of whom would resist bowing so readily to an unknown or junior monastic, let alone continue doing so after being openly laughed at for it. This closes the virtue of faith and its illustration; the remaining four virtues, each with their own classical praise, continue in the treatise's own account still ahead.
