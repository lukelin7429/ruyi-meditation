Fascicle 40 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十 · The Chapter on the Perfection of Discipline Opens

The Perfection of Discipline

With the perfection of giving now fully described, the treatise turns to the second of the six perfections — the bodhisattva precepts themselves, foundation of the entire noble path, unpacked through the same nine classical aspects already used for giving. This fascicle opens with discipline's own intrinsic nature: the four defining qualities that make the bodhisattva precepts what they genuinely are.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 40 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Chapter on Discipline Opens — Nine Aspects, the Same Framework as Giving

With the entire chapter on giving now complete, the treatise turns to the second of the six perfections: the bodhisattva precepts, foundation of every stage of the noble path, a topic so extensive it spans this fascicle and the two that follow. Just as giving was unpacked through nine classical aspects, discipline is unpacked through the very same nine, set out in an identical summarizing verse.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩戒波羅蜜多？嗢柁南曰：自性一切難，一切門善士，一切種遂求，二世樂清淨。如是九種相，是名略說戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's perfection of discipline? The summarizing verse states: intrinsic nature, comprehensiveness, difficulty, universal scope, purity of motive, completeness across every circumstance, responsiveness to what is asked for, benefit in both this life and the next, and freedom from taint — these nine aspects are, in brief, called discipline.

Chinese Source

謂九種相戒，名為菩薩戒波羅蜜多。一、自性戒，二、一切戒，三、難行戒，四、一切門戒，五、善士戒，六、一切種戒，七、遂求戒，八、此世他世樂戒，九、清淨戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

These nine aspects of discipline are called a bodhisattva's perfection of discipline: first, discipline in its intrinsic nature; second, comprehensive discipline; third, difficult discipline; fourth, discipline through every gateway; fifth, discipline of a genuinely good person; sixth, discipline complete in every kind; seventh, discipline that fulfills what is asked; eighth, discipline that brings joy in this life and the next; ninth, pure discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi situates this chapter within the treatise's own larger architecture: discipline is the tenth of eighteen sections comprising the treatise's foundational account of a bodhisattva's causal path toward buddhahood, and, following giving, the second of the six perfections cultivated under genuine learning's own broader structure. He notes discipline's own scope is vast enough to occupy this entire fascicle and roughly a third of the next two combined — a reflection of how foundational right conduct is to every later stage of practice, since without it, meditative concentration and wisdom alike lack the stable ground they need to actually develop. He previews each of the nine aspects briefly: intrinsic nature names discipline's own defining character; comprehensiveness distinguishes its many forms, for both householders and renunciants, organized around restraint, cultivating wholesome practice, and benefiting beings; difficulty names three specific ways discipline is hard-won; universal gateway names four channels through which discipline is received and practiced; a good person's discipline names conduct arising from a genuinely wholesome character; complete discipline organizes its content into six or seven further categories; discipline that fulfills what is asked addresses both the object of practice and its actual undertaking; discipline bringing joy in both lives names its benefit across time; and pure discipline names conduct entirely free of fault. This closes the ninefold overview; discipline's own intrinsic nature, unpacked first, follows next.

2. Milestone: Discipline's Intrinsic Nature Opens — Four Defining Qualities

The bodhisattva precepts, in their own intrinsic nature, rest on four defining qualities: properly received from a qualified preceptor, held with a genuinely good and pure aspiration, purified again through confession whenever a lapse occurs, and, in the rare and ideal case, held with such deep reverence and focused mindfulness that no violation ever occurs at all.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩自性戒？謂若略說具四功德，當知是名菩薩自性戒。何等為四？一、從他正受，二、善淨意樂，三、犯已還淨，四、深敬專念無有違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's discipline in its intrinsic nature? In brief, possessing four qualities is what should be understood as a bodhisattva's discipline in its intrinsic nature. What are the four? First, properly received from another; second, a genuinely good and pure aspiration; third, purified again after a lapse; fourth, deep reverence and focused mindfulness with no violation at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these four qualities as together defining what genuinely makes a set of precepts "bodhisattva discipline" rather than mere personal resolve — the precepts must actually be received through a proper transmission, held with the right underlying motivation, kept clean through honest correction when broken, and, ideally, kept with a devotion so complete that correction never even becomes necessary. He notes the first quality requires a qualified preceptor — someone with the wisdom and ability to genuinely teach and clarify the precepts, who has already made the great vow toward awakening, and who already holds the bodhisattva precepts themselves — since transmission from someone lacking these qualifications would not genuinely convey the precepts at all. This closes the overview of all four qualities; each is unpacked in turn, beginning with proper reception, next.

3. Milestone: Properly Received, and Held With a Genuinely Good Aspiration

The first quality, proper reception, requires the precepts be formally requested and received from a qualified preceptor — one with genuine wisdom and the ability to teach the precepts clearly, who has already made the great vow toward awakening and already holds the bodhisattva precepts themselves; and the second, a genuinely good and pure aspiration, requires the very same aspiration toward buddhahood above and toward saving beings below that continues, in ever-deepening form, all the way through every stage of the path to its final completion.

Chinese Source

從他正受者：他謂有智有力於語表義能授能開、已發大願同法菩薩。於如是等功德具足勝菩薩所，如法求請授與淨戒，乃至廣說，是名正受。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Received from another" means: another who has the wisdom and ability to convey and clarify the meaning of the precepts through language, who has made the great vow and holds the same teaching as a fellow bodhisattva. Toward such a person, fully possessing these qualities, one properly requests and receives the pure precepts in accordance with the teaching, as explained in full detail elsewhere — this is called proper reception.

Chinese Source

善淨意樂者：增上意樂品說：菩薩到究竟地所有一切增上意樂，是名菩薩善淨意樂。由願當證而作是說，非已證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"A genuinely good and pure aspiration" means: as the chapter on elevated aspiration explains, the entirety of the elevated aspiration a bodhisattva holds all the way through the final ground is called a bodhisattva's genuinely good and pure aspiration. This is spoken of in terms of the aspiration toward what is yet to be realized, not what has already been realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains proper reception's own careful requirements: a genuine preceptor must be capable of actually explaining the precepts' meaning through language, not merely reciting them, must have already made the great bodhisattva vow, and must already hold the precepts personally — someone lacking any of these cannot genuinely transmit them. A prospective student, not yet having received the precepts, formally requests them three times from such a qualified, more advanced bodhisattva, following the proper procedure described in detail elsewhere in the treatise, and only once genuinely granted are the precepts considered properly received; he notes this transmission, once made, endures across all future lives unless deliberately relinquished, a mark of how remarkable the bodhisattva precepts genuinely are.

He explains the second quality by tying it to the treatise's own account of "elevated aspiration" — an aspiration toward both buddhahood above and benefiting beings below, present already in a beginning bodhisattva who has not yet reached any noble stage, yet continuing, in ever-maturing form, all the way through to the tenth and final ground; the phrase describes an aspiration still oriented toward what is yet to be realized, not a claim that realization has already occurred. He notes this same aspiration, understood as seven distinct qualities of genuine compassion — fearless, principled, tireless, seeking nothing in return, untainted, expansive, and impartial — runs continuously beneath a bodhisattva's practice from the very first genuine resolve onward, which is precisely why even an ordinary, still-imperfect practitioner can meaningfully be said to hold this same "elevated aspiration" the moment their motivation is genuine. This closes the first two qualities; being purified again through confession after an unintended lapse follows next.

4. Milestone: Purified Again After a Lapse — Five Kinds of Wholesome Remorse

The third quality allows for genuine human imperfection: having received the precepts, a practitioner may still, through a lapse of mindfulness, commit an infraction — but any such infraction, so long as it remains a minor rather than a fundamental breach, is fully purified the moment it is honestly confessed to another qualified holder of the precepts, dissolved through any of five specific, wholesome forms of remorse rather than left to fester unaddressed.

Chinese Source

犯已還淨者：謂於所受淨戒若有毀犯，當知一切皆是惡作所攝。應對於他補特伽羅發露悔滅，由是除遣所生惡作。惡作有五，攝事分中別釋其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Purified again after a lapse" means: regarding the pure precepts received, should any violation occur, it should be understood as belonging entirely to the category of minor wrongdoing. One should confess this openly to another qualified person and dissolve it through repentance, thereby removing the resulting remorse. This minor wrongdoing has five forms, each explained separately elsewhere in the treatise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes minor infractions, fully purified through honest confession, from a fundamental root violation, which cannot be resolved this way and is addressed separately later in the treatise. He lists the five forms of wholesome remorse that can arise from even a minor lapse, each worth naming individually: remorse at the thought that this infraction will certainly bring one's own future regret; remorse at the thought that even heavenly beings would rightly reproach such conduct; remorse at the thought that wise fellow practitioners would rightly reprove it; remorse at the thought that word of such conduct would spread and tarnish one's own reputation; and, most serious of all, remorse at the thought that dying with this unresolved could lead to rebirth in the miserable realms.

He explains that confession to another qualified person — disclosing exactly what happened rather than quietly hoping it goes unnoticed — is what actually dissolves this remorse and restores the precepts to their original purity. This closes the third quality; the fourth and rarest quality, deep reverence sustained without any violation ever occurring at all, follows next.

5. Milestone: Deep Reverence Without Violation — The Rare, Ideal Case

The fourth quality describes a rarer, more complete devotion: a practitioner who holds the precepts in such profound reverence, and maintains such focused, unbroken mindfulness of them, that no violation occurs at all from the very moment of first receiving them — not because correction is unavailable, but because the underlying commitment is simply that thorough and sustained.

Chinese Source

深敬專念無有違犯者：謂由善淨意樂，於所受戒生起最極尊重恭敬。從初專精，不失正念，故無違犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Deep reverence and focused mindfulness with no violation" means: through a genuinely good and pure aspiration, giving rise to the utmost reverence and respect toward the precepts received. From the very outset, sustaining focused diligence and never losing mindfulness, no violation occurs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes this fourth quality as belonging to a practitioner for whom receiving the bodhisattva precepts feels genuinely rare and precious — reverence deep enough that the precepts are studied, recited, and held in mind constantly, and sustained mindfulness thorough enough that no lapse ever actually occurs. He notes this differs from the third quality not in the precepts' own content but in the practitioner's own sustained attentiveness — someone who never truly learns the precepts' own detailed content in the first place will inevitably violate them without even realizing it, while someone who studies them closely and never lets that attention lapse achieves this rarer, complete standard instead. He stresses the treatise itself never demands this fourth quality as the only acceptable path — purification through honest confession remains a fully legitimate, encouraged practice for ordinary, imperfect practitioners — but names this fourth quality as the ideal a bodhisattva continues aspiring toward regardless. This closes all four qualities individually; how they can be further grouped into three, and then into two, follows next.

6. Milestone: Four Qualities Regrouped — Shame Before Others, Shame Before Oneself, and Freedom From Lingering Remorse

The same four qualities regroup naturally into three: proper reception from a preceptor grounds an outward-facing sense of shame, since a lapse now means facing someone who transmitted the precepts in good faith; a genuinely good aspiration grounds an inward-facing sense of shame, since a lapse now means confronting one's own conscience directly; and purification through confession together with unbroken reverence, taken together, free a bodhisattva from lingering remorse altogether — the corrosive regret that, left unresolved, can quietly undermine meditative stability itself.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩從他正受故，於所學戒若有違犯，即外觀他深生愧恥；由諸菩薩善淨意樂故，於所學戒若有違犯，即內自顧深起慚羞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas properly receive the precepts from another, should they violate what they have learned, they look outward toward that other and feel deep shame. Because bodhisattvas hold a genuinely good and pure aspiration, should they violate what they have learned, they look inward at themselves and feel deep shame.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩於諸學處犯已還淨、深敬專念初無違犯二因緣故，離諸惡作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas, regarding the precepts, either purify a lapse through confession or, from the very start, hold such deep reverence and focused mindfulness that no violation ever occurs, through these two causes they are freed of lingering remorse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two forms of shame with real precision: the outward-facing kind arises from genuine awareness of wrongdoing paired with respectful concern for how a teacher or respected community would regard it, while the inward-facing kind arises from that same awareness paired with a private reckoning against one's own conscience, needing no witness at all. He treats both as healthy, functional responses to an actual lapse — not crippling guilt, but the ordinary moral sensitivity that keeps a practitioner honest with themselves and accountable to their community.

He then explains freedom from lingering remorse by citing a classical treatise's own definition: remorse is a state of anxious, regretful preoccupation over something done or left undone, whether that original act was itself wholesome or not, and its defining harm is that it directly obstructs the development of calm, settled meditation — a mind chewing over old regret cannot easily become still. He draws out the treatise's own quietly important point here: a bodhisattva who has genuinely confessed and resolved a lapse should not continue dwelling on it afterward, since re-opening settled regret serves no purpose and is, in its own way, simply another kind of confusion — confession clears the matter; continuing to relive it afterward does not deepen sincerity, it only compounds unnecessary suffering. This closes the regrouping into three; the further, final regrouping into just two follows next.

7. Milestone: Four Qualities Regroup Into Two, Through Three Different Lenses

The same four qualities admit a further, twofold reduction, viewed through three distinct lenses: viewed subjectively, proper reception and pure aspiration together produce the shame that enables the other two; viewed through the underlying mental factors involved, proper reception and pure aspiration are themselves the precepts' own defining substance, while the other two simply follow from them; and viewed through the presence or absence of an actual violation, three of the four qualities together describe the condition of never having violated at all, while the fourth alone describes the condition of having violated and then been restored.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩從他正受、善淨意樂為依止故，生起慚愧；由慚愧故，能善防護所受尸羅；由善防護所受戒故，離諸惡作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, resting on proper reception and pure aspiration, give rise to a sense of conscience; through that conscience, they are able to skillfully guard the precepts received; and through skillfully guarding the precepts received, they are freed of lingering remorse.

Chinese Source

又於是中，從他正受、善淨意樂，此二是法；犯已還淨、深敬專念無有違犯，此二是前二法所引。又於是中，從他正受、善淨意樂、深敬專念無有違犯，由此三法，應知能令不毀菩薩所受淨戒；犯已還淨，由此一法，應知能令犯已還出。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, among these: proper reception and pure aspiration are themselves the underlying substance; purification through confession and unbroken reverence are what these two produce. And further: proper reception, pure aspiration, and unbroken reverence, these three together, should be understood as what keeps the received precepts from ever being violated at all; purification through confession alone should be understood as what restores them once violated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first lens through simple lived experience: receiving the precepts properly and holding a genuinely good aspiration together produce real conscience, both the outward shame felt before a teacher and the inward shame felt before oneself, and this conscience is precisely what makes actually guarding the precepts psychologically possible, which in turn is what keeps a practitioner free of lingering remorse.

He explains the second lens more technically: proper reception corresponds to the mental factor of reception itself, the act of taking the precepts into one's own mind-stream at the moment of ordination, while pure aspiration corresponds to the mental factor of aspiration, the genuine wish underlying that reception — these two mental factors are, in a real sense, discipline's own defining substance, while purification through confession follows directly from having received the precepts in the first place (without reception, there would be nothing to violate), and unbroken reverence follows directly from the depth of that original aspiration. He explains the third lens as simply distinguishing the condition of never having violated the precepts, which draws on three of the four qualities together, from the condition of having violated and then been restored, which rests on the fourth quality alone. This closes the fourfold-to-twofold reduction across all three lenses; the treatise's own account of discipline's three great storehouses — restraint, wholesome practice, and benefiting beings — follows next.

8. Milestone: The Three Great Storehouses of Discipline — Restraint, Wholesome Practice, Benefiting Beings

Discipline's own intrinsic nature, held with all four qualities intact, unfolds into three great storehouses of boundless merit — restraint from wrongdoing, gathering wholesome practice, and benefiting beings directly — each one capable, on its own, of bringing genuine benefit and happiness to countless beings, and together forming the very cause that draws a bodhisattva toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening itself.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩具四功德自性尸羅，應知即是妙善淨戒，正受隨學，能利自他，利益安樂無量眾生，哀愍世間諸天人等，令得義利利益安樂故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who possess these four qualities in their discipline's own intrinsic nature should be understood to hold a discipline both excellent and pure — properly received and then followed through in practice, able to benefit both self and others, bringing benefit and happiness to countless beings, out of compassion for gods and humans throughout the world, so that they attain genuine benefit and happiness.

Chinese Source

應知即是無量淨戒，攝受無量菩薩所學故。應知即是饒益一切有情淨戒，現前能作一切有情利益安樂故。應知即是能獲大果勝利淨戒，攝受隨與無上正等菩提果故。是名菩薩自性戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood as a boundless pure discipline, gathering in everything countless bodhisattvas must learn. It should be understood as a pure discipline benefiting all beings, directly able to bring benefit and happiness to all beings. It should be understood as a pure discipline able to secure a great, supreme fruit, drawing one toward the fruit of unsurpassed, perfect awakening. This is called a bodhisattva's discipline in its intrinsic nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces here what becomes one of Mahāyāna Buddhism's most foundational ethical frameworks: the three collections of pure precepts. Restraint from wrongdoing is discipline in its most familiar sense, refraining from harm; gathering wholesome practice extends far beyond mere restraint to actively cultivating every wholesome quality that leads toward awakening — the six perfections and beyond; and benefiting beings directly turns discipline outward, toward concrete, active service to others' genuine welfare. He stresses none of the three stands alone — a bodhisattva who only refrains from harm but never actively cultivates virtue, or who cultivates virtue privately but never turns to serve others, has not yet completed the fullness of what discipline actually asks.

He reads the classical etymology behind "excellent and pure" as deliberately paired in reverse order — the text names "excellent" before explaining benefit, and "pure" before explaining happiness, a rhetorical inversion common in the treatise's own commentarial style, where genuine benefit for others is what makes conduct "excellent," and freedom from harming anyone is what makes it "happy" and thus "pure." He notes gods and humans specifically are named as the recipients able to genuinely take up and practice these precepts, since their circumstances allow for the sustained reflection and diligent practice the path requires, unlike beings in the lower realms whose suffering leaves little room for it. He closes by explaining the final storehouse of merit directly: realizing the unsurpassed, non-abiding nirvana that only buddhahood secures is called "genuine benefit," since it is entirely free of fault and universally desirable, while realizing the countless virtuous qualities that accompany it is called "advantage," the ultimate, irreversible gain the noble path alone provides. This closes discipline's intrinsic nature, the first of the nine aspects, in its entirety; comprehensive discipline, unfolding first into lay and monastic forms and then into the three storehouses just named, follows next.

9. Milestone: Comprehensive Discipline Opens — Lay and Monastic, and Restraint's Seven Assemblies

Comprehensive discipline first divides simply into precepts for householders and precepts for renunciants, and then, cutting across that same division, into the three storehouses already named; restraint itself, the first storehouse, is identified with the seven classical assemblies' own individually liberating precepts — monastic and lay alike — reflecting the Buddha's own compassionate accommodation of beings at very different points of readiness for renunciation.

Chinese Source

謂菩薩戒略有二種。一、在家分戒，二、出家分戒。是名一切戒。又即依此在家、出家二分淨戒，略說三種。一、律儀戒，二、攝善法戒，三、饒益有情戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattva discipline is, in brief, of two kinds: precepts for the householder portion, and precepts for the renunciant portion. This is called comprehensive discipline. And, resting on these same two portions of pure discipline, it may further, in brief, be described as three kinds: restraint discipline, discipline gathering wholesome practice, and discipline benefiting beings.

Chinese Source

律儀戒者，謂諸菩薩所受七眾別解脫律儀。即是苾芻戒、苾芻尼戒、正學戒、勤策男戒、勤策女戒、近事男戒、近事女戒。如是七種，依止在家、出家二分，如應當知。是名菩薩律儀戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Restraint discipline means the individually liberating precepts of the seven assemblies that bodhisattvas receive: the monk's precepts, the nun's precepts, the female probationer's precepts, the male novice's precepts, the female novice's precepts, the male lay follower's precepts, and the female lay follower's precepts. These seven, resting respectively on the householder and renunciant portions, should be understood accordingly. This is called a bodhisattva's restraint discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the Buddha's own reasoning for establishing exactly these seven categories, tied to three distinct kinds of practitioner: those genuinely able to renounce both wrongdoing and worldly desire altogether receive one of the five monastic precepts — monk, nun, female probationer, or novice of either gender; those able to avoid wrongdoing but not yet able to leave household life receive the lay precepts instead; and those unable to sustain either commitment for long receive the eight precepts of a single day and night's retreat, a distinct, temporary accommodation the Buddha established precisely for those not otherwise able to commit further, though not formally counted among the seven assemblies since it is held only briefly rather than for life.

He notes bodhisattva restraint precepts, though identical in content to the corresponding non-Mahāyāna precepts, are never called by that other name — the same code of conduct, undertaken with the motivation of universal awakening rather than personal liberation alone, is properly called bodhisattva discipline throughout. He highlights three further important clarifications: bodhisattva precepts are shared in content with these other traditions but not restricted only to those already ordained within them, not universally required of every member of the seven assemblies, and not received in any fixed sequence — unlike monastic ordination's own strict progression, a bodhisattva may receive these precepts in any order, and, strikingly, even someone who later disrobes from monastic life and returns to lay status retains the bodhisattva precepts fully intact regardless, since that vow was never tied to formal monastic status in the first place. This closes restraint discipline; gathering wholesome practice, the second storehouse, follows next.

10. Milestone: Wholesome-Practice Precepts — Everything Done for Great Awakening, Rooted in Restraint

Gathering wholesome practice encompasses everything a bodhisattva does with body, speech, and mind, once restraint discipline has already been received, in pursuit of great awakening — the entire range of the six perfections and beyond — resting on restraint as its own necessary foundation, since a bodhisattva unable to guard restraint discipline cannot genuinely guard the other two storehouses either, even though, in actual practice, all three storehouses are received together as a single act at ordination rather than one after another.

Chinese Source

攝善法戒者，謂諸菩薩受律儀戒後，所有一切為大菩提，由身語意積集諸善，總說名為攝善法戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Discipline gathering wholesome practice means: bodhisattvas, having received restraint discipline, gather every wholesome quality of body, speech, and mind undertaken for the sake of great awakening — all of this together is called discipline gathering wholesome practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why restraint is named as the foundation the other two storehouses depend on: restraint's own defining character is guarding against harm, and a bodhisattva unable to guard even this basic restraint has, in effect, already broken all three storehouses at once, since gathering wholesome practice and benefiting beings both presuppose the same underlying stability restraint alone establishes — a bodhisattva who violates restraint discipline is described as having violated the entirety of bodhisattva discipline, not merely one third of it.

He is careful to clarify, however, that this dependency describes a logical and not a chronological relationship: a bodhisattva does not first receive restraint discipline alone, practice it to some degree of mastery, and only afterward separately request the remaining two storehouses — all three are, in actual ordination practice, received together in a single ceremony, restraint discipline simply serving as their shared foundation rather than a separate, earlier stage. He adds one further clarification: a person of genuine bodhisattva lineage who has newly given rise to the great resolve is not required to first separately receive the individually liberating precepts of another vehicle before being permitted to receive the remaining two bodhisattva storehouses — the three storehouses of bodhisattva discipline form their own complete and sufficient whole. This closes discipline gathering wholesome practice in overview; how such wholesome qualities are actually generated, beginning with what a bodhisattva cultivates through their own sustained practice, follows next.

11. Milestone: Self-Generated Wholesome Practice — Hearing, Reflecting, and the Four Stages of Meditation

The first source of wholesome practice a bodhisattva gathers is entirely self-generated: resting on restraint discipline already received, diligently devoting oneself to hearing the teaching without fatigue, to reflecting on it through eight distinct facets, and to cultivating calm-abiding and insight through the same four progressive stages already described at length elsewhere in the treatise — hearing, reflection, and meditation together forming the genuine root from which all further wholesome practice actually grows.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩依戒、住戒，於聞、於思、於修止觀、於樂獨處精勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, resting on and abiding in discipline, diligently devote themselves to hearing the teaching, to reflecting on it, to cultivating calm-abiding and insight, and to delighting in solitude.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this passage directly back to the treatise's own extensive earlier account, in the chapter on the lineage of the powers, of exactly what genuine hearing, reflection, and cultivation look like — deep reverence for well-spoken teaching, never growing weary or satisfied in listening to it, is hearing diligently pursued; the eight facets of correct reflection already detailed there, from avoiding unfruitful speculation through the first breakthrough into genuine understanding, is reflection diligently pursued; and the four progressive stages of cultivation already detailed there — calm-abiding, insight, their union, and the eventual ease of their joint mastery — is meditation diligently pursued.

He adds a practical caution of his own: a beginning practitioner eager to rush straight into helping others, without first genuinely establishing settled understanding through sustained hearing, reflection, and practice, risks drifting into confused or even mistaken views for lack of a stable foundation — which is precisely why delighting in solitude, avoiding the company of those whose views diverge and instead seeking out fellow practitioners genuinely devoted to the same calm, reflective cultivation, belongs here as an essential condition for wholesome practice to actually take root, rather than being abandoned prematurely the moment some small progress has been made. This closes self-generated wholesome practice; wholesome practice generated through others, beginning with respect shown toward one's elders, follows next.

12. Wholesome Practice Generated Through Others Begins — Respecting One's Elders

A further source of wholesome practice arises through a bodhisattva's own conduct toward others, beginning with genuine respect shown to elders — those senior in both age and virtue — expressed through concrete, repeated acts of courtesy: joining the palms in greeting, rising to receive them, inquiring after their wellbeing, and bowing in reverence whenever the occasion calls for it.

Chinese Source

如是時時於諸尊長精勤修習合掌、起迎、問訊、禮拜、恭敬之業，即於尊長勤修敬事。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, again and again, one diligently practices, toward one's elders, joining the palms, rising to greet them, inquiring after them, bowing, and other acts of respect — this is diligently cultivating reverent service toward one's elders.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi defines an "elder" here as someone senior in both age and genuine virtue together, not age alone, and walks through each specific gesture named: joining the palms when welcoming or formally requesting something of an elder; rising to one's feet the moment they arrive; asking after their health and wellbeing with genuine concern; and bowing at their feet when making some formal request. He notes this first category of "other-generated" wholesome practice is deliberately concrete and behavioral, since genuine reverence, however sincerely felt inwardly, is meant to actually show outwardly as well — this closes respecting one's elders; the remaining categories of wholesome practice generated through one's relationships with others follow next.

13. Caring for the Sick, and Praising Genuinely Correct Teaching

Two further categories of other-generated wholesome practice: compassionate, attentive care for anyone ill, checking on them, tending to their needs, and providing medicine without the slightest carelessness or delay; and genuinely praising anyone who teaches correctly, whether pointing toward a favorable rebirth through giving and discipline, or pointing all the way toward liberation through the four noble truths.

Chinese Source

於疾病者，悲愍殷重瞻侍供給。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward the sick, one attends and provides for them with compassion and earnest care.

Chinese Source

於諸妙說，施以善哉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward genuinely correct teaching, one offers genuine praise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains genuine care for the sick as combining real compassion — the sincere wish to relieve suffering — with actual attentive follow-through, checking in and providing without any trace of carelessness or hurry. He explains genuinely correct teaching as covering both the worldly register — discourses on giving, discipline, and rebirth in the heavens — and the transcendent register — the four noble truths' own account of suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path — and stresses that praising such teaching sincerely whenever one hears it, rather than staying silent out of indifference, is itself a form of wholesome practice worth actively cultivating. This closes these two categories; genuine praise for another's virtue, and rejoicing aloud in the merit of beings everywhere, follow next.

14. Genuine Praise for Virtue, and Rejoicing Aloud in Everyone's Merit

Two further categories: sincerely praising anyone who genuinely holds even one of five qualities — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, or wisdom — never as flattery, but as honest recognition; and rejoicing, with settled conviction and genuine joy, in every wholesome deed performed by any being anywhere across the ten directions, expressing that rejoicing out loud rather than leaving it only as a private, silent feeling.

Chinese Source

於有功德補特伽羅，真誠讚美。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward anyone possessing genuine virtue, one offers sincere, genuine praise.

Chinese Source

於十方界一切有情一切福業，以勝意樂起淨信心發言隨喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward every wholesome deed performed by any being throughout the ten directions, one gives rise to pure faith through an elevated intention, and expresses that rejoicing aloud.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the five qualities worth praising wherever genuinely found — faith, discipline, learning, generosity, wisdom — and stresses genuine sincerity as the test distinguishing honest praise from flattery. He explains "elevated intention" as an aspiration running continuously from an ordinary beginner all the way through the tenth ground, rooted in genuine faith and reasoned conviction rather than mere sentiment, settled and unwavering the way a formal seal settles a document — once genuinely convinced of a truth, a bodhisattva's confidence in it no longer wavers. He notes that expressing rejoicing out loud, rather than merely feeling it privately, matters precisely because ordinary beings tend toward quiet envy at others' success, silently wishing they had accomplished it themselves instead — actively voicing genuine joy at another's merit is itself a direct antidote to that very envy. This closes these two categories; bearing others' wrongs through five specific contemplations, closing all six categories of other-generated practice, follows next.

15. Milestone: Bearing Others' Wrongs — Five Contemplations That Make Genuine Patience Possible

The sixth and final category of other-generated wholesome practice addresses being struck, cursed, mocked, or provoked by another — met not with retaliation but with genuine, considered patience, made possible through five specific contemplations: seeing the whole exchange as merely impersonal phenomena with no fixed self on either side; recalling that every being has, across countless past lives, once been a beloved parent or sibling; observing that the offense itself is already gone the instant it occurred; recognizing that the aggressor's own affliction is already a form of suffering that retaliation would only deepen; and treating the encounter as a genuine opportunity to win the aggressor over as a friend.

Chinese Source

於他所作一切違犯，思擇安忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward whatever offense another commits, one carefully reflects and patiently endures.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the five contemplations in turn. The first, seeing the exchange as mere impersonal phenomena, recognizes that what feels like "someone cursing me" is, examined closely, only mind-generated appearances arising and passing — no fixed self doing the cursing, no fixed self being cursed, both sides equally without solid, independent existence, which is precisely what allows the whole encounter to simply be released rather than gripped tightly. The second, recalling countless past lives of kinship, draws on the classical teaching that every being has, across beginningless time, at some point been one's own parent or sibling — regarding the person now provoking anger as a long-lost family member makes the anger itself far harder to sustain.

The third, observing the offense's own instantaneous passing, notes that a harsh word exists only for the single moment it is spoken; refusing to mentally string that instant together with a lingering grievance is what actually keeps the insult from taking root at all. The fourth, recognizing suffering already present in the aggressor, observes that anger and hostility burn the person feeling them first and worst, so retaliating simply piles more suffering onto someone already suffering, while patience alone breaks that cycle. And the fifth, treating the moment as an opportunity, reframes an insult as an unexpected chance to demonstrate genuine magnanimity, potentially winning over as a friend someone who approached as an adversary. He notes patience cultivated this way holds firm without either wavering resolve to benefit the other person or any trace of resentment creeping back in — genuine equanimity, not suppressed anger. This closes all six categories of wholesome practice generated through relationships with others; wholesome practice generated through especially elevated objects — dedicating merit to awakening, offering to the three jewels, and diligent cultivation of every wholesome practice — opens next.

16. Milestone: Dedicating Every Wholesome Root to Awakening Itself

A further, especially elevated source of wholesome practice is dedicating every wholesome root already accomplished or still to be accomplished, through body, speech, and mind alike, directly toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening — releasing any lingering attachment to worldly wealth or comfort as the aim — together with repeatedly giving rise to the specific vows that carry a bodhisattva's own aspiration forward at every stage of the path.

Chinese Source

以身語意已作、未作一切善根，迴向無上正等菩提，時時發起種種正願。

Ru-Yi Translation

One dedicates every wholesome root of body, speech, and mind, whether already accomplished or not yet accomplished, toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening, and again and again gives rise to various genuine vows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three classical modes of dedication at work here: dedicating one's own merit toward others, wishing every being might likewise reach awakening; dedicating a present cause toward its ultimate fruit, so that whatever wholesome root is cultivated now is aimed unwaveringly at buddhahood itself; and dedicating an ordinary act toward its ultimate underlying principle, so that even a small, everyday deed is understood as already connected to the truth awakening itself reveals. He notes genuine dedication is marked by releasing any lingering wish for wealth or comfortable rebirth as the goal — small in outward scale, the resulting merit remains boundless precisely because the intention behind it is boundless.

He names the five classical vows that continue to arise throughout a bodhisattva's path: the vow of first arousing the aspiration toward awakening; the vow shaping where and how one is reborn to best help beings; the vow to actually practice the six perfections wherever reborn; further vows gathering countless additional virtuous qualities; and the great vows, ten in number, elaborated elsewhere in the treatise. He recommends the Flower Ornament Sūtra's own chapter on pure conduct as a model worth memorizing outright, since it shows how literally any ordinary daily circumstance — waking, washing, eating, walking — can become the occasion for a genuine vow toward awakening, precisely the practice new novices are traditionally introduced to at the very start of monastic training. This closes dedication and vow-making; offering to the three jewels with six kinds of elevated intention follows next.

17. Offering to the Three Jewels With Six Kinds of Elevated Intention

Offering every kind of fine, precious object to the Buddha, the teaching, and the noble community is a further source of wholesome practice, made genuine through six specific qualities of intention behind it: broad in scope, sustained over time, genuinely joyful, motivated by gratitude for an immeasurable debt, shaped by a great aspiration to one day embody the very qualities being honored, and purely wholesome, entirely free of any desire for a resulting personal reward.

Chinese Source

以一切種上妙供具，供佛法僧。

Ru-Yi Translation

One offers every kind of fine, precious object in devotion to the Buddha, the teaching, and the community.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the six qualities directly: breadth, offering many kinds of fine objects rather than a single token gesture; sustained duration, offering repeatedly over time rather than only once; genuine joy, offering with a glad rather than dutiful heart; gratitude, recognizing how immeasurable a debt is owed to the three jewels in the first place; great aspiration, offering now while aspiring to one day embody these same qualities oneself; and purity, offering with a mind entirely free of any wish for personal gain in return. He notes such offerings, made with all six qualities present, become their own genuine source of merit regardless of the objects' worldly value, since the underlying intention is what actually does the work. This closes offering to the three jewels; diligent cultivation of every wholesome practice, closing the elevated sources of wholesome practice, follows next.

18. Diligent Cultivation of Every Wholesome Practice

The final elevated source of wholesome practice is simply sustained, courageous diligence applied to every wholesome quality that supports meditative concentration — approached not as a brief burst of effort but as a long, uninterrupted commitment, held without discouragement or retreat, until genuine mastery and ease in cultivating these qualities is actually reached.

Chinese Source

於諸善品，恆常勇猛精進修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward every wholesome quality, one constantly and courageously cultivates with diligence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "wholesome qualities" here as everything belonging to the broad support system for meditative concentration — a wide-ranging category detailed at length elsewhere in the treatise, covering everything from the basic conditions that sustain practice to the specific forms of attention that develop it. He reads "constant" as sustained, uninterrupted effort over a long stretch of time rather than sporadic bursts, and "courageous" as a mind that never grows fearful or gives up partway through, however difficult the practice proves — together describing the same unwavering diligence already emphasized many times throughout this treatise. This closes the third and final elevated source of wholesome practice, completing the entire threefold account of how wholesome practice is generated — through one's own sustained effort, through relationships with others, and through especially elevated objects of devotion; how such wholesome practice is actively protected once gathered, unfolding into five supports, follows next.

19. Milestone: Protecting Wholesome Practice — Five Supports From Vigilance to Nightly Cultivation

Once gathered, wholesome practice must also be actively protected, through five specific supports: genuine vigilance across body, speech, and mind; clear, undistracted mindfulness of what one has actually studied; carefully guarding the sense doors so they do not carelessly latch onto attractive or aversive appearances; moderation in eating, taken only to sustain the body for practice rather than for indulgence or vanity; and sustained wakeful practice during the first and last watches of the night rather than sleeping straight through them.

Chinese Source

於身語意住不放逸。於諸學處，正念正知。正行防守，密護根門。

Ru-Yi Translation

One abides in vigilance over body, speech, and mind. Regarding what one has studied, one holds clear mindfulness and awareness. Acting rightly, one guards and carefully protects the sense doors.

Chinese Source

於食知量。初夜後夜常修覺悟。

Ru-Yi Translation

One knows the right measure in eating. In the first and last watches of the night, one constantly cultivates wakeful practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains vigilance as combining two active efforts at once — diligently cultivating what is wholesome while simultaneously guarding against what is not, both undertaken together rather than either alone. He explains clear mindfulness and awareness as arising directly from sustained study of the Mahāyāna scriptures and their commentaries, letting genuine understanding settle so firmly in mind that a practitioner can readily discern, in any given moment, what a bodhisattva genuinely should or should not do. He explains guarding the sense doors as centered mainly on the sixth, mental faculty, since the first five senses generally follow wherever the mind itself directs them — actively engaging attention with wholesome objects while refusing to let it wander toward alluring or aversive appearances that would only stir up affliction.

He details moderation in eating through its own careful reasoning: neither eating so much that the body grows sluggish and unfit for practice, nor so little that it lacks the strength practice requires, and never for indulgence, vanity, or physical display, but strictly to sustain the body, ease hunger and thirst, support the practice of pure conduct, and prevent illness from arising or worsening — food obtained through entirely honest means, eaten with genuine reflection rather than mindless habit. And he explains wakeful nightly practice through the traditional division of night into four three-hour watches, the first and the last of which are set aside for walking meditation and seated practice rather than sleep, specifically to counter the five hindrances — sensory craving, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and worry, and doubt — that most easily creep in during quiet, unoccupied hours. This closes all five supports for protecting wholesome practice; how wholesome practice is further increased, through association with genuinely good friends, follows next.

20. Increasing Wholesome Practice — Association With Genuinely Good Friends

Wholesome practice is further increased through drawing close to genuinely virtuous people and relying on good friends — a good person being one who has thoroughly abandoned unwholesome conduct and cultivated a hundred distinct forms of wholesome practice in its place, and a good friend being one who embodies eight specific qualities, though even partial embodiment of these qualities in the present, more difficult age still makes someone genuinely worth learning from.

Chinese Source

親近善士，依止善友。

Ru-Yi Translation

One draws close to genuinely good people, and relies on good friends.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the "hundred forms of wholesome practice" as the ten wholesome courses of action, each considered across degrees of completeness, duration of practice, and whether undertaken personally, encouraged in others, praised, or rejoiced in — ten categories multiplying into a hundred distinct expressions of the same underlying wholesome conduct. He names the eight qualities of a genuine good friend directly: upholding discipline, extensive learning, genuine realization, compassion, fearlessness in teaching, patience, tirelessness, and skillful speech — acknowledging that finding all eight fully present in any one teacher grows harder in a later, more difficult age, but stressing that even one genuine quality present is reason enough to learn from that person, since witnessing wholesome conduct in someone else naturally encourages the same growth in oneself. He notes the deepest value of such association is protection from wrong view above all — the other nine wholesome courses of conduct are comparatively straightforward, but freedom from delusion and distorted view is genuinely difficult to sustain alone, which is precisely what a genuine teacher's presence most reliably safeguards. This closes how wholesome practice is gathered, protected, and increased in its entirety — the complete account of discipline gathering wholesome practice, the second storehouse; how all of this is properly named as discipline follows next.
