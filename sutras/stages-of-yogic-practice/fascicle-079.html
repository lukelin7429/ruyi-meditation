Fascicle 79 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十九 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Are All Buddha-Lands Equal, or Do They Differ?

This is the eighth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the previous fascicle's seven-chapter citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra now complete — "the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities," as the treatise itself names that whole span — this fascicle opens by closing out that same determination with the treatise's own direct discussion: are the five kinds of boundlessness said of a buddha (boundless beings, worlds, dharmas, and so on) equally true of every world, pure and impure alike, or do worlds genuinely differ? The treatise answers that they differ, distinguishes what makes a world "pure," asks who can actually be reborn there, and resolves an apparent scriptural contradiction through the classic Yogācāra teaching on the Buddha's four kinds of hidden intent. The fascicle then turns to an entirely new and much larger topic: the thirteen abodes (住) of bodhisattva practice, from the ground of resolute conviction through the Tathāgata's own abode, organized around four abodes said to accomplish four things.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 79 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Discerning Worlds — Do the Five Boundless Realms Hold Equally for Every World, or Do Worlds Differ?

Thus the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities has been explained. Question: as it is said, the five kinds of boundlessness — the boundlessness of the realm of sentient beings, and so forth. Should it be said that all these worlds are utterly equal, or do they differ? Answer: it should be said that they differ. They are, further, of two kinds: first, pure; second, impure. In a pure world, no hells, animals, or hungry ghosts are to be found, nor the desire realm, nor the form or formless realms; nor is any painful feeling to be found; only a saṅgha of bodhisattvas dwells there — for this reason it is called a pure world. A bodhisattva who has already entered the third ground, through the power of mastery over vows, is reborn there; no ordinary being is reborn there, nor any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being; even an ordinary bodhisattva cannot be reborn there.

Chinese Source

如是已說功德品決擇。問：如說五種無量，謂有情界無量等。彼一切世界，當言平等平等，為有差別？答：當言有差別。彼復有二種，一者、清淨，二者、不清淨。於清淨世界中，無那落迦、傍生、餓鬼可得，亦無欲界、色無色界，亦無苦受可得，純菩薩僧於中止住，是故說名清淨世界。已入第三地菩薩，由願自在力故，於彼受生，無有異生，及非異生聲聞、獨覺，若異生菩薩得生於彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities has been explained.
Question: as it is said, the five kinds of boundlessness — the boundlessness of the realm of sentient beings, and so forth. Should it be said that all these worlds are utterly equal, or do they differ?
Answer: it should be said that they differ. They are, further, of two kinds: first, pure; second, impure.
In a pure world, no hells, animals, or hungry ghosts are to be found, nor the desire realm, nor the form or formless realms; nor is any painful feeling to be found; only a saṅgha of bodhisattvas dwells there — for this reason it is called a pure world.
A bodhisattva who has already entered the third ground, through the power of mastery over vows, is reborn there; no ordinary being is reborn there, nor any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being; even an ordinary bodhisattva cannot be reborn there.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The opening line closes the book on everything the previous fascicle covered: the treatise's own name for the entire seven-chapter Saṃdhinirmocana citation running from fascicle 75 through fascicle 78 is simply "the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities" (功德品決擇), now finished. What follows is new — the treatise's own direct discussion, in its own analytic voice rather than as sūtra citation, of a question closing fascicle 78 left open in a different form: are pure and impure worlds really different, or does calling a buddha's qualities "boundless" mean every world is secretly the same? The answer distinguishes a pure world by five clean negations — no lower destinies, no three realms as ordinarily divided, no painful feeling whatsoever, nothing but a community of bodhisattvas — and then narrows sharply who can actually be born there: not third-ground and above generally, but specifically bodhisattvas who have entered the third ground and gained the particular mastery over vows that only that ground's meditative attainment makes possible. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this is a stricter standard than the four-Pure-Land scheme familiar from the Amitābha Sūtra (the land shared by ordinary and noble beings; the land of skillful means with residue; the land of true reward and adornment; the land of eternally tranquil light) — what this passage describes corresponds specifically to the third category, the bodhisattva's own land of true reward, not the more permissive "common dwelling" land where ordinary beings and śrāvakas alike may be born through simple aspiration.

2. Section: The Hidden Intent Behind "Simply Vow and You Will Be Reborn There" — Closing the Determination of Excellent Qualities Entirely

Question: if no ordinary being, and no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being, can be reborn there, then for what cause and condition do the bodhisattva teachings say: if a bodhisattva and others merely resolve to be reborn there, all such beings will accordingly be reborn there? Answer: it is for the sake of transforming those of a slothful disposition, who have not yet gathered wholesome roots, that this is said with hidden intent. Why is this? Once such beings, exhorted and encouraged in this way, then give up their sloth, apply themselves diligently to wholesome dharmas, and from this gradually become fit for that rebirth, in time attaining that very nature — know that this is the hidden intent within this teaching.

Chinese Source

問：若無異生菩薩，及非異生聲聞、獨覺得生彼者，何因緣故，菩薩教中作如是說：若菩薩等意願於彼，如是一切皆當往生？答：為化懈怠種類、未集善根所化眾生故，密意作如是說。所以者何？彼由如是蒙勸勵時，便捨懈怠，於善法中勤修加行，從此漸漸堪於彼生當得法性。應知是名此中密意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if no ordinary being, and no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being, can be reborn there, then for what cause and condition do the bodhisattva teachings say: if a bodhisattva and others merely resolve to be reborn there, all such beings will accordingly be reborn there?
Answer: it is for the sake of transforming those of a slothful disposition, who have not yet gathered wholesome roots, that this is said with hidden intent.
Why is this? Once such beings, exhorted and encouraged in this way, then give up their sloth, apply themselves diligently to wholesome dharmas, and from this gradually become fit for that rebirth, in time attaining that very nature — know that this is the hidden intent within this teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The apparent contradiction — scripture inviting anyone who simply vows to be reborn in a pure land, against this fascicle's own strict limit to third-ground bodhisattvas with mastery over vows — resolves through the classical Yogācāra doctrine of the Buddha's fourfold hidden intent (密意), which Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks at length here as background: intent to draw beings in (provisional teachings, such as the aggregates, meant to ease newcomers past their fear of a full teaching on non-self); intent regarding characteristics (teaching "no self-nature" to counter clinging to the imagined nature, without denying the dependent and perfected natures are real); intent to counteract (eight named remedies — for contempt toward the Buddha, contempt toward the Dharma, sloth, easy satisfaction with a little good, attachment to this impure world's pleasures, arrogance over a buddha's short human lifespan, regret over past generosity, and indecisive natures not yet settled on a vehicle); and intent through transformation (words whose literal sense differs from their intended meaning, as in the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's riddling verse on what counts as "firm"). The promise of rebirth through a single vow falls under the third kind, aimed at sloth specifically — cited directly from the Compendium of the Great Vehicle (juan 2) as a teaching of "a different time," golden leaves offered to quiet a crying child: not false, since sincere practice will indeed bring about that rebirth in time, but not to be taken as meaning the vow alone, without any actual cultivation, suffices on its own. With this exchange, discerning worlds (料簡世界) is fully explained, and with it the entire determination of the chapter on excellent qualities (功德品決擇) — spanning fascicles 75 through the opening of this fascicle — closes in full.

3. Section: The Thirteen Abodes Open — Four Abodes That Accomplish Four Things, Beginning with Utmost Joy

Furthermore, a bodhisattva, by means of four abodes, is able to accomplish four things. What are the four abodes? First, the abode of utmost joy; second, the abode of heightened moral discipline; third, the abode of heightened mind; and fourth, the abode of heightened wisdom. What is the abode of utmost joy? It is that bodhisattvas, whatever state they abide in, having already entered the ground of pure and heightened aspiration, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, need rely on no outward condition for their aspiration toward the Three Jewels to remain pure.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩依四種住能成四事。云何四住？一者、極歡喜住，二者、增上戒住，三者、增上心住，四者、增上慧住。云何極歡喜住？謂諸菩薩，隨所安住，已入清淨增上意樂地故，乃至當坐妙菩提座，於三寶所不藉他緣，意樂清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a bodhisattva, by means of four abodes, is able to accomplish four things.
What are the four abodes? First, the abode of utmost joy; second, the abode of heightened moral discipline; third, the abode of heightened mind; and fourth, the abode of heightened wisdom.
What is the abode of utmost joy? It is that bodhisattvas, whatever state they abide in, having already entered the ground of pure and heightened aspiration, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, need rely on no outward condition for their aspiration toward the Three Jewels to remain pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fascicle's second and far larger topic opens: the thirteen abodes (住) of bodhisattva practice named in the Bodhisattva Ground (juan 47–48) — twelve plus the Tathāgata's own abode — here organized under a fourfold scheme said to accomplish four things, corresponding respectively to the first ground, the second, the third, and the fourth ground and above together. The abode of utmost joy names the first ground itself: a bodhisattva who has realized the twofold emptiness of self and dharmas after the first incalculable eon of cultivation, whose aspiration toward the Three Jewels has become so settled and pure that it no longer needs any external support to sustain it. Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the "heightened aspiration" behind this abode at length, citing the Bodhisattva Ground's own account of a wise bodhisattva's sevenfold compassion for beings and fifteen kinds of excellent aspiration — from supreme aspiration (offering every kind of finest homage to the Three Jewels) through restraining aspiration (never knowingly violating a precept even at the cost of life, and swiftly repenting any lapse), the pāramitā aspiration (constant diligence, free of heedlessness), true-meaning aspiration (transmigrating for beings' sake without clinging, not entering nirvāṇa prematurely), and on through majestic-power, benefit, ease, liberation, firmness, non-falsity, impure and pure, well-purified, tamable, and innate aspiration — each yielding one of ten corresponding activities a bodhisattva at this stage naturally undertakes, from making offerings to swiftly progressing toward buddhahood for the benefit of gods and humans alike.

4. Section: Heightened Discipline and Heightened Mind — The Second and Third Grounds' Own Abodes

What is the abode of heightened moral discipline? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of utmost joy, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, possess moral discipline as their very nature, free of all miserliness and violation of precepts, and dedicate this very perfected discipline and generosity toward unsurpassed perfect awakening. What is the abode of heightened mind? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened moral discipline, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, become free of craving for the desire realm, attain the dhyānas and the various states of meditative equipoise, abide in loving-kindness and compassion, and, according to their capacity and strength, practice rightly and truly for the sake of all sentient beings.

Chinese Source

云何增上戒住？謂諸菩薩，即依如是極歡喜住，從此已上，隨所安住，具性尸羅，遠離一切慳吝犯戒，即以如是圓滿戒捨迴向無上正等菩提。云何增上心住？謂諸菩薩，即依如是增上戒住，從此已上，隨所安住，離欲界貪，獲得靜慮及諸等至，安住慈悲，於諸眾生隨能、隨力如實正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the abode of heightened moral discipline? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of utmost joy, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, possess moral discipline as their very nature, free of all miserliness and violation of precepts, and dedicate this very perfected discipline and generosity toward unsurpassed perfect awakening.
What is the abode of heightened mind? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened moral discipline, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, become free of craving for the desire realm, attain the dhyānas and the various states of meditative equipoise, abide in loving-kindness and compassion, and, according to their capacity and strength, practice rightly and truly for the sake of all sentient beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second ground's abode is marked by discipline that has become the bodhisattva's very nature (性戒) rather than a rule still being deliberately upheld — even the slightest of the ten unwholesome courses of action no longer arises, let alone anything grosser, and the ten wholesome courses of action manifest of themselves, effortlessly. Where the first ground perfected giving, the second perfects discipline together with the generosity of relinquishment, both now dedicated to unsurpassed awakening. The third ground's abode adds meditative attainment to that foundation: freed from desire-realm craving, a third-ground bodhisattva masters the four dhyānas and does not regress from them, abiding in the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity, and — willing now to give up form-realm rebirth for the sake of beings — practices the six pāramitās according to whatever capacity and strength each situation calls for. This is the same mastery over meditative absorption that the fascicle's opening section named as what makes rebirth in a pure land possible from the third ground onward.

5. Section: Heightened Wisdom — One Abode Spanning Seven Grounds, from Skill in the Factors of Awakening to a Buddha's Own Field of Knowledge

What is the abode of heightened wisdom? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened mind, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, gradually attain skill in the factors of awakening, skill in the truths, skill in dependent arising, and skill in the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others — surpassing the wisdom shared in common by all śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas.

Chinese Source

云何增上慧住？謂諸菩薩，即依如是增上心住，從此已上，隨所安住，漸能獲得菩提分法善巧、諸諦善巧、緣起善巧、不共法安立智善巧，出過一切聲聞、獨覺共所證智。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the abode of heightened wisdom? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened mind, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, gradually attain skill in the factors of awakening, skill in the truths, skill in dependent arising, and skill in the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others — surpassing the wisdom shared in common by all śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Unlike the first three abodes, each mapped to a single ground, the fourth abode — heightened wisdom — is one abode stretched across seven grounds, the fourth through the tenth, unified by a single continuous deepening of wisdom rather than divided by any further named abode. Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks this single dense root sentence ground by ground, drawing on the Bodhisattva Ground's own fuller account: the fourth ground attains skill in the thirty-seven factors of awakening themselves, discerning them clearly — "the awakening-factor-corresponding abode of heightened wisdom." The fifth ground attains skill in the four noble truths as its object of contemplation — "the truth-corresponding abode." The sixth ground attains skill in dependent arising, discerning exactly how ignorance drives the suffering of transmigration and how wisdom brings that same suffering to rest — "the abode corresponding to the cessation of transmigration through dependent arising." These first three sub-stages, Cháng Bǎi notes, all still share their ultimate object of knowledge with the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha vehicles, differing only in depth. The seventh ground then crosses a genuine threshold: sustained, effortful, uninterrupted contemplation of both the conditioned and unconditioned truths through wisdom free of all conceptual construction — "the abode with effort and application, free of characteristics" — attaining, for the first time, wisdom unshared with the two lesser vehicles. The eighth ground lets that same contemplation become entirely spontaneous, requiring no further effort at all — "the abode free of effort and application, free of characteristics." The ninth ground adds vast, unobstructed eloquence for teaching others, skillfully discerning every synonym, meaning, and gloss of the Dharma — "the abode of unobstructed understanding." And the tenth ground reaches "the abode of a bodhisattva's utmost and complete accomplishment" — the path brought to its consummation, having received the great empowerment toward unsurpassed awakening, bound to at most one more birth or already in one's final existence, from which, without any further interval, perfect awakening itself is realized and every activity a buddha performs becomes possible.

6. Section: Unshared Wisdom and Shared Wisdom — Closing the Detailed Account of the Four Abodes

Within this, the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others is: the wisdom of hidden and intended speech found within the bodhisattva canon, the wisdom of the truth that is not conventionally established, and the wisdom of the truth that is conventionally established. Within this, the wisdom shared in common is: the realized wisdom obtained through dependent arising.

Chinese Source

即於此中，不共法安立智者，謂於菩薩藏中密意言辭智、非安立諦智，及安立諦智。即於此中，共所得智者，謂依緣起所得證智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others is: the wisdom of hidden and intended speech found within the bodhisattva canon, the wisdom of the truth that is not conventionally established, and the wisdom of the truth that is conventionally established.
Within this, the wisdom shared in common is: the realized wisdom obtained through dependent arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A bodhisattva from the seventh ground onward possesses three kinds of wisdom unshared with the two lesser vehicles: mastery of the bodhisattva canon's own hidden and intended speech (the fourfold hidden intent already explained earlier in this fascicle); the wisdom of the truth not conventionally established, meaning suchness itself directly realized, free of all conceptual and verbal construction; and the wisdom of the truth conventionally established, meaning the four noble truths as the conventional framework through which the emptiness of self is realized — this last kind being the very wisdom śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas do attain, so a bodhisattva's superiority lies specifically in also possessing the first two. The wisdom shared in common, by contrast, names exactly what the fourth, fifth, and sixth grounds attain through contemplating dependent arising — realization the two lesser vehicles reach by the same route, even though a bodhisattva's grasp of it differs in scope and dedication. With this, the detailed exposition of the four abodes — utmost joy, heightened discipline, heightened mind, and heightened wisdom spanning the fourth through tenth grounds — is now complete.

7. Section: What the Four Abodes Each Accomplish — Never Abandoning Bodhicitta, Ever-Increasing Wealth, Return to the Desire Realm Unstained, and Swift Buddha-Wisdom

How, by means of these four abodes, are four things accomplished? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on the first abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, never abandon the great mind of awakening. Resting on the second abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, their own future body, wealth, and wholesome qualities steadily and continuously increase. Resting on the third abode, for the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they turn away from the dhyānas and, through the power of their great vow, are reborn again in the desire realm, yet are not defiled by the entangling afflictions of desire there. Resting on the fourth abode, they attain skill in fully penetrating the conventional establishment of all dharmas, and, in order to liberate sentient beings, take up the vow to undergo birth-and-death; through this vow they are then able to accumulate vast provisions. And because this abode's very purity serves as the cause, they need not wait upon the other abodes, nor rely on instruction or teaching from another, to swiftly realize a Tathāgata's sublime wisdom.

Chinese Source

云何依此四住能成四事？謂諸菩薩，依止初住，乃至當坐妙菩提座，終不棄捨大菩提心。依第二住，乃至當坐妙菩提座，當來自身財寶善品運運增長。依第三住，為欲利益諸有情故，轉諸靜慮，以大願力還生欲界，而不為彼欲纏煩惱之所染汙。依第四住，於一切法安立通達而得善巧，為度眾生，故發誓願受於生死，因此誓願，便能積集廣大資糧。則由此住清淨為因，不待餘住，亦不由他教誡教授，速能證得如來妙智。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, by means of these four abodes, are four things accomplished?
It is that bodhisattvas, resting on the first abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, never abandon the great mind of awakening.
Resting on the second abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, their own future body, wealth, and wholesome qualities steadily and continuously increase.
Resting on the third abode, for the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they turn away from the dhyānas and, through the power of their great vow, are reborn again in the desire realm, yet are not defiled by the entangling afflictions of desire there.
Resting on the fourth abode, they attain skill in fully penetrating the conventional establishment of all dharmas, and, in order to liberate sentient beings, take up the vow to undergo birth-and-death; through this vow they are then able to accumulate vast provisions. And because this abode's very purity serves as the cause, they need not wait upon the other abodes, nor rely on instruction or teaching from another, to swiftly realize a Tathāgata's sublime wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Each abode's accomplishment matches its own nature. The first ground's bodhicitta, once it becomes "decisive resolve" (證法性發心) through realizing the twofold emptiness, never again wavers — the treatise's own commentary elsewhere calls abandoning the aspiration to benefit beings, even for the sake of one's own liberation, a genuine violation of the bodhisattva's root precept. The second ground's ever-increasing wealth reflects the classical description of a bodhisattva at this stage most often taking rebirth as a wheel-turning king, complete with the thirty-two marks, the seven treasures, and a flourishing, peaceable realm — the natural fruit, per Candrakīrti's Madhyamakāvatāra, of discipline planted in the field first prepared by giving. The third ground's return to the desire realm unstained describes a bodhisattva who has already mastered the dhyānas deliberately declining a higher rebirth, choosing instead, through compassion and vow, to be reborn where beings actually need help, without being touched by the desire that ordinarily defines that realm. The fourth ground's vow to undergo birth-and-death maps onto a well-known sequence — fourth-ground bodhisattvas most often reborn as king of the Yāma heaven, fifth as king of Tuṣita, sixth as king of the heaven of transformed bliss, seventh as king of the heaven of mastery over others' transformations, eighth and ninth as Great Brahmā ruling one and then two thousand worlds, and the tenth as Maheśvara — each rebirth chosen, not compelled, in order to gather the vast provisions buddhahood requires; and because purity at this abode already implies the discipline and meditative mastery of the abodes beneath it, a bodhisattva here needs no further outside instruction to swiftly realize complete buddha-wisdom on their own.

8. Section: A Bodhisattva's Suffering, Joy, Attention, and Abiding — Beings' Harm, Beings' Benefit, Penetrating What Is Known, and Non-Discrimination

Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's suffering? Answer: the harm and affliction of sentient beings is their suffering. Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's joy? Answer: the benefit of sentient beings is their joy. Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's attention? Answer: awakening into the very limit of the domain of the knowable, and undertaking every activity that benefits sentient beings — this is their attention. Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's abiding? Answer: abiding in non-discrimination is their abiding.

Chinese Source

問：菩薩當言以何為苦？答：眾生損惱為苦。問：菩薩當言以何為樂？答：眾生饒益為樂。問：菩薩當言以何作意？答：悟入所知境界邊際，及作一切利眾生事以為作意。問：菩薩當言以何為住？答：以無分別為住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's suffering? Answer: the harm and affliction of sentient beings is their suffering.
Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's joy? Answer: the benefit of sentient beings is their joy.
Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's attention? Answer: awakening into the very limit of the domain of the knowable, and undertaking every activity that benefits sentient beings — this is their attention.
Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's abiding? Answer: abiding in non-discrimination is their abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four short questions define a bodhisattva's inner life entirely in terms of others. Suffering and joy are simply relocated onto beings themselves — "wishing only that beings be free of suffering, never seeking ease for oneself" — echoing the Bodhisattva Ground's threefold compassion and loving-kindness (juan 44): compassion focused on beings directly, wishing to remove their suffering; compassion focused on dharmas, seeing beings as a mere conventional label over the five aggregates while still wishing them well; and compassion focused on nothing at all, free of every conceptual construction, resting in the equality realized by non-discriminating wisdom. Attention divides into two matching activities: the subsequently attained wisdom that fully penetrates every knowable object, free of superimposition and denial alike, resting in the middle way; and the tireless work of the six pāramitās and four means of gathering beings, the giving and discipline and shared work through which a bodhisattva ripens both the buddha-qualities within and the beings without. Abiding, finally, names the root beneath all three — non-discriminating wisdom itself, free of every trace of self, person, being, or life-span, exactly as the Diamond Sūtra describes it — the same wisdom the Flower Ornament Sūtra circles through a dozen phrasings: all dharmas without characteristic, without substance, without true reality, empty, without an own-nature, like an illusion, like a dream. With this, discerning suffering, joy, and the rest — the second of the abode chapter's two topics — is complete, and with it the entire chapter on the thirteen abodes (the four abodes and what each accomplishes, together with this closing account of a bodhisattva's suffering, joy, attention, and abiding) closes in full.

9. Section: The Chapter on the Grounds Opens — Four Gross Obstructions That Block Entry Onto the Bodhisattva Grounds

Furthermore, a bodhisattva has, in brief, four gross obstructions; if these are not purified and removed, one is never able to enter the bodhisattva grounds, nor their successive progression. What are the four? First, committing a defiled violation within the bodhisattva's own discipline; second, slandering the sublime Dharma associated with the Great Vehicle; third, not having yet gathered wholesome roots; and fourth, having a mind of tainted attachment.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩略有四上品障，若不淨除，終不堪能入菩薩地及地漸次。何等為四？一者、於諸菩薩毗奈耶中起染汙犯；二者、毀謗大乘相應妙法；三者、未積集善根；四者、有染愛心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a bodhisattva has, in brief, four gross obstructions; if these are not purified and removed, one is never able to enter the bodhisattva grounds, nor their successive progression.
What are the four? First, committing a defiled violation within the bodhisattva's own discipline; second, slandering the sublime Dharma associated with the Great Vehicle; third, not having yet gathered wholesome roots; and fourth, having a mind of tainted attachment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fascicle's third major topic, the chapter on the grounds, opens by naming what must first be cleared away before the grounds can even be entered. The first obstruction distinguishes a defiled violation of the bodhisattva precepts — one arising from arrogance, resentment, spite, or envy, genuinely rooted in affliction — from a non-defiled one arising merely from laziness, carelessness, or a momentary lapse of mindfulness; committed with full attachment toward one of the four root transgressions (self-praise and disparaging others, miserliness with wealth or Dharma, refusing to forgive anger, or slandering the true teaching) and repeated, shameless, relished, and mistaken for a virtue, it costs the precepts outright and blocks the first ground entirely. The second, slandering the Great Vehicle's own teaching, the treatise elsewhere calls a fault graver than the five acts of immediate retribution, its karmic consequence described at length and in vivid terms. The third, not yet having gathered wholesome roots, points to the ten dharma-practices a bodhisattva must complete before the first ground — copying, honoring, transmitting, attentive listening, personal reading, retention, exposition, recitation, reflection, and practice — in essence, hearing, reflecting, and cultivating brought to their fullness. The fourth, a mind of tainted attachment, names practicing the six pāramitās while still secretly hoping for a pleasant reward — giving in hopes of wealth, keeping precepts in hopes of a fine appearance or another's gratitude — a subtle bondage to result and repayment that blocks not only the first ground but the excellences of the second ground and beyond as well.
