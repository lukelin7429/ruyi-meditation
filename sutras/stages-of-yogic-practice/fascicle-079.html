Fascicle 79 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十九 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Are All Buddha-Lands Equal, or Do They Differ?

This is the eighth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the previous fascicle's seven-chapter citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra now complete — "the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities," as the treatise itself names that whole span — this fascicle opens by closing out that same determination with the treatise's own direct discussion of pure and impure worlds, then turns through four major new topics: the thirteen abodes of bodhisattva practice; the chapter on the grounds (obstructions and their remedies, four subtle afflictions, the eleven marks of every ground from the first through the Tathāgata's own, and how śrāvaka and bodhisattva realization differ); the chapter on establishment (the eighteen dharmas exclusive to a buddha and the eighty minor marks); and the opening of the chapter on conduct's sixteen gateways of teaching — the first five, on wrong conduct, right conduct, its benefits, its thirty-two marks, and nineteen similes for the pure faith a bodhisattva inspires, ending where the source lecture-transcript itself ends, with the remaining eleven gateways continuing in fascicle 80.

1. Section: Discerning Worlds — Do the Five Boundless Realms Hold Equally for Every World, or Do Worlds Differ?

Thus the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities has been explained. Question: as it is said, the five kinds of boundlessness — the boundlessness of the realm of sentient beings, and so forth. Should it be said that all these worlds are utterly equal, or do they differ? Answer: it should be said that they differ. They are, further, of two kinds: first, pure; second, impure. In a pure world, no hells, animals, or hungry ghosts are to be found, nor the desire realm, nor the form or formless realms; nor is any painful feeling to be found; only a saṅgha of bodhisattvas dwells there — for this reason it is called a pure world. A bodhisattva who has already entered the third ground, through the power of mastery over vows, is reborn there; no ordinary being is reborn there, nor any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being; even an ordinary bodhisattva cannot be reborn there.

Chinese Source

如是已說功德品決擇。問：如說五種無量，謂有情界無量等。彼一切世界，當言平等平等，為有差別？答：當言有差別。彼復有二種，一者、清淨，二者、不清淨。於清淨世界中，無那落迦、傍生、餓鬼可得，亦無欲界、色無色界，亦無苦受可得，純菩薩僧於中止住，是故說名清淨世界。已入第三地菩薩，由願自在力故，於彼受生，無有異生，及非異生聲聞、獨覺，若異生菩薩得生於彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities has been explained.
Question: as it is said, the five kinds of boundlessness — the boundlessness of the realm of sentient beings, and so forth. Should it be said that all these worlds are utterly equal, or do they differ?
Answer: it should be said that they differ. They are, further, of two kinds: first, pure; second, impure.
In a pure world, no hells, animals, or hungry ghosts are to be found, nor the desire realm, nor the form or formless realms; nor is any painful feeling to be found; only a saṅgha of bodhisattvas dwells there — for this reason it is called a pure world.
A bodhisattva who has already entered the third ground, through the power of mastery over vows, is reborn there; no ordinary being is reborn there, nor any śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being; even an ordinary bodhisattva cannot be reborn there.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The opening line closes the book on everything the previous fascicle covered: the treatise's own name for the entire seven-chapter Saṃdhinirmocana citation running from fascicle 75 through fascicle 78 is simply "the determination of the chapter on excellent qualities" (功德品決擇), now finished. What follows is new — the treatise's own direct discussion, in its own analytic voice rather than as sūtra citation, of a question closing fascicle 78 left open in a different form: are pure and impure worlds really different, or does calling a buddha's qualities "boundless" mean every world is secretly the same? The answer distinguishes a pure world by five clean negations — no lower destinies, no three realms as ordinarily divided, no painful feeling whatsoever, nothing but a community of bodhisattvas — and then narrows sharply who can actually be born there: not third-ground and above generally, but specifically bodhisattvas who have entered the third ground and gained the particular mastery over vows that only that ground's meditative attainment makes possible. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this is a stricter standard than the four-Pure-Land scheme familiar from the Amitābha Sūtra (the land shared by ordinary and noble beings; the land of skillful means with residue; the land of true reward and adornment; the land of eternally tranquil light) — what this passage describes corresponds specifically to the third category, the bodhisattva's own land of true reward, not the more permissive "common dwelling" land where ordinary beings and śrāvakas alike may be born through simple aspiration.

2. Section: The Hidden Intent Behind "Simply Vow and You Will Be Reborn There" — Closing the Determination of Excellent Qualities Entirely

Question: if no ordinary being, and no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being, can be reborn there, then for what cause and condition do the bodhisattva teachings say: if a bodhisattva and others merely resolve to be reborn there, all such beings will accordingly be reborn there? Answer: it is for the sake of transforming those of a slothful disposition, who have not yet gathered wholesome roots, that this is said with hidden intent. Why is this? Once such beings, exhorted and encouraged in this way, then give up their sloth, apply themselves diligently to wholesome dharmas, and from this gradually become fit for that rebirth, in time attaining that very nature — know that this is the hidden intent within this teaching.

Chinese Source

問：若無異生菩薩，及非異生聲聞、獨覺得生彼者，何因緣故，菩薩教中作如是說：若菩薩等意願於彼，如是一切皆當往生？答：為化懈怠種類、未集善根所化眾生故，密意作如是說。所以者何？彼由如是蒙勸勵時，便捨懈怠，於善法中勤修加行，從此漸漸堪於彼生當得法性。應知是名此中密意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: if no ordinary being, and no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha who is not an ordinary being, can be reborn there, then for what cause and condition do the bodhisattva teachings say: if a bodhisattva and others merely resolve to be reborn there, all such beings will accordingly be reborn there?
Answer: it is for the sake of transforming those of a slothful disposition, who have not yet gathered wholesome roots, that this is said with hidden intent.
Why is this? Once such beings, exhorted and encouraged in this way, then give up their sloth, apply themselves diligently to wholesome dharmas, and from this gradually become fit for that rebirth, in time attaining that very nature — know that this is the hidden intent within this teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The apparent contradiction — scripture inviting anyone who simply vows to be reborn in a pure land, against this fascicle's own strict limit to third-ground bodhisattvas with mastery over vows — resolves through the classical Yogācāra doctrine of the Buddha's fourfold hidden intent (密意), which Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks at length here as background: intent to draw beings in (provisional teachings, such as the aggregates, meant to ease newcomers past their fear of a full teaching on non-self); intent regarding characteristics (teaching "no self-nature" to counter clinging to the imagined nature, without denying the dependent and perfected natures are real); intent to counteract (eight named remedies — for contempt toward the Buddha, contempt toward the Dharma, sloth, easy satisfaction with a little good, attachment to this impure world's pleasures, arrogance over a buddha's short human lifespan, regret over past generosity, and indecisive natures not yet settled on a vehicle); and intent through transformation (words whose literal sense differs from their intended meaning, as in the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's riddling verse on what counts as "firm"). The promise of rebirth through a single vow falls under the third kind, aimed at sloth specifically — cited directly from the Compendium of the Great Vehicle (juan 2) as a teaching of "a different time," golden leaves offered to quiet a crying child: not false, since sincere practice will indeed bring about that rebirth in time, but not to be taken as meaning the vow alone, without any actual cultivation, suffices on its own. With this exchange, discerning worlds (料簡世界) is fully explained, and with it the entire determination of the chapter on excellent qualities (功德品決擇) — spanning fascicles 75 through the opening of this fascicle — closes in full.

3. Section: The Thirteen Abodes Open — Four Abodes That Accomplish Four Things, Beginning with Utmost Joy

Furthermore, a bodhisattva, by means of four abodes, is able to accomplish four things. What are the four abodes? First, the abode of utmost joy; second, the abode of heightened moral discipline; third, the abode of heightened mind; and fourth, the abode of heightened wisdom. What is the abode of utmost joy? It is that bodhisattvas, whatever state they abide in, having already entered the ground of pure and heightened aspiration, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, need rely on no outward condition for their aspiration toward the Three Jewels to remain pure.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩依四種住能成四事。云何四住？一者、極歡喜住，二者、增上戒住，三者、增上心住，四者、增上慧住。云何極歡喜住？謂諸菩薩，隨所安住，已入清淨增上意樂地故，乃至當坐妙菩提座，於三寶所不藉他緣，意樂清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a bodhisattva, by means of four abodes, is able to accomplish four things.
What are the four abodes? First, the abode of utmost joy; second, the abode of heightened moral discipline; third, the abode of heightened mind; and fourth, the abode of heightened wisdom.
What is the abode of utmost joy? It is that bodhisattvas, whatever state they abide in, having already entered the ground of pure and heightened aspiration, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, need rely on no outward condition for their aspiration toward the Three Jewels to remain pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fascicle's second and far larger topic opens: the thirteen abodes (住) of bodhisattva practice named in the Bodhisattva Ground (juan 47–48) — twelve plus the Tathāgata's own abode — here organized under a fourfold scheme said to accomplish four things, corresponding respectively to the first ground, the second, the third, and the fourth ground and above together. The abode of utmost joy names the first ground itself: a bodhisattva who has realized the twofold emptiness of self and dharmas after the first incalculable eon of cultivation, whose aspiration toward the Three Jewels has become so settled and pure that it no longer needs any external support to sustain it. Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the "heightened aspiration" behind this abode at length, citing the Bodhisattva Ground's own account of a wise bodhisattva's sevenfold compassion for beings and fifteen kinds of excellent aspiration — from supreme aspiration (offering every kind of finest homage to the Three Jewels) through restraining aspiration (never knowingly violating a precept even at the cost of life, and swiftly repenting any lapse), the pāramitā aspiration (constant diligence, free of heedlessness), true-meaning aspiration (transmigrating for beings' sake without clinging, not entering nirvāṇa prematurely), and on through majestic-power, benefit, ease, liberation, firmness, non-falsity, impure and pure, well-purified, tamable, and innate aspiration — each yielding one of ten corresponding activities a bodhisattva at this stage naturally undertakes, from making offerings to swiftly progressing toward buddhahood for the benefit of gods and humans alike.

4. Section: Heightened Discipline and Heightened Mind — The Second and Third Grounds' Own Abodes

What is the abode of heightened moral discipline? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of utmost joy, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, possess moral discipline as their very nature, free of all miserliness and violation of precepts, and dedicate this very perfected discipline and generosity toward unsurpassed perfect awakening. What is the abode of heightened mind? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened moral discipline, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, become free of craving for the desire realm, attain the dhyānas and the various states of meditative equipoise, abide in loving-kindness and compassion, and, according to their capacity and strength, practice rightly and truly for the sake of all sentient beings.

Chinese Source

云何增上戒住？謂諸菩薩，即依如是極歡喜住，從此已上，隨所安住，具性尸羅，遠離一切慳吝犯戒，即以如是圓滿戒捨迴向無上正等菩提。云何增上心住？謂諸菩薩，即依如是增上戒住，從此已上，隨所安住，離欲界貪，獲得靜慮及諸等至，安住慈悲，於諸眾生隨能、隨力如實正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the abode of heightened moral discipline? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of utmost joy, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, possess moral discipline as their very nature, free of all miserliness and violation of precepts, and dedicate this very perfected discipline and generosity toward unsurpassed perfect awakening.
What is the abode of heightened mind? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened moral discipline, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, become free of craving for the desire realm, attain the dhyānas and the various states of meditative equipoise, abide in loving-kindness and compassion, and, according to their capacity and strength, practice rightly and truly for the sake of all sentient beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second ground's abode is marked by discipline that has become the bodhisattva's very nature (性戒) rather than a rule still being deliberately upheld — even the slightest of the ten unwholesome courses of action no longer arises, let alone anything grosser, and the ten wholesome courses of action manifest of themselves, effortlessly. Where the first ground perfected giving, the second perfects discipline together with the generosity of relinquishment, both now dedicated to unsurpassed awakening. The third ground's abode adds meditative attainment to that foundation: freed from desire-realm craving, a third-ground bodhisattva masters the four dhyānas and does not regress from them, abiding in the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity, and — willing now to give up form-realm rebirth for the sake of beings — practices the six pāramitās according to whatever capacity and strength each situation calls for. This is the same mastery over meditative absorption that the fascicle's opening section named as what makes rebirth in a pure land possible from the third ground onward.

5. Section: Heightened Wisdom — One Abode Spanning Seven Grounds, from Skill in the Factors of Awakening to a Buddha's Own Field of Knowledge

What is the abode of heightened wisdom? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened mind, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, gradually attain skill in the factors of awakening, skill in the truths, skill in dependent arising, and skill in the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others — surpassing the wisdom shared in common by all śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas.

Chinese Source

云何增上慧住？謂諸菩薩，即依如是增上心住，從此已上，隨所安住，漸能獲得菩提分法善巧、諸諦善巧、緣起善巧、不共法安立智善巧，出過一切聲聞、獨覺共所證智。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the abode of heightened wisdom? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on this very abode of heightened mind, from that point onward, whatever state they abide in, gradually attain skill in the factors of awakening, skill in the truths, skill in dependent arising, and skill in the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others — surpassing the wisdom shared in common by all śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Unlike the first three abodes, each mapped to a single ground, the fourth abode — heightened wisdom — is one abode stretched across seven grounds, the fourth through the tenth, unified by a single continuous deepening of wisdom rather than divided by any further named abode. Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks this single dense root sentence ground by ground, drawing on the Bodhisattva Ground's own fuller account: the fourth ground attains skill in the thirty-seven factors of awakening themselves, discerning them clearly — "the awakening-factor-corresponding abode of heightened wisdom." The fifth ground attains skill in the four noble truths as its object of contemplation — "the truth-corresponding abode." The sixth ground attains skill in dependent arising, discerning exactly how ignorance drives the suffering of transmigration and how wisdom brings that same suffering to rest — "the abode corresponding to the cessation of transmigration through dependent arising." These first three sub-stages, Cháng Bǎi notes, all still share their ultimate object of knowledge with the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha vehicles, differing only in depth. The seventh ground then crosses a genuine threshold: sustained, effortful, uninterrupted contemplation of both the conditioned and unconditioned truths through wisdom free of all conceptual construction — "the abode with effort and application, free of characteristics" — attaining, for the first time, wisdom unshared with the two lesser vehicles. The eighth ground lets that same contemplation become entirely spontaneous, requiring no further effort at all — "the abode free of effort and application, free of characteristics." The ninth ground adds vast, unobstructed eloquence for teaching others, skillfully discerning every synonym, meaning, and gloss of the Dharma — "the abode of unobstructed understanding." And the tenth ground reaches "the abode of a bodhisattva's utmost and complete accomplishment" — the path brought to its consummation, having received the great empowerment toward unsurpassed awakening, bound to at most one more birth or already in one's final existence, from which, without any further interval, perfect awakening itself is realized and every activity a buddha performs becomes possible.

6. Section: Unshared Wisdom and Shared Wisdom — Closing the Detailed Account of the Four Abodes

Within this, the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others is: the wisdom of hidden and intended speech found within the bodhisattva canon, the wisdom of the truth that is not conventionally established, and the wisdom of the truth that is conventionally established. Within this, the wisdom shared in common is: the realized wisdom obtained through dependent arising.

Chinese Source

即於此中，不共法安立智者，謂於菩薩藏中密意言辭智、非安立諦智，及安立諦智。即於此中，共所得智者，謂依緣起所得證智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, the wisdom that establishes what is unshared with others is: the wisdom of hidden and intended speech found within the bodhisattva canon, the wisdom of the truth that is not conventionally established, and the wisdom of the truth that is conventionally established.
Within this, the wisdom shared in common is: the realized wisdom obtained through dependent arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A bodhisattva from the seventh ground onward possesses three kinds of wisdom unshared with the two lesser vehicles: mastery of the bodhisattva canon's own hidden and intended speech (the fourfold hidden intent already explained earlier in this fascicle); the wisdom of the truth not conventionally established, meaning suchness itself directly realized, free of all conceptual and verbal construction; and the wisdom of the truth conventionally established, meaning the four noble truths as the conventional framework through which the emptiness of self is realized — this last kind being the very wisdom śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas do attain, so a bodhisattva's superiority lies specifically in also possessing the first two. The wisdom shared in common, by contrast, names exactly what the fourth, fifth, and sixth grounds attain through contemplating dependent arising — realization the two lesser vehicles reach by the same route, even though a bodhisattva's grasp of it differs in scope and dedication. With this, the detailed exposition of the four abodes — utmost joy, heightened discipline, heightened mind, and heightened wisdom spanning the fourth through tenth grounds — is now complete.

7. Section: What the Four Abodes Each Accomplish — Never Abandoning Bodhicitta, Ever-Increasing Wealth, Return to the Desire Realm Unstained, and Swift Buddha-Wisdom

How, by means of these four abodes, are four things accomplished? It is that bodhisattvas, resting on the first abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, never abandon the great mind of awakening. Resting on the second abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, their own future body, wealth, and wholesome qualities steadily and continuously increase. Resting on the third abode, for the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they turn away from the dhyānas and, through the power of their great vow, are reborn again in the desire realm, yet are not defiled by the entangling afflictions of desire there. Resting on the fourth abode, they attain skill in fully penetrating the conventional establishment of all dharmas, and, in order to liberate sentient beings, take up the vow to undergo birth-and-death; through this vow they are then able to accumulate vast provisions. And because this abode's very purity serves as the cause, they need not wait upon the other abodes, nor rely on instruction or teaching from another, to swiftly realize a Tathāgata's sublime wisdom.

Chinese Source

云何依此四住能成四事？謂諸菩薩，依止初住，乃至當坐妙菩提座，終不棄捨大菩提心。依第二住，乃至當坐妙菩提座，當來自身財寶善品運運增長。依第三住，為欲利益諸有情故，轉諸靜慮，以大願力還生欲界，而不為彼欲纏煩惱之所染汙。依第四住，於一切法安立通達而得善巧，為度眾生，故發誓願受於生死，因此誓願，便能積集廣大資糧。則由此住清淨為因，不待餘住，亦不由他教誡教授，速能證得如來妙智。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, by means of these four abodes, are four things accomplished?
It is that bodhisattvas, resting on the first abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, never abandon the great mind of awakening.
Resting on the second abode, right up to the point of sitting at the seat of subtle awakening, their own future body, wealth, and wholesome qualities steadily and continuously increase.
Resting on the third abode, for the sake of benefiting sentient beings, they turn away from the dhyānas and, through the power of their great vow, are reborn again in the desire realm, yet are not defiled by the entangling afflictions of desire there.
Resting on the fourth abode, they attain skill in fully penetrating the conventional establishment of all dharmas, and, in order to liberate sentient beings, take up the vow to undergo birth-and-death; through this vow they are then able to accumulate vast provisions. And because this abode's very purity serves as the cause, they need not wait upon the other abodes, nor rely on instruction or teaching from another, to swiftly realize a Tathāgata's sublime wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Each abode's accomplishment matches its own nature. The first ground's bodhicitta, once it becomes "decisive resolve" (證法性發心) through realizing the twofold emptiness, never again wavers — the treatise's own commentary elsewhere calls abandoning the aspiration to benefit beings, even for the sake of one's own liberation, a genuine violation of the bodhisattva's root precept. The second ground's ever-increasing wealth reflects the classical description of a bodhisattva at this stage most often taking rebirth as a wheel-turning king, complete with the thirty-two marks, the seven treasures, and a flourishing, peaceable realm — the natural fruit, per Candrakīrti's Madhyamakāvatāra, of discipline planted in the field first prepared by giving. The third ground's return to the desire realm unstained describes a bodhisattva who has already mastered the dhyānas deliberately declining a higher rebirth, choosing instead, through compassion and vow, to be reborn where beings actually need help, without being touched by the desire that ordinarily defines that realm. The fourth ground's vow to undergo birth-and-death maps onto a well-known sequence — fourth-ground bodhisattvas most often reborn as king of the Yāma heaven, fifth as king of Tuṣita, sixth as king of the heaven of transformed bliss, seventh as king of the heaven of mastery over others' transformations, eighth and ninth as Great Brahmā ruling one and then two thousand worlds, and the tenth as Maheśvara — each rebirth chosen, not compelled, in order to gather the vast provisions buddhahood requires; and because purity at this abode already implies the discipline and meditative mastery of the abodes beneath it, a bodhisattva here needs no further outside instruction to swiftly realize complete buddha-wisdom on their own.

8. Section: A Bodhisattva's Suffering, Joy, Attention, and Abiding — Beings' Harm, Beings' Benefit, Penetrating What Is Known, and Non-Discrimination

Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's suffering? Answer: the harm and affliction of sentient beings is their suffering. Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's joy? Answer: the benefit of sentient beings is their joy. Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's attention? Answer: awakening into the very limit of the domain of the knowable, and undertaking every activity that benefits sentient beings — this is their attention. Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's abiding? Answer: abiding in non-discrimination is their abiding.

Chinese Source

問：菩薩當言以何為苦？答：眾生損惱為苦。問：菩薩當言以何為樂？答：眾生饒益為樂。問：菩薩當言以何作意？答：悟入所知境界邊際，及作一切利眾生事以為作意。問：菩薩當言以何為住？答：以無分別為住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's suffering? Answer: the harm and affliction of sentient beings is their suffering.
Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's joy? Answer: the benefit of sentient beings is their joy.
Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's attention? Answer: awakening into the very limit of the domain of the knowable, and undertaking every activity that benefits sentient beings — this is their attention.
Question: what should be said to be a bodhisattva's abiding? Answer: abiding in non-discrimination is their abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four short questions define a bodhisattva's inner life entirely in terms of others. Suffering and joy are simply relocated onto beings themselves — "wishing only that beings be free of suffering, never seeking ease for oneself" — echoing the Bodhisattva Ground's threefold compassion and loving-kindness (juan 44): compassion focused on beings directly, wishing to remove their suffering; compassion focused on dharmas, seeing beings as a mere conventional label over the five aggregates while still wishing them well; and compassion focused on nothing at all, free of every conceptual construction, resting in the equality realized by non-discriminating wisdom. Attention divides into two matching activities: the subsequently attained wisdom that fully penetrates every knowable object, free of superimposition and denial alike, resting in the middle way; and the tireless work of the six pāramitās and four means of gathering beings, the giving and discipline and shared work through which a bodhisattva ripens both the buddha-qualities within and the beings without. Abiding, finally, names the root beneath all three — non-discriminating wisdom itself, free of every trace of self, person, being, or life-span, exactly as the Diamond Sūtra describes it — the same wisdom the Flower Ornament Sūtra circles through a dozen phrasings: all dharmas without characteristic, without substance, without true reality, empty, without an own-nature, like an illusion, like a dream. With this, discerning suffering, joy, and the rest — the second of the abode chapter's two topics — is complete, and with it the entire chapter on the thirteen abodes (the four abodes and what each accomplishes, together with this closing account of a bodhisattva's suffering, joy, attention, and abiding) closes in full.

9. Section: The Chapter on the Grounds Opens — Four Gross Obstructions That Block Entry Onto the Bodhisattva Grounds

What are the four? First, committing a defiled violation within the bodhisattva's own discipline; second, slandering the sublime Dharma associated with the Great Vehicle; third, not having yet gathered wholesome roots; and fourth, having a mind of tainted attachment.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩略有四上品障，若不淨除，終不堪能入菩薩地及地漸次。何等為四？一者、於諸菩薩毗奈耶中起染汙犯；二者、毀謗大乘相應妙法；三者、未積集善根；四者、有染愛心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a bodhisattva has, in brief, four gross obstructions; if these are not purified and removed, one is never able to enter the bodhisattva grounds, nor their successive progression.
What are the four? First, committing a defiled violation within the bodhisattva's own discipline; second, slandering the sublime Dharma associated with the Great Vehicle; third, not having yet gathered wholesome roots; and fourth, having a mind of tainted attachment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fascicle's third major topic, the chapter on the grounds, opens by naming what must first be cleared away before the grounds can even be entered. The first obstruction distinguishes a defiled violation of the bodhisattva precepts — one arising from arrogance, resentment, spite, or envy, genuinely rooted in affliction — from a non-defiled one arising merely from laziness, carelessness, or a momentary lapse of mindfulness; committed with full attachment toward one of the four root transgressions (self-praise and disparaging others, miserliness with wealth or Dharma, refusing to forgive anger, or slandering the true teaching) and repeated, shameless, relished, and mistaken for a virtue, it costs the precepts outright and blocks the first ground entirely. The second, slandering the Great Vehicle's own teaching, the treatise elsewhere calls a fault graver than the five acts of immediate retribution, its karmic consequence described at length and in vivid terms. The third, not yet having gathered wholesome roots, points to the ten dharma-practices a bodhisattva must complete before the first ground — copying, honoring, transmitting, attentive listening, personal reading, retention, exposition, recitation, reflection, and practice — in essence, hearing, reflecting, and cultivating brought to their fullness. The fourth, a mind of tainted attachment, names practicing the six pāramitās while still secretly hoping for a pleasant reward — giving in hopes of wealth, keeping precepts in hopes of a fine appearance or another's gratitude — a subtle bondage to result and repayment that blocks not only the first ground but the excellences of the second ground and beyond as well.

10. Section: Four Remedies — Confession, Requesting the Dharma, Rejoicing in Others' Merit, and Dedicating Every Root of Goodness — Closing the Chapter's Treatment of Obstructions and Remedies

In order to counteract these four obstructions, there are, further, four dharmas that purify and remove them. What are the four? First, going before the Tathāgatas of the ten directions everywhere, and, with deep sincerity, reproaching oneself and openly confessing one's transgressions; second, everywhere, for the benefit of all sentient beings of the ten directions, exhorting and requesting all the Tathāgatas to teach the Dharma; third, everywhere giving rise to sympathetic joy in the merit accomplished by all sentient beings of the ten directions; and fourth, dedicating every wholesome root that arises, without exception, toward unsurpassed perfect awakening.

Chinese Source

為欲對治如是四障，復有四種淨除障法。何等為四？一者、遍於十方諸如來所，深心懇責，發露悔過；二者、遍為利益一切十方諸有情類，勸請一切如來說法；三者、遍於十方一切有情所作功德，皆生隨喜；四者、凡所生起一切善根，皆悉迴向阿耨多羅三藐三菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to counteract these four obstructions, there are, further, four dharmas that purify and remove them.
What are the four? First, going before the Tathāgatas of the ten directions everywhere, and, with deep sincerity, reproaching oneself and openly confessing one's transgressions; second, everywhere, for the benefit of all sentient beings of the ten directions, exhorting and requesting all the Tathāgatas to teach the Dharma; third, everywhere giving rise to sympathetic joy in the merit accomplished by all sentient beings of the ten directions; and fourth, dedicating every wholesome root that arises, without exception, toward unsurpassed perfect awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Each remedy answers its matching obstruction directly. Confession before the buddhas of the ten directions — visualized as though physically present, bowing and openly admitting the fault — counteracts a defiled violation of the precepts. Requesting the Dharma counteracts slander of the Great Vehicle: the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Śāstra* (juan 7) explains at length why a buddha, who would teach regardless, still benefits from being asked — the Buddha himself sat silent for forty-nine days after his awakening, judging the Dharma too profound to be grasped, and turned the wheel only once petitioned by the gods; unrequested teaching invites the accusation of clinging to one's own doctrine, so a buddha waits to be asked precisely to demonstrate non-attachment. Rejoicing in others' merit counteracts not having gathered wholesome roots — the *Samantabhadra's Vows* chapter's own account of this practice extends it to literally every being's merit across all time and space, an inexhaustible, ongoing rejoicing that itself accumulates merit even though nothing was personally done. Dedicating every wholesome root toward unsurpassed awakening rather than toward a favorable rebirth (kingship, worldly power, or heavenly rulership) counteracts a mind of tainted attachment, ensuring merit keeps compounding rather than being spent on a lesser return. With this, obstructions and their remedies — the first of the grounds chapter's five topics — is complete.

11. Section: Four Subtle Afflictions Even Advanced Bodhisattvas Must Root Out — Attachment to the Dharma Itself, and the Demonic Work of a Wavering Mind

Furthermore, bodhisattvas who have already entered the great grounds have four subtle secondary afflictions, difficult to know exhaustively and difficult to cut off; these bodhisattvas should come to know them completely, and should rightly cut them off. What are the four? First, attachment to the Dharma; second, an attention still associated with the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicles; third, savoring attachment to meditative attainment; and fourth, the many workings of demonic activity. Within all characteristics whatsoever, every stirring and scattering of the mind — know that this, too, is entirely the working of demonic activity.

Chinese Source

復次，已入大地菩薩，有四微細難可遍知、難可除斷諸隨煩惱，彼諸菩薩應遍了知，當正除斷。何等為四？一者、法愛，二者、聲聞、獨覺相應作意，三者、味著等至，四者、眾魔事業。於諸相中，所有一切心動流散，當知皆是眾魔事業。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas who have already entered the great grounds have four subtle secondary afflictions, difficult to know exhaustively and difficult to cut off; these bodhisattvas should come to know them completely, and should rightly cut them off.
What are the four? First, attachment to the Dharma; second, an attention still associated with the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicles; third, savoring attachment to meditative attainment; and fourth, the many workings of demonic activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the previous topic's four obstructions block entry onto the grounds at all, these four secondary afflictions are subtler still — present even in bodhisattvas who have already entered the "great grounds," and belonging to the cognitive rather than the afflictive obstruction, as the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* (juan 21) notes in resolving an apparent tension with the earlier teaching that a bodhisattva's own afflictions must first be understood before they can even arise skillfully. Attachment to the Dharma is a lingering, ardent clinging to the Great Vehicle's own teaching, not yet seeing that the teaching too is ultimately empty — present through the fourth ground, and cut off to enter it. Attention still tinged with the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicles is the subtle wish, arising even in a fourth-ground bodhisattva wearied by saving beings across birth-and-death, to simply be free of saṃsāra oneself — cut off to enter the fifth ground. Savoring attachment to meditative attainment is a third-ground bodhisattva's residual relish of absorption itself — cut off to enter the fourth ground. And the many workings of demonic activity name any mind that still stirs and scatters when engaging worldly appearances at all — the very last of these four to fall away, only fully cut off upon entering the eighth ground's effortless, characteristic-free abiding.

12. Section: The Mark of the First Ground — Beyond the Ordinary Being's Condition, Beyond All Five Fears

Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the first ground — what mark should be said of them? Answer: it should be said that they have gone beyond the condition of every ordinary being, having already entered the true nature that is free of birth belonging to a bodhisattva; because they have already entered it, they are no longer called an ordinary being. Having gone beyond every kind of fear whatsoever, and having attained the unsurpassed Dharma never attained before, they are constantly able to abide in the abode of utmost joy.

Chinese Source

問：已入初地菩薩，當言何相？答：當言超過諸異生地，已入菩薩正性離生；由已入故，不名異生。超過一切所有怖畏，得未曾得無上法故，常能安住極歡喜住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the first ground — what mark should be said of them?
Answer: it should be said that they have gone beyond the condition of every ordinary being, having already entered the true nature that is free of birth belonging to a bodhisattva; because they have already entered it, they are no longer called an ordinary being. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The chapter's third topic — the marks distinguishing each ground, in eleven parts — opens with the first ground, whose defining mark is "the true nature free of birth" (正性離生): birth here meaning the affliction born of conceptual discrimination, and freedom from it meaning the non-discriminating wisdom that directly realizes the emptiness of both self and dharmas, uprooting the afflictive and cognitive obstructions born of conceptual thought at their conceptual root (subtler, innate forms of both obstructions still remain for higher grounds to address). Having crossed this threshold, a first-ground bodhisattva is no longer an "ordinary being" in the technical sense, and has gone beyond all five classical fears named in the Bodhisattva Ground (juan 47): fear of not surviving (a first-ground bodhisattva will give away their very last piece of bread rather than hold back out of dread of starvation); fear of ill repute (unmoved by whatever reputation helping beings might cost them); fear of death (willing to give up body and life itself where genuinely needed, having thoroughly seen through birth-and-death); and fear of the lower destinies (having severed every conceptually-arisen cause that could lead there, so any rebirth in such a realm now comes only from a deliberate vow to help beings there, never from compelled karma) — a fifth fear, of speaking before an assembly, is treated in the passages that follow.

13. Section: The Mark of the Second Ground — Discipline Becomes an Inborn Nature, Free of Every Stain of Violation

Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the second ground — what mark should be said of them? Answer: it should be said that, within the discipline, they naturally attain the cessation of the very own-nature that all śrāvakas train in, and are able to manifest pure conduct of body, speech, and mind — and are therefore able to remain far from every stain of violating the precepts.

Chinese Source

問：已入第二地菩薩，當言何相？答：當言於毗奈耶中，法爾獲得止息一切聲聞所學自性，能於身語意業清淨現行，故能遠離諸犯戒垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the second ground — what mark should be said of them?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Named the "ground free of stain" (離垢地), the second ground's mark is discipline that has become an inborn nature (性戒) rather than a rule still deliberately upheld — where the first ground's precepts still required careful, deliberate guarding, the second ground's precepts, shared in common with the śrāvaka's own training, are now kept effortlessly, purely, without even the subtlest inadvertent lapse. Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates this "nature that itself upholds the precepts" (性自能持) with the *Nirvāṇa Sūtra*'s image of one who, cast among a violent country, corrupt companions, or an age of false teaching, still keeps every precept unbroken simply because doing so has become their very character — and with the *Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā*'s tenfold account of the "straightforward mind" this discipline expresses: gentle, tamed, skillful, tranquil, genuine, unmixed, expecting nothing in return, sublime, and vast — none of it enforced from outside, the way the ocean simply never harbors a corpse by its own nature, without anyone compelling it to.

14. Section: The Third Through Sixth Grounds — Radiance, Blazing Wisdom, What Overcomes the Hardest Test, and Dwelling Face to Face with Suchness

Answer: it should be said that they inwardly attain a powerful path of calm-abiding, and through this realize the radiant light of what is to be known. Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the fourth ground — what mark should be said of them? Answer: it should be said that they inwardly attain a powerful path of insight, thereby establishing a blazing flame of wisdom capable of burning away affliction, and through this attain skill in fully establishing every factor of awakening exactly as they have realized it. Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the fifth ground — what mark should be said of them? Answer: it should be said that they surpass all worldly wisdom, and surpass the wisdom of all śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, being able to ascend and enter the path — most difficult to overcome — of the inconceivable truth. Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the sixth ground — what mark should be said of them? Four grounds, four names, one accelerating deepening of wisdom. The third, "radiant ground" (發光地), attains such powerful calm-abiding that insight into the knowable world naturally kindles into light from it. The fourth, "blazing-wisdom ground" (焰慧地), turns that light into a fire that actually burns away affliction — its skill in the thirty-seven factors of awakening now serving to teach others, not merely to advance itself. The fifth, "ground most difficult to overcome" (極難勝地), earns its name by reconciling what is hardest to reconcile: conventional and ultimate truth understood as genuinely one, the middle way's central paradox actually lived rather than merely stated, surpassing not only worldly wisdom but the wisdom of the two lesser vehicles as well, which the fourth ground still shared. The sixth, "ground of direct presence" (現前地), goes deep into dependent arising itself — ignorance conditioning formations, all the way to birth conditioning aging and death — and, wearied of every conditioned activity that dependent arising describes, turns decisively toward the signless realm, delighting in keeping the signless absorption of prajñā continuously present before the mind.

15. Section: The Seventh Through Ninth Grounds — Effort Perfected, Effort No Longer Needed, and the Great Dharma-Teacher's Fourfold Mastery

Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the seventh ground — what mark should be said of them? Answer: it should be said that their attention to the signless realm, sustained through effort, without interruption or gap, is able to reach its utmost furthest extent — the path of effortful application has here reached its consummation. Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the eighth ground — what mark should be said of them? Answer: it should be said that their attention to the signless realm, free of effort and application, has attained spontaneity, without any wavering whatsoever; having attained mastery over every characteristic, they abide in a pure ground. Question: a bodhisattva who has already entered the ninth ground — what mark should be said of them? Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh ground, "far-reaching ground" (遠行地), pushes effortful practice of the signless absorption to its absolute limit — the furthest any deliberate application of effort can carry a bodhisattva, since from the eighth ground onward no further effort is even needed. The eighth, "immovable ground" (不動地), is exactly that: the same signless attention now arises entirely spontaneously, without a trace of the wavering that even the seventh ground's subtlest residual afflictions still permitted, and mastery over every characteristic of conditioned things is complete. The ninth, "ground of excellent wisdom" (善慧地), is defined by the four kinds of unobstructed understanding that earn a bodhisattva here the title "great Dharma-teacher": unobstructed mastery over language itself (names, phrases, syllables), unobstructed and faultless grasp of meaning, and unobstructed, joyful eloquence in teaching — together making a ninth-ground bodhisattva's teaching genuinely capable of gladdening, purifying, and truly benefiting whoever hears it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The tenth ground, "cloud of Dharma" (法雲地), takes its name from a Dharma-body now vast enough to cover every being's rebirth the way a cloud covers the sky, and is marked by the great consecration every buddha bestows — light streaming from the crowns and soles of the buddhas of the ten directions onto the tenth-ground bodhisattva, exactly as an Indian prince was once anointed with water from the four great oceans upon ascending to rule the four continents — together with the full mastery of the six supernormal powers. Already so close to buddhahood in nature, a tenth-ground bodhisattva may even be called "Tathāgata," yet a real distinction remains: the tenth ground's Dharma-body is "complete" (圓滿), while a buddha's is "fully accomplished" (成滿) — the final, most subtle traces of both the afflictive and cognitive obstructions, present in even the most advanced bodhisattva, are only forever and completely removed upon entering the Tathāgata ground itself, where wisdom and vision become entirely, permanently pure. With this, the eleven marks — from the first ground's freedom from the five fears through the Tathāgata ground's final and complete purification — the third topic of the chapter on the grounds, is now fully explained.

17. Section: Cultivation, Attainment, Overflow, and Fulfillment — How the Grounds Are Practiced, Reached, Sustained, and Perfected — Closing the Chapter's Mark and Stage Topic Entirely

Question: with respect to these grounds, how is cultivation carried out? Answer: bodhisattvas abiding on the ground of resolute conviction cultivate the ten dharma-practices in reliance on the ten grounds. Question: with respect to these grounds, how is attainment reached? Answer: once bodhisattvas realize the true nature free of birth belonging to a bodhisattva, and further attain a purified aspiration, at that moment they suddenly attain all the grounds at once. Question: what is called the overflow of the grounds? Answer: within every ground, once it is attained, all the majestic-virtue paths of subsequent effortful practice that follow. Question: with respect to these grounds, how is fulfillment reached? Answer: bodhisattvas, within each and every one of these grounds, pass through countless hundreds of thousands of great eons, and, through this, spontaneously realize the very majestic virtues that each ground is praised for.

Chinese Source

問：於此諸地云何造修？答：若諸菩薩住勝解行地，依於十地修十法行。問：於此諸地云何而得？答：若諸菩薩證入菩薩正性離生，又復證得清淨意樂，爾時頓得一切諸地。問：何等名為諸地等流？答：一切地中證得已後，所有威德諸加行道。問：於此諸地云何成滿？答：若諸菩薩，於彼諸地一一地中，經於無量百千大劫，隨所稱讚諸地威德，於此威德任運能證。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: with respect to these grounds, how is cultivation carried out? Answer: bodhisattvas abiding on the ground of resolute conviction cultivate the ten dharma-practices in reliance on the ten grounds.
Question: with respect to these grounds, how is attainment reached? Answer: once bodhisattvas realize the true nature free of birth belonging to a bodhisattva, and further attain a purified aspiration, at that moment they suddenly attain all the grounds at once.
Question: what is called the overflow of the grounds? Answer: within every ground, once it is attained, all the majestic-virtue paths of subsequent effortful practice that follow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four questions trace a bodhisattva's progress from preparation to consummation. Cultivation belongs to the ground of resolute conviction — still short of the first ground itself — where the ten dharma-practices (copying, honoring, sharing, listening, reading, silent retention, recitation, teaching others, reflective analysis, and cultivating calm-abiding and insight on what has been understood) orient a practitioner toward all ten grounds without yet reaching any of them. Attainment names the leap into the first ground itself, at the moment of realizing the true nature free of birth — and here the text makes a striking claim: that moment "suddenly" attains all ten grounds at once, not because their full qualities are all already present, but because, as the treatise itself notes elsewhere, no later ground's virtues are entirely absent from an earlier one; the seed of every later attainment is already present in the first ground's provisions, differing only in how fully it has yet ripened. Overflow (等流) then names the ongoing effortful practice that follows attainment at each and every ground, since being present as a seed is not the same as being complete — even a first-ground bodhisattva must still work to perfect the second ground's discipline, and so on all the way up. Fulfillment names how long this actually takes: countless hundreds of thousands of eons within each single ground, spanning what tradition counts as three incalculable eons overall — one from the provisions stage to the first ground, one from the first through the seventh, and one from the eighth through the tenth — before each ground's praised virtues are spontaneously and fully realized. With this, the chapter's third topic — the eleven marks of the grounds, together with how they are cultivated, attained, sustained, and brought to fulfillment — is complete in full.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The chapter's fourth topic takes up a question the eleven marks left open: śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas alike are said to "enter the true nature free of birth" — traditionally counted as five kinds of entry, since a śrāvaka may enter it at three different points depending on how much affliction has already been suppressed before the breakthrough, alongside one kind each for pratyekabuddhas and bodhisattvas — but do they all realize the very same dharma-realm in the very same way? The answer distinguishes two characteristics under which the one dharma-realm can be approached. The differentiated characteristic realizes the dharma-realm by contrast with something else: as permanent (originally unborn, hence inexhaustible) against the impermanence of conditioned things, and as tranquil (freed from the bonds of affliction and suffering) against the turmoil of saṃsāra — a framework the Huayan Sūtra's own account of the first ground's "inexhaustible dharma-realm" echoes, teaching without end because the one true dharma-realm's nature is itself without end. This differentiated approach, the fascicle goes on to show, is specifically the śrāvaka's own way of realizing suchness, contrasting nirvāṇa against saṃsāra as two genuinely separate things.

19. Section: The Dharma-Realm's Own Characteristic, and How a Śrāvaka's Realization Differs by Approach and by Attention

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The dharma-realm's own characteristic realizes it by an altogether different route: not by contrasting nirvāṇa against saṃsāra, but by seeing directly, across the classical five-dharma framework (characteristic, name, conceptualization, suchness, and true wisdom), that the imagined own-nature projected onto anything — the very thing the mind takes a name to correctly pick out — never actually constituted anything real to begin with. This is the emptiness of dharmas themselves, not merely of a self within them, and it is specifically what a bodhisattva realizes that a śrāvaka does not. The śrāvaka's own realization, the passage explains, comes entirely through the differentiated route: "not sinking" (never again submerged in craving for the three realms) and "security" (a refuge finally free of every fear) together produce a settled notion of tranquility, and, once nirvāṇa is pictured as genuinely other than saṃsāra, an across-the-board weariness toward every conditioned activity naturally follows — the very attitude behind a śrāvaka's classic wish simply to exit birth-and-death for good. The 披尋記 glosses "not sinking" and "security" as two more names for that same tranquil dharma-realm, seen from slightly different angles: freedom from craving for the desire, form, and formless realms on one side, and a dwelling-place beyond every fear on the other. Crucially, this entire realization proceeds through the signless mind absorbed on the dharma-realm's differentiated characteristic — never through its own characteristic — which is exactly why a śrāvaka's realization, genuine as it is, remains narrower in scope than a bodhisattva's.

Not every śrāvaka stays a śrāvaka forever. Others, of fixed śrāvaka lineage, remain wholly oriented toward tranquility alone — the Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi calls them "of little faith, little awakening" in this matter — and even strenuous effort rarely brings them to realize the emptiness of dharmas; without that realization, buddhahood itself remains out of reach, however diligently they continue to practice within their own path.

21. Section: The Bodhisattva Realizes Both Characteristics at Once — Closing the Full Comparison Between Śrāvaka and Bodhisattva

Because when attention rests largely on the dharma-realm's differentiated characteristic, one swiftly hastens toward nirvāṇa; dwelling largely there is therefore not the right means toward unsurpassed perfect awakening. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The comparison resolves cleanly: both self-emptiness and dharma-emptiness are realized by a bodhisattva at the moment of entering the first ground, so there is no difference in what is realized — the difference lies entirely in where attention comes to rest afterward. A śrāvaka's attention on the differentiated characteristic pictures nirvāṇa and saṃsāra as two separate things, and that very picture generates an urgent pull toward exiting saṃsāra as fast as possible — exactly the orientation that makes cultivating the ten pāramitās for the sake of all beings across countless lifetimes impossible. A bodhisattva's attention rests instead on the dharma-realm's own characteristic, where saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are seen as not fundamentally two, and this frees a bodhisattva to remain within birth-and-death indefinitely, accumulating the merit and wisdom buddhahood requires without any urgency to escape. With this, the comparison between śrāvaka and bodhisattva entry into the true nature free of birth — the fourth topic of the chapter on the grounds — is complete.

Three more exclusive marks close out the karmas of mind. Where even an arhat teacher can run out of patience and give up on a student, a buddha's non-discriminating wisdom never does. 27. Section: Karma Always Led by Wisdom, and Knowledge Without Attachment or Obstruction Across the Three Times — All Eighteen Exclusive Buddha-Dharmas Complete

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The final two groups close the eighteen. An arhat's own karma of body, speech, and mind still sometimes falls into the ethically neutral category, arising without being actively led by wisdom in that moment; a buddha's three karmas, always both initiated by wisdom and sustained by it throughout, are never anything but wholesome — accounting for the sixteenth through eighteenth exclusive dharmas. And where an arhat's knowledge of the knowable across past, present, and future still requires effort to grasp and remains incomplete in scope — "attachment" naming the need for deliberate effort, "obstruction" naming what remains ungrasped — a buddha's knowledge is instantaneous and total the moment attention turns to any matter whatsoever, accounting for the thirteenth through fifteenth. With this, all eighteen dharmas exclusive to a buddha — three pure karmas, sixfold freedom from regression, unshared present activity, and unshared abiding wisdom — are now fully explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having answered the opening question item by item, the treatise now regroups all eighteen under three heads. The first four — flawless body and speech, and the freedom from forgetfulness and from varied notions that opened the account of mind — trace back to a buddha's never losing mindfulness and having fully uprooted every habitual residue, exactly the two qualities the earlier arhat comparisons kept turning on. The fifth, no mind ever out of absorption, traces to great compassion — a buddha's absorption never lapses precisely because compassionate engagement with beings never lapses either. Wrong conduct, the treatise explains, is simply doing what should not be done, or failing to do what should be — and here it is analyzed not as eight independent failures but as a single causal cascade, each stage the direct trigger of the next: a decline in the provisions of wisdom leads to forgetfulness, forgetfulness lets wholesome qualities erode, their erosion allows unbefitting conduct and ill will to surface, ill will makes a person hard to guide, being hard to guide leads one onto a wrong path, a wrong path draws one toward unworthy companions, and unworthy company finally causes a bodhisattva to fall short of the very meaning they set out to realize — one small lapse compounding, stage by stage, into a bodhisattva's genuine derailment.

Each of the four persons named earlier now has its matching confusion. Before a fellow practitioner who points out a genuine fault, refusing to admit it is the first confusion. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third wrong conduct addresses how even genuinely wholesome activity can go wrong. The first way is application "out of place" — the Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi's own example is two people building a stūpa together, each so set on their own design that a wholesome project collapses into quarreling; a good deed pursued through rivalry rather than harmony corrupts itself in the doing. The remaining three faults afflict wholesome activity even when undertaken in the right place: defiled attachment (itself twofold — wrongfully enjoying material support, as when a monastic bends the rules around donations for personal comfort; and excessive entanglement in worldly business, letting a householder's habits of restless activity creep into what should be settled practice), wrong view, and improper retention of the teaching — the latter two to be explained in what follows.

37. Section: Wrong View and Wrong Retention — Slandering Genuine Teachers, and Clinging to the Lesser While Dismissing the Definitive — Closing the Third Wrong Conduct

Through two causes and conditions, know the fault of wrong view: first, through slandering a person of true Dharma; second, through displaying attachment to what is not true Dharma as though it were true Dharma. Through two causes and conditions, know the fault of wrong retention: first, through retaining only narrow, non-definitive scriptures; second, through groundlessly slandering definitive scriptures never yet heard or received.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，應知惡見過失。一者、誹撥正法補特伽羅故；二者、於不正法顯示執著為正法故。由二因緣，應知受持過失。一者、受持狹小唯不了義經故；二者、於所未聞、未曾領受諸了義經懸誹撥故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through two causes and conditions, know the fault of wrong view: first, through slandering a person of true Dharma; second, through displaying attachment to what is not true Dharma as though it were true Dharma.
Through two causes and conditions, know the fault of wrong retention: first, through retaining only narrow, non-definitive scriptures; second, through groundlessly slandering definitive scriptures never yet heard or received.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wrong view cuts both ways at once: slandering a genuinely upright teacher's reputation on one side, and, on the other, actively promoting someone whose teaching is not genuine as though they were an advanced bodhisattva — both symptoms of never having cultivated genuine right view in the first place. Wrong retention likewise cuts two ways: contentedly settling for the narrower, non-definitive teachings of the lesser vehicle while having no taste for the Great Vehicle, and groundlessly dismissing the profound, definitive scriptures one has never even taken the trouble to hear. With this, the third wrong conduct — application out of place, together with the three faults of attachment, wrong view, and wrong retention that spoil even rightly-placed effort — is fully explained.

38. Section: The Fourth Wrong Conduct Opens — Four Twisted States of Mind Unbefitting a Bodhisattva

Furthermore, a bodhisattva has four kinds of ill will manifesting in conduct unbefitting a bodhisattva. First, ill will manifesting as disbelief and non-compliance toward the Great Teacher, contrary to reverent learning; second, ill will manifesting, toward fellow practitioners who take one in hand, point out one's faults, and are able to admonish, as failing to truly disclose one's own faults; third, ill will manifesting as the opposite of diligence amid the wholesome dharmas belonging to those of great wisdom and merit; and fourth, ill will manifesting, amid vast and profound resolute understanding, as the opposite of what would purify one's own obstructions.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩有四種非菩薩儀惡意現行。一者、於大師所生不信順，敬學相違惡意現行；二者、於同梵行攝受舉罪能教誡者，如實發露己過相違惡意現行；三者、於大智福諸善法中，精進相違惡意現行；四者、於廣大甚深勝解中，能令自障清淨相違惡意現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a bodhisattva has four kinds of ill will manifesting in conduct unbefitting a bodhisattva.
First, ill will manifesting as disbelief and non-compliance toward the Great Teacher, contrary to reverent learning; second, ill will manifesting, toward fellow practitioners who take one in hand, point out one's faults, and are able to admonish, as failing to truly disclose one's own faults; third, ill will manifesting as the opposite of diligence amid the wholesome dharmas belonging to those of great wisdom and merit; and fourth, ill will manifesting, amid vast and profound resolute understanding, as the opposite of what would purify one's own obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth wrong conduct — what the Ratnakūṭa's own commentary calls the "four crooked states of mind" — names four ways ill will surfaces in conduct a bodhisattva should never show: disbelief and resistance toward the Buddha's own transformed basis, boundless compassion, and attained wisdom; refusing to genuinely confess one's faults when a fellow practitioner rightly points them out; slacking in diligence amid the wholesome dharmas of the wise and meritorious; and failing to purify one's own obstructions through the vast, profound understanding available in the Great Vehicle's own teaching. Each, the fuller explanation that follows will show, has its own specific root: pride that will not bow to genuine authority, pride and fear that will not admit a fault, envy that mistakes proximity to the wise for actual practice, and simple neglect of what could otherwise purify.

39. Section: Disbelief Toward the Teacher, Hiding One's Own Faults, and Losing Diligence Through Envy — Closing the Fourth Wrong Conduct

Through three marks, know disbelief and non-compliance toward the Great Teacher: it is with respect to his having a real basis, his supreme excellence, and his attained wisdom. Through three marks, know the failure to truly disclose one's own faults: first, falsely displaying oneself as superior before those who take one in, thereby giving rise to a proud, exalted mind; second, concealing one's own transgressions before those able to point out faults; and third, before those able to admonish, because of the dominant force of their expelling one, giving rise to a defiled and turbid mind that seeks to cause them harm. Through two marks, know the loss of diligence in giving rise to wholesome dharmas: it is being attached to gain and reverence in the presence of those of great wisdom and merit, and delighting in that alone.

Chinese Source

由三種相，應知於大師所生不信順，謂於有體、尊勝、得智。由三種相，應知不如實發露己過。一者、於彼攝受諸有情所，邪妄顯示己為尊勝，因此發起憍舉心故；二者、於能舉罪諸有情所覆所犯故；三者、於能教誡諸有情所，因彼驅擯增上力故，發穢濁心作損惱故。由二種相，應知退失於諸善法發起精進。謂於大智福諸有情所，愛著利養恭敬故，及欣樂彼故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three marks, know disbelief and non-compliance toward the Great Teacher: it is with respect to his having a real basis, his supreme excellence, and his attained wisdom.
Through three marks, know the failure to truly disclose one's own faults: first, falsely displaying oneself as superior before those who take one in, thereby giving rise to a proud, exalted mind; second, concealing one's own transgressions before those able to point out faults; and third, before those able to admonish, because of the dominant force of their expelling one, giving rise to a defiled and turbid mind that seeks to cause them harm.
Through two marks, know the loss of diligence in giving rise to wholesome dharmas: it is being attached to gain and reverence in the presence of those of great wisdom and merit, and delighting in that alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The three-part explanation unpacks the fourth wrong conduct's first three items (the fourth, purifying one's own obstruction through profound understanding, is left without further separate elaboration here). Disbelief toward the Buddha concerns three things at once: his real transformed basis (having converted the eight consciousnesses into the four wisdoms), his supreme excellence (constantly working for others' benefit above his own), and his attained wisdom (realizing complete, all-knowing awakening) — refusing reverence toward any of these is the first ill will. Failing to confess one's own faults has three faces: pretending superiority before the very teacher meant to guide one, hiding a transgression from someone who has rightly pointed it out, and turning resentful — even wishing harm — toward an admonisher out of fear of being expelled. And losing diligence traces to something subtler than laziness: staying near people of great wisdom and merit not to genuinely learn from them but purely for the material support and status that proximity brings — the *Ratnakūṭa*'s own gloss calls this envy of another's gifts and merit standing in for diligence never actually exercised. With this, the fourth wrong conduct is fully explained.

40. Section: The Fifth Wrong Conduct — Four Disguises That Make a Bodhisattva Hard to Tame

Furthermore, there are four dharmas able to make a bodhisattva hard to tame. It is that, with respect to right cultivation, there are four obstructions. First, taking mere hearing to be the ultimate; second, wrongly grasping the meaning of instruction received; third, not rightly abiding in moral discipline, with much wrongdoing; and fourth, abiding in the view of clinging to one's own view.

Chinese Source

復次，有四種法，能令菩薩難可調伏。謂於正修有四種障。一、於聽聞執為究竟；二、於教授左謬領解；三、於尸羅不正安住，多諸惡作；四、於自見安住見取。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, there are four dharmas able to make a bodhisattva hard to tame. It is that, with respect to right cultivation, there are four obstructions.
First, taking mere hearing to be the ultimate; second, wrongly grasping the meaning of instruction received; third, not rightly abiding in moral discipline, with much wrongdoing; and fourth, abiding in the view of clinging to one's own view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth wrong conduct — the *Ratnakūṭa*'s "four marks of ruin" — names four disguises behind which genuine faults hide undetected, each glossed vividly by Master Yìn Shùn. Taking mere hearing as the ultimate produces the scholar who accumulates learning without ever cultivating it, mistaking erudition for practice. Wrongly grasping instruction produces the recluse who, unable to get along with a teacher's correction, withdraws to solitary practice less out of genuine cultivation than to disguise a simple falling-out as spiritual seriousness. Not rightly abiding in discipline produces the builder of merit — grand temples, elaborate ceremonies, visible good works — undertaken partly to mask a private laxity about the precepts that would otherwise squander donors' faith. And clinging to one's own view produces the movement leader, surrounded by a devoted following who reinforce that only this teaching is correct, using the crowd itself as cover for what may be simple pride in disguise. Each disguise is exactly what makes the underlying fault so hard to tame — the outward appearance of learning, retreat, generosity, or authority obscures the very affliction still at work beneath it.

41. Section: The Sixth Wrong Conduct — Four Ways a Bodhisattva Steers Beings Onto the Wrong Path — Closing the Fifth Wrong Conduct

A superior being is one of superior taming of the faculties and superior severance. Furthermore, a bodhisattva has four ways of leading sentient beings onto a wrong path. First, not teaching those beings in whom pure faith has not yet been established; second, toward beings of the lesser vehicle who seek the Great Vehicle, not speaking in a way suited to them; third, toward beings of the Great Vehicle who seek the lesser vehicle, speaking in a way that does not accord with the true meaning; and fourth, out of partiality toward one's own faction, whether toward those who abide in the precepts or those who do not, speaking of them without equal regard.

Chinese Source

勝有情者，謂根調伏勝及斷滅勝。復次，菩薩有四種於諸有情行於非道。一者、於未安立淨信有情而不為說；二者、於下乘希求大乘諸有情所，不隨所宜而有所說；三者、於大乘希求下乘諸有情所，不順其義而有所說；四者、於住禁戒、不住禁戒貪愛朋黨，不平等說。

Ru-Yi Translation

A superior being is one of superior taming of the faculties and superior severance.
Furthermore, a bodhisattva has four ways of leading sentient beings onto a wrong path. First, not teaching those beings in whom pure faith has not yet been established; second, toward beings of the lesser vehicle who seek the Great Vehicle, not speaking in a way suited to them; third, toward beings of the Great Vehicle who seek the lesser vehicle, speaking in a way that does not accord with the true meaning; and fourth, out of partiality toward one's own faction, whether toward those who abide in the precepts or those who do not, speaking of them without equal regard.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A "superior being," closing the fifth wrong conduct, is simply one whose six faculties are already well-tamed and whose afflictions are already, at least in part, severed — contesting for status with such a person, rather than genuinely learning from them, is exactly the arrogance that makes a bodhisattva hard to tame. The sixth wrong conduct then opens: four ways a bodhisattva actively misdirects beings rather than simply neglecting them. Withholding the Great Vehicle from someone not yet confident in it leaves their faith unbuilt. Discouraging a lesser-vehicle practitioner genuinely drawn toward the Great Vehicle — the 披尋記 specifies this means teaching in a way that violates a bodhisattva's own charge, praising renunciation of saṃsāra and abandonment of beings, causing them to trade the superior path for the inferior one — actively steers them backward. Failing to properly redirect a Great Vehicle practitioner tempted toward the lesser vehicle lets them drift away unchecked. And favoring one's own circle — praising the precept-keeper who happens to be a friend, excusing the precept-breaker who happens to be one, or the reverse toward strangers — corrupts a teacher's basic fairness. Each, in its own way, substitutes a bodhisattva's private preference for what a given being genuinely needs to hear. With this, the fifth wrong conduct is fully explained.

42. Section: Abiding and Not Abiding in the Precepts — Closing the Sixth Wrong Conduct

Through three marks, know one who abides in the precepts: first, because their conduct is without fault; second, because their moral discipline is without deficiency; and third, because they honor what they have learned. Through two marks, know one who does not abide in the precepts: first, because their moral discipline is deficient; and second, because they do not honor what they have learned.

Chinese Source

由三種相，當知是名安住禁戒。一者、事業無愆故；二者、尸羅無闕故；三者、恭敬所學故。由二種相，當知是名不住禁戒。一者、尸羅闕故；二者、不恭敬所學故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three marks, know one who abides in the precepts: first, because their conduct is without fault; second, because their moral discipline is without deficiency; and third, because they honor what they have learned.
Through two marks, know one who does not abide in the precepts: first, because their moral discipline is deficient; and second, because they do not honor what they have learned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This closing clarification defines the two terms the fourth item's "partiality" was measured against. Abiding in the precepts requires all three marks at once: faultless conduct in body, speech, and mind down to the smallest everyday action (the Śrāvaka Ground's own standard of "perfected comportment," conforming to worldly propriety without transgressing it, and to monastic discipline without transgressing that either), discipline kept without a single lapse, and genuine reverence for the training itself, not mere outward compliance. Lacking either the discipline itself or the reverence behind it is enough to count as not abiding in the precepts. A teacher's fairness, the previous section already showed, means applying this same objective standard to friend and stranger alike, rather than letting personal loyalty decide who gets praised and who gets excused. With this, the sixth wrong conduct is fully explained.

43. Section: The Seventh Wrong Conduct — Four Things Lost by Keeping Company With the Unworthy

Furthermore, a bodhisattva, through drawing close to the unworthy, loses four things. First, they lose the Great Vehicle itself; second, they lose the effort to benefit sentient beings; third, they lose the noble teaching; and fourth, they lose the uninterrupted cultivation of wholesome dharmas.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩由親近不賢良故，退失四事。一者、退失於乘；二者、退失利益有情加行；三者、退失聖教；四者、退失無間修諸善法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a bodhisattva, through drawing close to the unworthy, loses four things. First, they lose the Great Vehicle itself; second, they lose the effort to benefit sentient beings; third, they lose the noble teaching; and fourth, they lose the uninterrupted cultivation of wholesome dharmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh wrong conduct names four losses that follow from keeping company with the wrong teachers, each traced to a different kind of unworthy companion in the *Ratnakūṭa*'s own account: befriending one who seeks only śrāvaka liberation for themselves — genuinely admirable on its own terms, yet corrosive to a bodhisattva's own resolve if absorbed by osmosis — erodes the Great Vehicle itself, which the treatise elsewhere defines by seven kinds of greatness (its teaching, its resolve, its understanding, its heightened aspiration, its provisions, the eons it spans, and its perfected realization, none of which a self-focused path shares); befriending one who, like a pratyekabuddha, delights in solitude and few affairs erodes the very willingness to take on the effort of helping others; befriending one absorbed in outside literature and non-Buddhist classics erodes familiarity with the noble teaching itself; and befriending one whose company brings only worldly advantage — money, connections, reputation — without any benefit to precepts, concentration, or wisdom erodes the uninterrupted cultivation that sustains the path day to day. With this, the seventh wrong conduct is fully explained.

44. Section: The Eighth and Final Wrong Conduct — Four Bodhisattvas Who Fall Short of the True Meaning — Closing All Eight Wrong Conducts, and the First Gateway, Entirely

Furthermore, there are four kinds of bodhisattva who fall short of the true meaning. What are the four? First, one who upholds the true Dharma; second, one who dwells in a forest retreat; third, one who diligently cultivates meritorious works; and fourth, one who governs a great assembly. If bodhisattvas wish to make others believe in and submit to them, then although they uphold the true Dharma, this too falls short of the meaning, not according with the true meaning. If bodhisattvas seek reputation and renown, then although they dwell in a forest retreat, this too falls short of the meaning, not according with the true meaning. If bodhisattvas fix their minds single-mindedly on a tainted fruit, then although they diligently cultivate meritorious works, this too falls short of the meaning, not according with the true meaning. If bodhisattvas fix their minds single-mindedly on being served and gaining renown, then although they govern a great assembly, this too falls short of the meaning, not according with the true meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth and final wrong conduct closes the list with four counterfeit bodhisattvas — each engaged in an outwardly admirable activity, yet motivated by something that hollows it out entirely. A scholar upholding the true Dharma purely to be believed and deferred to has the words right but not the substance. A recluse in a forest retreat seeking only a reputation for holiness has the setting right but not the substance. One who diligently builds merit while secretly fixated on a favorable karmic reward — or on being repaid — has the activity right but not the substance. And one who governs a great assembly purely to be served and renowned as a great teacher has the role right but not the substance. Each of the four, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, mirrors a genuine and valuable bodhisattva activity so closely that only the motivation behind it — vanity, reputation, self-interested reward, or the desire for a following — reveals it as merely resembling the path rather than actually walking it. With this, all eight wrong conducts — the provisions of wisdom, mindfulness, wholesome dharmas, ill will, difficulty to tame, the wrong path, unworthy company, and falling short of the true meaning — are now complete in full, closing the chapter on conduct's first gateway, wrong conduct, entirely.

45. Section: The Second Gateway Opens — Right Conduct as the Direct Remedy for Wisdom's Decline

Furthermore, what is right conduct? It is what is contrary to the above, proclaiming the remedy by way of each distinct fault. Know that the eight modes of conduct each triggering the next in succession are called right conduct. It is said: why does not listening oneself cause wisdom to decline? Because it is displayed through disrespect toward the person of true Dharma. Through this a transgression occurs; even without transgression, there is still no resolute understanding — hence the decline. It is said: why does not causing others to listen cause wisdom to decline? Because it is displayed through wishing to make others believe in and submit to oneself. Through this a transgression occurs; even without transgression, one turns toward a wrong dharma — hence the decline. It is said: why does obstructing others' listening cause wisdom to decline? Because it is displayed through unwillingness, through not hearing, and through not retaining. Through this a transgression occurs; even without transgression, there is sloth and laziness — hence the decline. It is said: why does listening while holding to a distorted view cause wisdom to decline? Because it is displayed through seeing no merit in actual practice, taking mere hearing of words as the ultimate. Through this a transgression occurs; even without transgression, wisdom fails to become genuine — hence the decline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Right conduct, the treatise's second gateway, takes an unusual approach: rather than describing right conduct positively (as the *Ratnakūṭa* itself does, in verses on revering the Dharma, teaching freely without seeking gain, pursuing learning "as though one's hair were on fire," and practicing what is heard rather than merely reciting it), the treatise proceeds negatively — explaining exactly why each of the eight wrong conducts causes what it causes, so that avoiding the cause becomes the remedy. Disrespect toward a genuine teacher of the Dharma is the specific root of not listening; wanting others to believe in and defer to oneself is the specific root of silencing others; unwillingness, inattention, and failure to retain are the specific roots of obstructing others' hearing; and mistaking mere hearing for the finished product, without ever seeing the results actual practice bears, is the specific root of distorted listening. In every case, even where no formal precept is technically broken, the underlying attitude alone is enough to cause wisdom's decline — showing that these are matters of genuine inner orientation, not merely external rule-keeping.

46. Section: Why Mindfulness Is Actually Lost — Wrong Turning on the Training, Disrespect Toward Genuine Bodhisattvas, and Confusing the Revealed With the Hidden — Closing the Second Right Conduct

Three roots for the remaining causes of lost mindfulness. Confusion about one's own training traces to a mind that never genuinely turns to follow a teacher's actual guidance, substituting its own crooked interpretation instead — the *Ratnakūṭa*'s matching virtue is dealing with others always through a straightforward, unflattering mind, free of any scheming. Confusion about the Great Vehicle traces to withholding the reverence genuinely accomplished bodhisattvas deserve, and actively concealing or belittling their real virtues — the *Ratnakūṭa*'s remedy is holding such bodhisattvas in the same regard as the World-Honored One, spreading their good name in every direction rather than tearing it down. And confusion between what is revealed (the Great Vehicle's own explicit teaching) and what is hidden (the lesser vehicle's more provisional teaching) traces to actively discouraging delight in the Great Vehicle, whether in oneself or in others — the remedy is guiding every being one can toward unsurpassed awakening rather than leaving them content with a lesser goal. In each case, the treatise notes a subtle distinction between loss caused by karmic obstruction (a genuine mental transgression, even unspoken) and loss caused merely by an offense of speech, such as an outright lie — both equally erode mindfulness, though by different routes. With this, the second right conduct is fully explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third right conduct explains why wholesome activity actually gets destroyed. Misplaced effort traces to a narrow, self-focused mind still drawn to the lesser vehicle's teaching, too proud to humbly turn back toward the Great Vehicle once shown a better path — the *Ratnakūṭa*'s remedy is abandoning wrong teachings for the true scriptures of the six pāramitās, approaching every being with genuine humility rather than pride. Defiled attachment traces to plain discontent with whatever material support one already receives, dressing up demands for more behind a show of spiritual accomplishment — the remedy, cited from the *Ratnakūṭa* itself, is accepting support lawfully, knowing when enough is enough, and abiding in the noble lineage's traditional contentment rather than any of the wrong livelihoods that trade on appearances for gain.

Through this a transgression occurs — decline is obtained through wrong conduct, and decline is obtained through deceiving others. It is said: why does the fault of wrong retention destroy pure wholesome dharmas? Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wrong view traces to constantly watching others with a fault-finding eye and confusing what śrāvaka and Great Vehicle teachings each actually hold — the *Ratnakūṭa*'s remedy is never pointing out another's faults, true or false, since the bodhisattva one fails to recognize might be standing right beside oneself. Wrong retention traces to a specific and important conceit the treatise names "the conceit of equating what genuinely differs" (等覺慢): failing to see that the Buddha's teachings of definitive and provisional meaning genuinely differ in intent, and instead insisting the Great Vehicle and lesser vehicle are simply the same — a conceit that produces two kinds of decline, slandering profound teachings never properly studied, and wrongly appraising as equal what a buddha's own skillful wisdom deliberately differentiated. The *Ratnakūṭa*'s own remedy: when a teaching is not yet understood, trust that the Buddha alone truly knows why it was taught as it was, and withhold judgment rather than reject it outright. With this, the third right conduct is fully explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth right conduct — countering the "four crooked states of mind" — begins with two of its four causes. Insufficient reverence toward training traces to leaving a fault unconfessed and unremoved, which leaves the mind scattered regarding the bodhisattva precepts themselves, unclear about what does and does not actually constitute a violation. Failing to disclose one's own faults traces to attachment to one's own body and material support — fearing that confession might mean losing a community's continued support — together with a taste for comfortable falsehood over the single truth (諦語, here meaning speech that accords with the one, non-illusory reality) rather than facing an uncomfortable fact; this leaves the mind scattered regarding the noble teaching itself. The *Ratnakūṭa*'s own remedy for the second: never speaking falsely even if doing so would cost one's country, body, life, or wealth — confessing a genuine fault plainly, without excuse or elaboration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Slothful diligence traces to a simple lack of forbearance — no capacity to bear insult, slander, beating, or confinement without collapsing into resentment. The *Ratnakūṭa*'s own remedy: when such harm comes, a bodhisattva blames only their own past karma and never resents the one causing harm, trusting the law of karmic ripening rather than reacting with anger. Impure purification traces to simply never having developed a strong, heightened aspiration and resolute confidence toward the Great Vehicle in the first place — without that inner conviction, the mind's attention keeps drifting toward the lesser vehicle's narrower aims instead of remaining settled on the vast path. With this, the fourth right conduct — countering all four crooked states of mind — is fully explained.

The fifth right conduct exposes exactly what the earlier "four disguises" were covering. Behind the scholar's front lies nothing but the satisfaction of winning debates, mistaking rhetorical victory for genuine understanding. Behind the recluse's front lies simple inability to accept correction — solitude chosen not for practice but to avoid ever being challenged again. Behind the merit-builder's front lies actual laxity about the precepts, dressed up to keep donors' confidence and support flowing regardless. And behind the community-leader's front lies flat refusal to revere, approach, hear, or actually follow bodhisattvas genuinely pure in the pāramitās — leading a following becomes the shield that hides an unwillingness to ever be taught by anyone else. None of these four is a genuine spiritual friend; a bodhisattva who draws close to any of them risks losing the very qualities the friendship appears to offer. With this, the fifth right conduct is fully explained.

52. Section: The Sixth Right Conduct — Closing the Roots of Steering Beings Onto the Wrong Path

Because they are displayed through weariness and unequal regard in teaching from one time to another, through not knowing the skillful means for what is taught, through a tainted attachment to resolute understanding of the lesser vehicle, and through the effort of admonishing one's own followers. Through this a transgression occurs — because wholesome roots are left incomplete, because vast wholesome roots are not embraced, because vast wholesome roots are abandoned, because demerit is generated, and because the sentient beings being taught are thereby deceived.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Each of the four causes matches, in order, the four wrong paths named earlier. Weariness and unequal regard toward teaching produces "not teaching," leaving a not-yet-faithful being's wholesome roots incomplete for want of timely instruction. Not knowing the right skillful means produces "not teaching accordingly," failing to embrace the vast wholesome roots of a lesser-vehicle practitioner genuinely drawn toward the Great Vehicle. A lingering, tainted fondness for the lesser vehicle's own resolute understanding produces "not speaking in accord with the meaning," letting a Great Vehicle practitioner tempted toward the lesser vehicle simply abandon their vast wholesome roots unchallenged. And the effort spent managing one's own circle of followers produces "not speaking with equal regard" — praising the precept-keeper and excusing the precept-breaker by personal loyalty rather than truth — which generates demerit and, ultimately, deceives the very beings a teacher is meant to serve. With this, the sixth right conduct is fully explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh right conduct traces the earlier four losses back to a single root: keeping unworthy company breeds miserliness, whether toward the Dharma (unwillingness to teach it), toward wealth (clinging to gain and reverence instead), or toward beings (lacking any real compassion), together with too little learning, too little familiarity with the noble teaching, and simply not listening to the Buddha's own word. From this single failure to cultivate wholesome roots, the four specific losses follow in order: dread of saṃsāra's suffering causes the Great Vehicle itself to be lost; inability to act for others causes the effort to benefit beings to be lost; narrow wholesome roots cause the noble teaching to be lost; and lingering doubt about the dharmas causes uninterrupted cultivation of wholesome dharmas to be lost. With this, the seventh right conduct is fully explained.

54. Section: The Eighth and Final Right Conduct — What Actually Lies Behind Four Counterfeit Bodhisattvas — Closing the Entire Second Gateway

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four roots, matched in order to the four counterfeit bodhisattvas named earlier. Self-love blind to its own subtle faults is what actually drives the scholar who wants belief and submission — impressive erudition masking an ordinary attachment to self never even examined. A merely apparent, not genuinely realized, understanding of selflessness — one-sidedly dreading saṃsāra and craving nirvāṇa rather than seeing them as ultimately not two — is what actually drives the seeker of reputation who retreats to solitude. Delight in an undiscriminating crowd, anyone welcome regardless of genuine cultivation, is what actually drives the community-leader who wants to be served and renowned. And craving a future life of wealth and rank is what actually drives the merit-builder who appears devoted to good works. In each case the outward form perfectly resembles genuine bodhisattva conduct, yet the motivation beneath it is exactly what a true bodhisattva has already relinquished. With this, the eighth and final right conduct is fully explained, and with it, all eight right conducts — the direct remedies for wisdom's decline, lost mindfulness, destroyed wholesome dharmas, ill will, difficulty to tame, the wrong path, unworthy company, and falling short of the true meaning — are complete, closing the chapter on conduct's second gateway, right conduct, entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third gateway names the payoff of everything the first two gateways established: a chain of four benefits, each the necessary foundation for the next. Genuine right conduct first accumulates the provisions of merit and wisdom a bodhisattva needs; those provisions in turn purify the very obstructions that would otherwise block the path; a purified path in turn allows pure wholesome dharmas — the pāramitās in full — to be completed through every possible approach; and a bodhisattva equipped with the completed pāramitās is finally able to undertake every kind of effort to benefit beings, generating boundless merit that keeps compounding further still. Nothing here is optional or interchangeable — each benefit genuinely depends on the one before it, which is why the text insists on knowing them "each as resting on what precedes it."

56. Section: What Accumulates the Provisions, and What Purifies the Obstructions

First, honoring and serving all the Tathāgatas through this very right conduct; second, purity of hearing; third, purity of reflection; and fourth, purity of cultivation. Furthermore, there are four dharmas able to purify the obstructions. First, being naturally unmoved with respect to the Vehicle; second, being far removed, with respect to sentient beings, from any cause or condition of not acting for their benefit; third, being far removed from the cause and condition of wrong conduct; and fourth, being far removed from the cause and condition of incomplete right conduct.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first benefit's four causes map onto the classical threefold wisdom: genuine right conduct is itself the truest offering to every buddha (the Bodhisattva Ground's own account of "offering through practice" — extending loving-kindness and compassion to every being, contemplating impermanence and suffering, and resting in non-conceptual wisdom toward the Three Jewels, however briefly, again and again); purity of hearing, reflection, and cultivation are simply the wisdom accomplished through listening, thinking, and meditating brought to full clarity, elaborated at length in the Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on the power of one's spiritual lineage (juan 38). The second benefit's four causes describe a bodhisattva secured by the three sets of bodhisattva precepts: naturally unmoved in the Vehicle through resting on an unabandoned vow to unsurpassed awakening while cultivating calm-abiding and insight; never failing to act for beings' benefit through the precept of benefiting sentient beings in every way; free of wrong conduct through the precept of moral restraint; and free of incomplete right conduct through the precept of embracing every wholesome dharma — the three groups of pure precepts (三聚淨戒) that structure a bodhisattva's entire ethical life.

57. Section: What Completes the Pure Dharmas, and What Rescues Beings — Closing the Third Gateway Entirely

Furthermore, there are four dharmas able to bring pure wholesome dharmas to completion through every gateway. First, what is accomplished through cultivation itself; second, what is accomplished through ripening sentient beings; third, what is accomplished through enduring what is hard to endure; and fourth, what is accomplished through never tiring of hearing and reflection. Furthermore, there are four dharmas able to bring about every kind of activity that benefits sentient beings — rescuing beings in four situations. First, where doubt and hesitation prevail; second, where beings are falling toward the most impure and evil destinies; third, where beings hold only to the resolute understanding of the lesser vehicle; and fourth, where beings harbor a mind of hatred, resenting and turning away from the noble teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third benefit's four causes map onto the six pāramitās in a specific pattern the 披尋記 lays out directly: cultivation itself accomplishes the pāramitā of meditative absorption; ripening sentient beings accomplishes giving and discipline together; enduring what is hard to endure accomplishes forbearance; and never tiring of hearing and reflection accomplishes the provisions of wisdom — together these complete the "white," or pure, dharmas comprising the pāramitās through every possible approach the Bodhisattva Ground catalogues (own-nature, universal scope, difficulty, every gateway, the virtuous person, every kind, fulfilling requests, benefit in both this world and the next, and purity). The fourth and final benefit names four specific crises where a bodhisattva's help matters most: resolving a being's genuine doubt about which path to follow, rescuing one about to fall into the lower destinies through unwholesome action, drawing one attached only to the lesser vehicle's resolve back toward the Great Vehicle, and meeting with compassion a being who has actually come to resent and reject the noble teaching itself. With this, all four excellent benefits of right conduct — accumulating provisions, purifying obstructions, completing pure dharmas, and rescuing beings in their moment of greatest need — are fully explained, closing the chapter on conduct's third gateway entirely.

58. Section: The Fourth Gateway Opens — The Five Marks of Conduct According to Dharma

Furthermore, how does a bodhisattva, while engaged in right conduct, still obtain these excellent benefits? It is by possessing conduct according to Dharma, equal conduct, good conduct, and the marks of conduct that abides in Dharma. What is a bodhisattva's conduct according to Dharma, and what is its mark? It is that bodhisattvas, in whatever they practice, never transgress the true Dharma — for this reason it is called conduct according to Dharma. Know that this conduct has five marks. First, toward sentient beings who delight in unwholesome conduct harmful to themselves, wishing to lead them into what is good, embracing them with compassion; second, toward sentient beings of the bodhisattva lineage who lack the external conditions needed, exhorting them to give rise to bodhicitta; third, personally understanding the excellence found within the pāramitās; fourth, toward what deserves reverence, giving rise to respectful homage and prostration; and fifth, toward hostile non-Buddhist beings, abiding in the noble teaching without being shaken.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth gateway explains how right conduct's benefits actually manifest in the present — through thirty-two marks organized into four groups (conduct according to Dharma's five, equal conduct's eight, good conduct's seven, and Dharma-abiding conduct's twelve), of which the first five open here. Conduct according to Dharma names conduct that never strays from the true Dharma in any of five situations: meeting harmful conduct with compassion rather than condemnation; actively encouraging a being of bodhisattva lineage who simply lacks a teacher to awaken bodhicitta; genuinely understanding, not merely repeating, why the pāramitās are excellent — the Bodhisattva Ground (juan 43) specifies this excellence as twofold, vast (embracing every being without exception, every field of learning without exception, every moment without exception) and undefiled (practiced with pure joy, without partiality, with genuine seriousness, and without dependence on any hoped-for reward); showing reverence where reverence is due; and remaining unshaken by hostile non-Buddhists precisely because a bodhisattva's conviction in the Great Vehicle's own benefit cannot be argued away, and their compassion extends equally to friend and enemy alike, leaving no opening for anger — the single affliction the treatise elsewhere calls most destructive to the bodhisattva path.

59. Section: Equal Conduct's Eight Marks — Loving Every Being Alike, and Dedicating Every Root of Goodness to Great Awakening

What is a bodhisattva's equal conduct, and what is its mark? It is that bodhisattvas practice with equal regard, universally, every activity that benefits sentient beings — for this reason it is called equal conduct. Know that this conduct has eight marks. First, loving all sentient beings equally; second, consoling them equally, with a mind of affection free of defilement, undifferentiated by body, by time, or by what is sought; third, abandoning all agitation, with a serene and pleasant countenance, bearing equally what one has already taken up; fourth, equally taking up what has not yet been taken up; fifth, equally enduring every kind of suffering; sixth, equally seeking boundless skillful means of taming; seventh, mutually and equally speaking right speech, speech that can be received; and eighth, equally dedicating every wholesome root toward great awakening.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's equal conduct, and what is its mark? It is that bodhisattvas practice with equal regard, universally, every activity that benefits sentient beings — for this reason it is called equal conduct.

First, loving all sentient beings equally; second, consoling them equally, with a mind of affection free of defilement, undifferentiated by body, by time, or by what is sought; third, abandoning all agitation, with a serene and pleasant countenance, bearing equally what one has already taken up; fourth, equally taking up what has not yet been taken up; fifth, equally enduring every kind of suffering; sixth, equally seeking boundless skillful means of taming; seventh, mutually and equally speaking right speech, speech that can be received; and eighth, equally dedicating every wholesome root toward great awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Eight marks, all variations on treating every being — friend, enemy, or stranger — exactly alike. Loving affection paired with reverence, since love without reverence collapses into idle familiarity and reverence without love into cold distance. Consolation undifferentiated by whether a being is friend or foe, by which of the three times the relationship belongs to, or by what a being happens to be seeking — offered, the treatise specifies, with an unfurrowed brow, kind words spoken first, a level and welcoming gaze, and a smile before speech even begins. Bearing what has already been undertaken and taking up what has not yet been undertaken, both without partiality toward any one being over another. Enduring every kind of suffering equally, whether it comes from someone dear or from an enemy. Seeking the boundless skillful means (ultimately the five fields of classical learning) needed to guide beings of every disposition. The fourfold "winning speech" — comforting words, congratulatory words on another's good fortune, and words of genuine benefit — offered without playing favorites. And, closing the set, dedicating every wholesome root toward great awakening rather than any lesser worldly reward, echoing the five marks of a pāramitā already explained in the previous fascicle: freedom from attachment, from lingering hope of reward, from any admixture of fault, from conceptual grasping at its own characteristic, and right dedication toward the very highest goal.

60. Section: Good Conduct's Seven Marks — The Six Pāramitās and the Four Means of Gathering Beings, Maturing Both Self and Others

What is a bodhisattva's good conduct, and what is its mark? It is that bodhisattvas, inwardly maturing the buddha-dharmas within, and outwardly maturing sentient beings without, practice good conduct — for this reason it is called good conduct. Know that this conduct has seven marks. First, giving without any dependence; second, keeping precepts without any dependence; third, cultivating forbearance out of a compassionate mind; fourth, cultivating diligence not toward any partial goal; fifth, cultivating meditative absorption for the sake of benefiting sentient beings; sixth, cultivating sublime wisdom where views no longer conflict with it; and seventh, maturing skillful means.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩具於善行？此何行相？謂諸菩薩，於內成熟諸佛法故，於外成熟諸有情故，修行善行，是故說名具於善行。當知此行有七行相。一者、無所依止而惠施故；二者、無所依止而持戒故；三者、由哀愍心而修忍故；四者、非於少分修精進故；五者、為作利益諸有情處修靜慮故；六者、見不相應修妙慧故；七者、成熟方便善巧故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's good conduct, and what is its mark? It is that bodhisattvas, inwardly maturing the buddha-dharmas within, and outwardly maturing sentient beings without, practice good conduct — for this reason it is called good conduct.
Know that this conduct has seven marks. First, giving without any dependence; second, keeping precepts without any dependence; third, cultivating forbearance out of a compassionate mind; fourth, cultivating diligence not toward any partial goal; fifth, cultivating meditative absorption for the sake of benefiting sentient beings; sixth, cultivating sublime wisdom where views no longer conflict with it; and seventh, maturing skillful means.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Good conduct's seven marks are the six pāramitās plus the four means of gathering beings folded into a seventh, together maturing a bodhisattva inwardly and beings outwardly at once. Giving and keeping precepts "without dependence" means free of hope for praise, reputation, or any worldly reward — understanding, as the Bodhisattva Ground puts it, that praise itself is empty sound, no more substantial than dry leaves rustling. Forbearance arises specifically from compassion rather than fear, the genuine patience of one who feels for a being's suffering rather than one merely too intimidated to react. Diligence refuses any "partial" satisfaction — never resting content with a lesser attainment (a first taste of concentration, say) when a greater one remains within reach. Meditative absorption is cultivated expressly to benefit beings — the Bodhisattva Ground's own list of eleven such benefits includes lending a hand with a task, easing suffering, teaching rightly, repaying kindness, protecting from fear, resolving grief, supplying what is lacking, and guiding a community well. Wisdom "where views no longer conflict with it" names insight finally aligned with suchness itself, free of the discrepancy between how things merely appear and how they truly are. And the seventh mark, maturing skillful means, names the four ways of gathering beings — giving, kind speech, beneficial action, and working alongside others — through which a bodhisattva turns every other being, not just oneself, into a fellow traveler on the path, exactly the spirit behind Avalokiteśvara's own thousand outstretched hands: not one hero acting alone, but everyone reaching out together.

61. Section: Dharma-Abiding Conduct Opens — From Not Judging a Teacher's Precepts to the Fruit of Great Merit and Majestic Virtue, the First Seven of Twelve Marks

What is a bodhisattva's Dharma-abiding conduct, and what is its mark? It is that bodhisattvas do not take mere seeking as the ultimate, do not take mere reading and recitation as the ultimate, do not take mere teaching as the ultimate, and do not take mere reflection as the ultimate; rather, in the excellent cultivation of calm-abiding within the mind itself, they exert diligent effort and cultivate with equal regard — for this reason it is called Dharma-abiding conduct. Know that this abiding has twelve marks. First, being without discrimination regarding whether one who can instruct abides in the precepts or does not; second, resting on this, respectfully receiving the instruction given; third, resting on this, bodily withdrawal; fourth, resting on this, mental withdrawal; fifth, resting on this, transcending attention associated with the śrāvaka vehicle and reflecting with attention associated with the Great Vehicle; sixth, resting on this, not abandoning the yoke of withdrawal, dwelling together with sentient beings, and dwelling together with what remains besides; and seventh, resting on this, receiving the fruit of pure worldly wisdom, great provisions of merit, and majestic virtue cultivated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth gateway's final and largest group, twelve marks of "Dharma-abiding conduct," describes how genuine practice actually takes root in the mind — going beyond mere seeking, recitation, teaching, or intellectual reflection to actual cultivation of calm-abiding and insight together, the only thing that makes a teaching genuinely one's own rather than borrowed learning. The first seven marks unfold in a single causal chain, each resting on the one before: not judging a teacher by whether they personally keep the precepts, only by whether their teaching is genuine — the Bodhisattva Ground's own instruction is simply "do not think, this teacher has broken precepts, I should not learn from him"; this in turn allows genuinely respectful reception of what is taught; which allows bodily withdrawal from worldly entanglement; which allows mental withdrawal from affliction; which allows attention to shift decisively from the lesser vehicle toward the Great Vehicle; which allows a bodhisattva to remain bound to beings by the "yoke" of guiding them — as a monastic properly leading a community, and, for a lay bodhisattva, as a householder or ruler rightly caring for family, household, or subjects; and which finally yields the fruit of purified worldly wisdom and great provisions of merit, the majestic virtue that comes from each of the six pāramitās' own fourfold power (severing its corresponding affliction, maturing the provisions, benefiting both self and other, and yielding its own future fruit — giving's future wealth, discipline's future favorable rebirth, forbearance's future freedom from enmity, and meditation's future clarity and supernormal ability, exactly as the Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on the six pāramitās' power lays out at length).

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth virtue closes the first set: however much worldly wisdom a bodhisattva accumulates on the provisional path, it remains insufficient until genuinely transcendent, non-conceptual wisdom is also sought and realized — worldly wisdom alone is still contaminated, and only wisdom realized from the first ground onward is truly reliable. The four cut-off faults then describe what a bodhisattva with genuinely pure wisdom no longer does when leading others: growing provoked by a challenging question rather than welcoming it as a sign of the questioner's own sincerity; teaching so indecisively that the confused remain confused, the already-clear never grow clearer, and doubt goes unresolved; urging others toward practices one does not oneself actually undertake; and favoring whichever followers happen to serve and flatter oneself. With this, all thirty-two marks of right conduct — conduct according to Dharma's five, equal conduct's eight, good conduct's seven, and Dharma-abiding conduct's twelve — are now complete in full, closing the chapter on conduct's fourth gateway, the marks of right conduct, entirely.

63. Section: The Fifth Gateway Opens — Similes for Pure Faith, Beginning With the Earth That Bears All Things, and the Water That Nourishes Them

Rather, bodhisattvas cause wholesome roots to grow through pure faith and joy, able to moisten them — like great water. Yet bodhisattvas are not like great water, which can also work against the ripening of the crops it moistens.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth and final gateway covered in this fascicle turns to nineteen similes for the pure faith a bodhisattva inspires — each following the same two-part pattern of taking what is genuinely apt in a natural image, then correcting for where the image falls short, unlike the *Ratnakūṭa*'s own version of these similes, which states only the positive comparison. The earth simile captures a bodhisattva's total impartiality, bearing whoever walks upon it, friend and enemy alike, without favor — but earth itself is inert, offering nothing unless beings labor to cultivate it themselves; a bodhisattva, unlike earth, actively draws forth wholesome roots rather than merely tolerating their growth. The water simile captures a bodhisattva's nourishing joy, moistening wholesome roots the way water moistens crops — but too much water actually ruins a harvest; a bodhisattva's joy, unlike water, can never be excessive, since more pure faith and joy is always better, without any corresponding risk of ruin.

Four similes in quick succession. Fire captures a bodhisattva's power to burn away the eight worldly concerns (gain and loss, praise and blame, and the rest) through deep weariness toward them — but unlike the fire that ends an eon and destroys a world entirely, leaving no buddha able to awaken there, a bodhisattva's burning purifies without ever destroying the very ground beings need to reach awakening. Wind captures a bodhisattva's power to draw already-ripened beings toward awakening — arousing bodhicitta and gathering bodhisattvas together, exhausting a śrāvaka's contaminants and bringing direct realization — but unlike wind, which always eventually dies down to nothing, a bodhisattva's effect never simply dissipates. The moon captures a bodhisattva's ever-increasing pure dharmas — but unlike the moon, bright only through its waxing half and dark through its waning half, a bodhisattva's brightness never has a waning phase at all. And the sun captures a bodhisattva's wisdom shining equally on every dharma, bright and dark alike — but unlike the sun, which the legendary asura Rāhu can seize and darken in an eclipse, nothing can ever seize or dim a bodhisattva's own wisdom.

65. Section: Four More Similes — The Lion, the Tamed Dragon-Elephant, the Red Lotus, and the Great Tree Whose Root Survives

Yet bodhisattvas, amid every destiny, never fear being seized and turned by affliction — like a lion. Yet bodhisattvas are not like a lion, which balks at a heavy burden. Yet bodhisattvas are able to bear every great burden of suffering — like a well-tamed dragon-elephant. Yet bodhisattvas are not like a dragon-elephant, which, meeting gain or loss, pleasant or harsh words, joy or pain, is stained by attachment or aversion. Yet bodhisattvas, amid worldly dharmas, are never stained by attachment or aversion — like the red lotus. Yet bodhisattvas are not like the red lotus, which, once its stem is severed, grows no further. Yet bodhisattvas, though they have subdued affliction, are sustained by the power of their wholesome roots and are born again within birth-and-death — like a great tree whose root is not destroyed. Yet bodhisattvas are not like a great tree, whose root will eventually, in time, certainly be destroyed.

Chinese Source

然諸菩薩一切趣中，終不怖畏煩惱所執而旋轉故，譬如師子。而諸菩薩非如師子，怯於大擔。然諸菩薩能擔一切大苦擔故，如善調龍。而諸菩薩非如龍象，若遭利衰、軟非軟語、若樂若苦，則為愛恚之所塗染。然諸菩薩於諸世法，不為愛恚所塗染故，如紅蓮華。而諸菩薩非如紅蓮，斷其莖已不復生長。然諸菩薩雖伏煩惱，由善根力之所任持，於生死中復生長故，猶如大樹根未損壞。而諸菩薩非如大樹，其根後時定當損壞。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four more similes. The lion captures fearlessness before any assembly, but a fable of a lion forced to carry a deer on its own back (rather than let a scheming fox go first and risk being eaten) shows even the lion balking at a heavy load, unlike a bodhisattva, who never hesitates to shoulder beings' full burden. The dragon-elephant captures the capacity to bear great suffering, but even a well-trained one is still swayed by gain and loss, praise and blame, exactly the eight worldly concerns a bodhisattva is never moved by. The red lotus captures purity untouched by the world's mud, but cut its stem and it grows no further — a bodhisattva's purity, unlike the lotus, is never at the mercy of external damage. And the great tree captures a bodhisattva who deliberately retains a trace of affliction to fuel further rebirth for beings' sake (留惑潤生), its root still alive and capable of new growth — but an actual tree's root eventually dies for good, while a bodhisattva's wholesome roots only ever keep increasing.

66. Section: Four More Similes — Every River Flowing to the Sea, Mount Sumeru, the Ministers Who Serve a King, and the Cloud That Waters the Crops

Yet bodhisattvas dedicate all their wholesome roots toward nirvāṇa and great awakening — like every stream flowing into the great ocean. Yet bodhisattvas are not like these streams, which, upon entering the great ocean, simply take on the ocean's own nature. Yet bodhisattvas, resting on the wholesome roots of nirvāṇa and great awakening, engage in playful mastery — like the gods who dwell resting on Sumeru. Yet bodhisattvas are not like the gods dwelling on Sumeru, wholly absorbed in their own heedless pleasure. Yet bodhisattvas, upheld by skillful means and wisdom, accomplish everything a buddha does — like the ministers who serve and support a great king. Yet bodhisattvas are not like the ministers who serve a king, who guard their people for their own benefit alone. Yet bodhisattvas, giving no thought to their own benefit, protect sentient beings — like a great cloud. Yet bodhisattvas are not like a great cloud, which cannot bring the harvest to completion after all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four similes describing a bodhisattva's mastery. Every river captures wholesome roots all flowing toward one destination, nirvāṇa and great awakening — but a river entering the ocean simply dissolves into ocean-water, while a bodhisattva's transformed basis remains inconceivable, neither simply identical with nor different from what it merges into. The gods on Sumeru capture a bodhisattva's playful ease, neither bound to saṃsāra nor fixed in nirvāṇa — but those gods are absorbed purely in their own heedless enjoyment, while a bodhisattva's ease always serves both self-cultivation and others' liberation at once. The king's ministers capture skillful means and wisdom accomplishing every buddha-activity — but ordinary ministers ultimately serve their king's and their own interest, while a bodhisattva's skillful means serves beings alone. And the great cloud captures selfless protection of beings — but even the heaviest rain cannot guarantee a harvest, while a bodhisattva's care for beings, unlike a cloud's, never falls short of its purpose.

67. Section: The Final Five Similes — A Wheel-Turning King With Room for Others, Manure That Feeds the Sugarcane — And Why No Simile Truly Suffices, Closing the Fifth Gateway and All of Fascicle 79

Yet bodhisattvas cause the factors of awakening to grow to their utmost completion — like a wheel-turning king appearing in the world. Yet bodhisattvas are not like a wheel-turning king, beside whom there can be no second great being. Yet bodhisattvas, equal in liberation, give rise to many wholesome roots appearing together — like a wish-fulfilling gem. Yet bodhisattvas are utterly unlike a wish-fulfilling gem, which cannot compare at all to the treasures and vehicles of the two lesser paths. Yet bodhisattvas enter the uncontaminated realm, their activity equal, receiving joy equally — like the gods who have already entered the Grove of Sundry Trees. Yet bodhisattvas are not like the gods who have entered that grove, whose afflictions only grow and who will, in the future, fall. Yet bodhisattvas subdue their afflictions and do not fall — their remaining afflictions are like poisons subdued by an incantation. Yet a bodhisattva's afflictions are not like poison subdued by an incantation, which merely does no further harm and has no other virtue. Yet bodhisattvas, through their own remaining affliction, are able to benefit all sentient beings — so this affliction is like a heap of manure within a great city. It is thus that a bodhisattva's every virtue can, on the surface, be likened to things commonly known in the world, and for this reason similes can be given; yet, because these virtues are so excellent, there is finally no simile for them at all. Therefore know: a bodhisattva's virtue is something no simile whatsoever can reach.

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The final five similes, and the conclusion that closes the whole set. A wheel-turning king captures the factors of awakening brought to full completion — but only one wheel-turning king ever rules a given world at a time, while a bodhisattva actively wants every being to become a bodhisattva too, with room for infinitely many. A wish-fulfilling gem captures many wholesome roots appearing together in perfect equality — but no gem approaches the treasures and vehicles even of the lesser paths, let alone a bodhisattva's own attainment. The gods of the Grove of Sundry Trees (in Trāyastriṃśa heaven) capture equal, joyful activity in the uncontaminated realm attained through the forbearance of non-arising — but those gods merely enjoy contaminated pleasure that only deepens affliction and ends in a future fall, unlike a bodhisattva's uncontaminated joy. Subdued poison captures affliction rendered harmless through a bodhisattva's deliberate retention of it (for the sake of continued rebirth to help beings) — but subdued poison serves no purpose beyond not causing harm, while a bodhisattva's residual affliction is put to positive use. And the closing simile of manure — repugnant on its own, yet exactly what makes sugarcane and grapevines flourish when properly applied — captures precisely this: a bodhisattva's very affliction, retained on purpose rather than fully severed, becomes the fuel for continued rebirth and continued benefit to beings. The set closes with an explicit acknowledgment of its own limits: every one of these nineteen similes borrows something "roughly" (麤) similar from ordinary experience, useful enough to give some purchase on what a bodhisattva actually is — but a bodhisattva's true virtue so exceeds every one of them that, in the end, no simile can truly reach it at all. With this, the fifth and final gateway covered in this fascicle — the similes that give rise to pure faith — closes in full, and with it, the first five of the sixteen gateways of the chapter on conduct (wrong conduct, right conduct, right conduct's excellent benefits, right conduct's thirty-two marks, and these nineteen similes for pure faith) are now complete. The remaining eleven gateways continue in fascicle 80.
