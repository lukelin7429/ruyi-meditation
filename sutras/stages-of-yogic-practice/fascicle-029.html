Fascicle 29 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十九 · The Second Yogic Process Concludes

The Four Right Efforts and Beyond

Fascicle 29 completes the second yogic process of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, continuing directly from fascicle 28's close of the four foundations of mindfulness. It works through the remaining thirty-three factors of enlightenment and closes out all seventeen questions of "establishing categories" — the final fascicle of this entire second yogic process.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 29 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Four Right Efforts Open, Continuing Directly from the Foundations

Fascicle 29 opens exactly where fascicle 28 closed: a practitioner who has thoroughly cultivated the four foundations of mindfulness has begun dismantling the coarsest distorted views and can now genuinely tell wholesome from unwholesome — the necessary foundation for the second factor, the four right efforts, to actually function.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之四……如是於四念住串習行故，已能除遣麤麤顛倒，已能了達善不善法。從此無間，於諸未生惡不善法，為不生故；於諸已生惡不善法，為令斷故；於其未生一切善法，為令生故；於其已生一切善法，為欲令住、令不忘失；廣說如前，乃至策心、持心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the fourth part of the second yogic process … Thus, through repeated practice of the four foundations of mindfulness, one becomes able to dispel the coarsest of the distorted views, and becomes able to clearly distinguish wholesome states from unwholesome ones. Immediately following this, one strives — in order to prevent unwholesome states not yet arisen from arising; to end unwholesome states already arisen; to give rise to wholesome states not yet arisen; and to make wholesome states already arisen abide and not be forgotten — as has been extensively explained before, spurring and sustaining the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by placing this fascicle precisely: the second yogic process's seventeen-question structure runs from fascicle 26 through this one, and fascicle 29 carries it to completion — from the second factor of cultivation through the factors of enlightenment (the four right efforts) all the way through the seventeenth and final question, setting out toward liberation yet arriving at no fruit. He notes the treatise is careful not to overclaim what sustained mindfulness practice has actually accomplished at this point: the text says only that the coarsest distorted views have been dispelled and wholesome states can now be told from unwholesome ones — not that any noble path has yet been attained, whether through hearing, reflection, or cultivation. This matters, Cháng Bǎi stresses, because it explains why the right efforts follow the foundations rather than preceding them: without first learning to actually recognize a wholesome state from an unwholesome one through sustained mindfulness practice, energetic effort has no way to know which direction to push. Diligence without discernment is just agitation; the four right efforts only become genuinely "right" once the four foundations have supplied the judgment they depend on.

2. What Counts as Unwholesome; Arisen and Unarisen

Chinese Source

云何名為惡不善法？謂欲纏染汙身語意業，是身語意惡行所攝，及能起彼所有煩惱。……若未和合、未現在前，說名未生；若已和合、已現在前，說名已生。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called an evil, unwholesome state? It is bodily, verbal, and mental action defiled by the manifest afflictions bound to the desire realm — action classed as bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct — together with all the afflictions capable of giving rise to such misconduct. Whatever has not yet come together through conditions, not yet appearing before one, is called unarisen; whatever has already come together through conditions, already appearing before one, is called arisen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out a precise technical distinction: "unwholesome" names two related but distinct things — the actual bodily, verbal, or mental misconduct that results (the visible action), and the underlying afflictions (greed, hatred, delusion) capable of producing it, whether or not they've yet erupted into action. Desire, aversion, and delusion sitting quietly in the mind are affliction; the moment they drive an actual act of body or speech, that act becomes misconduct proper. He notes the treatise specifically names desire-realm affliction here, since a practitioner at this stage — still working through the foundations toward access concentration — hasn't yet risen even to the coarser levels of form-realm or formless-realm subtlety; the coarsest, most immediate work still lies in the desire realm itself. The arisen/unarisen distinction closing this section is, Cháng Bǎi notes, deceptively simple but load-bearing: it is precisely this distinction the four right efforts are organized around, targeting unwholesome states differently depending on whether they've already manifested or merely lie in wait.

3. What Counts as Wholesome; Generating the Aspiration

Chinese Source

云何名為一切善法？謂若彼對治、若蓋對治、若結對治。未生、已生，應知如前惡不善法。……若時未生惡不善法，先未和合，為令不生，發起希願：我當令彼一切一切皆不復生。是名於諸未生惡不善法，為不生故生欲。若時已生惡不善法，先已和合，為令斷故，發起希願：我當於彼一切一切皆不忍受，斷滅、除遣。是名於諸已生惡不善法，為令斷故生欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called every kind of wholesome state? It is whatever counters ordinary affliction, whatever counters the hindrances, and whatever counters the fetters. Arisen and unarisen should be understood as with the unwholesome states above. When an unwholesome state has not yet arisen, its conditions not yet come together, one generates the aspiration, for the sake of its not arising: "May I ensure this, and everything like it, never arises at all." This is generating the aspiration, for unarisen unwholesome states, that they not arise. When an unwholesome state has already arisen, its conditions already come together, one generates the aspiration, for the sake of ending it: "I will not tolerate this, or anything like it, at all — I will cut it off, remove it, and expel it." This is generating the aspiration, for arisen unwholesome states, that they be ended.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps wholesome states onto exactly the three-stage antidote structure this project has followed throughout: ordinary counter-measures for affliction arising in daily activity (the discipline that directly counters desire, aversion, and delusion), hindrance-counters used specifically in seated practice (the eight paired mental states — contracted versus scattered, lifted versus sunken, unagitated versus agitated, tranquil versus untranquil — each answering one of the five hindrances), and fetter-counters used once genuine concentration has been reached (concentrated, well-cultivated, and well-liberated mind, answering the deeper afflictive fetters). Generating the aspiration itself, he notes, is simply the practitioner's own resolved wish stated in the clearest possible terms — not yet the effort itself, but the settled intention that effort will then carry out, aimed with equal force at what hasn't yet happened and what already has.

4. Self-Spurring and Diligent Effort: Three Ways of Drawing the Line

Chinese Source

當知此中，於緣不現見境惡不善法……自策、自勵，是名策勵。於緣現見境惡不善法……勇猛正勤，是名發勤精進。……又於下品、中品諸纏……故自策勵。於上品纏……發勤精進。……又若行於過去境界……是名策勵。若行現在所緣境界……是名發勤精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that here: regarding unwholesome states connected to an object not directly seen … spurring and rousing oneself is called self-spurring. Regarding unwholesome states connected to an object directly seen … courageous and correct exertion is called diligent effort. Further, regarding fetters of the lower and middling grades … one spurs oneself. Regarding fetters of the upper grade … one exerts diligent effort. Further, when engaging with a past object … this is called self-spurring. When engaging with a presently experienced object … this is called diligent effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through three independent ways the treatise draws the line between "self-spurring" (策勵) and "diligent effort" (發勤精進) — not contradictory definitions but three lenses on the same underlying energy. By object: affliction tied to a past or future event, not currently visible, calls for quieter self-spurring — noticing an old grudge or an anticipated worry arise, and firmly declining to indulge it, the way a practitioner might catch themselves replaying an old debt never repaid and choose, deliberately, to let it go as if it had been given freely rather than stolen. Affliction tied to something directly in front of one right now — an actual sight, sound, or encounter provoking desire or anger in the moment — calls for the more forceful, courageous exertion of diligent effort, since a live temptation demands more immediate resistance than a remembered one.

By grade of affliction: Cháng Bǎi flags this second framework as running counter to how Elder Miàojìng usually explains the relative strength of coarse versus subtle affliction — here, the lower and middling grades (the coarser, more obvious afflictions) are handled by the gentler self-spurring, while the upper grade (paradoxically, the subtlest and hardest to detect) demands the full force of diligent effort, precisely because subtle affliction is so easily overlooked that only vigorous, sustained exertion catches it at all. And by time: past and future objects call for self-spurring, while the present object — whatever the five senses and mind are engaging with right now — calls for diligent effort, since only the present moment offers a live opportunity to actually intervene. Three frameworks, one underlying point, per Cháng Bǎi: effort must be calibrated to the difficulty of the target, not applied uniformly regardless of what's actually being faced.

5. Milestone: A Fourth Framework Closes the First Two Right Efforts

A final framework distinguishes self-spurring from diligent effort by whether an unwholesome state arises from mental discrimination alone or from discrimination triggered by an actual sense-object — closing the first two right efforts (ending unwholesome states, whether arisen or not) with all four complementary lenses now in place.

Chinese Source

又或有惡不善法，唯由分別力生，非境界力；或有惡不善法，由分別力生，亦境界力。……當知此中，惡不善法唯由分別力生，非境界力者……是名策勵。若由分別力生，亦境界力者……是名發勤精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, some unwholesome states arise solely through the power of discrimination, not through the power of an object; some unwholesome states arise through the power of discrimination together with the power of an object. Know that here: where an unwholesome state arises solely through the power of discrimination, not the power of an object … this is called self-spurring. Where it arises through the power of discrimination together with the power of an object … this is called diligent effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the power of discrimination" as the sixth consciousness alone — mental proliferation running on its own, as when a seated practitioner's mind wanders into old grievances or imagined futures with no actual sense-object present to trigger it. "The power of an object" only ever operates jointly with discrimination, never alone, since the five sense-consciousnesses can merely register a sight or sound; they cannot themselves generate craving or aversion — that judgment is always the sixth consciousness's work, deciding whether to keep looking, keep listening, keep dwelling. When affliction arises purely from unprompted mental wandering, gentler self-spurring suffices; when a live object joins forces with that same discrimination, the more forceful diligent effort is called for. With this fourth framework, Cháng Bǎi notes, the first two right efforts — ending what unwholesome has already arisen, and preventing what hasn't yet arisen — are fully explained from every relevant angle: by whether the object is visible, by the affliction's grade, by time, and now by whether an actual object is even involved.

6. The Remaining Two Right Efforts: Generating and Sustaining Wholesome States

Chinese Source

於其未生一切善法，為令生故生欲者……發起猛利求獲得欲、求現前欲……是名於其未生一切善法，為令生故生欲。於其已生一切善法，為欲令住、令不忘失、令修圓滿生欲者……於此善法已得不失、已得不退，依是說言：為欲令住。於此善法明了現前，無闇鈍性，依是說言：令不忘失。於此善法已得現前，數數修習，成滿究竟，依是說言：令修圓滿。……策勵者，為於已得令現前故。發勤精進者，為於未得令其得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Generating the aspiration, for unarisen wholesome states, that they arise, is generating an intense wish to obtain them and to bring them before the mind. Generating the aspiration, for arisen wholesome states, that they abide, not be forgotten, and be brought to completion: that they be obtained and not lost, obtained and not regressed from, is called "that they abide"; that they remain clear and present, free of any dullness, is called "that they not be forgotten"; that, already present, they be practiced again and again until brought to final completion, is called "that they be brought to completion." Self-spurring is for the sake of bringing what has already been obtained before the mind; diligent effort is for the sake of obtaining what has not yet been obtained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third and fourth right efforts — generating unarisen wholesome states and sustaining arisen ones — as answering a genuinely common experience: wholesome qualities are hard-won and easily lost. He offers the plain example of memorizing a sūtra passage: skip reciting it for a few days and it fades, however solidly it once seemed learned — precisely why the treatise insists on three distinct dimensions of "sustaining" a wholesome state already gained: not merely retained but not regressed from; not merely present but vividly, readily available rather than dull and sluggish (able, for instance, to bring impurity-contemplation instantly to mind the moment attraction arises, with no delay); and not merely maintained but actively carried toward full completion through repeated practice, since standing still on the path is itself a kind of quiet loss.

Self-spurring and diligent effort divide this same territory cleanly, Cháng Bǎi notes: self-spurring handles what has already been gained — keeping it present, unforgotten, and complete — while diligent effort handles what remains ungained, actively pursuing new wholesome ground rather than merely defending existing ground. Together, all four right efforts — ending, preventing, generating, and sustaining — cover the entire terrain a practitioner's ongoing relationship with wholesome and unwholesome states can occupy, with no gap between them.

7. Spurring the Mind and Sustaining It — Balancing Sinking and Agitation

Chinese Source

言策心者，謂若心於修奢摩他一境性中精勤方便……其心於內極略下劣、或恐下劣，觀見是已，爾時隨取一種淨妙舉相，殷勤策勵、慶悅其心。是名策心。云何持心？謂修舉時，其心掉動、或恐掉動，觀見是已，爾時還復於內略攝其心，修奢摩他。是名持心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Spurring the mind is: when the mind, diligently applying itself within the one-pointedness of calm-abiding practice … becomes, or threatens to become, extremely contracted and low within — upon observing this, one then takes up one of the pure, excellent images used for lifting the mind, vigorously spurring and gladdening it. This is spurring the mind. What is sustaining the mind? When, during the practice of lifting, the mind becomes, or threatens to become, agitated — upon observing this, one then returns the mind, contracting it inward again, to the practice of calm-abiding. This is sustaining the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these two final terms in the right-efforts formula as the practical, moment-to-moment corrective mechanism running underneath everything else described so far: spurring the mind is the antidote specifically for sinking during calm-abiding practice — noticing the mind going slack or drowsy and immediately reaching for one of the six recollections (the Buddha's virtues, one's own generosity) to lift it back into alertness. Sustaining the mind is the antidote for the opposite failure, agitation arising during the more energetic practice of "lifting" itself — noticing the mind has overshot into restlessness and deliberately narrowing it back down into calm-abiding's one-pointed focus. Cháng Bǎi notes the two work as a constant, self-correcting pair: too much stillness calls for spurring, too much lifting calls for sustaining, and a practitioner who can catch each drift early enough, before it fully manifests, eventually settles into the balanced equanimity where neither correction is needed. With this, the full sequence of the four right efforts — generating the aspiration, self-spurring, diligent effort, spurring the mind, and sustaining it — is complete.

8. Milestone: Two Further Names — the Four Right Victories and the Four Right Cuttings

The treatise closes the four right efforts with two alternate names, each highlighting a different aspect of the same practice: "four right victories," framed against dark and bright states, and "four right cuttings," each cutting given its own precise technical name.

Chinese Source

如是四種，亦名正勝。謂於黑品諸法，其未生者為令不生，其已生者為令斷滅，生欲、策勵、發勤精進、策心、持心，是二正勝。於白品諸法……如前黑品廣說，應知是二正勝。如是四種，亦名正斷。一名律儀斷。謂於已生惡不善法，為令斷故……二名斷斷。謂於未生惡不善法，為不生故……由於已生惡不善事，應修律儀，令其斷滅，不應忍受。由是因緣，名律儀斷。於其未生惡不善事，為欲令彼不現行斷，為欲令彼不現前斷，為斷故斷，故名斷斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

These same four are also called the four right victories: regarding dark states, preventing what has not arisen and ending what has — this is two right victories; regarding bright states, generating what has not arisen and sustaining what has, exactly as explained for dark states — this is the other two right victories. These same four are also called the four right cuttings. The first is called discipline-cutting: regarding unwholesome states already arisen, generating the aspiration and effort to end them. The second is called cutting-upon-cutting: regarding unwholesome states not yet arisen, generating the aspiration and effort that they not arise. Because one should, through discipline, end what unwholesome has already arisen rather than tolerate it, this is called discipline-cutting. Regarding what unwholesome has not yet arisen: cutting off its coming to manifest, cutting off its coming to appear, cutting for the very sake of cutting — hence it is called cutting-upon-cutting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "victory" (勝) as naming the same four efforts from the standpoint of triumph over affliction: two victories over dark, unwholesome states (preventing and ending), two victories in cultivating bright, wholesome ones (generating and sustaining) — the identical structure, simply named for its outcome rather than its method. "Cutting" (斷) highlights something more specific: discipline-cutting names the straightforward work of actually ending an unwholesome state that has already manifested, through the direct application of discipline rather than tolerance or delay. Cutting-upon-cutting — Cháng Bǎi notes the doubled name is deliberate, not redundant — names the more subtle work of preventing an unwholesome state that hasn't yet appeared, itself unpacked into three angles: cutting off its coming to actually manifest in action, cutting off its coming to appear even as a mental event, and cutting simply for the sake of cutting itself, before any specific target has even arisen — a general readiness to refuse affliction root and branch, not merely reactive vigilance. With both alternate names now given, the four right efforts — training, right victory, and right cutting all naming the identical practice — close in full, and the treatise turns next to the third factor of enlightenment: the four bases of power.

9. Milestone: Cultivation-Cutting and Protection-Cutting Close the Four Right Efforts

The remaining two cuttings — cultivation-cutting for unarisen wholesome states, protection-cutting for arisen ones — complete all four names, and the treatise closes with a compact summary: the entire practice reduces to two perfections, aspiration and application.

Chinese Source

三名修斷。謂於未生一切善法，為令生故……由於善法數修數習，先所未得能令現前，能有所斷，故名修斷。四名防護斷。謂於已生一切善法，為欲令住……由於已得、已現在前諸善法中，遠離放逸，修不放逸，能令善法住不忘失……能有所斷，故名防護斷。……謂為顯示於黑白品捨取事中，增上意樂圓滿及加行圓滿，是故宣說四種正斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third is called cultivation-cutting: regarding all wholesome states not yet arisen, generating them through repeated cultivation and practice, bringing what was previously ungained before the mind, and thereby cutting off what obstructed it. The fourth is called protection-cutting: regarding all wholesome states already arisen, remaining free of negligence so that they abide, are not forgotten, and are protected from loss — thereby likewise cutting off what would obstruct them. All of this is taught in order to display, with respect to what should be abandoned among dark states and what should be taken up among bright ones, the perfection of aspiration and the perfection of application — this is why the four right efforts are taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes cultivation-cutting and protection-cutting complete the fourfold naming by covering the two remaining cases: gaining what has never yet been gained requires sustained cultivation over time (the same "digging a well" persistence needed anywhere real skill is built), while protecting what has already been gained requires simple, vigilant freedom from negligence — a very different kind of effort from first acquisition, closer to maintenance than construction. He closes by drawing out the treatise's own compact summary of the entire four-right-efforts topic: everything explained — generating the aspiration, self-spurring, diligent effort, spurring and sustaining the mind, and all four alternate names — reduces to exactly two perfections. The perfection of aspiration (增上意樂圓滿) is the settled inner resolve, corresponding to "generating the aspiration" alone; the perfection of application (加行圓滿) is everything that actually carries that resolve into action — self-spurring, diligent effort, and the moment-to-moment corrections of spurring and sustaining the mind together. Resolve without follow-through accomplishes nothing; follow-through without resolve has nothing to sustain it. With both perfections in place, the second factor of enlightenment closes, and the treatise turns to the third: the four bases of power.

10. Milestone: The Four Bases of Power Open — Aspiration-Concentration in Detail

The third factor of enlightenment opens: four kinds of concentration, each driven by a different power — aspiration, effort, mind, and investigation — beginning with aspiration-concentration's own causal conditions and its genuine but limited function.

Chinese Source

從此復修四三摩地。謂欲三摩地、勤三摩地、心三摩地、觀三摩地。……若於是時純生樂欲，生樂欲已，於諸所有惡不善法自性、因緣、過患、對治，正審思察，住一境念。……於諸善法自性、因緣、功德、出離，正審思察，住一境念。即由如是多修習故，觸一境性。於諸所有惡不善法現行諸纏，能令遠離，而未永害煩惱隨眠。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point, one further cultivates the four kinds of concentration: aspiration-concentration, effort-concentration, mind-concentration, and investigation-concentration. When, at this time, a pure aspiration arises, and having arisen, one carefully and thoroughly examines the intrinsic nature, causes and conditions, faults, and antidote of all evil, unwholesome states, abiding in mindfulness on a single object — and likewise carefully and thoroughly examines the intrinsic nature, causes and conditions, merit, and liberating power of wholesome states, abiding in mindfulness on a single object — then, through this repeated cultivation, one attains one-pointedness. This is able to keep at a distance the manifest fetters of all evil, unwholesome states, though it has not yet permanently destroyed the latent seeds of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the four bases of power as a natural next step once a practitioner has cultivated the right efforts but finds their concentration still insufficient — each of the four names a different engine capable, on its own, of driving genuine absorption, so a practitioner can lean on whichever comes most naturally. Aspiration-concentration, the first, is built from pure, uncontaminated wish for the goal, joined to careful, structured contemplation: examining unwholesome states along the same four dimensions used throughout this project (their basic nature, what causes them, the harm they do, and how to counter them) and wholesome states along a parallel four (their nature, their causes, their genuine benefit, and their power to liberate) — sustained contemplation of this kind, repeated, naturally settles the mind onto a single object and produces one-pointedness.

Cháng Bǎi flags the closing line as an important honesty check the treatise builds into every stage of this account: even successful aspiration-concentration only suppresses affliction's active manifestation, keeping the fetters at bay — it does not yet destroy the latent seeds underneath. This distinction between suppressing and destroying, he notes, recurs throughout the treatise precisely because it's so easy for a practitioner to mistake temporary quiet for permanent freedom; genuine destruction of the seeds themselves requires supramundane wisdom, still further down the path than concentration alone can reach.

11. Effort-Concentration and Mind-Concentration

Chinese Source

若於過去、未來、現在所緣境界，能順所有惡不善法……自策、自勵、發勤精進，於彼所緣，於彼境界自性、因緣、過患、對治，正審思察，住一境念。即由如是多安住故，能正生起心一境性。……是名勤增上力所得三摩地。……若復策發諸下劣心，或復制持諸掉舉心，又時時間修增上捨。……是名心增上力所得三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding objects belonging to the past, future, or present that tend toward evil, unwholesome states … self-spurring, self-rousing, and diligent effort, carefully and thoroughly examining that object's nature, causes, faults, and antidote, abiding in mindfulness on a single object — through this repeated abiding, one-pointedness rightly arises. This is called the concentration attained through the power of effort. Further lifting a sunken mind, restraining an agitated one, and cultivating excellent equanimity from time to time — this is called the concentration attained through the power of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes effort-concentration simply applies the same self-spurring and diligent-effort distinction from the four right efforts (past and future objects met with quieter self-spurring, present objects with more forceful diligent effort) directly toward building one-pointedness itself, rather than merely toward ending or preventing specific unwholesome states — the identical energy redirected at concentration as its own goal. It carries the identical limitation as aspiration-concentration: manifest affliction kept at a distance, latent seeds untouched.

Mind-concentration, the third, is built entirely from the lift-calm-equanimity technique already established throughout this project: a sunken, drowsy mind is lifted through an inspiring recollection; an agitated, scattered mind is drawn back through calming focus; and once neither correction is needed, the mind settles into equanimity — clear, undisturbed, requiring no further adjustment in either direction. Cháng Bǎi notes this three-part rhythm, repeated patiently over time, is what actually produces stable one-pointedness for a practitioner whose natural tendency is neither strongly aspirational nor strongly energetic, but simply needs the oscillation between too little and too much energy smoothed out through practice.

12. Milestone: Investigation-Concentration Closes All Four; Eight Kinds of Abandoning-Conditioning Open

Investigation-concentration — built from honest mental engagement and unflinching self-observation — closes all four concentrations with the same candid admission repeated at each stage: affliction's activity has been kept away, but its seeds remain. The treatise then opens a second component of this factor, eight kinds of conditioning aimed specifically at what concentration alone cannot reach.

Chinese Source

便自思惟：我今為有現有惡不善法不覺知耶？為無現無惡不善法不覺知耶？我今應當遍審觀察。……由是因緣，離增上慢，如實自知：我唯於纏心得解脫，未於一切一切隨眠心得解脫。……是名觀增上力所得三摩地。……彼由如是四三摩地增上力故，已遠諸纏；復為永害一切一切惡不善法諸隨眠故，及為修習能對治彼諸善法故，便更生起樂欲策勵……彼於如是正修習時，有八斷行，為欲永害諸隨眠故，為三摩地得圓滿故，差別而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

One then reflects: "Is there, right now, some evil unwholesome state present that I am simply not aware of? Or is there, right now, truly none present, and I correctly know there is none? I should thoroughly and carefully examine this." … Through this, one remains free of overestimation, truly knowing for oneself: "I have only gained freedom of mind from affliction's active manifestation — I have not yet gained freedom of mind from every latent seed of every kind." … This is called the concentration attained through the power of investigation. Having, through the power of these four concentrations, already kept the fetters at a distance, and further wishing to permanently destroy every latent seed of evil, unwholesome states of every kind, and to cultivate the very wholesome states that counter them, one generates aspiration and self-spurring once again. In genuinely cultivating this way, there are eight kinds of conditioning, operating with their own distinct functions, for the sake of permanently destroying the latent seeds and for the sake of bringing concentration itself to full completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats investigation-concentration as the most self-aware of the four: rather than simply applying aspiration, effort, or the lift-calm rhythm, it is built from continuously and honestly asking oneself whether affliction is secretly present without one's own knowledge — a check against exactly the self-flattery that produces false confidence in one's own progress. He reads the closing admission — "freed only from affliction's activity, not from any latent seed" — as the treatise's deliberate refusal to let any of the four concentrations be mistaken for final attainment, however genuinely they settle the mind.

This shared limitation, Cháng Bǎi explains, is precisely why the four bases of power don't stop at four concentrations: having built genuine one-pointedness through whichever of the four approaches suits them, a practitioner still faces the deeper task of actually destroying affliction's seeds, which concentration alone — however refined — cannot do. The treatise's answer is eight kinds of conditioning, built from the same five terms already established in the four right efforts (aspiration, self-spurring, diligent effort, spurring the mind, sustaining it) but now aimed at two specific goals at once: permanently uprooting the latent seeds, and bringing concentration itself to complete fullness. What these eight specifically are is unpacked next.

13. The Eight Kinds of Conditioning Named in Full

Chinese Source

一者、欲。謂起如是希望樂欲：我於何時修三摩地當得圓滿？我於何時當能斷滅惡不善法所有隨眠？二者、策勵。謂乃至修所有對治，不捨加行。三者、信。謂不捨加行正安住故，於上所證，深生信解。四者、安。謂清淨信而為上首，心生歡喜；心歡喜故，漸次息除諸惡不善法品麤重。五者、念。謂九種相，於九種相安住其心，奢摩他品能攝持故。六者、正知。謂毗鉢舍那品慧。七者、思。謂心造作。於斷、未斷正觀察時，造作其心，發起能順止觀二品身業、語業。八者、捨。謂行過去、未來、現在隨順諸惡不善法中，心無染汙、心平等性。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, aspiration: generating this very wish — "When will my cultivation of concentration reach completion? When will I be able to destroy all the latent seeds of evil, unwholesome states?" Second, self-spurring: cultivating every antidote without abandoning the effort. Third, faith: through not abandoning the effort and remaining rightly settled in it, generating deep confidence in what is to be realized above. Fourth, pliancy: with pure faith leading the way, gladness arises in the mind; through that gladness, the grossness of evil, unwholesome states is gradually calmed and removed. Fifth, mindfulness: the nine aspects, the mind abiding in them, sustained by the calm-abiding factor. Sixth, clear comprehension: the wisdom belonging to insight. Seventh, volition: the mind's own activity — when carefully observing whether affliction is cut or not yet cut, activating the mind to generate bodily and verbal action in accord with calm-abiding and insight. Eighth, equanimity: whether engaging past, future, or present objects tending toward evil, unwholesome states, the mind remains undefiled, in a state of genuine evenness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all eight — also called the "eight excellent practices" — as a single developmental arc. Aspiration is the settled wish for two specific goals: full concentration, and the actual destruction of affliction's seeds. Self-spurring is simply never abandoning the antidotes already known — impurity for desire, loving-kindness for aversion, dependent origination for delusion, and so on. Faith follows from sustained effort itself, deepening into genuine confidence that the path actually works. Pliancy is the settled ease, grounded in that faith, that gradually dissolves affliction's grossness. Mindfulness draws on the nine stages of mental abiding already established in this project's account of calm-abiding — Cháng Bǎi notes this eight-fold list simply names, rather than re-derives, that same nine-stage progression as its fifth member. Clear comprehension is specifically insight's own wisdom, the discernment needed to actually recognize whether affliction has been cut. Volition is the mind's own follow-through, translating recognition into actual bodily and verbal conduct consistent with calm-abiding and insight — Cháng Bǎi identifies this with "concentration-born discipline" (定共戒), the effortless, spontaneous rectitude that accompanies genuine absorption, distinct from both ordinary precept-keeping and the still-higher path-born discipline of a noble one. And equanimity closes the list: genuine evenness of mind toward any object, past, present, or future, that might otherwise tend toward affliction — undisturbed whether the object is remembered, anticipated, or directly faced.

14. Milestone: The Four Bases of Power Close in Full

The treatise closes with a simple diagnostic — two conditions for knowing whether the latent seeds have actually been cut — then matches the eight kinds of conditioning back onto the four-part faith/aspiration/diligence/means framework from fascicle 28, before naming the entire structure together as the four bases of power.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，於隨眠斷分別了知。謂由境界不現見思，及由境界現見捨故。……如是名為八種斷行，亦名勝行。如是八種斷行、勝行，即是為害隨眠瑜伽。此中欲者，即是彼欲；此中策勵，即彼精進；此中信者，即是彼信；此中安、念、正知、思、捨，即彼方便。……若先欲等四三摩地，若今所說八種斷行，於為永斷所有隨眠圓滿成辦三摩地時，一切總名欲三摩地斷行成就神足、勤三摩地斷行成就神足、心三摩地斷行成就神足、觀三摩地斷行成就神足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through two conditions, one clearly discerns whether the latent seeds have been cut off: through thinking about an object not directly seen, and through equanimity toward an object directly seen. This is called the eight kinds of conditioning, also called the excellent practices. These eight kinds of conditioning, or excellent practices, are precisely the yogic discipline for destroying the latent seeds. Here, aspiration is that same aspiration named earlier; here, self-spurring is that same diligence; here, faith is that same faith; and here, pliancy, mindfulness, clear comprehension, volition, and equanimity are that same means. Thus, both the four concentrations explained earlier and the eight kinds of conditioning explained now, taken together, at the point of permanently cutting off every latent seed and bringing concentration to full completion, are together called the basis of power through aspiration-concentration and conditioning, the basis of power through effort-concentration and conditioning, the basis of power through mind-concentration and conditioning, and the basis of power through investigation-concentration and conditioning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing diagnostic as beautifully simple: a practitioner tests their own progress by noticing whether calm reflection on a past or future trigger, and genuine equanimity when directly facing a present one, both hold steady — if either cracks, the seed is still there, however quiet it has seemed. He then highlights the treatise's own act of unification: the eight kinds of conditioning are not a new, sixth topic alongside faith, aspiration, diligence, and means from fascicle 28's account of yogic practice — they are that same fourfold structure, simply expanded to its full eight-part detail, with aspiration and self-spurring keeping their own names, faith unchanged, and the remaining five (pliancy, mindfulness, clear comprehension, volition, equanimity) all falling under "means." With this equivalence established, the treatise gives its final, formal name to everything covered under the third factor of enlightenment: the four bases of power, each one naming a concentration paired with its conditioning, together aimed at concentration's full completion and affliction's genuine, permanent destruction — not merely its temporary suspension. This closes the four bases of power, the third of the thirty-seven factors, in full.

15. Milestone: Why "Bases of Power" — Two Explanations, Worldly and Supramundane

The treatise closes the third factor of enlightenment by explaining its name: like feet that carry a person out and back, the four concentrations and eight conditionings carry a practitioner first to ever-higher worldly absorptions, and ultimately to supramundane realization itself.

Chinese Source

何因緣故說名神足？答：如有足者，能往能還，騰躍勇健，能得能證世間所有殊勝之法。世殊勝法說名為神，彼能到此，故名神足。……如是若有如是諸法，有三摩地圓滿成辦，彼心如是清淨鮮白，無諸瑕穢，離隨煩惱，安住正直，有所堪能，獲得不動，能往能還，騰躍勇健，能得能證出世間法。由出世法最勝自在，是最勝神，彼能證此，故名神足。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the reason it is called the basis of power? Answer: just as one who has feet can go out and return, leaping vigorously, able to reach and realize all the excellent states the world has to offer — worldly excellence is here called "power," and that which arrives there is called its "basis." Likewise, when these very factors bring concentration to full completion, the mind becomes pure and bright, free of every blemish, free of the secondary afflictions, settled in uprightness, genuinely capable, having attained the unshakeable — able to go out and return, leaping vigorously, able to reach and realize supramundane states. Since the supramundane teaching is the most excellent and free of all, it is called the "greatest power," and that which realizes it is called its "basis."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the two explanations as tracking real stages of a single journey rather than two different journeys. In the worldly sense, "power" simply names each successively higher absorption — second surpassing first, third surpassing second — and the four concentrations with their conditionings are the "feet" that actually carry a practitioner there, rung by rung. In the supramundane sense, the same feet carry a practitioner further still: a mind grown genuinely pure of the subtler defilements that even accomplished meditators can fall into — delight in the absorption itself, subtle conceit about one's attainment, wrong view, doubt about the teaching, or a lingering ignorant belief in a permanent self — becomes settled, unshakeable, and finally capable of realizing the supramundane path itself, the "greatest power" of all. Cháng Bǎi adds a caution about the thirty-seven factors as a whole: though listed in a fixed sequence (foundations, efforts, bases of power, faculties, powers, enlightenment-factors, path), they need not be read as one rigid, linear staircase — the four foundations alone, or the four right efforts alone, or the eight kinds of conditioning alone, can each in principle carry a sincere practitioner the entire distance from ordinary being to the noble path; the sevenfold structure organizes the teaching for clarity, not a single mandatory sequence every practitioner must climb rung by literal rung.

16. The Five Faculties Open: Faith Generating Everything Beyond It

Chinese Source

彼由如是勝三摩地為所依持……能進修習增上心學、增上慧學所有瑜伽。由進修習此瑜伽故，於他大師、弟子所證，深生勝解、深生淨信。此清淨信增上義故，說名信根。……於能生起出世間法而為上首，及於能起精進、念、定、慧為其增上。餘精進等，於能生起出世間法，及於能起展轉乃至慧為其增上。乃至其慧，唯於能起出世間法為其增上。是故信等說名五根。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on such excellent concentration as its support, the practitioner is able to advance in cultivating all yogic practice belonging to higher mind-training and higher wisdom-training. Through advancing in this cultivation, one generates deep confidence and deep pure faith in what the Teacher and his disciples have realized. Because this pure faith has the meaning of increasing further excellence, it is called the faculty of faith. It is the head, generating the supramundane teaching, and also increases diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. The remaining faculties — diligence and the rest — likewise generate the supramundane teaching, and successively increase one another up through wisdom. Wisdom, finally, only generates the supramundane teaching itself. This is why faith and the rest are called the five faculties.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four bases of power's genuine completion as the actual precondition for the faculties to arise at all: only once concentration is stable enough to support it does deep, settled confidence in the Buddha's and the noble disciples' own realization become possible — not blind belief, but confidence earned through one's own practice actually beginning to work. He traces the cascading structure the treatise gives each faculty: faith increases diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom all at once, standing as the whole chain's head; diligence in turn increases mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom; mindfulness increases concentration and wisdom; concentration increases wisdom alone; and wisdom, having nothing left above it to increase, generates only the supramundane teaching directly. Cháng Bǎi's summary: each faculty in this sequence is both an increase over what preceded it and a foundation for what follows — a five-step ladder where every rung supports every rung above it, culminating in wisdom's own direct access to liberation.

17. The Five Powers: Faith Unshakeable by Any Force

Chinese Source

若復了知前後所證而有差別，隨此能於後後所證出世間法，深生勝解、深生淨信。此清淨信難伏義故，說名信力。……問：誰不能伏？答：此清淨信，若天、若魔、若諸沙門、若婆羅門、若餘世間，無有如法能引奪者，諸煩惱纏亦不能屈，故名難伏。……由此諸力具大威勢，摧伏一切魔軍勢力，能證一切諸漏永盡，是故名力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further knowing the difference between what has already been realized and what comes progressively after, and, in accordance with this, generating deep confidence and deep pure faith regarding the successively higher supramundane teachings to be realized — because this pure faith has the meaning of being difficult to subdue, it is called the power of faith. Question: subdued by whom? Answer: this pure faith cannot be legitimately taken away by any god, any demon, any ascetic of another path, any brahmin, or anyone else in the world whatsoever — nor can it be overcome even by one's own remaining afflictions. This is why it is called difficult to subdue. Because these powers possess such great and majestic force, able to overcome the entire host of Māra's armies and to realize the complete and permanent exhaustion of every defilement, they are therefore called powers.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the powers as the same five faculties matured past mere presence into genuine resilience — faith that has moved from simply being there to being unshakeable by anything: not a god's temptation, not Māra's interference, not a rival ascetic's rhetoric, not a learned brahmin's argument, and — the most demanding test of all — not even the practitioner's own residual afflictions, which might otherwise pull a still-imperfect mind back toward doubt. He notes the treatise's own list of Māra's ten armies elsewhere (desire, sorrow, hunger and thirst, craving, drowsiness, fear, doubt, malice, love of gain and false renown, and self-exaltation with contempt for others) as exactly what these five powers, matured, are capable of routing outright — the martial image of "power" is deliberate, naming genuine strength adequate to actual combat, not merely private conviction. With power sufficient to defeat every one of these and to bring about the complete exhaustion of affliction, the five powers close, having explained both faculties and powers individually; the treatise now turns to the relationship between them.

18. Faith's Faculty and Power as Cause of the Four Confirmed Purities

Chinese Source

當觀此中信根、信力，即四證淨中所有淨信。……以其證入正性離生所有證淨，皆由此因、此緣、此序，由彼即是此增上果。是故世尊就其因果相屬道理，說言當觀即彼證淨，非即彼體、非即彼相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should observe that the faculty and power of faith here are precisely the pure faith found within the four confirmed purities. This is because the confirmed purity attained upon entering the true nature that departs from arising all comes about through this very cause, this very condition, this very inception — that confirmed purity is simply this faith's own increased result. This is why the Bhagavān, in terms of how cause and result belong to one another, said one should regard the two as the same confirmed purity — though not identical in essence, nor identical in characteristic.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces the "four confirmed purities" (四證淨) as the undefiled, unshakeable confidence in the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and the noble precepts that a stream-enterer gains upon breakthrough — confidence no longer borrowed on trust but personally verified, and never again reversible. His key point: an ordinary practitioner's faith, once it has matured all the way to genuine faculty and then genuine power, stands to that future confirmed purity as cause to effect — the same seed, continuous, not yet the mature fruit but already growing toward it, exactly as a well-planted seed is not the tree yet inevitably becomes one given the right conditions. This is why the Buddha could say the two "are" the same confirmed purity in one sense (cause and effect belong to each other, continuous and inseparable) while being careful to add that they are not identical in essence or characteristic (an ordinary being's faith, however mature, is still a tainted, mundane state; the stream-enterer's confirmed purity is genuinely undefiled) — a precise middle position between conflating the two and treating them as wholly unrelated.

19. Milestone: The Remaining Four Faculties and Powers Close in Full

The same cause-effect relationship is traced through diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — each faculty and power shown to be the seed of a specific fruit realized at the seven factors of enlightenment, closing both the fourth and fifth factors of enlightenment entirely.

Chinese Source

當觀此中精進根、力，即四正斷中所有精進。……謂能永斷見道所斷一切煩惱方便正斷……當觀此中念根、念力，即四念住中所有正念。謂四念住能無餘斷一切顛倒。當觀此中定根、定力，即四靜慮中所有正定。謂諸靜慮能為方便，證不還果。當觀此中慧根、慧力，即四聖諦中所有正智。謂聖諦智於四聖諦能證現觀，得沙門果。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should observe that the faculty and power of diligence here are precisely the diligence found within the four right efforts — namely, the preparatory correct cutting capable of permanently ending every affliction abandoned at the path of seeing. One should observe that the faculty and power of mindfulness here are precisely the correct mindfulness found within the four foundations — the four foundations being capable of ending every distorted view without remainder. One should observe that the faculty and power of concentration here are precisely the correct concentration found within the four absorptions — the absorptions serving as the means to realize the fruit of non-returning. One should observe that the faculty and power of wisdom here are precisely the correct knowledge found within the four noble truths — the truth-knowledge capable of realizing direct insight into the four truths and attaining the fruit of a renunciant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces each remaining pair to its own specific fruit. Diligence, matured to faculty and power, is the seed of the vigor that — at the seven factors of enlightenment's own diligence-factor — permanently ends every one of the roughly hundred and twelve conceptually-constructed afflictions abandoned at the path of seeing, most simply summarized as self-view, attachment to rites and rituals, and doubt. Mindfulness, matured, is the seed of the mindfulness that finally and completely uproots the four distorted views — impermanence mistaken for permanence, suffering for pleasure, impurity for purity, non-self for self — the same four foundations first cultivated at the very start of this whole sequence, now bearing their full fruit. Concentration, matured, is the seed specifically enabling a "transcending" practitioner — one who has already attained the four form-realm absorptions before breakthrough — to leap directly to the third fruit, non-returning, rather than progressing fruit by fruit in the ordinary sequence. And wisdom, matured, is the seed of the direct, non-conceptual insight into the four noble truths that actually constitutes the fruit of a genuine renunciant — not inferred understanding, but truth personally witnessed.

Cháng Bǎi closes the fourth and fifth factors of enlightenment with a story about Chan master Dòngshān, illustrating what this whole cultivation is ultimately for: nearing death, Dòngshān told his gathered students he was departing for "the place beyond change" — and when pressed that a truly changeless place could have no "going" to it at all, he answered that even the going itself was unchanging, since one who dwells in nirvana carries it into every action, walking or sitting, coming or going. When his students wept at his passing, he opened his eyes, scolded them for clinging to his body rather than resting nowhere at all, and delayed his death seven days — ordering a farewell "feast for fools" prepared, precisely to give his grieving disciples time to compose themselves — before finally bathing and passing peacefully. Cháng Bǎi's point: a mind that has genuinely matured faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom into faculty and then power arrives, in the end, at exactly this freedom — able to come and go without disturbance, because it was never truly holding on to anything in the first place. This closes the fourth and fifth factors of enlightenment, the five faculties and five powers, in full.

20. Milestone: The Fire-Drilling Simile — Warmth, Summit, Forbearance, and the Supreme Worldly Dharma

Matured faculties and powers give rise to four roots of goodness leading directly to breakthrough — warmth, summit, forbearance in according with the truths, and the supreme worldly dharma — illustrated with the treatise's own extended simile of drilling wood to produce fire.

Chinese Source

即由如是諸根、諸力，漸修、漸習、漸多修習為因緣故，便能發起下中上品順決擇分四種善根。一、煖，二、頂，三、順諦忍，四、世第一法。譬如有人，欲以其火作火所作……下安乾木，上施鑽燧，精勤、策勵、勇猛鑽求……於下木上最初生煖；次煖增長，熱氣上衝；次倍增盛，其煙遂發；次無焰火欻然流出；火出無間發生猛焰；猛焰生已，便能造作火之所作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through gradual practice, gradual habituation, and gradual thorough cultivation of these very faculties and powers as cause and condition, one gives rise to the lower, middle, and upper grades of the four roots of goodness conducive to penetration: warmth, summit, forbearance in according with the truths, and the supreme worldly dharma. It is like a person wishing to produce fire and make use of it — laying dry wood below, applying the drill above, diligently, vigorously, courageously drilling in pursuit of fire: warmth first arises upon the wood below; next the warmth increases, and heat rises; next it redoubles, and smoke appears; next flameless fire suddenly issues forth; the fire, without interruption, gives rise to a fierce blaze; and once that fierce blaze has arisen, it becomes able to do fire's own work.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the full mapping: warmth is the very first sign of heat, before any fire exists — the practitioner's own eagerness for practice, needing no external prodding, the earliest hint that undefiled wisdom's fire is on its way. Summit is warmth intensified into rising heat — a more advanced but still reversible good root, traditionally said never to cut off one's roots of goodness entirely even if lost. Forbearance is the appearance of smoke — genuine, settled acceptance of the four truths, from which point a practitioner can no longer fall into the three lower realms. And the supreme worldly dharma is the flameless spark that appears just before ignition — the single highest moment available to an unenlightened being, immediately followed, without any gap at all, by the fierce blaze of breakthrough itself: direct entry into the true nature that departs from arising. Cháng Bǎi notes different classical sources map these four roots onto slightly different stages of the thirty-seven factors (some pairing warmth with the right efforts and summit with the bases of power, others distributing them differently across the faculties and powers), but this treatise treats faculties and powers together as underlying all four roots at their various depths — the exact correspondence matters less than the vivid image itself: patient, sustained effort, seemingly producing nothing for a long while, then warmth, then heat, then smoke, then a spark, and finally — without any further gap — genuine fire capable of doing fire's actual work. This closes the four roots of goodness and, with them, the section on conditions favoring the arising of effort.

21. Milestone: The Seven Factors of Enlightenment Open

The sixth factor of enlightenment opens: seven qualities that arise together at the moment of breakthrough itself, named "factors of enlightenment" because a practitioner who has genuinely entered the true nature that departs from arising uses them as the very supports of realization.

Chinese Source

此復云何？謂七覺支。諸已證入正性離生補特伽羅如實覺慧用此為支，故名覺支。

Ru-Yi Translation

What follows this? The seven factors of enlightenment. Those beings who have already entered the true nature that departs from arising make use of these as the very supports of their truly awakened wisdom — this is why they are called factors of enlightenment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Madhyānta-vibhāga's own condensation of the seven into five functional groups, useful for holding the whole structure in mind at once: the factor that serves as enlightenment's support is mindfulness — awakened wisdom needs something to stand on, and unbroken mindfulness is that ground. The factor that is enlightenment's own intrinsic nature is investigation of phenomena — the actual discerning wisdom that recognizes non-self in both persons and things, seeing through every apparently solid concept to the shifting causes and conditions underneath. The factor of enlightenment's outlet is diligence — insight alone doesn't liberate without the sustained energy to carry it through. The factor of enlightenment's benefit is joy — the genuine gladness that follows real attainment, comparable to the great bodhisattva path's own "ground of joy." And the factor of undefiled enlightenment is, properly speaking, three at once — pliancy, concentration, and equanimity — the settled ease, stability, and evenness that together characterize a mind now genuinely free of affliction's disturbance. Seven factors, five functions: a complete account, per Cháng Bǎi, of everything present at the moment breakthrough actually occurs, still to be unpacked one by one.

22. Milestone: The Seven Factors Sorted by Calm and Insight; the Eightfold Path Opens

The seven factors close with a threefold grouping into calm-abiding, insight, and what pervades both — and the treatise turns to the eighth and final factor, the noble eightfold path, opening with four distinct "paths of progress" describing how different practitioners actually travel it.

Chinese Source

即此七種如實覺支，三品所攝。謂三覺支奢摩他品攝，三覺支毗鉢舍那品攝，一覺支通二品攝，是故說名七種覺支。謂擇法覺支、精進覺支、喜覺支，此三觀品所攝；安覺支、定覺支、捨覺支，此三止品所攝；念覺支一種，俱品所攝，說名遍行。……彼於爾時，最初獲得七覺支故，名初有學見聖諦跡。已永斷滅見道所斷一切煩惱，唯餘修道所斷煩惱。為斷彼故，修習三蘊所攝八支聖道。

Ru-Yi Translation

These same seven factors of true realization are comprised within three categories: three factors belong to the calm-abiding category, three belong to the insight category, and one factor belongs to both categories — this is why they are called the seven factors of enlightenment. Investigation of phenomena, diligence, and joy belong to the insight category; pliancy, concentration, and equanimity belong to the calm-abiding category; mindfulness alone belongs to both, and is called pervasive. At that time, having just attained the seven factors of enlightenment, one is called a trainee who has newly seen the trace of the noble truths. Having permanently ended everything abandoned at the path of seeing, only what remains to be abandoned at the path of cultivation is left; in order to end this, one cultivates the eightfold noble path, comprised within the three aggregates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this threefold sorting simply restates the earlier five-function grouping in terms of the two core disciplines: investigation, diligence, and joy do insight's active, discerning work; pliancy, concentration, and equanimity do calm-abiding's settling, stabilizing work; and mindfulness alone serves both, since neither discipline can function without it. This closes the sixth factor of enlightenment entirely.

The eighth and final factor, the noble eightfold path, opens by naming four distinct "paths of progress" a practitioner might travel, sorted by two independent variables: whether one has attained the fundamental absorptions (bringing genuine bliss) or only access concentration (leaving residual bodily discomfort), and whether one's faculties are sharp or dull. Painful-and-slow progress belongs to a dull-facultied practitioner relying on access concentration alone; painful-and-swift progress belongs to a sharp-facultied practitioner working from that same modest foundation, who nonetheless progresses quickly; blissful-and-slow progress belongs to a dull-facultied practitioner who has attained the actual absorptions; and blissful-and-swift progress belongs to a sharp-facultied practitioner with the same attainment — the fastest and most comfortable of the four routes. Cháng Bǎi's point: the eightfold path itself is one path, but no two practitioners walk it under identical conditions, and the treatise is careful to name this variation explicitly rather than assume uniform difficulty for everyone.

23. Right View and Right Intention

Chinese Source

當知此中，若覺支時所得真覺；若得彼已，以慧安立如證而覺；總略此二，合名正見。由此正見增上力故，所起出離、無恚、無害分別思惟，名正思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that here, the true realization attained at the time of the seven factors, together with the wisdom that, once that realization is attained, establishes it in words matching what has been directly witnessed — these two together are called right view. Through the power of this right view, the resulting deliberation marked by renunciation, freedom from ill will, and freedom from harm is called right intention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies right view as two wisdoms working together: the fundamental undefiled wisdom that directly witnesses the four truths without any conceptual mediation at all, and the subsequently-attained wisdom that can then put that direct witnessing into words — teaching others what was seen. He draws out the four specific insights the subsequently-attained wisdom yields, useful for actually explaining the dharma to others: that only phenomena are at work, with no self or owner behind them; that this is not nihilism, since the causal chain of affliction, action, and suffering continues uninterrupted; that nothing is truly permanent, each moment freshly arising and perishing; and that all conditioned formations are illusion-like, real in function but without solid essence. Right intention follows directly from right view's own strength: deliberation naturally colored by renunciation (no further pull toward sense-pleasure), freedom from ill will (no reactive anger, whether praised or blamed), and freedom from harm (no impulse to injure another) — not effortful restraint, but the natural shape a mind already correctly seeing reality takes when it turns to plan and consider.

24. Why "Noble-Loved Precepts"; Right Effort, Mindfulness, and Concentration

Chinese Source

何故此名聖所愛戒？答：以諸聖者、賢善、正至，長時愛樂、欣慕、悅意……由彼長夜於此尸羅深心愛樂、欣慕、悅意，故獲得時，名聖所愛。獲得如是聖愛戒已，終不正知而說妄語，終不故思害眾生命，終不故思不與而取，終不故思行欲邪行，終不非法求衣服等。……依止正見及正思惟、正語、業、命勤修行者，所有一切欲、勤、精進、出離勇猛、勢力發起、策勵其心，相續無間，名正精進。成就如是正精進者，由四念住增上力故，得無顛倒九種行相所攝正念，能攝九種行相心住，是名正念及與正定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are these called the noble-loved precepts? Answer: because noble ones, the good and worthy, those who have rightly arrived, have long delighted in, longed for, and taken joy in this very question — "when shall I rightly attain this restraint from verbal misconduct, bodily misconduct, and wrongful livelihood?" Because of this long-held delight in this discipline, when it is finally attained, it is called noble-loved. Having attained this noble-loved discipline, one never again knowingly speaks falsehood, never deliberately takes a being's life, never deliberately takes what is not given, never deliberately engages in sexual misconduct, and never seeks clothing and the rest by improper means. … Relying on right view, right intention, right speech, right action, and right livelihood, the diligent practitioner's every aspiration, exertion, courageous striving toward release, and continuous, uninterrupted spurring of the mind is called right effort. One who has accomplished such right effort, through the power of the four foundations of mindfulness, attains undistorted mindfulness comprised of nine aspects, sustaining the mind's abiding through those same nine aspects — this is called right mindfulness together with right concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells the story behind this naming with real warmth: precepts kept purely out of obligation are one thing; precepts a noble one has spent lifetimes actually longing to attain are another — hence "loved," not merely "kept." He illustrates the depth of a noble one's honesty with the account of Mahāmaudgalyāyana, who once predicted rain in seven days during a drought; when the rain failed to come, townspeople accused him of lying, and the matter reached the Buddha himself. The Buddha's ruling: no falsehood had occurred, since Mahāmaudgalyāyana had genuinely seen rain coming in meditation — conditions simply shifted afterward. A noble one, Cháng Bǎi stresses, never knowingly speaks what they know to be false; being wrong through changed circumstance is not the same as lying, and the distinction matters enormously for understanding what "noble-loved precepts" actually guarantees.

Right effort, resting on right view through right livelihood already established, is simply sustained diligence with nothing left to second-guess — the same energy named throughout this project, now operating from a genuinely purified base. Right mindfulness and right concentration close the eightfold path by drawing directly on the nine stages of mental abiding first established in this project's account of calm-abiding: the same nine stages, reaching their final maturity here as the noble path's own mindfulness and concentration, inseparable from one another since concentration cannot arise without the mindfulness that sustains it.

25. Milestone: The Eightfold Path Closes — and with It, All Thirty-Seven Factors of Enlightenment

The treatise closes the eightfold path with a final twofold division — non-action and abiding action, mapped onto calm-abiding and insight — and clarifies that the eightfold path belongs specifically to the path of cultivation, not the path of seeing. With this, all thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, and cultivation through them, close entirely.

Chinese Source

如是一切八支聖道，總立二種。謂無所作及住所作。無所作者，謂正語、正業、正命。住所作者，復有二種。謂奢摩他、毗鉢舍那。正見、正思惟、正精進是毗鉢舍那，正念、正定是奢摩他。……率爾智生，名為見道，暫時智起，即能永斷諸煩惱故。由是因緣，正語、業、命，於修道中方始建立。……由如是等，漸次修習三十七種菩提分法加行方便，是名菩提分修。

Ru-Yi Translation

All of this eightfold noble path is, in total, established as two kinds: non-action, and abiding action. Non-action is right speech, right action, and right livelihood. Abiding action is, further, of two kinds: calm-abiding and insight. Right view, right intention, and right effort belong to insight; right mindfulness and right concentration belong to calm-abiding. … The sudden arising of wisdom is called the path of seeing — a momentary wisdom that immediately and permanently ends every such affliction. For this reason, right speech, right action, and right livelihood are established only within the path of cultivation. … Through gradually cultivating the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment and their preparatory means in this way — this is called cultivation through the factors of enlightenment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the final twofold division as elegantly simple: "non-action" names the noble-loved precepts' abstention from wrongdoing — nothing actively done, simply misconduct that never arises; "abiding action" names the genuine activity of seated practice, itself splitting cleanly into insight's three factors (view, intention, effort) and calm-abiding's two (mindfulness, concentration). He then flags a genuine point of disagreement among the classical treatises: some sources, including the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra and the Abhidharmakośa, place the eightfold path at the path of seeing and the seven factors of enlightenment at the path of cultivation; this treatise reverses that assignment, placing the seven factors at the momentary breakthrough of seeing (too swift, arising and completing in a single flash of wisdom, for anything as sustained as right speech or right livelihood to be meaningfully established there) and the eightfold path across the whole subsequent path of cultivation, where actual sustained conduct in the world becomes possible again once one has emerged from that momentary realization.

With this, the entire structure closes: the twelfth question — cultivation of yogic practice — has now been given both of its promised treatments in full, cultivation through perception and cultivation through all thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, from the four foundations of mindfulness through the noble eightfold path. This is, by a wide margin, the longest of the seventeen questions, spanning from late in fascicle 26 through most of fascicle 29.

26. Milestone: The Fruit of Cultivation Opens — Topic Thirteen of Seventeen

With cultivation itself fully explained, the thirteenth question opens: what fruit does all of this cultivation actually bear?

Chinese Source

云何修果？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the fruit of cultivation?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi marks this transition as a genuine turning point: having spent nearly four fascicles unpacking the machinery of cultivation itself — every object of meditation, every kind of instruction, every form of training, every stage of mental engagement, and now all thirty-seven factors of enlightenment in exhaustive individual detail — the treatise finally asks the plainest possible question: what does a practitioner actually get for all of this? The answer, still to come, will name the fruit directly.

27. Four Fruits Named; Why "Renunciant," Why "Fruit"

Chinese Source

謂四沙門果。一、預流果，二、一來果，三、不還果，四、最上阿羅漢果。此中云何名沙門？云何名果？謂聖道名沙門，煩惱斷名果。又後生道或中或上，是前生道所生之果。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fruit of cultivation is the four fruits of the renunciant: the fruit of stream-entry, the fruit of once-returning, the fruit of non-returning, and the supreme fruit of arhatship. What here is called "renunciant"? What is called "fruit"? The noble path itself is called the renunciant; the cutting off of affliction is called the fruit. Further, a later-arising path — whether middling or supreme — is itself the fruit produced by an earlier-arising path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi gives the four fruits their classical names and marks: stream-entry, permanently cutting off the conceptually-constructed afflictions of the three realms and entering the stream of the noble path; once-returning, having cut the first six of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction abandoned on the path of cultivation, requiring one further birth in the desire realm; non-returning, having cut all nine grades, never again to be born there; and arhatship, the complete and final cutting of every affliction, conceptually-constructed and innate alike, the highest attainment the vehicle of the hearer-disciples offers. He glosses the naming precisely: "renunciant" names the noble path itself — specifically the purified wisdom that does the work — while "fruit" names what that path actually accomplishes, the affliction it cuts away. And in a second, complementary sense, each later fruit is itself simply the further path generated by the fruit before it — the four fruits forming a single continuous chain rather than four unrelated attainments, ordinarily unfolding one after another, though Cháng Bǎi notes exceptional cases (such as the venerable Upagupta, who had already reached the third fruit before ordination) can pass through all four in rapid succession.

28. Milestone: Why Exactly Four Fruits — Three Obstructions in Three Kinds of Person

The treatise explains why the path divides into exactly four fruits: each addresses a specific affliction that blocks a specific kind of person from ever setting out on the noble path in the first place — self-view for householders, attachment to rites and rituals for those following a poorly-taught path, and doubt for sincere but insufficiently practiced followers of the true teaching.

Chinese Source

何故建立如是四果？答：對治四種諸煩惱故。……依在家品，有薩迦耶見。由此見故，先生怖畏，最初不欲發趣聖道。依惡說法毗奈耶品，有戒禁取。由此取故，雖已發趣，而行邪僻，由是不能生起聖道。依善說法毗奈耶品，有疑。由此疑故，雖已發趣，不行邪僻，而於正道未串習故，於如實見所知事中猶豫疑惑，障礙聖道不令生起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are exactly these four fruits established? Answer: because they counter four kinds of affliction. … Belonging to the category of the householder, there is the view of a substantial self; because of this view, fear arises beforehand, and one has no wish, from the very start, to set out toward the noble path. Belonging to the category of those following a poorly-taught teaching and discipline, there is attachment to rites and rituals; because of this attachment, though one has already set out, one's conduct is deviant and false, and so the noble path cannot arise. Belonging to the category of those following a well-taught teaching and discipline, there is doubt; because of this doubt, though one has set out and one's conduct is not deviant, one has not yet thoroughly practiced the true path, and so, wavering and doubting regarding what is directly seen to be known, the noble path is obstructed from arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a genuinely compassionate piece of diagnostic psychology: three different kinds of person, three different reasons the path never gets underway, or gets underway wrongly. The householder, comfortable with family, property, and possessions, is held back before ever starting by self-view itself — the felt reality of "my spouse, my child, my house" makes a teaching promising no-self and renunciation sound frightening rather than liberating, so such a person settles for generosity and good conduct aimed at a pleasant rebirth rather than genuine release. The follower of a poorly-taught path — the various non-Buddhist ascetic disciplines Cháng Bǎi lists (self-starvation, plunging into freezing water, five-fold fire-scorching, sitting exposed through every season, vowed silence in charnel grounds, imitating cattle or dogs in hope of heaven) — has genuinely set out toward liberation, but is derailed by attachment to rites and rituals: mistaking painful, unrelated austerity for the actual cause of liberation, so the effort, however sincere, cannot bear the fruit intended. And the sincere follower of the Buddha's own well-taught teaching — free of both self-view's fear and rites-and-rituals' misdirection — can still be blocked by simple doubt: not yet having practiced enough to see reality directly for themselves, still wavering over whether the four truths, the three jewels, or their own capacity to attain are genuinely real. Cutting exactly these three specific obstructions in exactly these three kinds of person is what stream-entry, the first fruit, actually accomplishes — and Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise will show each of the remaining three fruits addressing its own further, more subtle set of afflictions in turn.

29. Milestone: The Remaining Three Fruits Close; the Fruit of Cultivation Named in Full

Once-returning, non-returning, and arhatship each cut a progressively deeper layer of affliction — matched to the Buddha's own scriptural formula of the three knots thinned, the five lower fetters ended, and every affliction exhausted — closing the fruit of cultivation entirely.

Chinese Source

若斷再生相續煩惱，生無重續，立一來果。……若已永斷能感還來生此煩惱，唯於天有當可受生，即於爾時立不還果。若已永斷一切能感生有煩惱，建立最上阿羅漢果。而薄伽梵說：永斷三結，薄貪瞋癡，立一來果。永斷能順五下分結，立不還果。永斷一切煩惱究竟，建立最上阿羅漢果。是名修果。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one has cut off the affliction that leads to repeated rebirth here, such that birth no longer continues without end, the fruit of once-returning is established. … If one has already permanently cut off the affliction that would cause a return to birth here, with only heavenly rebirth still to come, at that time the fruit of non-returning is established. If one has already permanently cut off every affliction capable of producing further existence, the supreme fruit of arhatship is established. And the Bhagavān said: with the three knots permanently cut and craving, aversion, and delusion thinned, the fruit of once-returning is established; with what accords with the five lower fetters permanently cut, the fruit of non-returning is established; with every affliction permanently and completely exhausted, the supreme fruit of arhatship is established. This is called the fruit of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lines up the two ways the treatise states the same progression. Once-returning cuts the first six of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction, leaving at most one further heavenly and one further human rebirth — the scripture's "three knots cut, three poisons thinned" describing exactly this state, where self-view, rites-and-rituals attachment, and doubt are gone entirely and craving, aversion, and delusion are merely faint rather than absent. Non-returning cuts all nine grades of desire-realm affliction completely, corresponding to the five lower fetters (self-view, rites-and-rituals, doubt, craving, and aversion) all being severed — such a person, if not immediately liberated, is reborn only in the form or formless realms, never again in the sense-desire realm. And arhatship adds the five higher fetters — attachment to form-realm and formless-realm states, restlessness, conceit, and subtle ignorance — bringing every affliction, coarse and subtle, conceptually-constructed and innate, to complete and final exhaustion: "my births are ended, the holy life fulfilled, what had to be done is done, no further existence awaits." This closes the thirteenth question, the fruit of cultivation, in full.

30. Seven Kinds of Practitioner and the Objects Suited to Each

Chinese Source

又於此中，貪、瞋、癡、慢、尋思行者，彼先應於淨行所緣淨修其行，然後方證心正安住。……若等分行補特伽羅，隨所愛樂，攀緣彼境勤修加行。如是勤修，唯令心住，非淨其行。如等分行補特伽羅，薄塵行者，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, in this matter, practitioners dominated by desire, aversion, delusion, pride, or discursive thought should first purify their own conduct using the purifying objects, and only then attain the mind's correct settling. … A practitioner of balanced temperament, following whatever they find agreeable, engages that object and diligently applies effort; such diligent practice serves only to settle the mind, not to purify conduct. As with the practitioner of balanced temperament, so too should one understand the practitioner of naturally light affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this back to the twenty-eight kinds of practitioner named at the very opening of the second yogic process: the five dominated by a single strong affliction must first apply the matching purifying object — impurity for desire, loving-kindness for aversion, dependent origination for delusion, the six elements for pride, breath-mindfulness for discursive thought — treating that object as medicine for a specific ailment before concentration can even properly begin. The practitioner of balanced temperament, by contrast, carries no single dominant affliction severe enough to demand a specific antidote; such a person may simply choose whichever object appeals to them and proceed straight to building concentration, since the object isn't doing corrective work, only settling work. And the practitioner of naturally light affliction is even freer still — any of the objects will serve equally well to produce absorption, precisely because there is little affliction left needing to be specifically countered. Cháng Bǎi's practical takeaway: before choosing a meditation object, a practitioner should honestly diagnose which of these categories they actually belong to — treating a genuine, serious affliction as though it were merely average temperament wastes years on an object that was never going to touch the real problem.

31. Balanced and Light-Affliction Practitioners; the Three Obstructions

Chinese Source

等分行者勤修行時，不甚久遠，能證心住。薄塵行者勤修行時，最極速疾，能證心住。……其薄塵行補特伽羅行相差別者，謂無重障，最初清淨，資糧已具，多清淨信，成就聰慧，具諸福德，具諸功德。無重障者，謂無三障。何等為三？一者、業障，二、煩惱障，三、異熟障。言業障者，謂五無間業，及餘所有故思造業諸尤重業。彼異熟果若成熟時，能障正道，令不生起，是名業障。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner of balanced temperament, diligently practicing, attains the mind's settling in not too long a time. A practitioner of naturally light affliction, diligently practicing, attains it with the greatest speed. … The distinguishing marks of the practitioner of naturally light affliction are these: freedom from serious obstruction; purity from the very start; the provisions already complete; abundant pure faith; accomplished intelligence; possession of merit; and possession of virtue. Freedom from serious obstruction means freedom from three obstructions. What are the three? Karmic obstruction, afflictive obstruction, and retributive obstruction. Karmic obstruction is the five acts of immediate retribution, together with any other especially grave deliberately-committed karma. When their ripened result matures, it is able to obstruct the correct path, preventing it from arising — this is called karmic obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts the two easier temperaments with the effort-laden path of the five dominant-affliction types: the balanced practitioner carries every affliction in mild form and settles into concentration in ordinary time, while the naturally light-affliction practitioner — the fastest of the seven — carries seven distinguishing marks, foremost among them freedom from three deep obstructions rather than merely mild affliction. Karmic obstruction names specifically the five acts of immediate retribution (patricide, matricide, killing an arhat, wounding a Buddha, and causing schism in the monastic community) together with any comparably grave deliberate act — karma so severe, per Cháng Bǎi, that it cannot be lightened by later practice the way ordinary unwholesome karma can, and once its result ripens it makes genuine spiritual attainment in that lifetime impossible, however sincerely one later tries to sit and practice. He is careful to distinguish this from ordinary wrongdoing: it is not that lesser faults don't matter, but that these five specifically foreclose the path itself for the remainder of that life, which is why freedom from them is named first among the marks of a practitioner naturally suited to swift progress.

32. Milestone: Affliction and Ripened-Result Close the Three Obstructions

The second and third obstructions close the account of "freedom from serious obstruction": affliction so fierce or so persistent that no purifying object can settle it, and being born into circumstances where the path itself has no room to take root.

Chinese Source

煩惱障者，謂猛利煩惱、長時煩惱。由此煩惱，於現法中，以其種種淨行所緣不能令淨，是名煩惱障。異熟障者，謂若生處，聖道依彼不生不長，於是生處異熟果生。或有生處，聖道依彼雖得生長，而於其中異熟果生，聾騃愚鈍，盲瞽瘖瘂，以手代言，無有力能解了善說、惡說法義。是名異熟障。

Ru-Yi Translation

Afflictive obstruction is affliction that is either fierce or long-lasting. Because of such affliction, in this present life, none of the various purifying objects of meditation are able to purify the mind — this is called afflictive obstruction. Retributive obstruction is: either being born into circumstances where the noble path cannot arise or grow at all, the ripened result of that birth itself being the obstruction; or being born where the path could in principle arise and grow, yet the ripened result manifests as deafness, dullness, blindness, or muteness — reduced to gesture in place of speech, without the capacity to understand either well-taught or poorly-taught teachings. This is called retributive obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes afflictive obstruction from ordinary affliction by two independent measures: intensity and duration. Fierce-but-brief affliction can still be worn down and eventually eliminated at breakthrough; mild-but-constant affliction, left unchecked, compounds over time into something far worse; the genuinely obstructive case is affliction both fierce and constant at once, so severe and so relentless that none of the purifying objects — impurity, loving-kindness, dependent origination, the elements, breath-mindfulness — actually take hold, however earnestly applied. He notes the practical corollary: a practitioner shouldn't mechanically apply one fixed antidote regardless of results: if a given object genuinely isn't working after real effort, the wiser response is switching approaches rather than continuing to force a method that keeps failing.

Retributive obstruction names being born somewhere the path structurally cannot take root at all — traditionally the eight "inopportune" states: the three lower realms, the northern continent of effortless plenty, the long-lived heavens (where refined but view-less absorption or the cessation of perception itself leaves no room for actual insight), the physically or sensorily incapacitated, those trapped in clever-but-wrong worldly views, and those born in the gap between one Buddha's teaching and the next — or, more subtly, being born where the teaching is present but one's own ripened body lacks the faculties to receive it. With this, all three obstructions close, completing the first of the seven marks of a practitioner naturally suited to swift progress; freedom from all three is what actually makes rapid attainment possible.

33. Purity from the Start: Well-Purified Discipline and an Upright View

Chinese Source

最初清淨者，謂善淨戒及正直見。……正直見者，謂若有見，淨信相應故、勝解相應故、遠離誑諂故、善思法義無惑無疑加行出離故，名為正直。如是正直見，淨信相應故，於佛正法及毗奈耶不可引奪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Purity from the very start means well-purified discipline together with an upright view. An upright view is one accompanied by pure faith, accompanied by resolute understanding, free of deceit and flattery, and skillfully considering the meaning of the teaching without confusion or doubt, applying effort toward genuine release — this is called upright. Such an upright view, being accompanied by pure faith, cannot be drawn away from the Buddha's true teaching and discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties well-purified discipline directly back to the ten conditions favoring pure discipline already established in this project — past causes, conduct according with instruction, correct application, earnest uninterrupted practice, intense aspiration, sustaining yogic power, calming bodily and mental grossness, repeated self-examination, freedom from timidity, and freedom from overestimation — the naturally light-affliction practitioner simply already has these ten in place from the very start, rather than needing to build them from scratch. The upright view that accompanies this discipline is marked by pure faith unshakeable by any outside teaching, genuine resolute understanding rather than superficial agreement, complete freedom from deceit or flattery toward oneself or others, and clear thinking about the dharma's meaning free of both outright confusion and half-understood doubt. Cháng Bǎi's summary: purity from the start is simply having, already in place before serious practice even begins, the same discipline and view that a less fortunate practitioner must spend years slowly constructing.

34. Milestone: Resolute Understanding of the Inconceivable Closes Purity from the Start

An upright view is further marked by confidence facing what cannot be settled by ordinary reasoning — the Buddha's inconceivable power, the fourteen questions the Buddha declined to answer — together with freedom from deceit and skillful, doubt-free reflection, closing both the second mark of a naturally light-affliction practitioner and the account of the upright view itself.

Chinese Source

勝解相應故，於諸如來及聖弟子不可思議威德神力、不可思議生處差別、甚深法教、不可記事，深生勝解，無驚、無恐、無有怖畏。遠離誑諂故，其見正直，是正直類，如其聖教而正修行，如其真實而自顯發。善思法義無惑無疑加行出離故，於一切法無常、苦、空、無我等義，善正思惟、善正籌量、善正觀察。由是為因，無惑無疑，遠離二路，逮得昇進。由此四相，先所說見，名正直見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being accompanied by resolute understanding, one generates deep resolute understanding — free of alarm, free of fear, free of dread — regarding the inconceivable majestic power of the Tathāgatas and noble disciples, the inconceivable variety of realms of rebirth, the profound teaching, and the matters the Buddha left unanswered. Being free of deceit and flattery, one's view is upright, of an upright kind, practicing correctly in accordance with the noble teaching and manifesting genuinely just as one has actually realized. Skillfully considering the meaning of the teaching without confusion or doubt and applying effort toward release, one skillfully and correctly reflects, deliberates, and observes the meaning of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self in all phenomena; by this cause, free of confusion and doubt, one departs from the two divergent paths of wavering and attains genuine advancement. Through these four marks, the view spoken of earlier is called an upright view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats resolute understanding of the inconceivable as confidence calibrated correctly to its object: certain matters — a Buddha's actual power, the full particulars of karmic rebirth across countless realms, emptiness and dependent origination in their deepest sense, and the classical fourteen unanswerable questions (whether the world is eternal, finite, what happens to a Tathāgata after death, whether self and body are one) — are simply beyond what ordinary reasoning can settle, not because they are false but because they exceed a still-unenlightened mind's present capacity. A practitioner with genuinely upright view neither panics at this limit nor rejects the teaching because of it, but trusts that these are the Buddha's own domain, to be personally verified through practice rather than argued out in advance; the same fourteen questions, Cháng Bǎi notes, are unanswerable specifically because they were raised by non-Buddhist interlocutors as speculative traps, built on assumptions (a fixed, findable self; a substantial world with real edges) the teaching itself rejects — answering directly would concede the false premise.

Freedom from deceit and flattery is straightforward integrity: practicing for its own sake rather than for appearances, and confessing faults honestly and promptly rather than concealing them. And skillful, doubt-free reflection on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self — corresponding, Cháng Bǎi notes, to the three wisdoms of hearing, reflection, and cultivation in turn — is what actually dissolves the wavering, two-directions-at-once uncertainty that doubt produces, opening the way to genuine advancement. Together, these four marks — pure faith, resolute understanding, freedom from deceit, and skillful reflection — complete the "upright view" half of purity from the start, itself the second of the seven marks of a practitioner naturally suited to swift progress.

35. The Provisions Already Complete: Merit and Wisdom

Chinese Source

資糧已具者，廣說資糧，如前應知。略有四種。一、福德資糧，二、智慧資糧，三、先世資糧，四、現法資糧。福德資糧者，謂由此故，於今獲得隨順資具、豐饒財寶，遇真福田為善知識，離諸障礙，能勤修行。智慧資糧者，謂由此故，成就聰慧，有力有能解了善說、惡說法義，獲得隨順法教、義教、教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

The provisions already complete are, in detail, the provisions explained earlier — briefly, four kinds: the provision of merit, the provision of wisdom, the provision from past lives, and the provision within this present life. The provision of merit is that through which one now obtains fitting requisites and abundant wealth, encounters a genuine field of merit as a spiritual friend, and is free of obstruction, able to diligently practice. The provision of wisdom is that through which one becomes accomplished in intelligence, having the capacity to understand both well-taught and poorly-taught teachings, and obtains what accords with the teaching itself, its meaning, and instruction and admonition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties "provisions already complete" — the third of the seven marks — directly back to the fourteen path-provisions unpacked at length in fascicles 22 through 25 of this project, now reorganized into four simpler categories. The provision of merit is what actually secures the outer conditions practice depends on: sufficient material support, freedom from want, and — most valuably — the good fortune of actually encountering a qualified teacher, someone whose own attainment makes them a genuine "field of merit" worth relying on, rather than settling for whoever happens to be available. The provision of wisdom is the inner counterpart: an already-sharp intelligence capable of correctly distinguishing genuine teaching from counterfeit, and of actually absorbing instruction once received — the same capacities, Cháng Bǎi notes, that make the difference between a student who merely hears the dharma and one who is transformed by it. Both provisions, he stresses, are less a matter of luck than of cumulative cause: a practitioner who arrives already equipped this way didn't get there by accident, which the next two categories make explicit.

36. Milestone: The Seven Marks of the Light-Affliction Practitioner Close

The remaining marks — present-life provision, abundant pure faith, accomplished intelligence, possession of merit, and possession of virtue — close the full sevenfold portrait of the practitioner naturally suited to swift progress.

Chinese Source

現法資糧者，謂於今世有善法欲、諸根成熟、具戒律儀及根律儀，如前廣說。多清淨信者，謂於大師所無惑無疑，深生淨信及以勝解。……成就聰慧者，謂由此故，於法於義速能領受；經久遠時，於法於義能無忘失，於法於義速能通達。具諸福德者，謂由此故，形色端嚴，眾所樂見，發清淨信，無病長壽……具諸功德者，謂本性成就極少欲等種種功德。如是等類，應知是名諸薄塵行補特伽羅行相差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The provision within this present life is having, in this very life, wholesome aspiration, mature faculties, and both precept-discipline and sense-restraint-discipline fully in place, as explained at length before. Abundant pure faith is generating, regarding the Teacher, deep pure faith and resolute understanding free of confusion and doubt — and likewise regarding the teaching and the training. Accomplished intelligence is swiftly grasping the teaching and its meaning, retaining them without forgetting across a long span of time, and swiftly penetrating them. Possession of merit is having a dignified and pleasing form that others delight to see, inspiring pure faith in them, together with good health, longevity, and dignified, courteous speech. Possession of virtue is having, by one's very nature, wanting little and every other such virtue as already explained among the ornaments of the renunciant. All of these together should be known as the distinguishing marks of the practitioner of naturally light affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the sevenfold portrait by tying each remaining mark to its own specific karmic cause: a dignified, well-formed body traces to past generosity in offering light and clean clothing; good health and long life trace to past generosity in food and to genuinely never harming other beings; courteous, trusted speech traces to having abandoned the four verbal misdeeds; being born into an accomplished family traces to past freedom from arrogance; and being widely known and respected traces to past reverence shown toward the three jewels and one's elders. Taken together, all seven marks — freedom from the three obstructions, purity of discipline and view from the very start, the four kinds of provision already gathered, deep unwavering faith, sharp and retentive intelligence, a body and circumstance that naturally invite trust, and an innate disposition toward contentment — complete the portrait of the practitioner for whom concentration, and the whole path built on it, comes with the greatest possible speed. This closes the extended discussion of practitioner types that has run throughout the thirteenth question.

37. Milestone: Synonyms for the Practitioner Open — Topic Fourteen

The fourteenth question opens: what are the different terms used for a practitioner, and what does each specifically name? The treatise lists six broad terms, the first of which — renunciant — itself divides into four very different kinds.

Chinese Source

云何補特伽羅異門？謂有六種。何等為六？一、沙門，二、婆羅門，三、梵行，四、苾芻，五、精勤，六、出家。第一沙門復有四種。何等為四？一、勝道沙門，二、說道沙門，三、活道沙門，四、壞道沙門。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the synonyms for a practitioner? There are six: renunciant, brāhmaṇa, one who practices the holy life, monk, the diligent one, and one gone forth from home. The first, renunciant, is itself of four kinds. What are the four? The renunciant of superior attainment, the renunciant who merely teaches the path, the renunciant who merely lives by the path, and the renunciant who corrupts the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the fourteenth question as a shift from describing what a practitioner does to naming what a practitioner actually is, in the many words scripture uses for the role — renunciant (沙門), brāhmaṇa, holy-life practitioner, monk, the diligent one, and one gone forth — each term carrying its own particular emphasis even though all six can point to the same person. He flags that the very first term, "renunciant," is far from a single uniform category: the treatise's own fourfold division — the renunciant of genuine superior attainment, one who merely teaches the path without having walked it, one who simply lives off the outward form of renunciant life without either teaching or attaining, and one who actively corrupts the path through wrong conduct — makes clear that wearing the robes and using the title guarantees nothing about what is actually inside. Each of these four kinds is unpacked individually next.

38. The Four Renunciants, Strictly and Broadly Defined

Chinese Source

當知諸善逝，名勝道沙門；諸說正法者，名說道沙門；諸修善行者，名活道沙門；諸行邪行者，名壞道沙門。……又學無學名勝道沙門，以無漏道摧滅一切見、修所斷諸煩惱故。若無如來及諸菩薩，為菩提故勤修正行，諸聲聞眾持三藏者，名說道沙門……若諸異生補特伽羅，其性調善……為得未得、為觸未觸、為證未證勤修加行……名活道沙門。若諸犯戒補特伽羅，多行惡法，廣說乃至實非梵行自稱梵行，名壞道沙門。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that the well-gone ones are called renunciants of superior attainment; those who teach the true dharma are called renunciants who teach the path; those who cultivate wholesome conduct are called renunciants who live by the path; and those of deviant conduct are called renunciants who corrupt the path. Further, both trainees and those beyond training are called renunciants of superior attainment, since both destroy, through the undefiled path, every affliction abandoned at the paths of seeing and cultivation. Where there is no Tathāgata or bodhisattva present, those disciples who, for the sake of awakening, diligently cultivate correct conduct and uphold the three baskets are called renunciants who teach the path. Any ordinary being of gentle, well-disciplined nature, diligently applying effort for the sake of attaining what is not yet attained, touching what is not yet touched, and realizing what is not yet realized, is called a renunciant who lives by the path. Any who violate discipline, engaging in much unwholesome conduct — up to falsely claiming to practice the holy life while not truly doing so — is called a renunciant who corrupts the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through both a strict and a broad reading of each term. Strictly, "renunciant of superior attainment" names only a fully liberated arhat, having permanently exhausted every trace of desire, aversion, and delusion; broadly, it extends to any noble one from stream-entry onward, since each of the four fruits genuinely destroys its own share of affliction through the undefiled path. "Teaches the path" strictly includes the Buddha and bodhisattvas alongside disciples who expound the teaching; more narrowly, it names specifically the community of learned disciples who carry the teaching forward when no Buddha or bodhisattva is present to teach it directly, keeping the "eye of the true dharma" from going dark. "Lives by the path" strictly spans every stage from ordinary being through full realization; more narrowly, it names specifically a sincere ordinary practitioner already at the preparatory stage of applied effort — genuinely warm with the fire of practice, as Cháng Bǎi puts it, even though still short of breakthrough. And "corrupts the path" names someone who has broken the very foundation of discipline itself — outwardly resembling a genuine renunciant the way a water snail's shell resembles something clean while concealing what's foul within, or a conch shell's impressive sound conceals nothing behind it — genuinely without the capacity to either teach or attain, however convincingly the outward form is maintained.

39. Milestone: Only the Buddha's Teaching Contains These Four

The Buddha's own verdict closes the fourfold renunciant: wherever the eightfold noble path can genuinely be established, all four kinds of renunciant — including, notably, the corrupting kind — can be found; outside that teaching, none of the four exist at all.

Chinese Source

世尊依彼作如是說：此初沙門，廣說乃至第四沙門，於外沙門、婆羅門教空無所有。若於是處，八支聖道安立可得；即於是處有初沙門，廣說乃至第四沙門。

Ru-Yi Translation

Relying on this, the Bhagavān spoke thus: this first renunciant, and so on through the fourth renunciant, are utterly absent from the teachings of outside renunciants and brāhmaṇas. Wherever the eightfold noble path can genuinely be established, there too the first renunciant, and so on through the fourth renunciant, are found.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the classical verse behind this teaching: "empty space leaves no trace of a bird's flight; no renunciant exists among the outside teachers; the foolish delight in idle speculation; the Tathāgata has none of this." His reading of the logic: all four kinds of renunciant — even the corrupting one — presuppose the actual existence of the eightfold noble path as their reference point; a tradition that never taught the genuine path in the first place has no path to corrupt, no path to live by, no path to teach, and certainly no superior attainment to reach. This is why even the "corrupting" renunciant, paradoxically, could only exist within the Buddha's own teaching — the very possibility of counterfeit presupposes a genuine original it fails to live up to. This closes the account of the first of the six synonyms, renunciant, in full.

40. Brāhmaṇa and Holy-Life Practitioner, Each in Three Kinds

Chinese Source

第二婆羅門復有三種。一、種性婆羅門，二、名想婆羅門，三、正行婆羅門。……當知婆羅門，更無有所作；所作事已辦，是謂婆羅門。第三梵行復有三種。一、受遠離梵行，二、暫時斷梵行，三、畢竟斷梵行。……畢竟斷梵行者，謂諸聖者得不還果，復得最上阿羅漢果。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second, brāhmaṇa, is of three kinds: brāhmaṇa by caste, brāhmaṇa in name only, and brāhmaṇa of true conduct. As it is said: know that a true brāhmaṇa has nothing further to be done; that what was to be done is already accomplished — this is what is meant by brāhmaṇa. The third, holy-life practitioner, is of three kinds: undertaking abstention, temporary cessation, and final cessation. Final cessation is: noble ones who have attained the fruit of non-returning, and further attained the supreme fruit of arhatship.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats brāhmaṇa's three kinds as a deliberate reclaiming of a term ancient India already prized: caste-brāhmaṇa names mere birth into the priestly class, an accident of parentage with no bearing on actual attainment; name-only brāhmaṇa names anyone simply called this by common usage, likewise empty of real content; and the true-conduct brāhmaṇa — the sense the Buddha's own teaching actually endorses — names one who has genuinely finished the work of ending affliction, from stream-entry at the loosest reading up to arhatship at the strictest, the true nobility scripture actually cares about.

The holy-life practitioner's three kinds trace increasing depth: undertaking abstention is simply keeping the precepts against sexual misconduct, the first and most basic layer; temporary cessation is an ordinary being's mundane-path suppression of desire-realm craving through the four absorptions — genuine but reversible, the seed untouched; and final cessation belongs only to the non-returner and arhat, who have permanently uprooted desire-realm craving itself. Cháng Bǎi illustrates the difference with real weight, recounting the account of the laywoman Viśākhā, whose thirty-two sons were executed and sent to her in a box disguised as a royal gift; the Buddha, aware of what the box contained, delayed her from opening it, first teaching her the non-self nature of the five aggregates until her insight matured from the first fruit to the third — only then permitting her to open it and view her sons' remains without being overwhelmed by grief, since a mind that has genuinely and finally ceased desire-realm attachment meets even devastating loss without collapsing into anguish, seeing it instead as karma simply working itself out.

41. Five Kinds of Monk

Chinese Source

第四苾芻復有五種。一、乞丐苾芻，二、自稱苾芻，三、名想苾芻，四、破壞煩惱苾芻，五、白四羯磨受具足戒苾芻。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth, monk, is of five kinds: the mendicant monk, the self-proclaimed monk, the monk in name only, the monk who has destroyed affliction, and the monk who has received full ordination through the formal fourfold motion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi sorts these five from loosest to most substantive. The mendicant monk names simply anyone who lives by begging for food, whether or not genuinely ordained — even a layperson begging for a living could technically fit this bare description. The self-proclaimed monk names someone who has broken a major precept yet still calls themselves a monk, a claim with no real basis behind it. The monk in name only names anyone merely called "monk" as a personal name or label, with no ordination or practice implied at all. The monk who has destroyed affliction names any noble one, from stream-enterer through arhat, who has genuinely broken affliction's fetters — the substantive sense scripture actually cares about. And the monk who has received full ordination through the formal fourfold motion names one properly received into the monastic community through the traditional ordination procedure before the assembly. Cháng Bǎi's point in laying out all five side by side: the same word covers everything from an empty label to genuine sainthood, and only careful attention to which sense is meant in a given passage keeps the term from being misunderstood.

42. Milestone: Diligence Closes with Guarding the Senses and Eliminating Affliction Itself

The remaining two kinds of diligence — guarding against affliction's triggers and eliminating affliction at its root — close the fifth synonym, distinguishing throughout between merely suppressing affliction's active manifestation and genuinely destroying its underlying seed.

Chinese Source

二、止息境界精勤，謂密護根門，修防守念及常委念，如前廣說。三、止息煩惱精勤，謂能永斷見、修所斷一切煩惱……隨所生起能不忍受，尋即斷滅、除遣、變吐。當知此中，略有二種止息煩惱。一、止息隨眠，二、止息諸纏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, diligence in stilling the objects of affliction: closely guarding the sense-gates, cultivating protective mindfulness and constant, deliberate mindfulness, as explained at length before. Third, diligence in stilling affliction itself: being able to permanently cut off every affliction abandoned at the paths of seeing and cultivation, and, whatever arises — whether it is desire, aversion, or harm, or craving, hatred, wrong view, or resentment, malice, deceit, or flattery — refusing to tolerate it, immediately cutting it off, expelling it, and casting it out. Know that here there are, briefly, two kinds of stilling affliction: stilling the latent seed, and stilling its active manifestation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties the second kind of diligence directly back to the earlier account of guarding the sense-gates: keeping the six senses from grasping at their objects in ways that invite affliction to arise in the first place, prevention rather than cure. The third and deepest kind closes with the crucial distinction the treatise draws explicitly: stilling the latent seed means genuine, permanent elimination through the wisdom that cuts affliction abandoned at both the path of seeing and the path of cultivation — the seed itself destroyed, never to sprout again; stilling active manifestation means simply refusing to indulge affliction once it has already arisen, expelling it in the moment even though its underlying seed remains untouched, ready to arise again given the right conditions. Together, this closes the fifth of the six synonyms, diligence.

43. Milestone: One Gone Forth Closes the Six Synonyms — Topic Fourteen Complete

The sixth and final synonym, one gone forth from home, distinguishes ordination within the Buddha's own well-taught teaching from ordination among outside ascetics — and, within the former, nominal ordination from the genuine article. With this, all six synonyms for a practitioner close together.

Chinese Source

第六出家復有二種。一、於善說法毗奈耶中而出家者，二、於惡說法毗奈耶中而出家者。……又若自能出離身中所有一切惡不善法，當知是名真實出家。……是故說言：若諸沙門、若婆羅門、若修梵行、若諸苾芻、若精勤者、若出家者，如是一切，是數取趣所有異門。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth, one gone forth, is of two kinds: one gone forth within a well-taught teaching and discipline, and one gone forth within a poorly-taught teaching and discipline. Further, one who is able to personally free the body of every evil, unwholesome state within it should be known as one who has genuinely gone forth. This is why it is said: renunciant, brāhmaṇa, holy-life practitioner, monk, the diligent one, and one gone forth — all of these are simply the various synonyms belonging to a being who takes up rebirth again and again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi runs through the five formally-ordained ranks within the Buddha's teaching — monk, nun, the probationary nun undertaking a preparatory stage between novice and full ordination (established, per the traditional account, after a pregnant laywoman ordained without anyone realizing, so a period of observation became necessary to distinguish genuine vocation from external circumstance), male novice, and female novice — but immediately relativizes mere formal ordination against something deeper: genuine going-forth is actually freeing the body and mind of every unwholesome state, not merely receiving the outward ceremony. A shaved head and robes without inner transformation, Cháng Bǎi notes bluntly, is sometimes called "a bald layman" — nominally ordained but still thoroughly worldly within. Outside the Buddha's teaching, ordination exists too, among India's many ascetic traditions — the unclothed ascetics, those wearing coarse dull-colored robes, those smeared in ash, and the many other schools pursuing self-inflicted suffering under the mistaken belief that suffering itself, rather than the affliction that produces it, is what needs to be exhausted.

With this, the treatise closes topic fourteen with its own summary statement: renunciant, brāhmaṇa, holy-life practitioner, monk, the diligent one, and one gone forth are simply six different lenses on the same being journeying through birth after birth — each term emphasizing a different facet (attainment, nobility of conduct, sexual restraint, communal ordination, effort, or formal renunciation) of what is, underneath all six labels, a single path toward the same liberation. This closes the fourteenth question, synonyms for the practitioner, in full.

44. Milestone: How Many Kinds of Practitioner — Eight, by Four Causes

The fifteenth question opens — one of the final three questions remaining in the entire seventeen-question structure. The treatise names eight kinds of practitioner, established according to four underlying causes.

Chinese Source

補特伽羅略有八種。建立因緣略有四種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Practitioners, briefly, are of eight kinds. The causes establishing them are, briefly, of four kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi marks the fifteenth question as returning to a classification theme this fascicle has now approached from several angles — first the twenty-eight practitioner types back in fascicle 26, then the seven marks of the light-affliction practitioner just closed, and now an eightfold scheme organized around four distinct causes rather than temperament or attainment level. What these eight kinds and four causes specifically are is named next.

45. Milestone: Eight Kinds of Practitioner Fully Explained — All Four Causes Must Converge

The treatise matches each of the eight practitioner-kinds to its cause, then delivers a decisive verdict: genuine attainment requires all four conditions together — mature faculties, skillful method, uninterrupted practice, and long habituation — with any one missing enough to block success entirely.

Chinese Source

云何八種補特伽羅？一、有堪能者，二、無堪能者，三、善知方便者，四、不善知方便者，五、有無間修者，六、無無間修者，七、已串修習者，八、未串修習者。……若諸所有補特伽羅根未成熟……皆不能辦。若諸所有補特伽羅根雖成熟，而未善知善巧方便，於諸所有亦不能辦。若諸所有補特伽羅根已成熟，善知方便，無無間修，即不能得速疾通慧。若諸所有補特伽羅根已成熟，善知方便，有無間修，未串修習，即於所有自所作事未得成辦。若諸所有補特伽羅根已成熟，善知方便，有無間修，已串修習，彼於所有皆能成辦。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the eight kinds of practitioner? The capable and the incapable; the skilled in method and the unskilled in method; those who practice without interruption and those who do not; and those who have long habituated themselves through practice and those who have not. … Whatever practitioner's faculties are not yet matured cannot accomplish any of this at all. Whatever practitioner's faculties, though matured, has not yet skillfully learned the methods, likewise cannot accomplish it. Whatever practitioner has matured faculties and skillful method, but lacks uninterrupted practice, cannot swiftly attain penetrating wisdom. Whatever practitioner has matured faculties, skillful method, and uninterrupted practice, but lacks long habituation, has not yet accomplished the task at hand. Whatever practitioner has matured faculties, skillful method, uninterrupted practice, and long habituation accomplishes everything.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi matches each pair cleanly to its cause: capability tracks whether the faith-diligence-mindfulness-concentration-wisdom roots have actually ripened; skill in method tracks whether a practitioner genuinely understands how to apply the four principles of reasoning, the four truths, and the various skillful-object frameworks rather than merely having heard of them; uninterrupted practice tracks sheer consistency, without the stop-start pattern that lets hard-won ground slip away between sessions; and long habituation tracks accumulated time itself, the slow process by which a technique becomes second nature rather than effortful. His key point, drawn directly from the treatise's own layered verdict: these four build cumulatively, and any single one missing caps what the remaining three can accomplish — mature faculties without skillful method go nowhere; skillful method without uninterrupted practice never gains momentum; and even uninterrupted practice, lacking sufficient time to mature into habituation, falls short of actually completing the task. Only when all four converge does genuine, swift attainment follow.

Cháng Bǎi illustrates single-pointed uninterrupted practice with a story from the Sūtra on the Stages of Practice: a king, testing a candidate for prime minister, ordered him to carry a bowl brimming with oil on a seven-kilometer walk through the marketplace and back, on pain of execution if a single drop spilled. Along the way, onlookers gossiped that he must be a condemned criminal; his own weeping family pushed through the crowd begging to know what he had done; performers sang, dancers spun, drunks brawled — and he registered none of it, his entire awareness fixed on the bowl in his hands. He returned without spilling a drop, and the king made him minister. This same image, Cháng Bǎi notes, recurs later in this treatise as a direct simile for one-pointed concentration itself: a mind that must guard its object as though an executioner stood ready behind it, blade drawn, the instant attention wavers — that is the intensity uninterrupted practice actually requires.

46. Milestone: Demonic Forces and Their Activities Open — Topic Sixteen

The sixteenth question opens — the second-to-last of the seventeen. The treatise names four kinds of demonic force, whose activities are said to be countless, urging every serious practitioner to know them thoroughly and keep clear of them.

Chinese Source

當知諸魔略有四種，魔所作事有無量種，勤修觀行諸瑜伽師，應善遍知，當正遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that demonic forces are, briefly, of four kinds, while the activities demons carry out are countless. Every yoga practitioner diligently cultivating meditative practice should thoroughly and correctly understand these, and should rightly keep clear of them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the sixteenth question as a fitting companion to the fifteenth: having just shown what genuine, swift attainment actually requires, the treatise now turns to what actively works against it. He previews that "demon" here names something far broader than a single mythological tempter — a compact set of four categories capable of generating essentially limitless specific obstructions, which is precisely why a serious practitioner needs a systematic account of the type rather than a list of every possible instance. The four kinds themselves, and their countless expressions, are named next.
