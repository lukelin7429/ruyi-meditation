Fascicle 29 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十九 · The Second Yogic Process Concludes

The Four Right Efforts and Beyond

Fascicle 29 completes the second yogic process of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, continuing directly from fascicle 28's close of the four foundations of mindfulness. It works through the remaining thirty-three factors of enlightenment and closes out all seventeen questions of "establishing categories" — the final fascicle of this entire second yogic process.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 29 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Four Right Efforts Open, Continuing Directly from the Foundations

Fascicle 29 opens exactly where fascicle 28 closed: a practitioner who has thoroughly cultivated the four foundations of mindfulness has begun dismantling the coarsest distorted views and can now genuinely tell wholesome from unwholesome — the necessary foundation for the second factor, the four right efforts, to actually function.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之四……如是於四念住串習行故，已能除遣麤麤顛倒，已能了達善不善法。從此無間，於諸未生惡不善法，為不生故；於諸已生惡不善法，為令斷故；於其未生一切善法，為令生故；於其已生一切善法，為欲令住、令不忘失；廣說如前，乃至策心、持心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the fourth part of the second yogic process … Thus, through repeated practice of the four foundations of mindfulness, one becomes able to dispel the coarsest of the distorted views, and becomes able to clearly distinguish wholesome states from unwholesome ones. Immediately following this, one strives — in order to prevent unwholesome states not yet arisen from arising; to end unwholesome states already arisen; to give rise to wholesome states not yet arisen; and to make wholesome states already arisen abide and not be forgotten — as has been extensively explained before, spurring and sustaining the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by placing this fascicle precisely: the second yogic process's seventeen-question structure runs from fascicle 26 through this one, and fascicle 29 carries it to completion — from the second factor of cultivation through the factors of enlightenment (the four right efforts) all the way through the seventeenth and final question, setting out toward liberation yet arriving at no fruit. He notes the treatise is careful not to overclaim what sustained mindfulness practice has actually accomplished at this point: the text says only that the coarsest distorted views have been dispelled and wholesome states can now be told from unwholesome ones — not that any noble path has yet been attained, whether through hearing, reflection, or cultivation. This matters, Cháng Bǎi stresses, because it explains why the right efforts follow the foundations rather than preceding them: without first learning to actually recognize a wholesome state from an unwholesome one through sustained mindfulness practice, energetic effort has no way to know which direction to push. Diligence without discernment is just agitation; the four right efforts only become genuinely "right" once the four foundations have supplied the judgment they depend on.

2. What Counts as Unwholesome; Arisen and Unarisen

Chinese Source

云何名為惡不善法？謂欲纏染汙身語意業，是身語意惡行所攝，及能起彼所有煩惱。……若未和合、未現在前，說名未生；若已和合、已現在前，說名已生。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called an evil, unwholesome state? It is bodily, verbal, and mental action defiled by the manifest afflictions bound to the desire realm — action classed as bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct — together with all the afflictions capable of giving rise to such misconduct. Whatever has not yet come together through conditions, not yet appearing before one, is called unarisen; whatever has already come together through conditions, already appearing before one, is called arisen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out a precise technical distinction: "unwholesome" names two related but distinct things — the actual bodily, verbal, or mental misconduct that results (the visible action), and the underlying afflictions (greed, hatred, delusion) capable of producing it, whether or not they've yet erupted into action. Desire, aversion, and delusion sitting quietly in the mind are affliction; the moment they drive an actual act of body or speech, that act becomes misconduct proper. He notes the treatise specifically names desire-realm affliction here, since a practitioner at this stage — still working through the foundations toward access concentration — hasn't yet risen even to the coarser levels of form-realm or formless-realm subtlety; the coarsest, most immediate work still lies in the desire realm itself. The arisen/unarisen distinction closing this section is, Cháng Bǎi notes, deceptively simple but load-bearing: it is precisely this distinction the four right efforts are organized around, targeting unwholesome states differently depending on whether they've already manifested or merely lie in wait.

3. What Counts as Wholesome; Generating the Aspiration

Chinese Source

云何名為一切善法？謂若彼對治、若蓋對治、若結對治。未生、已生，應知如前惡不善法。……若時未生惡不善法，先未和合，為令不生，發起希願：我當令彼一切一切皆不復生。是名於諸未生惡不善法，為不生故生欲。若時已生惡不善法，先已和合，為令斷故，發起希願：我當於彼一切一切皆不忍受，斷滅、除遣。是名於諸已生惡不善法，為令斷故生欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called every kind of wholesome state? It is whatever counters ordinary affliction, whatever counters the hindrances, and whatever counters the fetters. Arisen and unarisen should be understood as with the unwholesome states above. When an unwholesome state has not yet arisen, its conditions not yet come together, one generates the aspiration, for the sake of its not arising: "May I ensure this, and everything like it, never arises at all." This is generating the aspiration, for unarisen unwholesome states, that they not arise. When an unwholesome state has already arisen, its conditions already come together, one generates the aspiration, for the sake of ending it: "I will not tolerate this, or anything like it, at all — I will cut it off, remove it, and expel it." This is generating the aspiration, for arisen unwholesome states, that they be ended.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps wholesome states onto exactly the three-stage antidote structure this project has followed throughout: ordinary counter-measures for affliction arising in daily activity (the discipline that directly counters desire, aversion, and delusion), hindrance-counters used specifically in seated practice (the eight paired mental states — contracted versus scattered, lifted versus sunken, unagitated versus agitated, tranquil versus untranquil — each answering one of the five hindrances), and fetter-counters used once genuine concentration has been reached (concentrated, well-cultivated, and well-liberated mind, answering the deeper afflictive fetters). Generating the aspiration itself, he notes, is simply the practitioner's own resolved wish stated in the clearest possible terms — not yet the effort itself, but the settled intention that effort will then carry out, aimed with equal force at what hasn't yet happened and what already has.

4. Self-Spurring and Diligent Effort: Three Ways of Drawing the Line

Chinese Source

當知此中，於緣不現見境惡不善法……自策、自勵，是名策勵。於緣現見境惡不善法……勇猛正勤，是名發勤精進。……又於下品、中品諸纏……故自策勵。於上品纏……發勤精進。……又若行於過去境界……是名策勵。若行現在所緣境界……是名發勤精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that here: regarding unwholesome states connected to an object not directly seen … spurring and rousing oneself is called self-spurring. Regarding unwholesome states connected to an object directly seen … courageous and correct exertion is called diligent effort. Further, regarding fetters of the lower and middling grades … one spurs oneself. Regarding fetters of the upper grade … one exerts diligent effort. Further, when engaging with a past object … this is called self-spurring. When engaging with a presently experienced object … this is called diligent effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through three independent ways the treatise draws the line between "self-spurring" (策勵) and "diligent effort" (發勤精進) — not contradictory definitions but three lenses on the same underlying energy. By object: affliction tied to a past or future event, not currently visible, calls for quieter self-spurring — noticing an old grudge or an anticipated worry arise, and firmly declining to indulge it, the way a practitioner might catch themselves replaying an old debt never repaid and choose, deliberately, to let it go as if it had been given freely rather than stolen. Affliction tied to something directly in front of one right now — an actual sight, sound, or encounter provoking desire or anger in the moment — calls for the more forceful, courageous exertion of diligent effort, since a live temptation demands more immediate resistance than a remembered one.

By grade of affliction: Cháng Bǎi flags this second framework as running counter to how Elder Miàojìng usually explains the relative strength of coarse versus subtle affliction — here, the lower and middling grades (the coarser, more obvious afflictions) are handled by the gentler self-spurring, while the upper grade (paradoxically, the subtlest and hardest to detect) demands the full force of diligent effort, precisely because subtle affliction is so easily overlooked that only vigorous, sustained exertion catches it at all. And by time: past and future objects call for self-spurring, while the present object — whatever the five senses and mind are engaging with right now — calls for diligent effort, since only the present moment offers a live opportunity to actually intervene. Three frameworks, one underlying point, per Cháng Bǎi: effort must be calibrated to the difficulty of the target, not applied uniformly regardless of what's actually being faced.

5. Milestone: A Fourth Framework Closes the First Two Right Efforts

A final framework distinguishes self-spurring from diligent effort by whether an unwholesome state arises from mental discrimination alone or from discrimination triggered by an actual sense-object — closing the first two right efforts (ending unwholesome states, whether arisen or not) with all four complementary lenses now in place.

Chinese Source

又或有惡不善法，唯由分別力生，非境界力；或有惡不善法，由分別力生，亦境界力。……當知此中，惡不善法唯由分別力生，非境界力者……是名策勵。若由分別力生，亦境界力者……是名發勤精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, some unwholesome states arise solely through the power of discrimination, not through the power of an object; some unwholesome states arise through the power of discrimination together with the power of an object. Know that here: where an unwholesome state arises solely through the power of discrimination, not the power of an object … this is called self-spurring. Where it arises through the power of discrimination together with the power of an object … this is called diligent effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the power of discrimination" as the sixth consciousness alone — mental proliferation running on its own, as when a seated practitioner's mind wanders into old grievances or imagined futures with no actual sense-object present to trigger it. "The power of an object" only ever operates jointly with discrimination, never alone, since the five sense-consciousnesses can merely register a sight or sound; they cannot themselves generate craving or aversion — that judgment is always the sixth consciousness's work, deciding whether to keep looking, keep listening, keep dwelling. When affliction arises purely from unprompted mental wandering, gentler self-spurring suffices; when a live object joins forces with that same discrimination, the more forceful diligent effort is called for. With this fourth framework, Cháng Bǎi notes, the first two right efforts — ending what unwholesome has already arisen, and preventing what hasn't yet arisen — are fully explained from every relevant angle: by whether the object is visible, by the affliction's grade, by time, and now by whether an actual object is even involved.

6. The Remaining Two Right Efforts: Generating and Sustaining Wholesome States

Chinese Source

於其未生一切善法，為令生故生欲者……發起猛利求獲得欲、求現前欲……是名於其未生一切善法，為令生故生欲。於其已生一切善法，為欲令住、令不忘失、令修圓滿生欲者……於此善法已得不失、已得不退，依是說言：為欲令住。於此善法明了現前，無闇鈍性，依是說言：令不忘失。於此善法已得現前，數數修習，成滿究竟，依是說言：令修圓滿。……策勵者，為於已得令現前故。發勤精進者，為於未得令其得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Generating the aspiration, for unarisen wholesome states, that they arise, is generating an intense wish to obtain them and to bring them before the mind. Generating the aspiration, for arisen wholesome states, that they abide, not be forgotten, and be brought to completion: that they be obtained and not lost, obtained and not regressed from, is called "that they abide"; that they remain clear and present, free of any dullness, is called "that they not be forgotten"; that, already present, they be practiced again and again until brought to final completion, is called "that they be brought to completion." Self-spurring is for the sake of bringing what has already been obtained before the mind; diligent effort is for the sake of obtaining what has not yet been obtained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third and fourth right efforts — generating unarisen wholesome states and sustaining arisen ones — as answering a genuinely common experience: wholesome qualities are hard-won and easily lost. He offers the plain example of memorizing a sūtra passage: skip reciting it for a few days and it fades, however solidly it once seemed learned — precisely why the treatise insists on three distinct dimensions of "sustaining" a wholesome state already gained: not merely retained but not regressed from; not merely present but vividly, readily available rather than dull and sluggish (able, for instance, to bring impurity-contemplation instantly to mind the moment attraction arises, with no delay); and not merely maintained but actively carried toward full completion through repeated practice, since standing still on the path is itself a kind of quiet loss.

Self-spurring and diligent effort divide this same territory cleanly, Cháng Bǎi notes: self-spurring handles what has already been gained — keeping it present, unforgotten, and complete — while diligent effort handles what remains ungained, actively pursuing new wholesome ground rather than merely defending existing ground. Together, all four right efforts — ending, preventing, generating, and sustaining — cover the entire terrain a practitioner's ongoing relationship with wholesome and unwholesome states can occupy, with no gap between them.

7. Spurring the Mind and Sustaining It — Balancing Sinking and Agitation

Chinese Source

言策心者，謂若心於修奢摩他一境性中精勤方便……其心於內極略下劣、或恐下劣，觀見是已，爾時隨取一種淨妙舉相，殷勤策勵、慶悅其心。是名策心。云何持心？謂修舉時，其心掉動、或恐掉動，觀見是已，爾時還復於內略攝其心，修奢摩他。是名持心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Spurring the mind is: when the mind, diligently applying itself within the one-pointedness of calm-abiding practice … becomes, or threatens to become, extremely contracted and low within — upon observing this, one then takes up one of the pure, excellent images used for lifting the mind, vigorously spurring and gladdening it. This is spurring the mind. What is sustaining the mind? When, during the practice of lifting, the mind becomes, or threatens to become, agitated — upon observing this, one then returns the mind, contracting it inward again, to the practice of calm-abiding. This is sustaining the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these two final terms in the right-efforts formula as the practical, moment-to-moment corrective mechanism running underneath everything else described so far: spurring the mind is the antidote specifically for sinking during calm-abiding practice — noticing the mind going slack or drowsy and immediately reaching for one of the six recollections (the Buddha's virtues, one's own generosity) to lift it back into alertness. Sustaining the mind is the antidote for the opposite failure, agitation arising during the more energetic practice of "lifting" itself — noticing the mind has overshot into restlessness and deliberately narrowing it back down into calm-abiding's one-pointed focus. Cháng Bǎi notes the two work as a constant, self-correcting pair: too much stillness calls for spurring, too much lifting calls for sustaining, and a practitioner who can catch each drift early enough, before it fully manifests, eventually settles into the balanced equanimity where neither correction is needed. With this, the full sequence of the four right efforts — generating the aspiration, self-spurring, diligent effort, spurring the mind, and sustaining it — is complete.

8. Milestone: Two Further Names — the Four Right Victories and the Four Right Cuttings

The treatise closes the four right efforts with two alternate names, each highlighting a different aspect of the same practice: "four right victories," framed against dark and bright states, and "four right cuttings," each cutting given its own precise technical name.

Chinese Source

如是四種，亦名正勝。謂於黑品諸法，其未生者為令不生，其已生者為令斷滅，生欲、策勵、發勤精進、策心、持心，是二正勝。於白品諸法……如前黑品廣說，應知是二正勝。如是四種，亦名正斷。一名律儀斷。謂於已生惡不善法，為令斷故……二名斷斷。謂於未生惡不善法，為不生故……由於已生惡不善事，應修律儀，令其斷滅，不應忍受。由是因緣，名律儀斷。於其未生惡不善事，為欲令彼不現行斷，為欲令彼不現前斷，為斷故斷，故名斷斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

These same four are also called the four right victories: regarding dark states, preventing what has not arisen and ending what has — this is two right victories; regarding bright states, generating what has not arisen and sustaining what has, exactly as explained for dark states — this is the other two right victories. These same four are also called the four right cuttings. The first is called discipline-cutting: regarding unwholesome states already arisen, generating the aspiration and effort to end them. The second is called cutting-upon-cutting: regarding unwholesome states not yet arisen, generating the aspiration and effort that they not arise. Because one should, through discipline, end what unwholesome has already arisen rather than tolerate it, this is called discipline-cutting. Regarding what unwholesome has not yet arisen: cutting off its coming to manifest, cutting off its coming to appear, cutting for the very sake of cutting — hence it is called cutting-upon-cutting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "victory" (勝) as naming the same four efforts from the standpoint of triumph over affliction: two victories over dark, unwholesome states (preventing and ending), two victories in cultivating bright, wholesome ones (generating and sustaining) — the identical structure, simply named for its outcome rather than its method. "Cutting" (斷) highlights something more specific: discipline-cutting names the straightforward work of actually ending an unwholesome state that has already manifested, through the direct application of discipline rather than tolerance or delay. Cutting-upon-cutting — Cháng Bǎi notes the doubled name is deliberate, not redundant — names the more subtle work of preventing an unwholesome state that hasn't yet appeared, itself unpacked into three angles: cutting off its coming to actually manifest in action, cutting off its coming to appear even as a mental event, and cutting simply for the sake of cutting itself, before any specific target has even arisen — a general readiness to refuse affliction root and branch, not merely reactive vigilance. With both alternate names now given, the four right efforts — training, right victory, and right cutting all naming the identical practice — close in full, and the treatise turns next to the third factor of enlightenment: the four bases of power.

9. Milestone: Cultivation-Cutting and Protection-Cutting Close the Four Right Efforts

The remaining two cuttings — cultivation-cutting for unarisen wholesome states, protection-cutting for arisen ones — complete all four names, and the treatise closes with a compact summary: the entire practice reduces to two perfections, aspiration and application.

Chinese Source

三名修斷。謂於未生一切善法，為令生故……由於善法數修數習，先所未得能令現前，能有所斷，故名修斷。四名防護斷。謂於已生一切善法，為欲令住……由於已得、已現在前諸善法中，遠離放逸，修不放逸，能令善法住不忘失……能有所斷，故名防護斷。……謂為顯示於黑白品捨取事中，增上意樂圓滿及加行圓滿，是故宣說四種正斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third is called cultivation-cutting: regarding all wholesome states not yet arisen, generating them through repeated cultivation and practice, bringing what was previously ungained before the mind, and thereby cutting off what obstructed it. The fourth is called protection-cutting: regarding all wholesome states already arisen, remaining free of negligence so that they abide, are not forgotten, and are protected from loss — thereby likewise cutting off what would obstruct them. All of this is taught in order to display, with respect to what should be abandoned among dark states and what should be taken up among bright ones, the perfection of aspiration and the perfection of application — this is why the four right efforts are taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes cultivation-cutting and protection-cutting complete the fourfold naming by covering the two remaining cases: gaining what has never yet been gained requires sustained cultivation over time (the same "digging a well" persistence needed anywhere real skill is built), while protecting what has already been gained requires simple, vigilant freedom from negligence — a very different kind of effort from first acquisition, closer to maintenance than construction. He closes by drawing out the treatise's own compact summary of the entire four-right-efforts topic: everything explained — generating the aspiration, self-spurring, diligent effort, spurring and sustaining the mind, and all four alternate names — reduces to exactly two perfections. The perfection of aspiration (增上意樂圓滿) is the settled inner resolve, corresponding to "generating the aspiration" alone; the perfection of application (加行圓滿) is everything that actually carries that resolve into action — self-spurring, diligent effort, and the moment-to-moment corrections of spurring and sustaining the mind together. Resolve without follow-through accomplishes nothing; follow-through without resolve has nothing to sustain it. With both perfections in place, the second factor of enlightenment closes, and the treatise turns to the third: the four bases of power.

10. Milestone: The Four Bases of Power Open — Aspiration-Concentration in Detail

The third factor of enlightenment opens: four kinds of concentration, each driven by a different power — aspiration, effort, mind, and investigation — beginning with aspiration-concentration's own causal conditions and its genuine but limited function.

Chinese Source

從此復修四三摩地。謂欲三摩地、勤三摩地、心三摩地、觀三摩地。……若於是時純生樂欲，生樂欲已，於諸所有惡不善法自性、因緣、過患、對治，正審思察，住一境念。……於諸善法自性、因緣、功德、出離，正審思察，住一境念。即由如是多修習故，觸一境性。於諸所有惡不善法現行諸纏，能令遠離，而未永害煩惱隨眠。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point, one further cultivates the four kinds of concentration: aspiration-concentration, effort-concentration, mind-concentration, and investigation-concentration. When, at this time, a pure aspiration arises, and having arisen, one carefully and thoroughly examines the intrinsic nature, causes and conditions, faults, and antidote of all evil, unwholesome states, abiding in mindfulness on a single object — and likewise carefully and thoroughly examines the intrinsic nature, causes and conditions, merit, and liberating power of wholesome states, abiding in mindfulness on a single object — then, through this repeated cultivation, one attains one-pointedness. This is able to keep at a distance the manifest fetters of all evil, unwholesome states, though it has not yet permanently destroyed the latent seeds of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the four bases of power as a natural next step once a practitioner has cultivated the right efforts but finds their concentration still insufficient — each of the four names a different engine capable, on its own, of driving genuine absorption, so a practitioner can lean on whichever comes most naturally. Aspiration-concentration, the first, is built from pure, uncontaminated wish for the goal, joined to careful, structured contemplation: examining unwholesome states along the same four dimensions used throughout this project (their basic nature, what causes them, the harm they do, and how to counter them) and wholesome states along a parallel four (their nature, their causes, their genuine benefit, and their power to liberate) — sustained contemplation of this kind, repeated, naturally settles the mind onto a single object and produces one-pointedness.

Cháng Bǎi flags the closing line as an important honesty check the treatise builds into every stage of this account: even successful aspiration-concentration only suppresses affliction's active manifestation, keeping the fetters at bay — it does not yet destroy the latent seeds underneath. This distinction between suppressing and destroying, he notes, recurs throughout the treatise precisely because it's so easy for a practitioner to mistake temporary quiet for permanent freedom; genuine destruction of the seeds themselves requires supramundane wisdom, still further down the path than concentration alone can reach.

11. Effort-Concentration and Mind-Concentration

Chinese Source

若於過去、未來、現在所緣境界，能順所有惡不善法……自策、自勵、發勤精進，於彼所緣，於彼境界自性、因緣、過患、對治，正審思察，住一境念。即由如是多安住故，能正生起心一境性。……是名勤增上力所得三摩地。……若復策發諸下劣心，或復制持諸掉舉心，又時時間修增上捨。……是名心增上力所得三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding objects belonging to the past, future, or present that tend toward evil, unwholesome states … self-spurring, self-rousing, and diligent effort, carefully and thoroughly examining that object's nature, causes, faults, and antidote, abiding in mindfulness on a single object — through this repeated abiding, one-pointedness rightly arises. This is called the concentration attained through the power of effort. Further lifting a sunken mind, restraining an agitated one, and cultivating excellent equanimity from time to time — this is called the concentration attained through the power of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes effort-concentration simply applies the same self-spurring and diligent-effort distinction from the four right efforts (past and future objects met with quieter self-spurring, present objects with more forceful diligent effort) directly toward building one-pointedness itself, rather than merely toward ending or preventing specific unwholesome states — the identical energy redirected at concentration as its own goal. It carries the identical limitation as aspiration-concentration: manifest affliction kept at a distance, latent seeds untouched.

Mind-concentration, the third, is built entirely from the lift-calm-equanimity technique already established throughout this project: a sunken, drowsy mind is lifted through an inspiring recollection; an agitated, scattered mind is drawn back through calming focus; and once neither correction is needed, the mind settles into equanimity — clear, undisturbed, requiring no further adjustment in either direction. Cháng Bǎi notes this three-part rhythm, repeated patiently over time, is what actually produces stable one-pointedness for a practitioner whose natural tendency is neither strongly aspirational nor strongly energetic, but simply needs the oscillation between too little and too much energy smoothed out through practice.

12. Milestone: Investigation-Concentration Closes All Four; Eight Kinds of Abandoning-Conditioning Open

Investigation-concentration — built from honest mental engagement and unflinching self-observation — closes all four concentrations with the same candid admission repeated at each stage: affliction's activity has been kept away, but its seeds remain. The treatise then opens a second component of this factor, eight kinds of conditioning aimed specifically at what concentration alone cannot reach.

Chinese Source

便自思惟：我今為有現有惡不善法不覺知耶？為無現無惡不善法不覺知耶？我今應當遍審觀察。……由是因緣，離增上慢，如實自知：我唯於纏心得解脫，未於一切一切隨眠心得解脫。……是名觀增上力所得三摩地。……彼由如是四三摩地增上力故，已遠諸纏；復為永害一切一切惡不善法諸隨眠故，及為修習能對治彼諸善法故，便更生起樂欲策勵……彼於如是正修習時，有八斷行，為欲永害諸隨眠故，為三摩地得圓滿故，差別而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

One then reflects: "Is there, right now, some evil unwholesome state present that I am simply not aware of? Or is there, right now, truly none present, and I correctly know there is none? I should thoroughly and carefully examine this." … Through this, one remains free of overestimation, truly knowing for oneself: "I have only gained freedom of mind from affliction's active manifestation — I have not yet gained freedom of mind from every latent seed of every kind." … This is called the concentration attained through the power of investigation. Having, through the power of these four concentrations, already kept the fetters at a distance, and further wishing to permanently destroy every latent seed of evil, unwholesome states of every kind, and to cultivate the very wholesome states that counter them, one generates aspiration and self-spurring once again. In genuinely cultivating this way, there are eight kinds of conditioning, operating with their own distinct functions, for the sake of permanently destroying the latent seeds and for the sake of bringing concentration itself to full completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats investigation-concentration as the most self-aware of the four: rather than simply applying aspiration, effort, or the lift-calm rhythm, it is built from continuously and honestly asking oneself whether affliction is secretly present without one's own knowledge — a check against exactly the self-flattery that produces false confidence in one's own progress. He reads the closing admission — "freed only from affliction's activity, not from any latent seed" — as the treatise's deliberate refusal to let any of the four concentrations be mistaken for final attainment, however genuinely they settle the mind.

This shared limitation, Cháng Bǎi explains, is precisely why the four bases of power don't stop at four concentrations: having built genuine one-pointedness through whichever of the four approaches suits them, a practitioner still faces the deeper task of actually destroying affliction's seeds, which concentration alone — however refined — cannot do. The treatise's answer is eight kinds of conditioning, built from the same five terms already established in the four right efforts (aspiration, self-spurring, diligent effort, spurring the mind, sustaining it) but now aimed at two specific goals at once: permanently uprooting the latent seeds, and bringing concentration itself to complete fullness. What these eight specifically are is unpacked next.

13. The Eight Kinds of Conditioning Named in Full

Chinese Source

一者、欲。謂起如是希望樂欲：我於何時修三摩地當得圓滿？我於何時當能斷滅惡不善法所有隨眠？二者、策勵。謂乃至修所有對治，不捨加行。三者、信。謂不捨加行正安住故，於上所證，深生信解。四者、安。謂清淨信而為上首，心生歡喜；心歡喜故，漸次息除諸惡不善法品麤重。五者、念。謂九種相，於九種相安住其心，奢摩他品能攝持故。六者、正知。謂毗鉢舍那品慧。七者、思。謂心造作。於斷、未斷正觀察時，造作其心，發起能順止觀二品身業、語業。八者、捨。謂行過去、未來、現在隨順諸惡不善法中，心無染汙、心平等性。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, aspiration: generating this very wish — "When will my cultivation of concentration reach completion? When will I be able to destroy all the latent seeds of evil, unwholesome states?" Second, self-spurring: cultivating every antidote without abandoning the effort. Third, faith: through not abandoning the effort and remaining rightly settled in it, generating deep confidence in what is to be realized above. Fourth, pliancy: with pure faith leading the way, gladness arises in the mind; through that gladness, the grossness of evil, unwholesome states is gradually calmed and removed. Fifth, mindfulness: the nine aspects, the mind abiding in them, sustained by the calm-abiding factor. Sixth, clear comprehension: the wisdom belonging to insight. Seventh, volition: the mind's own activity — when carefully observing whether affliction is cut or not yet cut, activating the mind to generate bodily and verbal action in accord with calm-abiding and insight. Eighth, equanimity: whether engaging past, future, or present objects tending toward evil, unwholesome states, the mind remains undefiled, in a state of genuine evenness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all eight — also called the "eight excellent practices" — as a single developmental arc. Aspiration is the settled wish for two specific goals: full concentration, and the actual destruction of affliction's seeds. Self-spurring is simply never abandoning the antidotes already known — impurity for desire, loving-kindness for aversion, dependent origination for delusion, and so on. Faith follows from sustained effort itself, deepening into genuine confidence that the path actually works. Pliancy is the settled ease, grounded in that faith, that gradually dissolves affliction's grossness. Mindfulness draws on the nine stages of mental abiding already established in this project's account of calm-abiding — Cháng Bǎi notes this eight-fold list simply names, rather than re-derives, that same nine-stage progression as its fifth member. Clear comprehension is specifically insight's own wisdom, the discernment needed to actually recognize whether affliction has been cut. Volition is the mind's own follow-through, translating recognition into actual bodily and verbal conduct consistent with calm-abiding and insight — Cháng Bǎi identifies this with "concentration-born discipline" (定共戒), the effortless, spontaneous rectitude that accompanies genuine absorption, distinct from both ordinary precept-keeping and the still-higher path-born discipline of a noble one. And equanimity closes the list: genuine evenness of mind toward any object, past, present, or future, that might otherwise tend toward affliction — undisturbed whether the object is remembered, anticipated, or directly faced.

14. Milestone: The Four Bases of Power Close in Full

The treatise closes with a simple diagnostic — two conditions for knowing whether the latent seeds have actually been cut — then matches the eight kinds of conditioning back onto the four-part faith/aspiration/diligence/means framework from fascicle 28, before naming the entire structure together as the four bases of power.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，於隨眠斷分別了知。謂由境界不現見思，及由境界現見捨故。……如是名為八種斷行，亦名勝行。如是八種斷行、勝行，即是為害隨眠瑜伽。此中欲者，即是彼欲；此中策勵，即彼精進；此中信者，即是彼信；此中安、念、正知、思、捨，即彼方便。……若先欲等四三摩地，若今所說八種斷行，於為永斷所有隨眠圓滿成辦三摩地時，一切總名欲三摩地斷行成就神足、勤三摩地斷行成就神足、心三摩地斷行成就神足、觀三摩地斷行成就神足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through two conditions, one clearly discerns whether the latent seeds have been cut off: through thinking about an object not directly seen, and through equanimity toward an object directly seen. This is called the eight kinds of conditioning, also called the excellent practices. These eight kinds of conditioning, or excellent practices, are precisely the yogic discipline for destroying the latent seeds. Here, aspiration is that same aspiration named earlier; here, self-spurring is that same diligence; here, faith is that same faith; and here, pliancy, mindfulness, clear comprehension, volition, and equanimity are that same means. Thus, both the four concentrations explained earlier and the eight kinds of conditioning explained now, taken together, at the point of permanently cutting off every latent seed and bringing concentration to full completion, are together called the basis of power through aspiration-concentration and conditioning, the basis of power through effort-concentration and conditioning, the basis of power through mind-concentration and conditioning, and the basis of power through investigation-concentration and conditioning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing diagnostic as beautifully simple: a practitioner tests their own progress by noticing whether calm reflection on a past or future trigger, and genuine equanimity when directly facing a present one, both hold steady — if either cracks, the seed is still there, however quiet it has seemed. He then highlights the treatise's own act of unification: the eight kinds of conditioning are not a new, sixth topic alongside faith, aspiration, diligence, and means from fascicle 28's account of yogic practice — they are that same fourfold structure, simply expanded to its full eight-part detail, with aspiration and self-spurring keeping their own names, faith unchanged, and the remaining five (pliancy, mindfulness, clear comprehension, volition, equanimity) all falling under "means." With this equivalence established, the treatise gives its final, formal name to everything covered under the third factor of enlightenment: the four bases of power, each one naming a concentration paired with its conditioning, together aimed at concentration's full completion and affliction's genuine, permanent destruction — not merely its temporary suspension. This closes the four bases of power, the third of the thirty-seven factors, in full.

15. Milestone: Why "Bases of Power" — Two Explanations, Worldly and Supramundane

The treatise closes the third factor of enlightenment by explaining its name: like feet that carry a person out and back, the four concentrations and eight conditionings carry a practitioner first to ever-higher worldly absorptions, and ultimately to supramundane realization itself.

Chinese Source

何因緣故說名神足？答：如有足者，能往能還，騰躍勇健，能得能證世間所有殊勝之法。世殊勝法說名為神，彼能到此，故名神足。……如是若有如是諸法，有三摩地圓滿成辦，彼心如是清淨鮮白，無諸瑕穢，離隨煩惱，安住正直，有所堪能，獲得不動，能往能還，騰躍勇健，能得能證出世間法。由出世法最勝自在，是最勝神，彼能證此，故名神足。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the reason it is called the basis of power? Answer: just as one who has feet can go out and return, leaping vigorously, able to reach and realize all the excellent states the world has to offer — worldly excellence is here called "power," and that which arrives there is called its "basis." Likewise, when these very factors bring concentration to full completion, the mind becomes pure and bright, free of every blemish, free of the secondary afflictions, settled in uprightness, genuinely capable, having attained the unshakeable — able to go out and return, leaping vigorously, able to reach and realize supramundane states. Since the supramundane teaching is the most excellent and free of all, it is called the "greatest power," and that which realizes it is called its "basis."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the two explanations as tracking real stages of a single journey rather than two different journeys. In the worldly sense, "power" simply names each successively higher absorption — second surpassing first, third surpassing second — and the four concentrations with their conditionings are the "feet" that actually carry a practitioner there, rung by rung. In the supramundane sense, the same feet carry a practitioner further still: a mind grown genuinely pure of the subtler defilements that even accomplished meditators can fall into — delight in the absorption itself, subtle conceit about one's attainment, wrong view, doubt about the teaching, or a lingering ignorant belief in a permanent self — becomes settled, unshakeable, and finally capable of realizing the supramundane path itself, the "greatest power" of all. Cháng Bǎi adds a caution about the thirty-seven factors as a whole: though listed in a fixed sequence (foundations, efforts, bases of power, faculties, powers, enlightenment-factors, path), they need not be read as one rigid, linear staircase — the four foundations alone, or the four right efforts alone, or the eight kinds of conditioning alone, can each in principle carry a sincere practitioner the entire distance from ordinary being to the noble path; the sevenfold structure organizes the teaching for clarity, not a single mandatory sequence every practitioner must climb rung by literal rung.

16. The Five Faculties Open: Faith Generating Everything Beyond It

Chinese Source

彼由如是勝三摩地為所依持……能進修習增上心學、增上慧學所有瑜伽。由進修習此瑜伽故，於他大師、弟子所證，深生勝解、深生淨信。此清淨信增上義故，說名信根。……於能生起出世間法而為上首，及於能起精進、念、定、慧為其增上。餘精進等，於能生起出世間法，及於能起展轉乃至慧為其增上。乃至其慧，唯於能起出世間法為其增上。是故信等說名五根。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on such excellent concentration as its support, the practitioner is able to advance in cultivating all yogic practice belonging to higher mind-training and higher wisdom-training. Through advancing in this cultivation, one generates deep confidence and deep pure faith in what the Teacher and his disciples have realized. Because this pure faith has the meaning of increasing further excellence, it is called the faculty of faith. It is the head, generating the supramundane teaching, and also increases diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. The remaining faculties — diligence and the rest — likewise generate the supramundane teaching, and successively increase one another up through wisdom. Wisdom, finally, only generates the supramundane teaching itself. This is why faith and the rest are called the five faculties.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four bases of power's genuine completion as the actual precondition for the faculties to arise at all: only once concentration is stable enough to support it does deep, settled confidence in the Buddha's and the noble disciples' own realization become possible — not blind belief, but confidence earned through one's own practice actually beginning to work. He traces the cascading structure the treatise gives each faculty: faith increases diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom all at once, standing as the whole chain's head; diligence in turn increases mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom; mindfulness increases concentration and wisdom; concentration increases wisdom alone; and wisdom, having nothing left above it to increase, generates only the supramundane teaching directly. Cháng Bǎi's summary: each faculty in this sequence is both an increase over what preceded it and a foundation for what follows — a five-step ladder where every rung supports every rung above it, culminating in wisdom's own direct access to liberation.

17. The Five Powers: Faith Unshakeable by Any Force

Chinese Source

若復了知前後所證而有差別，隨此能於後後所證出世間法，深生勝解、深生淨信。此清淨信難伏義故，說名信力。……問：誰不能伏？答：此清淨信，若天、若魔、若諸沙門、若婆羅門、若餘世間，無有如法能引奪者，諸煩惱纏亦不能屈，故名難伏。……由此諸力具大威勢，摧伏一切魔軍勢力，能證一切諸漏永盡，是故名力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further knowing the difference between what has already been realized and what comes progressively after, and, in accordance with this, generating deep confidence and deep pure faith regarding the successively higher supramundane teachings to be realized — because this pure faith has the meaning of being difficult to subdue, it is called the power of faith. Question: subdued by whom? Answer: this pure faith cannot be legitimately taken away by any god, any demon, any ascetic of another path, any brahmin, or anyone else in the world whatsoever — nor can it be overcome even by one's own remaining afflictions. This is why it is called difficult to subdue. Because these powers possess such great and majestic force, able to overcome the entire host of Māra's armies and to realize the complete and permanent exhaustion of every defilement, they are therefore called powers.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the powers as the same five faculties matured past mere presence into genuine resilience — faith that has moved from simply being there to being unshakeable by anything: not a god's temptation, not Māra's interference, not a rival ascetic's rhetoric, not a learned brahmin's argument, and — the most demanding test of all — not even the practitioner's own residual afflictions, which might otherwise pull a still-imperfect mind back toward doubt. He notes the treatise's own list of Māra's ten armies elsewhere (desire, sorrow, hunger and thirst, craving, drowsiness, fear, doubt, malice, love of gain and false renown, and self-exaltation with contempt for others) as exactly what these five powers, matured, are capable of routing outright — the martial image of "power" is deliberate, naming genuine strength adequate to actual combat, not merely private conviction. With power sufficient to defeat every one of these and to bring about the complete exhaustion of affliction, the five powers close, having explained both faculties and powers individually; the treatise now turns to the relationship between them.
