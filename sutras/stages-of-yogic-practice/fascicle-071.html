Fascicle 71 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十一 · The Śrāvaka Ground, Continued

Seven Kinds of Meaning, Seven Kinds of Joy

Continuing directly from fascicle 70, Maitreya opens a fourteen-part discussion of assorted categories of meaning, beginning with seven kinds of meaning and the seven joys each one brings, three archery virtues standing for precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and then the vast analysis of the six kinds of direct realization examined from twenty-two distinct angles. The transcript continues through the Ghee-Simile Sūtra's nine wholesome dharmas and four kinds of reasoning, dwelling with correct knowledge in time, measure, assembly, speech, and silence, the thirty causes that keep a practitioner from ever entering the path, and why a disciple with supernatural power would still visit the realm of hungry ghosts — coming to rest exactly where the source lecture-transcript ends, with the entire Determination of the Śrāvaka Ground brought to a close.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 71 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Seven Kinds of Meaning a Practitioner Should Master

This fifth part of the Śrāvaka Ground's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 70, taking up a fourteen-part discussion of assorted categories of meaning still gathered under the eighth of the śrāvaka canon's own thirteen topics, its own explanation of miscellaneous meanings. The topic opens with seven kinds of meaning a practitioner should recognize within the Buddha's own teaching. The first, meaning to be investigated, refers to the teaching itself, the aggregates, sense-fields, elements, noble truths, and dependent origination among the specific topics whose own essential nature, distinctions, specific characteristics, and shared characteristics a practitioner should seek to understand. The second, meaning to be grasped, refers to whatever doctrine that same teaching actually expresses, impermanence, non-self, the peace of nirvana, emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness among the specific doctrines a practitioner should take hold of and genuinely comprehend. The third, meaning to be avoided, refers to incorrect views and teachings, unwholesome friends, and unwholesome circumstances, all of which should be kept at a distance so that mistaken thinking and mistaken conduct never take root. The fourth, meaning to be drawn forth, refers to the virtues cultivated within concentration, precepts, concentration, and wisdom together, which a practitioner should deliberately bring forth through sustained practice. The fifth, meaning to be restrained, refers to whatever unwholesome conduct of body, speech, or mind, together with unwholesome habits and unwholesome views, should be actively checked. The sixth, meaning to be upheld, refers to the practices of precepts, concentration, and wisdom benefiting oneself, together with the very meaning of nirvana, which should be firmly maintained. And the seventh, meaning to be developed, refers to how precepts give rise to concentration and concentration gives rise to wisdom in turn, this same development extending from benefiting oneself all the way to the great practice of a bodhisattva benefiting others as well. A practitioner who genuinely penetrates all seven of these meanings, from investigation through development, thereby gives rise to seven kinds of genuine joy.

Chinese Source

復次，有七種義。一、應推義，二、應攝義，三、應避義，四、應引義，五、應遮義，六、應持義，七、應發義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seven kinds of meaning exist: meaning to be investigated, meaning to be grasped, meaning to be avoided, meaning to be drawn forth, meaning to be restrained, meaning to be upheld, and meaning to be developed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Notes on the Yogācārabhūmi's own gloss on each of these seven meanings, noting that "meaning to be drawn forth" applies broadly to every virtue cultivated within concentration, precepts, concentration, and wisdom included, while "meaning to be developed" carries the discussion all the way from a practitioner's own benefit to the bodhisattva's own turn toward benefiting others as well, this final meaning quietly opening the door toward the Mahāyāna path still further ahead.

2. Section: Seven Kinds of Joy Answering the Seven Kinds of Meaning

Seven kinds of joy then answer these same seven kinds of meaning in turn. The first, joy drawn from hearing, arises from the wisdom born of hearing as a practitioner investigates and penetrates the teaching itself, corresponding to meaning to be investigated. The second, joy drawn from reflection, arises from the wisdom born of reflection as a practitioner grasps the doctrine actually taught and avoids unwholesome friends, circumstances, and conduct in turn, corresponding to both meaning to be grasped and meaning to be avoided. The third, joy drawn from cultivation, arises from the wisdom born of cultivation as a practitioner draws forth the virtues of precepts, concentration, and wisdom, corresponding to meaning to be drawn forth. The fourth, joy drawn from freedom from the hindrances, arises as that same cultivation frees a practitioner from desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt, corresponding to meaning to be restrained understood inwardly. The fifth, joy drawn from debate, arises as a practitioner subdues non-Buddhist arguments and checks unwholesome teaching from without, corresponding to meaning to be restrained understood outwardly. The sixth, joy drawn from recollecting one's own virtue, arises as a practitioner reflects on precepts kept, concentration cultivated, and wisdom developed for one's own genuine benefit, corresponding to meaning to be upheld. And the seventh, joy drawn from never resting content with what is inferior, arises as a practitioner, recognizing that whatever has been achieved so far still falls short, presses on toward further virtue and toward benefiting others as well, corresponding to meaning to be developed.

Chinese Source

復次，有七種喜。一、聞所引喜，二、思所引喜，三、修所引喜，四、離蓋所引喜，五、議論所引喜，六、念自功德所引喜，七、於諸下劣不生知足所引喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seven kinds of joy answer these seven kinds of meaning: joy drawn from hearing, from reflection, from cultivation, from freedom from the hindrances, from debate, from recollecting one's own virtue, and from never resting content with what is inferior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws this correspondence from several commentators cited in the Notes on the Yogācārabhūmi, Master Jǐng and Master Shéntài among them, and highlights the final joy's own quiet ambition, a practitioner genuinely satisfied with precepts, concentration, and wisdom already cultivated still recognizing that a bodhisattva's own path requires benefiting others as well, self-benefit alone never quite enough to rest content with.

3. Section: Five Kinds of Dharma

A further topic then opens: five kinds of Dharma. The first, the taught Dharma, comprises the scriptures, the discipline, and the fundamental treatises the Buddha himself expounded. The second, the practiced Dharma, comprises the actual practice of precepts and related conduct. The third, the Dharma to be embraced, comprises both material and spiritual support a practitioner gathers in. The fourth, the Dharma to be enjoyed, likewise comprises both material and spiritual benefit a practitioner makes genuine use of. And the fifth, the realized Dharma, comprises the principle and the specific reality a practitioner ultimately comes to realize. A related passage compares the renunciant and the Brahmin through this same fivefold structure, teaching, precept, embracing, enjoyment, and realization, while an earlier fascicle of this same treatise compressed embracing and enjoyment together, describing only three kinds of Dharma, teaching, practice, and ultimate realization.

Chinese Source

復次，有五種法。一、教法，二、行法，三、攝受法，四、受用法，五、證法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Five kinds of Dharma exist: the taught Dharma, the practiced Dharma, the Dharma to be embraced, the Dharma to be enjoyed, and the realized Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references fascicle 70's own threefold account of what makes the Dharma uncommon among non-Buddhist teachings, teaching, practice, and ultimate realization, noting that this fivefold version simply unfolds "practice" there into embracing and enjoyment here, the same underlying structure examined at two different levels of detail.

4. Section: A Bow's Own Strength and an Arrow's Own Aim, Two Archery Virtues Standing for Precepts and Concentration

A further topic then opens: three virtues of skillful archery, offered here as a sustained image for the precepts, the concentration, and the wisdom a practitioner needs. The first, the bow's own virtue, stands for the precepts, since an arrow depends entirely on the bow that launches it, exactly as concentration depends entirely on precepts kept purely beforehand. A bow possesses two virtues of its own: a genuinely sturdy, durable nature, standing for how precepts, once firmly established, resist being broken down by outside temptation; and the capacity to bring the entire task to completion, since without a bow, an arrow has nothing to rely on and can never be loosed at all, exactly as concentration can never arise, and wisdom never be drawn forth, without pure precepts as their own foundation. The second, the arrow's own virtue, stands for concentration, an arrow possessing a single virtue of its own, likewise the capacity to bring the task to completion, since it is the arrow, resting on the bow, that actually flies toward the target, exactly as it is concentration, sustained through continuous, focused contemplation, that actually gives rise to the insight capable of penetrating reality.

Chinese Source

復次，具三種德，方能善射。一、由弓德，二、由箭德，三、由中的德。弓有二德。一、其性堅牢，二、善作究竟。箭有一德。善作究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Three virtues together make for skillful archery: the bow's own virtue, the arrow's own virtue, and the virtue of striking the target's center. The bow possesses two virtues: a genuinely sturdy nature, and the capacity to bring the task to completion. The arrow possesses one virtue: the capacity to bring the task to completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this archery image as a genuinely elegant way of showing precepts, concentration, and wisdom as a single continuous action rather than three separate achievements, a bow's sturdiness meaningless without an arrow to launch, and an arrow meaningless without a bow to launch it from, exactly as precepts without concentration, or concentration without the wisdom it makes possible, remain similarly incomplete.

5. Section: Three Kinds of Skill Behind Striking the Target's Center, Closing the Archery Image

The third virtue, striking the target's own center, stands for wisdom, and comprises three further kinds of skill. The first, ultimate skill, describes an archer so thoroughly trained that every single shot strikes true, standing for the wisdom of an arhat beyond further learning, whose knowledge that affliction is exhausted and that no further rebirth awaits is itself fully and finally accomplished. The second, skill through sustained practice, describes how repeated practice alone brings an archer to strike the center reliably, standing for the wisdom cultivated along the path of cultivation, a practitioner who has already seen the truth directly still needing to continue that same cultivation in order to sever ever subtler affliction and allow wisdom to keep maturing. And the third, skill through a teacher's instruction, describes how a beginning archer must first learn the fundamental method and technique from an instructor before ever striking the center at all, standing for the wisdom belonging to the path of seeing, a practitioner who studies the Buddha's own teaching, penetrates its essential principles, and cultivates precepts, concentration, and wisdom in order to first attain that direct seeing of the truth. This closes the sustained image of the bow, the arrow, and the target's center standing for the precepts, the concentration, and the wisdom a practitioner needs.

Chinese Source

中的有三德。一、究竟工巧，二、串習工巧，三、師學工巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Striking the target's center comprises three kinds of skill: ultimate skill, skill through sustained practice, and skill through a teacher's instruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three kinds of skill as tracing wisdom's own full arc across the path, a beginner's skill first learned from a teacher at the path of seeing, refined through sustained practice along the path of cultivation, and only fully perfected at the path beyond further learning, no shortcut available from beginner's instruction straight to an arhat's own effortless accuracy, closing this sustained archery image for precepts, concentration, and wisdom together.

6. Section: Three Kinds of Teaching, Three Kinds of Underlying Approach

A fifth topic then opens: three kinds of teaching the Buddha employed. The first, the gradual teaching, describes the step-by-step guidance given to śrāvaka practitioners over an extended period. The second, the uninterrupted teaching, describes the sudden, single-vehicle teaching delivered all at once, as when the Buddha, newly awakened, expounded the Flower Adornment Sūtra in a single unbroken session. And the third, the teaching never repeated, describes a final, fully explicit teaching requiring no later restatement in verse, as with the Nirvana Sūtra delivered just before the Buddha's own passing, or the Diamond Sūtra, consisting entirely of prose with no repeated verses at all. A further threefold structure then reduces the Buddha's own boundless teaching to three underlying approaches: an approach distinguished by doctrine, since a given teaching may emphasize emptiness in one passage and existence in another; an approach distinguished by wording, since the same doctrine may be expressed through entirely different phrasing; and an approach distinguished by the difficulty of a given passage's own meaning, since some portions of the teaching require considerably more explanation than others.

Chinese Source

復次，如來教有三種。一、長時教，二、無間教，三、不重說教。復次，如來無量法教，皆由三種理趣。一、由義差別理趣，二、由文差別理趣，三、由難釋差別理趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha's teaching is of three kinds: the gradual teaching, the uninterrupted teaching, and the teaching never repeated. The Buddha's boundless teaching, moreover, is expressed through three underlying approaches: an approach distinguished by doctrine, one distinguished by wording, and one distinguished by the difficulty of a given passage's own meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Determination's own considerably more elaborate account of the same underlying territory, six gateways of principle and, more expansively still, sixteen distinct underlying intentions said to run throughout the Buddha's own teaching, together with a further passage describing two conditions for genuinely entering that teaching, penetrating it through the wisdom that abides in things as they are, and settling it through the wisdom that directly realizes the truth, this brief threefold structure standing here as a compact summary of considerably more extensive material found elsewhere in the treatise.

7. Section: Five Antidotes for the Journey from Birth and Death to Nirvana

A sixth topic then opens: an extended image of a traveler on the road, offered here for the five antidotes a practitioner needs on the journey from birth and death to nirvana. Just as a traveler shields against blazing sunlight with a parasol, a practitioner counters wrong view and wrong wisdom with the correct view gained through the Buddha's own teaching. Just as a traveler counters bandits and danger along a difficult road with weapons, a practitioner counters the risk of falling into a lower rebirth by keeping the precepts. Just as a traveler crosses a river by boat or by bridge, a practitioner crosses the flooding waters of craving by relying on the wisdom of prajñā, the only vessel capable of carrying anyone across an ocean of suffering and endless waves of attachment. Just as a traveler relieves thirst brought on by a long, sun-scorched road with water, a practitioner counters a scattered mind with the water of concentration. And just as a traveler relieves bodily fatigue through massage, a practitioner counters the coarseness that settles into body and mind with the pliancy that concentration itself brings.

Chinese Source

復次，夫涉道者，須五對治。一、炎光對治，二、艱險對治，三、江河對治，四、枯竭對治，五、身勞對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

A traveler on the road needs five antidotes: against blazing sunlight, against danger, against a river to cross, against thirst, and against bodily fatigue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this traveler's-road image as a genuinely vivid way of mapping precepts, concentration, and wisdom onto a journey's own ordinary hazards, correct view shielding against wrong belief exactly as a parasol shields against the sun, and prajñā ferrying a practitioner across craving's own flood exactly as a boat ferries a traveler across a river too wide to wade.

8. Section: Two Marks of Precept-Keeping That Lead to a Favorable Rebirth

A seventh topic then opens: two marks of precept-keeping capable of leading a practitioner to a favorable rebirth among humans or gods. The first, receiving the precepts, means first taking on precepts at all, whether the five precepts or the bodhisattva precepts for a lay practitioner, or the full monastic precepts, together with the bodhisattva precepts' own four grave and forty-eight lesser rules, for one who has gone forth. And the second, keeping the precepts without any gap, means genuinely guarding those same precepts once received, free of any violation. Receiving the precepts and then keeping them purely are together the two paths that lead a practitioner toward a favorable rebirth, and this same treatise's own fascicle 70 already described four further ways of observing exactly how well a given practitioner has actually kept them, through shared dwelling, through hardship, through ordinary daily affairs, and through discussion and skillful discernment.

Chinese Source

復次，尸羅有二種相，能生善趣。一、攝受尸羅，二、不闕尸羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precept-keeping bears two marks capable of leading a practitioner to a favorable rebirth: receiving the precepts, and keeping them without any gap.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references fascicle 70's own fourfold set of criteria for observing precept-keeping, through shared dwelling, hardship, daily affairs, and discussion, as the natural companion to this simpler twofold structure, receiving the precepts and keeping them without gap describing what genuine precept-keeping consists of, and the earlier fourfold set describing how another observer might actually verify it.

9. Section: Three Stages Behind Enjoying Desire in This Very Life

An eighth topic then opens: three specific undertakings involved whenever an ordinary being enjoys the pleasures of desire in this present life, all of which depend on material means. The first is seeking out wealth, the work and effort needed to actually acquire it through one means or another. The second is guarding that wealth once acquired, protecting it from loss or ruin. And the third is indulging in its enjoyment, growing attached to the pleasure that wealth makes possible, the mind defiled by craving in the process.

Chinese Source

復次，諸受欲者，於現法中有三種義。一、追尋財寶，二、守護財寶，三、耽著受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Enjoying the pleasures of desire in this present life involves three undertakings: seeking out wealth, guarding it once acquired, and indulging in its enjoyment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this brief threefold analysis as a plain, unsentimental description of what pursuing worldly pleasure actually entails from beginning to end, seeking, guarding, and indulging together forming a cycle that, left unexamined, simply repeats itself without ever genuinely satisfying the craving that drives it.

10. Section: Coarse and Subtle Decay Across a Lifespan

A ninth topic then opens: the decay of a lifespan considered through two further pairs of distinctions. Considered by its own timescale, decay is twofold: coarse decay, the death that finally arrives once an entire lifespan has run its course, and subtle decay, the moment-to-moment arising and ceasing of body and mind that means every being is, in truth, dying continuously with each passing instant. Considered instead by its own stage, that same decay is likewise twofold: a coarse stage, the visible signs of aging, wrinkled skin, whitened hair, loosening teeth, and clouded vision among them, and a subtle stage, invisible to the naked eye, such as cellular renewal slowing, the senses dulling, and function gradually declining beneath the surface.

Chinese Source

復次，壽命變壞有二種。一、麤變壞，二、細變壞。變壞分位亦有二種。一、麤，二、細。

Ru-Yi Translation

The decay of a lifespan is twofold, considered by timescale: coarse decay, the death that finally arrives, and subtle decay, the moment-to-moment arising and ceasing of body and mind. Considered by stage, decay is likewise twofold: a coarse, visible stage, and a subtle, invisible one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats subtle decay, both the moment-to-moment arising and ceasing of body and mind and its invisible cellular counterpart, as this passage's own quiet reminder that impermanence is never confined to the dramatic, visible changes old age eventually brings, death already fully underway, in a genuine sense, at every single instant of a life still considered young.

11. Section: Swift, Decisive, and Subtle Conduct; Wisdom's Own Unshakeable Excellence

A tenth topic then opens: two virtues belonging to wisdom itself. The first, the virtue of correct conduct, comprises three qualities: swiftness, a wise practitioner's own thoughts and actions turning toward correct conduct without delay, mind and mental factors moving together in ready accord; decisiveness, wisdom itself able to sever doubt and settle correct conduct firmly, never left uncertain; and subtlety, wisdom penetrating deeply enough into the most refined principles that the conduct expressing it becomes correspondingly refined and unobstructed. The second, wisdom's own intrinsic virtue, describes wisdom belonging specifically to the stage of concentration, never retrogressing, transcendent rather than merely worldly, already thoroughly cultivated rather than newly begun, moving through its own proper domain without any hindrance whatsoever, and surpassing every other wholesome root of its own kind as well as every wholesome root belonging to any other kind, cultivation's own wisdom surpassing the wisdom born of mere hearing or reflection, and transcendent wisdom surpassing every merely worldly counterpart.

Chinese Source

正行義德者，謂速疾正行、決定正行、微細正行。自性德者，謂是定地，不可退轉，是出世間，已善修習，於自所行無有罣礙，勝餘一切自類善根，勝於一切他類善根。

Ru-Yi Translation

The virtue of correct conduct comprises swift conduct, decisive conduct, and subtle conduct. Wisdom's own intrinsic virtue belongs to the stage of concentration, never retrogressing, transcendent, already thoroughly cultivated, moving without hindrance through its own domain, and surpassing every wholesome root of its own kind as well as every wholesome root of any other kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Determination's own account of the Buddha-ground's two remaining subtleties, an extremely subtle attachment even to what can genuinely be known and an extremely subtle obstruction together with its own coarse residue, both permanently severed only at the very threshold of buddhahood itself, as showing just how far "subtlety" can be pressed, wisdom's own refinement continuing to deepen all the way to full awakening rather than reaching any earlier ceiling.

12. Section: Three Forms of Attachment the Four Foundations of Mindfulness Counter

An eleventh topic then opens: three forms of attachment an ordinary being typically clings to, each countered through cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness. The first, attachment to one's own support, describes a lingering fondness for the body itself, or for a spouse regarded as a source of comfort, treating either as somehow genuinely pure, countered through mindfulness of the body, contemplating the body's own impurity directly. The second, attachment to material support and veneration, describes a lingering fondness for fame, gain, and the offerings of the faithful, a practitioner accepting an offering without recalling how difficult even a single drop of water is to properly repay, countered through mindfulness of feeling, observing directly that receiving itself is a form of suffering. And the third, attachment to future existence, describes a lingering craving for the next life still to come, countered through mindfulness of mind and mindfulness of mental phenomena together, contemplating mind's own impermanence and every phenomenon's own lack of a self.

Chinese Source

復次，有三顧戀，修四念住能為對治。一、顧戀助伴，二、顧戀利養，三、顧戀後有。

Ru-Yi Translation

An ordinary being clings to three forms of attachment, each countered by cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness: attachment to one's own support, attachment to material support and veneration, and attachment to future existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the traditional reminder recited before accepting an offering, that even three thoughts' worth of unexamined intention can make a single drop of water difficult to properly digest, as this second attachment's own vivid illustration, a donor's generosity never something to be received carelessly, however small.

13. Section: The Mark of Reflective Realization, Unshakeable Even Before the Path Is Seen

A twelfth topic then opens: the differences among six kinds of direct realization, a topic vast enough to require twenty-two separate categories of analysis, beginning with the first of these twenty-two, each kind's own characteristic mark. A practitioner who has genuinely accomplished reflective realization is one who has come to a settled, decisive understanding that every conditioned formation is impermanent, that every conditioned formation is suffering, that every phenomenon is without self, and that nirvana alone is genuine peace, this same decisive understanding, once reached even at the level of an ordinary being still short of the path itself, no longer capable of being shaken by any non-Buddhist renunciant, any Brahmin, any heavenly demon or Brahmā king, or any other influence whatsoever, however severe the hardship or however great the temptation. A practitioner who has genuinely accomplished the realization of faith, whether still an ordinary being or already a noble one, never in this present life or in any life to come falsely claims that some other teacher is the true teacher, that some other teaching is genuinely well spoken, or that some other community practices correctly, this unshakeable faith in the Three Jewels present already at the ordinary level and, at the level of a noble one who has entered the path of seeing, accompanying the four kinds of unbreakable faith, in the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, and the precepts.

Chinese Source

問：思現觀有何相？答：若有成就思現觀者，能決定了諸行無常、一切行苦、諸法無我、涅槃寂靜。住異生位，已能證得如是決定，非諸沙門、若婆羅門、若天魔梵及餘所能如法引奪。問：信現觀有何相？答：若有成就信現觀者，或是異生、或非異生，或於現法及後法中，終不妄稱餘是大師、餘法善說、餘僧正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mark of reflective realization? A practitioner who has accomplished it comes to a settled understanding that every formation is impermanent and suffering, that every phenomenon is without self, and that nirvana alone is peace, an understanding that, once reached even as an ordinary being, no renunciant, Brahmin, demon, Brahmā king, or other influence can shake. What is the mark of the realization of faith? A practitioner who has accomplished it, whether an ordinary being or a noble one, never in this life or any future life falsely claims another teacher, teaching, or community to be genuinely correct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sheer scale of this topic, six kinds of realization examined through twenty-two distinct categories, as itself a sign of how central direct realization is to this whole treatise's own path, everything from an ordinary being's first settled confidence through an arhat's own final attainment mapped out here with unusual precision, beginning with what each kind of realization actually looks like from the outside.

14. Section: Precepts Kept Without Fail, and Seven Marks of Seeing the Truth Directly

A practitioner who has genuinely accomplished the realization of precepts never again deliberately takes the life of even an animal, never again takes what is not given, never again engages in sexual misconduct, never again knowingly speaks falsely, and never again drinks rice wine or any other intoxicant that leads to heedlessness, precept-keeping itself made pure and unshakeable once guided by genuine faith and the wisdom of direct realization. A practitioner who has genuinely accomplished the realization belonging to direct insight into the truth, the non-discriminating wisdom, fundamental and subsequently attained alike, that takes every phenomenon's own inexpressible nature as its object, shows seven further marks: never again acting on any mistaken view, since correct, outflow-free view has already been attained; never again doubting what has already been directly realized; never again growing attached to any of the excellent realms of rebirth still available within the three realms; never again mistaking worldly prosperity, auspicious signs, or even excellent meditative attainment for anything genuinely pure or ultimate; never again committing the karma of slandering śrāvakas, solitary realizers, or the Mahāyāna and its noble ones, since all three vehicles are equally the Buddha's own teaching; never again committing any of the five acts of immediate retribution that lead directly to the most severe rebirths; and, however lacking in diligence, never again undergoing an eighth round of rebirth among humans and gods, the stream-enterer's own path guaranteeing arhatship within, at most, seven further such rounds.

Chinese Source

問：戒現觀有何相？答：若有成就戒現觀者，終不復能乃至故心斷傍生命、不與而取、習欲邪行、知而妄語、飲米等酒諸放逸處。問：現觀智諦現觀有何相？答：若有成就現觀智諦現觀者，終不復能依諸妄見而有所作，於自所證而有疑惑，於諸生處而有貪染，現行世相計為清淨，誹謗聲聞、獨覺、大乘作惡趣業，何況能造害父母等諸無間業，乃至不能生第八有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mark of the realization of precepts? A practitioner who has accomplished it never again deliberately kills, steals, engages in sexual misconduct, speaks falsely, or drinks intoxicants. What is the mark of the realization belonging to direct insight into the truth? A practitioner who has accomplished it never acts on mistaken view, never doubts what has been realized, never grows attached to excellent rebirths, never mistakes worldly prosperity for what is pure, never slanders any of the three vehicles, never commits an act of immediate retribution, and will never undergo an eighth round of rebirth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the sub-commentary's own example of ordinary people hanging auspicious herbs on their doors at the Dragon Boat Festival, believing this wards off evil and brings purity, as a concrete illustration of exactly the kind of mistaken attachment to worldly auspiciousness that a practitioner who has genuinely realized the truth directly no longer falls into.

15. Section: Fearless Before Any Challenge, and Fifteen Marks of the Arhat's Own Final Realization, Closing the First of Twenty-Two Categories

A practitioner who has genuinely accomplished the realization at the edge of direct insight, the subsequently attained wisdom that continues to reflect on the Four Noble Truths once a practitioner has already emerged from the non-discriminating wisdom of direct realization itself, never again feels fear before any challenge or difficult question raised by another, whether a non-Buddhist opponent or a fellow Buddhist, this fearlessness itself the fruit of the settled wisdom such a practitioner has already reached. And a practitioner who has genuinely accomplished the final and complete realization, the arhat's own attainment, never again violates any of five specific precepts, never again fears being questioned about matters the Buddha himself declined to answer, and never again mistakes any feeling of pleasure or pain as something produced by oneself alone, by another alone, by both together, or by neither and with no cause at all. Considered still more broadly, an arhat's own realization is said to bring fifteen further marks altogether: the ten factors of one beyond further learning fully accomplished; complete mastery over which of the noble, heavenly, or Brahmā-like abidings to dwell in at will; the very culmination of severing affliction and attaining wisdom; passage beyond every remaining obstacle into the city of nirvana itself; guarding the four supports of monastic life having fully abandoned what would undermine them; complete freedom from craving; both mind and wisdom fully liberated; constant equanimity toward whatever is seen, heard, smelled, tasted, touched, or known; direct experience that greed, anger, and delusion have all been permanently exhausted; a mind so purified that it becomes a genuine field of merit worthy of the offerings of gods and kings alike; abiding in the nirvana that still retains a physical basis; ultimately entering the nirvana that retains no basis at all; complete and permanent freedom from the five specific violations already named; freedom from mistakenly attributing feeling to any cause whatsoever; and freedom from every remaining fear. Such varied marks together are what should be understood as the characteristic marks belonging to each of the six kinds of direct realization, closing the first of the twenty-two categories through which this whole discussion proceeds.

16. Section: What Reflective Realization, Faith, and Precepts Actually Consist Of

The second of the twenty-two categories then examines each kind of realization's own intrinsic nature. Reflective realization consists, in its essential nature, of the superior wisdom born of reflection belonging to the desire realm, or, more broadly, of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment operating together with that wisdom. The realization of faith consists of a superior, purified confidence, whether the confidence born of hearing and reflection that takes the Three Jewels as its object while still within the world, or the still more excellent confidence born of cultivation that accompanies direct realization of the truth once a practitioner has genuinely gone beyond it, or, more broadly, of the factor of enlightenment specifically concerned with joy operating together with either kind of confidence, this realization of faith thus spanning both the ordinary being still bound to the world and the noble one who has already transcended it. The realization of precepts consists of the seven branches of bodily and verbal conduct a noble one holds dear, entirely free of outflow, since precepts themselves are defined by conduct of body and speech, or, more broadly, of the joy and the eightfold noble path operating together with that conduct.

17. Section: What Direct Insight, Its Own Subsequent Wisdom, and Final Realization Actually Consist Of, Closing the Second Category

The realization belonging to direct insight into the truth consists of the wisdom that takes the inexpressible truth, suchness itself, beyond every word and concept, as its object, whether the fundamental non-discriminating wisdom of the genuine path of seeing or the subsequently attained non-discriminating wisdom that follows it, or, more broadly, of the factors of enlightenment operating together with that wisdom. The realization at the edge of direct insight consists instead of the discriminating, subsequently attained wisdom that takes the truth as expressed through ordinary language and concepts as its object, or, more broadly, of the factors of enlightenment operating together with it. And the final and complete realization consists of the knowledge that affliction has been exhausted and that no further rebirth remains, both the fundamental and the subsequently attained forms of this same knowledge, the very root of affliction's own stilling and suffering's own cessation, or, more broadly, of the factors of enlightenment operating together with it. This closes the second of the twenty-two categories, each kind of realization's own intrinsic nature.

18. Section: Bound to the Three Realms, or Genuinely Free of Them, Closing the Third Category

The third of the twenty-two categories then examines whether each kind of realization remains bound to the desire, form, and formless realms, or stands genuinely free of them. Reflective realization is bound to the desire realm alone, since it does not depend on any level of meditative absorption at all. The realization of faith divides into two parts: the portion belonging to an ordinary being remains bound to all three realms, whether the desire realm, the form realm, or the formless realm, depending on where that same being happens to dwell, while the portion belonging to a noble one, already free of outflow, stands entirely unbound. That same unbound portion of the realization of faith, together with the realization of precepts, the realization belonging to direct insight, and the final and complete realization, are thus four kinds of realization genuinely free of the three realms altogether. And the realization at the edge of direct insight is the one kind spanning both conditions at once, since this subsequently attained wisdom remains bound to the three realms whenever its object is some worldly phenomenon, yet stands entirely unbound whenever its object is suchness itself.

19. Section: Which Level of Absorption Each Realization Rests On, and How the Unconceptualized Arises Amid Thought

The fourth of the twenty-two categories then examines which level of meditative absorption each kind of realization actually depends on, a question earlier traditions answered variously, some counting seven such supports, the four absorptions together with the three lower formless attainments, others counting nine by adding the preparatory access concentration and the intermediate absorption, and this treatise's own broader tradition generally counting eight. Reflective realization alone rests on no absorption whatsoever, arising instead within an ordinary, unconcentrated mind, while the remaining five all arise in dependence on some genuine level of absorption. More specifically, the realization of precepts, the realization belonging to direct insight, and the realization at the edge of direct insight, together with the outflow-free portion of the realization of faith, all arise in dependence on any of five levels, the access concentration together with the four absorptions proper. An objection then follows: if the realization belonging to direct insight is called unconceptualized precisely because it is free of every mark whatsoever, how can it possibly arise in dependence on the first absorption, which still possesses both applied and sustained thought? The answer is that a practitioner enters that same absorption by seeking out and examining suchness itself with all five of the first absorption's own branches genuinely present, and so, although the absorption relied upon still possesses applied and sustained thought during the preparatory stage, the realization actually attained within it is free of every mark and entirely without conceptualization, applied and sustained thought falling away at the very moment suchness itself is genuinely realized.

20. Section: Which Truth Each Realization Takes as Its Object, Closing the Fifth Category

The fifth of the twenty-two categories then examines which truth each kind of realization actually takes as its object, the conventional or the ultimate. Reflective realization takes the conventional truth entirely as its object, as does the portion of the realization of faith belonging to an ordinary being, still bound up with outflow, while that same realization's own portion belonging to a noble one takes the ultimate truth instead. The realization of precepts takes no object at all, since its very nature consists of bodily and verbal conduct rather than of any cognizing wisdom. The realization at the edge of direct insight takes the ultimate truth as expressed through conventional designation as its object, entirely so, as does one portion of the final and complete realization, namely its own subsequently attained, discriminating wisdom. And the realization belonging to direct insight takes the ultimate truth beyond every designation as its object, entirely so, as does the other portion of that same final realization, namely its own fundamental, non-discriminating wisdom, an arhat's own complete realization thus operating in two distinct modes, taking the inexpressible ultimate as its object whenever fundamental wisdom is active and the designated ultimate whenever subsequently attained wisdom is active instead.

21. Section: With Marks or Without, With Conceptualization or Without, Closing Two Parallel Categories

The seventh category then examines whether each kind possesses conceptualization or is free of it, and the answer simply follows the sixth exactly, whatever possesses marks likewise possesses conceptualization, and whatever is free of marks is likewise free of conceptualization, the two distinctions tracking one another precisely across all six kinds of realization.

23. Section: Four Kinds of Antidote and Which Realizations Serve as Each, Closing the Ninth Category

The ninth of the twenty-two categories then examines each kind of realization against four distinct kinds of antidote, the antidote that wears affliction down through weariness, belonging to worldly wholesome paths generally; the antidote that actually severs affliction, consisting of attention that takes suchness itself as its object; the antidote that maintains what severing has already accomplished, belonging to the path of liberation that follows and to subsequently attained wisdom; and the antidote that keeps affliction permanently distant, belonging to the path of further advancement pursued once affliction has already been severed at the path of seeing. Two kinds of realization, reflective realization and the realization of faith, serve only as the wearing-down antidote, since both approach their objects through worldly weariness. One kind, the realization belonging to direct insight, serves as all three of the severing, maintaining, and distancing antidotes together, since it functions as the uninterrupted path that actually severs affliction, then as the path of liberation that follows, then as the path of further advancement beyond. One kind, the realization at the edge of direct insight, serves as both the maintaining and the distancing antidotes, belonging as it does to the path of liberation. And two kinds serve as no antidote at all: the realization of precepts, since its nature is conduct rather than wisdom, and the final and complete realization, since every affliction has already been exhausted and nothing remains for any antidote to counter.

24. Section: Suppressing Affliction's Activity Against Destroying Its Seed, and the One Realization That Genuinely Severs, Closing Two More Categories

The eleventh category then asks which realizations serve as the antidote actually severing the weak, middling, and strong grades of affliction belonging to each of the three realms' own nine levels, and the answer is one alone: the realization belonging to direct insight. 25. Section: Nine Complete Knowings, and the One Realization That Attains Them All

The twelfth of the twenty-two categories then examines which of the nine complete knowings each realization attains, complete knowing here naming the severing itself, the result, by the name of the outflow-free wisdom that caused it. Severing the afflictions abandoned by seeing the truth of cessation within the desire realm establishes the third, ten in all, and severing them within the form and formless realms establishes the fourth, eighteen in all. As for which realization actually attains these nine, one alone attains them all: the realization belonging to direct insight, since every fruit from stream-entry through arhatship requires the non-discriminating wisdom that severs affliction, only the depth and coarseness of what is severed differing from fruit to fruit. 26. Section: Affliction Severed at the Very Moment the Antidote Arises, Closing the Thirteenth Category

The thirteenth of the twenty-two categories then asks a question of timing: when a realization serves as the antidote severing affliction, does it sever after having already arisen, or before arising at all? The answer is that it does not sever before arising, since what has not arisen can sever nothing; and although the text says "having already arisen," this does not mean the severing happens at some later moment. The severing of affliction and the arising of its antidote are entirely simultaneous, perfectly equal in time, and it is only as a conventional way of speaking that we say the antidote arises and then afflictions are severed. Just as darkness vanishes at the very moment light appears, rather than light appearing first and darkness withdrawing afterward, so too on the uninterrupted path the arising of outflow-free wisdom and the severing of affliction occur together in a single moment. A related passage in the Determination clarifies further that what is actually severed is never the manifest entanglement, already relinquished beforehand, but the underlying latent tendency, and that severing belongs strictly to none of the three times, since a past latent tendency has already ceased of itself, a future one has not yet come into being, and a present one will not persist beyond this very instant; rather, through hearing another's teaching and through correct attention within, a mind associated with correct view arises exactly as the mind carrying the latent tendency ceases, the arising of one and the ceasing of the other perfectly equal, and this is how the arising and ceasing of an antidote should be understood.

27. Section: Which Fruits Each Realization Attains, Closing the Fourteenth Category

The fourteenth of the twenty-two categories then examines which of the renunciant's four fruits each realization actually attains. One, the realization belonging to direct insight, attains all four, from stream-entry through arhatship, since the non-discriminating wisdom it consists of is required at the attainment of every one of them. One, the final and complete realization, is what obtains at the moment the renunciant's fruit reaches its own fullness, namely arhatship itself. Of the remaining four, the realization of precepts and the realization at the edge of direct insight serve as companions accompanying the attainment of a given fruit, incidental virtues arising alongside it, while reflective realization and the realization of faith serve as the preliminary practice leading toward any such fruit, a practitioner's settled understanding of the teaching and unshakeable confidence in the Three Jewels both preparing the ground well before any fruit is actually reached.

Of these realizations, which attains how many fruits? One attains all four fruits; one is what obtains when the renunciant's fruit reaches its fullness; and the rest are either companions accompanying the attainment of a fruit or the preliminary practice leading toward it.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold sorting, preliminary practice, companion, attainer of all four fruits, and fullness itself, as a compact summary of the whole topic's own recurring lesson, that among six genuinely valuable realizations only one actually attains the fruits, the others preparing for it, accompanying it, or arising as its completion.

28. Section: Transforming the Faculties and Drawing Forth Superknowledges, Closing Two More Categories

The fifteenth of the twenty-two categories then asks which realizations are able to transform a practitioner's own faculties, and the answer excludes one alone: every kind except the realization of precepts can do so, since precepts consist of bodily and verbal conduct rather than of any inward training of the mind, while the other five all belong to inward cultivation and are therefore able to transform the wholesome faculties of faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. The sixteenth category then asks which realizations are able to draw forth the superknowledges and other excellent virtues, and the answer follows exactly the same pattern: every kind except the realization of precepts can do so, the remaining five all capable of drawing forth the superknowledges, the four unobstructed eloquences, the concentration of wish-fulfilling knowledge, and virtues of that order.

Chinese Source

問：是諸現觀，幾能轉根？答：除一，餘一切。問：是諸現觀，幾能引發諸神通等殊勝功德？答：除一，餘一切。

Ru-Yi Translation

Of these realizations, how many can transform the faculties? All except one. Of these realizations, how many can draw forth the superknowledges and other excellent virtues? All except one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the realization of precepts is excluded from both categories for the same reason it was excluded earlier from having any object and from serving as any antidote, its nature being conduct rather than wisdom, a consistency worth noticing across the whole twenty-two-category analysis rather than a series of unrelated exclusions.

29. Section: What Reflection, Faith, and Precepts Each Accomplish

The seventeenth of the twenty-two categories then examines what each kind of realization actually accomplishes. Reflective realization accomplishes six things: generating the pure, wholesome qualities belonging to correct practice, such as precepts, concentration, wisdom, generosity, and cultivation; generating blameless joy, since a mind possessing this realization is free of greed, anger, and delusion; resolving every doubt, since such a practitioner holds a settled conviction regarding impermanence, non-self, and the peace of nirvana; entering into the virtue of cultivation, since a settled understanding of the teaching becomes the very basis for the seven attentions that sever affliction; drawing forth the remaining kinds of realization; and leading toward every favorable destiny. The realization of faith, through the sheer strength of its own aspiration, accomplishes three: generating an unshakeable conviction regarding the Three Jewels that no outside teaching and no circumstance whatsoever can move; purifying correct practice, whether the four factors of stream-entry, drawing near a genuine teacher, hearing the true teaching, attending correctly, and practicing in accordance with the Dharma, or the further set comprising unagitated faculties, undertaking the training, compassion toward beings, and diligent offering to the Three Jewels; and, in the portion still bound up with outflow, leading toward a favorable destiny. And the realization of precepts accomplishes one thing: liberating a practitioner from the sufferings of the lower destinies entirely, since one who possesses the precepts a noble one holds dear simply does not commit the deeds that lead there.

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the realization of faith's third accomplishment is deliberately qualified as belonging to only one portion of it, precisely to distinguish the outflow-free faith of a noble one, which leads beyond every destiny whatsoever, from the ordinary faith still bound up with outflow, whose reward remains a favorable rebirth within the round.

30. Section: What Direct Insight, Its Subsequent Wisdom, and Final Realization Each Accomplish, Closing the Seventeenth Category

The realization belonging to direct insight accomplishes four things: attaining every one of the renunciant's four fruits, since it is the non-discriminating wisdom that severs affliction at each; drawing forth the purity of every virtue, since a wisdom free of discriminating permanence from annihilation, being from non-being, or high from low leaves every practice untainted by attachment; drawing forth the remaining kinds of realization, since a practitioner who attains it simultaneously possesses outflow-free faith and precepts and goes on to draw forth edge-realization and final realization alike; and, within a favorable destiny, contributing toward a luminous, purified fruit and its own ripening. The realization at the edge of direct insight accomplishes three: answering questions skillfully regarding every truth as conventionally expressed, resolving whatever doubts an inquirer brings; bringing about a swift and penetrating wisdom into the nature of all phenomena; and drawing forth the further realizations that follow it. And the final and complete realization accomplishes three: drawing forth the foremost ease of dwelling in this very life, whether through any of the four absorptions, the formless attainments accompanied by equanimity, or the attainment of cessation itself; liberating a practitioner from the entire great suffering of birth and death, since with the knowledge that affliction is exhausted and no further rebirth remains there is no further existence to undergo; and sustaining the final body, this present life's own result carrying no successor whatsoever, exactly as the scriptures describe an arhat: birth is exhausted, the holy life is established, what needed doing is done, and no further existence awaits. This closes the seventeenth of the twenty-two categories.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the final realization's third accomplishment, sustaining the final body, as this whole category's quiet culmination, an arhat's remaining lifetime carried forward without any successor waiting behind it, the familiar scriptural formula of birth exhausted and nothing further to undergo naming exactly this condition, closing the seventeenth of the twenty-two categories.

31. Section: Countless Kinds of Reflection, Countless Objects of Faith

The eighteenth of the twenty-two categories then examines how many kinds each realization comprises. Reflective realization comprises countless kinds, since the whole of the Buddha's teaching serves as its object: reflection on discourses, on verses repeating what prose has already said, on prophetic explanations, and so on through the remaining limbs of the traditional twelvefold classification of scripture, including stanzas, spontaneous utterances, occasions, parables, past events, past lives of the Buddha, extensive teachings, marvels, and doctrinal expositions; reflection on suffering, on its origin, its cessation, and the path; reflection on suchness, on the ultimate limit, and on the realm of phenomena; reflection on the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields; reflection on the śrāvaka vehicle and on the Mahāyāna. Such varied kinds together make the distinctions of reflection genuinely countless. The realization of faith likewise comprises countless kinds, since a practitioner may correctly recollect the countless perfectly awakened buddhas of the past together with their teaching and their community, and likewise those of the future and of the present, and may further correctly recollect all the tathāgatas of this world-system together with those of countless other world-systems throughout the ten directions, along with their teaching and their community, the number of distinct kinds of this realization matching exactly the number of such recollections a practitioner may hold.

32. Section: Countless Kinds of Precepts and of the Three Higher Realizations, Closing the Eighteenth Category

The realization of precepts likewise comprises countless kinds, distinguished in two ways. Considered by course of action, it varies according to which of the ten unwholesome courses of action a practitioner refrains from, three of body, four of speech, and three of mind, killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and the rest, each admitting many further distinctions of its own. Considered by the continuum in which it arises, it likewise varies without limit, since the precepts a noble one holds dear arise within the continuum of a stream-enterer, of an arhat, of a solitary realizer, of a bodhisattva, or of a tathāgata alike, each continuum differing from every other. The realization belonging to direct insight likewise comprises countless kinds, since it is accomplished through the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment in all their variety, the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right exertions, the four bases of supernatural power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of enlightenment, and the eightfold path. And as with the realization belonging to direct insight, so too the realization at the edge of direct insight and the final and complete realization should be understood to comprise countless kinds as well. All six kinds of realization are thus shown to be countless in their own varieties, closing the eighteenth of the twenty-two categories.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second way of counting the realization of precepts, by the continuum in which it arises, as quietly extending this whole analysis well beyond the śrāvaka path it began with, since the precepts a noble one holds dear are said here to arise in a bodhisattva's and a tathāgata's continuum just as genuinely as in a stream-enterer's, closing the eighteenth of the twenty-two categories.

33. Section: Whether These Very Names Capture What They Name, Closing the Nineteenth Category

The nineteenth of the twenty-two categories then turns a question back upon the analysis itself: since these six realizations have been established by just such names and just such words, should those names be said to constitute their actual nature, or should they not? The answer is twofold. Speaking conventionally, the names may indeed be said to constitute their nature, since conventional designation is precisely how anything is distinguished and discussed at all. But speaking from the standpoint of ultimate truth, the names should not be said to constitute their nature. Why not? Because the actual meaning of every phenomenon whatsoever is, by its very nature, inexpressible. Every phenomenon's own genuine characteristic lies beyond words, and a practitioner who takes conventional designations for the reality itself remains permanently confined within conventional truth, never able to pass beyond mere naming, and therefore never able to actually touch the emptiness and true character of things at all.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Since these realizations are established by just such names and just such words, should those names be said to constitute their nature, or not? Conventionally speaking, they should be said to constitute their nature; from the standpoint of ultimate truth, they should not. Why? Because the meaning of every phenomenon is, by its very nature, inexpressible.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this brief exchange as the whole twenty-two-category analysis turning honestly upon itself, an enormously detailed taxonomy pausing to acknowledge that none of its own careful names actually reaches the reality being named, a warning aimed squarely at any student who might mistake mastery of the categories for the realization they describe.

34. Section: What Causes Each Realization and What Each Produces, Closing the Twentieth Category

The twentieth of the twenty-two categories then examines what causes each realization and what each in turn produces. Reflective realization arises from five causes: a buddha appearing in the world, drawing near a genuine teacher, hearing the true teaching, one's own continuum having genuinely matured so that the wholesome faculties of faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom make one a fit vessel for the path, and correct attention; and its result is precisely the six accomplishments already described. As with reflective realization, so too every other realization arises from these same five causes and produces its own accomplishments as its result. Certain differences, however, should be noted. The realization of faith additionally takes the other realizations themselves as its causes, understanding, practice, and realization all contributing to deepen it, and the realization of precepts is the same in this respect. The realization belonging to direct insight additionally takes reflective realization as a cause, together with the wholesome roots conducive to penetration, warmth, summit, forbearance, and the highest worldly quality, and further takes one's own spiritual lineage as a cause, since the realization attained by one of śrāvaka lineage differs from that attained by one of bodhisattva lineage. And the realization at the edge of direct insight additionally takes the realization belonging to direct insight as its cause, since subsequently attained wisdom can only follow from fundamental wisdom, as does the final and complete realization, which likewise requires direct insight as the foundation from which it is gradually reached.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the mutual causation quietly acknowledged here, faith and precepts each taking the other realizations as their own causes, so that these six are not a simple sequence running one direction only but a genuinely interdependent set, each deepening the others even as the overall movement runs from reflection toward final realization.

35. Section: Which Attentions Subsume Which Realizations, Closing the Twenty-First Category

The twenty-first of the twenty-two categories then compares the six realizations against the seven attentions, asking which subsumes which. Two realizations are subsumed by no attention at all: reflective realization, since it consists of the wisdom born of reflection and has not yet reached the wisdom born of cultivation, and the realization of precepts, since its nature is bodily and verbal form rather than mind. One, the realization of faith, is subsumed by the attention of delighted absorption, since confidence in the Three Jewels naturally brings delight in severing affliction and in cultivating calming and insight. One, the realization at the edge of direct insight, is subsumed by both the attention of delighted absorption and the attention of investigation, since a practitioner possessing subsequently attained wisdom both delights in that same practice and examines whether affliction has genuinely been exhausted. One, the realization belonging to direct insight, is subsumed by three, the attention of seclusion, the attention of delighted absorption, and the attention of the culmination of practice, since across the desire realm's own nine grades of affliction the first of these severs the three coarsest, the second the three middling, and the third the three subtlest. And one, the final and complete realization, is subsumed by the attention that is the fruit of practice's culmination, every task of practice having been fully accomplished. The remaining two attentions, the attention discerning characteristics and the attention of firm conviction, should be understood as outflows of realization rather than as realization itself, merely inclining a practitioner toward it without yet entering it. A related question then asks where the knowledge of the boundless and the wholesome roots conducive to penetration belong, the former naming the conviction a practitioner develops upon understanding that everything within beginningless birth and death is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self; and the answer is that neither is subsumed by any realization at all, both likewise being outflows of realization, cultivated before the truth has actually been seen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the recurring verdict "an outflow of realization rather than realization itself" as this category's most useful distinction for a practitioner, since attentions genuinely cultivated and wholesome roots genuinely developed may still fall short of realization proper, valuable as preparation without yet being the thing prepared for.

36. Section: Four-Line Analysis and the Art of Careful Questioning, Closing All Twenty-Two Categories

The twenty-second and final category then examines these realizations through the formal patterns of analysis. Asking whether every reflective realization is also a realization of faith, and whether every realization of faith is also reflective, the answer must take four lines. There is reflective realization that is not a realization of faith, namely every settled reflection whose object is something other than the Three Jewels. There is a realization of faith that is not reflective, namely the confidence in the Three Jewels born of hearing or of cultivation rather than of reflection. There is what is both, namely settled reflection whose object is precisely the Three Jewels. And whatever falls outside all three of these constitutes the fourth line. By this same reasoning, the remaining realizations should likewise be analyzed through four lines each, and commentators accordingly work through every pairing, noting for instance that faith and precepts never subsume one another since one is mental and the other material, that faith is a realization of direct insight only when non-discriminating wisdom happens to accompany it, and that the realization at the edge and the final realization never subsume one another since one is incomplete and the other complete. Beyond four-line analysis, countless further patterns of careful questioning apply as well, the sustained series of paired questions, the answer affirming only the earlier term where a narrower category is asked against a wider one, the answer affirming only the later term where a wider is asked against a narrower, the four-line answer proper where two categories genuinely overlap without either containing the other, together with the empty line where a question rests on no real basis at all, the wholly affirming answer where two terms coincide exactly, and further two-line, three-line, and question-blocking answers used more rarely; all of which, according with the reasoning proper to the śrāvaka vehicle, should be understood as they are set out there. With this final category, all twenty-two categories examining the six kinds of direct realization are brought to a close.

There is faith that is not reflective: confidence in the Three Jewels born of hearing or cultivation. There is what is both: settled reflection on the Three Jewels. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi surveys these questioning patterns at length with worked examples, noting for instance that all greed is craving while not all craving is greed, since wholesome aspiration toward the Dharma is craving without being defiled, and that all suffering is impermanent while not all that is impermanent is suffering, since the path itself is impermanent yet not suffering. He treats this whole apparatus as the Indian tradition's own disciplined method for making a doctrine's exact boundaries visible, a fitting close for an analysis that has examined six realizations from twenty-two distinct angles.

37. Section: Four Defilements That Give Stinginess Its Footing

A thirteenth topic then opens: stinginess and defilement taken together as a single term, since eight specific defilements stain a being's own mind-continuum and serve as the very footing on which stinginess stands. The first is never having grown accustomed to giving, so that a practitioner in this present life treasures wealth and food, unwilling to let others make use of either. The second is clinging so heavily to one's own body and life that future lives are entirely disregarded, a person concerned only with looking after themselves now and never considering that generosity itself brings prosperity later. The third is constantly dwelling together with the stingy and going along with them, whether through a defiled agreement with their attitude or through a merely neutral deference offered because one is a subordinate, a junior, or in a weaker position, this same company gradually breeding stinginess in turn, exactly as keeping company with the genuinely generous gradually breeds delight in giving. And the fourth is seeing no excellent virtue in whoever would receive the gift, and grading fields of merit against one another, a practitioner weighing up how much virtue a recipient supposedly possesses, concluding they are not a worthy field, and so never generating the delight in giving that would actually produce the gift.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Stinginess and defilement together are called the defilement of stinginess, since eight defilements stain the mind-continuum and serve as stinginess's own footing. What are the eight? First, never having grown accustomed to giving, treasuring wealth and food in this present life. Second, clinging so heavily to one's own body and life that future lives are disregarded. Third, constantly dwelling with the stingy and going along with them. Fourth, seeing no excellent virtue in the field one would give to, and grading fields of merit against one another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a practical conclusion from the third defilement in particular, that since stinginess is caught from the company one keeps, exactly as generosity is, the choice of fellow practitioners is itself a genuine factor in whether a practitioner's own giving deepens or quietly withers.

38. Section: Four More Defilements, from Uncultivated Compassion to Giving with Strings Attached, Closing the Thirteenth Topic

The fifth defilement is never having cultivated compassion, so that a practitioner encountering someone genuinely pitiable feels no impulse to give at all, or else judges that the person possesses no particular virtue and even looks for faults in them, wondering why anyone of roughly equal standing should be supported. The sixth is repeatedly dwelling on the thought that wealth is hard to accumulate, reasoning that money is earned only through great difficulty and should therefore not be parted with lightly, this thought breeding both laziness, an unwillingness to give at all, and slackness, a giving once undertaken and then never continued. The seventh is clinging to wrong view, together with defilement mixed into whatever giving is done, whether the mistaken view, attributed to certain non-Buddhist practitioners who observed in meditation that a donor failed to be reborn in the heavens and concluded from that partial glimpse that giving bears no fruit, that there is no point to generosity, to supporting one's parents, or to making offerings, or the more ordinary defilement of a giving performed for the sake of reputation, praise, and acclaim rather than freely. And the eighth is giving in the hope of gaining greater wealth and dedicating the merit toward exactly that, a motive still bound up with greed and therefore impure however generous the act may look from outside. These eight together are the defilements on which stinginess takes its footing.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, never having cultivated compassion, and seeing no excellent virtue in the one before you. Sixth, repeatedly dwelling on the thought that wealth is hard to accumulate, thereby breeding laziness and slackness. Seventh, clinging to wrong view, and defilement mixed into whatever giving is done. Eighth, giving in the hope of gaining wealth, and dedicating the merit toward exactly that.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the seventh defilement's wrong view arose from a genuine but partial observation, meditators seeing that a particular donor was not reborn in the heavens and mistaking the absence of an immediate result for the absence of any result at all, a caution that even direct meditative perception can be misread when its scope is narrower than the conclusion drawn from it, closing the eight defilements on which stinginess takes its footing.

39. Section: Five Kinds of Well-Tempered Conduct

A fourteenth topic then opens: five kinds of conduct called well-tempered. The first, conduct producing wealth, consists of meritorious activity whose nature is giving, since generosity by its very nature produces prosperity whether or not the giver has any such result in mind. The second, conduct producing a favorable destiny, consists of meritorious activity whose nature is precepts, since keeping the precepts by its very nature produces rebirth among humans or gods. The third, conduct producing freedom from suffering, consists of meritorious activity whose nature is cultivation, chiefly the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, since one who cultivates these makes no enemies and therefore encounters none of the distress that living surrounded by hostility inevitably brings. The fourth, conduct producing one's own benefit, consists of the path of the śrāvaka and the solitary realizer, accomplishing the genuine benefit of nirvana for oneself. And the fifth, conduct producing others' benefit, consists of the bodhisattva path, genuinely bringing others to that same benefit.

40. Section: Five Perceptions That Produce Them, Closing the Fourteen Categories of Meaning

To attain these five kinds of well-tempered conduct, five corresponding perceptions should be cultivated, each producing its own kind of conduct in turn. Cultivating the perception of impurity toward the objects of desire counters stinginess and frees a practitioner to give, since one who lives simply and takes no delight in sensual objects has a surplus to share, and this meritorious giving produces the conduct that yields wealth. Cultivating the perception that one's own body and life will swiftly perish, recalling that death may arrive at any moment and fearing rebirth in the lower destinies, moves a practitioner to keep the precepts in order to obtain a human rebirth and continue practicing, and this meritorious precept-keeping produces the conduct that yields a favorable destiny. Cultivating the perception that the desire realm itself is fearsome moves a practitioner to cultivate the unshakeable karma of meditative absorption and so escape returning to the desire realm's suffering, and this meritorious cultivation produces the conduct that yields freedom from suffering. Cultivating the perception of impermanence toward all conditioned formations carries a practitioner toward nirvana and beyond the three realms, and this practice along the śrāvaka and solitary-realizer path produces the conduct that yields one's own benefit. And cultivating the perception of compassion toward all beings, loving others more than oneself as the scriptures put it, sets a practitioner on the bodhisattva path, which produces the conduct that yields others' benefit. This closes the fourteen categories of meaning taken up since the opening of this fascicle.

41. Section: Nine Wholesome Dharmas, from Aspiration as Root to Renunciation as the Final Edge

A further topic then opens: an explanation of the Ghee-Simile Sūtra, so called because that scripture traces five flavors from milk through to ghee, ghee itself standing for the great nirvana. Nine wholesome dharmas are set out there. Aspiration toward the wholesome is the root of every wholesome quality, since it is aspiration that generates any hope for goodness at all. Correct attention is what such qualities are born from, since attending correctly repeatedly rouses whatever wholesome seed should arise. Contact is what gathers them together, since contact meeting an excellent object alongside the other mental factors is what actually brings a wholesome quality into play. Feeling is what draws them in, since an excellent object received into the mind is what generates the wholesome quality. Mindfulness is their master, since it keeps whatever is wholesome clearly held and never forgotten. Concentration is their chief, since concentration is what settles and clarifies the mind. Wisdom is their highest, since wisdom discerns the wholesome from the unwholesome. Liberation is their firmness, since it stills affliction's own binding entanglement. And renunciation is their final edge, since escaping every obstruction is what brings the noble path to its own fullness. Among these nine, an earlier member does not guarantee a later one, while a later one necessarily presupposes every earlier one.

42. Section: Knowing Provisional Names and Knowing What Words and Phrases Gather In

The sūtra's own explanation then turns to what a practitioner should know, beginning with how to understand the twelvefold classification of scripture, from discourses through doctrinal expositions, through five specific aspects: knowing their provisional names, knowing what they gather in, knowing their sequence, knowing whose teaching they are, and knowing their basis. Knowing provisional names means recognizing that the various teachings, the four truths, the twelve links, the six perfections and the rest, are all designations conceptually established, a point the scriptures press by asking whether what is imagined has any real nature at all, whether what arises dependently amounts to anything beyond designation and expression, and whether the perfectly accomplished nature's own emptiness of self is itself form or not form, the conclusion being that the imagined is definitively non-existent, the dependent exists only as designation and expression, and the perfectly accomplished nature's emptiness of self alone genuinely exists. Knowing what they gather in means recognizing that the teachings are expounded through bodies of names, of phrases, and of syllables, names conveying a thing's own nature, phrases conveying its distinctions, and syllables serving as the basis for both, this triad together conveying the entire teaching, so that knowing names, phrases, and syllables, and the meanings they convey, is what allows a practitioner to genuinely take in what is being taught.

43. Section: Knowing Sequence, Whose Teaching, and Why It Was Taught, Closing the Five Aspects

Knowing sequence means recognizing the order running from one teaching to another and from one phrase to the next, whether the order of exposition, as when suffering is explained first among the four truths and form before feeling and the rest among the five aggregates; the order of establishing a position, as when a thesis is stated first, its reason next, and its illustration last; or the order of completion, as when the cause of practice is taught before its fruit, and the path of accumulation before the final path. Knowing whose teaching it is means recognizing that a given teaching was spoken by the Tathāgata himself, or by a disciple, or by a layperson, or by one who has gone forth. And knowing its basis means recognizing the motive behind a teaching's establishment, that some teachings, the śrāvaka and solitary-realizer paths, were taught for one's own benefit, that others, the bodhisattva path, were taught for the benefit of others, and that still others were taught so that gods and humans might obtain benefit and ease. These five aspects together are how the twelvefold classification of scripture is understood, and only a practitioner who knows all five can genuinely reflect on and discern a teaching's wording and meaning, its specific and shared characteristics, and the full extent and true nature of what it describes.

44. Section: Knowing Why a Teaching Was Given, How Its Phrasing Varies, and In What Order It Unfolds

Understanding the meaning a teaching actually conveys likewise proceeds through five aspects. Knowing the occasion means recognizing why the Buddha spoke as he did, since beings differ in capacity: some needed a display of the Tathāgata's own marvelous power in order to generate confidence at all, others needed direct instruction in precepts, concentration, and wisdom, others needed encouragement and praise while still practicing, and others, having genuinely accomplished something, needed to be congratulated and rejoiced with, so that recognizing which of these a given passage answers to is itself knowing the occasion. Knowing the variation of phrasing means recognizing that the same teaching may be presented through different approaches, described through different aspects, and explained through different terms, as when a single reality is called a person, a youth, or one who repeatedly takes up existence, all such variations being what a student of the Dharma should learn to see through. And knowing sequence means recognizing three orders at once: the order of exposition, as when suffering is explained first among the four truths and ignorance before old age and death among the twelve links; the order of establishing a position, thesis first, then reason, then illustration; and the order of completion, cause of practice before its fruit, as when the ten stages of faith open a course that only reaches fullness at the completion of the ten grounds.

45. Section: Four Kinds of Reasoning and the Art of Summarizing, Closing the Ghee-Simile Sūtra

Knowing the reasonings means recognizing four in particular. The reasoning of dependence covers both how a thing arises and how it is designated: the five aggregates arise only in dependence on their own causes and conditions, and are designated as form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness only in dependence on bodies of names, phrases, and syllables. The reasoning of establishment covers how a claim is actually verified, through direct perception, through inference, and through authoritative teaching together, so that a practitioner who has been told the aggregates are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self must still confirm this personally rather than resting on scripture alone. The reasoning of function covers how each phenomenon does its own particular work, the eye seeing form, the ear hearing sound, and so on through the mind knowing phenomena. And the reasoning of natural law covers what simply holds whether or not a tathāgata appears in the world, earth being hard by nature and water wet, matters about which it is pointless to keep asking why, since forcing such questions yields nothing a practitioner can actually use. Knowing the summarized meaning, finally, means recognizing which topic a stretch of teaching actually belongs to, whether it concerns the aggregates, the elements, the sense-fields, dependent origination, what is and is not possible, the truths, the foundations of mindfulness and the rest through the eightfold noble path, karma, affliction, or the higher trainings in precepts, concentration, and wisdom, since only a practitioner able to summarize what has been read at length can be said to have genuinely understood it. These five aspects together close the explanation of the Ghee-Simile Sūtra.

46. Section: Knowing the Right Time to Still the Mind, to Rouse It, and to Simply Let It Rest

A further topic then opens: dwelling with correct knowledge, explained through knowing the time, knowing the measure, knowing the assembly, and knowing when to go and when to remain silent. Knowing the time proceeds through five aspects. The first is recognizing the defilement presently manifesting, chiefly dullness and agitation. The second is recognizing the defilement about to manifest. The third is recognizing the undefiled condition in which both have already been cleared. The fourth is arousing the corresponding antidote as the occasion requires, applying calming when the mind is agitated or about to become so, insight when it is sinking or about to sink, and rousing when it needs lifting. And the fifth is applying the antidote of equanimity once the mind has genuinely been freed from both, calming and rousing now in balance. The Śrāvaka Ground describes this same skill as timely practice, cultivating calming at the times for calming, insight at the times for insight, rousing at the times for rousing, and equanimity at the times for equanimity, a practitioner knowing not only each of these four practices and its own characteristic mark but also, and just as importantly, exactly when each is called for.

47. Section: Knowing the Measure in What Sustains the Body and in Effort Itself

Knowing the measure applies to what is eaten, drunk, and chewed, and on through every activity down to the exertion of effort itself. In brief, two things here accord with the severing of affliction. The first is what accords through sustaining: eating, drinking, and chewing in right measure allows food to be properly digested, keeps the body healthy and largely free of illness, and thereby leaves a practitioner genuinely capable of sustained practice. The second is what accords through effort: knowing the right measure of exertion, at what times, in what places, and toward what tasks effort should be applied, so that one walks when walking is called for and rests from sleep when resting is called for, thereby countering both the excessive dullness and the excessive agitation that undisciplined effort produces. Accordingly, knowing the measure in what sustains the body means knowing the right measure in all that is eaten, drunk, chewed, and tasted, while knowing the measure in effort means knowing the right measure while walking, standing, sitting, lying down, practicing wakefulness, speaking, remaining silent, and even in taking brief rest to relieve fatigue.

48. Section: When, From Whom, What, and How Much a Monastic May Rightly Receive

Knowing the measure in what is received answers four questions: at what time one should receive, from where one should receive, what one should receive, and up to what limit one should receive. As to time, food should be received during the day's first portion, from sunrise until noon, which is also when it is to be consumed. As to place, five kinds of household are excluded from an alms round: the butcher's, where the intent to kill runs strong and the sight of it wounds compassion and undermines the very root of goodness; the wine-seller's, since wine is the occasion of transgression and breeds faults; the brothel, whose conduct is unclean, whose reputation is disreputable, and whose sensual associations obstruct the path at its root; the royal household, since palaces and the quarters of the powerful are strictly guarded and not to be intruded upon, their modern equivalents being no less entangled in dispute; and the households of outcastes and of those who slaughter dogs or preside over executions, whose conduct of body, speech, and mind is unwholesome. As to what may be received, only pure food, obtained from a pure source and permitted by the Tathāgata, wine being excluded and, on the Mahāyāna's own reckoning, meat as well. And as to the limit, one receives knowing the measure, neither so much as to harm oneself through excess nor so little as to leave oneself weak and unable to sustain practice, and never so as to cause harm to another.

49. Section: Eating Without Hunger or Imbalance, and Effort Neither Sunk Nor Overstrained, Closing Knowing the Measure

Knowing the measure in what is actually consumed likewise answers four questions. As to time, the day's first portion, as already stated. As to place, a suitable setting, whether the refectory of a monastery or within a village, avoiding wherever is too noisy or unclean. As to what may be consumed, the same pure food already described, nothing being consumed that is itself unclean or that leaves the mind unclean. And as to the limit, one consumes knowing the measure, so as not to be left hungry and distressed by having taken too little, not to consume anything that fails to support the severing of affliction, such as food that stirs agitation or sensual desire, and not to throw the body's own elements out of balance through food too heating, too salty, or too strong. Knowing the measure in effort likewise answers four questions: as to time, exerting oneself in walking meditation when walking is called for, and on through resting from sleep when that is called for; as to place, a quiet forest, a monastery, a secluded inner court, or a walking path; as to activity, walking meditation, monastic duties, sitting practice, the yoga of wakefulness, and even relieving fatigue through brief rest; and as to limit, exerting effort in a measure suited to one's own condition, so that the effort itself produces neither excessive dullness nor excessive agitation. 50. Section: Knowing an Assembly, Whether of Nobles, Householders, or Fellow Renunciants

Knowing the assembly means recognizing what kind of gathering one is among. Regarding an assembly of the noble class, it means knowing their names, their lineage, their standing, what they consume, and on through the extent of their lifespan; and the same holds for assemblies of brahmins, of elders, and of householders alike, a practitioner being expected to understand others as clearly as they understand themselves. Regarding an assembly of renunciants, it means knowing their names in religion, whether a given member is young, senior, or venerable with age and virtue, whether one specializes in upholding the discourses, the discipline, or the treatises, and whether one is a teacher of the scriptures or a practitioner of yoga, together with each one's particular strength. The Śrāvaka Ground distinguishes three kinds of yoga practitioner in this connection: one just beginning the practice, one already habituated to it, and one who has already passed beyond mere attention, having genuinely accomplished absorption or realized the noble path. The Buddha himself listed knowing the assembly among seven qualities by which a monastic attains the joy of the noble ones and moves directly toward the exhaustion of outflow, a monastic who cannot tell which kind of assembly is before them, nor what conduct suits the occasion, being said not to know the assembly at all.

51. Section: Nine Reasons a Monastic May Rightly Call on Lay Households

Knowing when to go and when to remain silent means understanding both why one should visit a lay household or speak or stay silent, and how one should do so. As to why, nine reasons are given: to seek the requisites of life; to bring those without confidence to confidence and to deepen the confidence of those who already have it; to console those suffering serious illness or heavy affliction; to relieve those weighed down by grief and sorrow; to reconcile parties who have fallen into mutual hostility; to accommodate something another needs done; to respond to another's own request; to accompany one's preceptor, instructor, or fellow practitioners of the holy life; or to attend to business belonging to the monastic community. For these reasons a monastic may rightly go, and speak or remain silent accordingly.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes how much of this list concerns others rather than the monastic's own needs, only the first reason being about obtaining requisites at all, the remaining eight covering consolation, reconciliation, accompaniment, and service, so that a monastic's contact with lay households is framed here overwhelmingly as something undertaken for others' sake.

52. Section: How to Walk There, How to Stand at the Door, and How to Take a Seat

As to how one should go, the Śrāvaka Ground has already described it: while travelling or already on the way, a monastic encounters all manner of forms, some of which should be looked at and some of which should not. Aging, sickness, and death may rightly be observed, since seeing them rouses mindfulness of impermanence and spurs diligence; but forms likely to stir desire should not be looked at, and toward these one draws in the eye and guards the sense faculties well, while toward those that should be observed one abides in mindfulness and observes correctly. As to how one should stand, upon arriving a monastic does not simply walk in, but announces themselves at the door or arranges the visit beforehand, stands near the inner door having subdued any conceit, then enters unhurriedly and without suspicion, standing composedly at the place of meeting, offering a word of greeting first, with an open and smiling face, refraining from any frown or grimace even if the household is noisy or unclean, and only then speaking words of kindness. As to how one should sit, a monastic takes whatever seat is proper and permitted, a large or small corded seat, a mat of grass and leaves, spread bedding, or a sitting cloth, and sits with correct and dignified bearing, cross-legged or upright, never dropping carelessly into a seat without first checking it, never sprawling, never crossing the legs or ankles, and neither pressing the feet together nor spreading them wide.

How should one go? As the Śrāvaka Ground has already described. How should one stand? Having arrived, one does not enter at once, but stands beside the inner door with conceit subdued, then enters unhurriedly and without suspicion, standing composedly at the place of meeting, offering greeting first with an open, smiling face, free of any frown, and only then speaking words of kindness. How should one sit? On whatever proper seat is permitted, with correct and dignified bearing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi grounds the instruction to subdue conceit at the door in something concrete: a monastic whose practice is not yet accomplished depends entirely on lay generosity to maintain the body that makes practice possible, and conscience about that dependence is precisely what should replace any sense of superiority on the threshold. He adds a practical modern note about seating, that one should ask before sitting, since a household may reserve particular seats for purposes a visitor would not guess.

53. Section: Speaking at the Right Moment, to Someone Actually Willing to Listen

As to how one should speak, a monastic should know how to speak at the right time, in accordance with reason, in the right measure, with a settled mind, and with straightforwardness: at the right time, meaning when the listener genuinely wishes to hear, is composed in proper bearing, and is untroubled by defilement; in accordance with reason, meaning consistently with the teaching; in the right measure, meaning neither padded with repetition nor left incomplete; with a settled mind, meaning free of agitation and distraction; and with straightforwardness, meaning from a mind that is honest, unpretending, gentle, and pliant. The right time is itself threefold. The first is when the listener actually wishes to hear rather than not: one does not teach someone who is hungry, thirsty, physically exhausted, or afflicted by fever, chill, or other bodily distress. The tradition preserves an illustration of exactly this, the Buddha waiting before teaching an assembly because a cowherd who was to attain the first fruit that day had not yet eaten, and instructing Ānanda to let him eat first, so that the wheel of food might turn before the wheel of Dharma. The second is when the listener is composed in proper bearing rather than not: one does not stand to teach someone seated, unless illness prevents them from sitting upright, and the Prātimokṣa lists further cases, not teaching someone whose head is covered or wrapped, or someone mounted on a vehicle, or teaching from behind someone walking ahead, since a listener's improper bearing reflects a lack of respect toward the teaching itself.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

How should one speak? Knowing well how to speak at the right time, in accordance with reason, in the right measure, with a settled mind, and with straightforwardness. The right time is threefold. First, when the listener wishes to hear rather than not; one who does not wish to hear is hungry, thirsty, physically exhausted, or afflicted by fever or chill. Second, when the listener is composed in proper bearing rather than not; improper bearing means, for instance, that one should not stand to teach someone seated, except in serious illness, as the Prātimokṣa describes at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the plain lesson from the cowherd story that a hungry, sick, or exhausted listener simply cannot take the teaching in, so that waiting for someone to be fed and rested is not a delay in teaching them but a precondition of it, the Buddha himself having judged the matter exactly that way.

54. Section: An Untroubled Listener, a Proper Request, and Speech Neither Tangled Nor Repeated

The third right time for speaking is when the listener's mind is free of defilement rather than troubled by it. A mind is troubled when someone is hurried and pressed, straining hard at one task after another, flustered, scattered, or muddled in thought, and likewise when the listener is a servant on an errand, someone in the midst of work, someone engaged in killing, an adversary, or one who bears a grudge; none of these is a fit occasion or a fit person to teach. Speaking in accordance with reason is likewise threefold: one speaks only when there has been a request, one teaches in accordance with the Dharma when the request itself is proper in bearing, and one teaches what genuinely benefits when the request is made in fitting measure, so that a teacher never speaks unprompted, and adjusts what is taught, whether the ways of gods and humans, the śrāvaka teaching, or the bodhisattva teaching, to the aspiration and need of the one asking. And speech in the right measure is known by three marks: it is orderly and unmixed, presented with structure and sequence rather than tangled with idle talk; its wording is complete, each passage supporting the next so that the meaning is brought to fullness; and it never repeats what has already been said, since speech that is useless and without benefit should not be spoken even a little, let alone at length, exactly as the Lotus Sūtra advises that even someone who deeply loves the Dharma should not be taught excessively.

Third, when the listener's mind is free of defilement rather than troubled. A troubled mind is hurried and pressed, flustered, scattered, or muddled, or belongs to a servant on an errand, someone at work, someone killing, an adversary, or one bearing a grudge. Speaking in accordance with reason is threefold: one speaks only when asked, teaches in accordance with the Dharma when the request is proper, and teaches what benefits when the request is in fitting measure. Speech in the right measure has three marks: it is orderly and unmixed; its wording is complete; and it never repeats what has already been said.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the requirement that speech follow a request as more than etiquette, since teaching unprompted disregards whether the hearer has any actual aspiration to receive it, and the measure of the request itself is what tells a teacher which level of the teaching will genuinely benefit the person asking.

55. Section: Stillness in Bearing, Voice, and Mind, and Speech Whose Inside Matches Its Outside

Speech from a settled mind is likewise threefold. Stillness of bearing means that the teacher's own faculties are settled, free of restless agitation, neither arrogant nor excitable, the head and limbs not moving about while speaking. Stillness of voice means that what is said is neither too loud nor too rapid, since speaking hurriedly leaves the listener unable to follow and things get lost. And stillness of mind means that even when a questioner is genuinely provoking, the teacher speaks without any flare of resentment, still less when there is no provocation at all, speaking always from an undefiled mind that seeks no gain, offering, honor, or praise. Straightforward speech is likewise threefold: it may arise from the skill accumulated through past practice, so that speaking at the right time, in accordance with reason, in measure, and with stillness comes naturally; or from habituation and deliberate application within this present life; or from delight in the training itself, a practitioner restraining themselves through the power of discernment and speaking only after having weighed the matter. In every case there is no falsity whatsoever, what is held within and what is shown outwardly being one and the same, the mind and the mouth in complete accord.

Ru-Yi Translation

Stillness is likewise threefold: stillness of bearing, of voice, and of mind. Stillness of bearing means the faculties are settled, free of agitation, neither arrogant nor excitable, the limbs not moving while speaking. Stillness of voice means what is said is neither too loud nor too rapid. Stillness of mind means that even when provoked one speaks without resentment, still less when unprovoked, and always from an undefiled mind. Straightforward speech is likewise threefold, and at all times free of falsity, what is hidden and what is shown being one and the same.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the third kind of stillness as the most demanding of the three, since bearing and voice can be composed deliberately while a teacher's freedom from resentment under genuine provocation, and from any hope of gain or praise, can only come from a mind that is actually settled rather than one merely appearing so.

56. Section: Five Occasions for Falling Silent, Closing Dwelling with Correct Knowledge

As to when one should fall silent, five occasions should be recognized. The first is commotion: while a teacher is speaking, if a listener deliberately interjects something else so as to stir up disturbance, the teacher should fall silent, since respect has already been lost and there is no point continuing. The second is refusal: if a listener changes the subject and says outright that they do not wish to hear, the teacher should fall silent. The third is contention: if a listener begins looking for faults in the teacher, speaks contentiously, and shows every sign of wanting an argument rather than a teaching, the teacher should fall silent, since confidence is plainly absent. The fourth is an invitation: if a donor comes offering robes, food, or the like, the one receiving should fall silent and simply accept, allowing the donor's own act of generosity to be completed. And the fifth is a challenge: if an opponent arrives intent on debate, the teacher should fall silent and listen to what the other has to say. These five together close the instruction on when a monastic should visit lay households, when to speak, and when to remain silent, and with it the whole discussion of dwelling with correct knowledge.

Ru-Yi Translation

How should one know when to be silent? By recognizing five occasions. First, when a listener deliberately interjects so as to stir commotion. Second, when a listener says outright, stop, I do not wish to hear. Third, when a listener looks for faults and speaks contentiously, showing every sign of opposition. Fourth, when a donor comes offering robes or food, at which the receiver should be silent and accept. Fifth, when an opponent arrives intent on debate, at which one should be silent and listen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the range these five cover, from a listener's open hostility through a donor's simple generosity, silence being called for in both cases though for entirely opposite reasons, and treats the last in particular as a matter of composure, an opponent arriving for a contest being answered first by listening rather than by speaking, closing the discussion of dwelling with correct knowledge.

57. Section: Knowing for Yourself Whether Faith Has Actually Taken Root

A further topic then opens: the differences in entering the path. A practitioner should recognize for themselves, through three aspects, whether they genuinely possess faith and whether they may rightly be counted a person of good family. By its basis: knowing truthfully that the Three Jewels are what faith rests upon, and that one's own faith arises in dependence on them and is definitively grounded there. By its own nature: knowing truthfully which grade of faith one actually possesses, weak, middling, or strong. And by its time: knowing truthfully at what point one came to possess such faith; and as with faith, so too with precepts, eloquence, and the rest, each of which a practitioner should assess through these same three aspects. Reviewing the five wholesome roots together, faith is the factor of entering the path, since confidence in the Three Jewels is what first brings anyone in at all; precepts are the factor of calming once entered, since concentration rests on them; learning is the factor of insight, since hearing the true teaching produces the wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and finally cultivation; generosity is the provision supporting both calming and insight; and the wisdom born of one's own reflection together with a teacher's instruction is what actually takes hold of calming and insight. Relying on these, a practitioner realizes calming and insight and attains the renunciant's fruits; teaches what they have realized to whoever seeks to know and is troubled by doubt; needs eloquence in order to answer opponents; and needs the patience and gentleness of a person of good family whenever another's conduct of body or speech goes wrong.

Chinese Source

復次，且由三相，應自了知己所有信，乃至是善男子。一、由依處故；二、由自性故；三、由時分故。⋯⋯此中信是趣入支；戒是入已奢摩他支；聞是毗鉢舍那支；捨是奢摩他、毗鉢舍那資糧支；內思所成慧及他隨順教授教誡，是能攝受奢摩他、毗鉢舍那支。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three aspects one should know for oneself whether one possesses faith, and whether one is a person of good family: by its basis, by its own nature, and by its time. Among these, faith is the factor of entering; precepts are the factor of calming once entered; learning is the factor of insight; generosity is the provision supporting calming and insight; and the wisdom born of one's own reflection, together with a teacher's instruction, is what takes hold of calming and insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi quotes the Flower Adornment Sūtra's line that faith is the source of the path and the mother of every virtue, nourishing all wholesome roots, to explain why faith is placed first as the factor of entering, and treats this whole three-aspect self-examination as a practitioner's own honest audit, beginning with faith and running through to whether patience and gentleness are genuinely present.

58. Section: Why Faith Fails to Arise, and Why Some Never Seek Out Quiet Places or Good Teachers

The causes of entering the path are then examined, beginning with what obstructs it. Three causes give rise to a lack of faith: not correctly knowing the virtues of the Three Jewels, so that one merely follows along with others' devotions without genuine confidence; following non-Buddhist views while nominally counted a disciple; and never having encountered any of the three kinds of marvel by which buddhas and noble disciples train beings, namely the marvel of supernatural manifestation, which inspires the reverence that makes someone willing to listen, the marvel of declaring what another is thinking, which produces conviction, and the marvel of instruction, by which a teacher who has genuinely severed affliction guides a practitioner along the path. Three causes prevent someone from frequenting quiet groves: being bound by heedlessness and sloth, preferring what involves greed, anger, and delusion over any diligent cultivation; being burdened with too many affairs; and depending entirely on others' direction, whether another teacher's or a non-Buddhist's, so that one neither wishes nor is able to practice alone. And three causes make someone disinclined by temperament to seek out a teacher: a nature that fears nothing and so sees no need; a nature given to conceit, judging oneself already sufficient; and reliance on the written word alone, preferring private study to consultation, and never discovering how much faster understanding grows when it is tested against another's.

復次，由三因緣，發生不信。一、由不正知三寶功德故；二、行外道見故；三、未遇諸佛及聖弟子三種神變，隨其一種所調伏故。復次，由三因緣，不能數往寂靜園林。一、放逸懈怠所拘執故；二、多事業故；三、信順人故。復次，由三因緣，為性不好造詣於他。一、性無畏故；二、性高慢故；三、依文字故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Three causes give rise to a lack of faith: not correctly knowing the virtues of the Three Jewels; following non-Buddhist views; and never having been trained by any of the three marvels of buddhas and noble disciples. Three causes prevent frequenting quiet groves: being bound by heedlessness and sloth; having too many affairs; and depending on others' direction. Three causes make one disinclined to seek out a teacher: a nature that fears nothing; a nature given to conceit; and reliance on the written word alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the three marvels as corresponding to three of the higher knowledges, supernatural power, knowing another's mind, and the exhaustion of outflow, and notes that they are deployed in that order for a reason, since reverence must be won before conviction is possible, and conviction before instruction can actually take hold.

59. Section: Why Some Never Draw Near a Teacher, Never Ask, Never Listen Closely, and Never Retain

Three causes make someone disinclined by temperament to draw near a teacher: a nature not sharp enough to discern which teacher is genuinely worth approaching; judging whoever is encountered not to be a worthy field and so feeling no inclination; and simply lacking any strong delight in doing so, preferring private study. Three causes make someone disinclined to ask questions: being unskilled regarding the teaching itself, so that one does not know what to ask and simply lets what was heard pass; being unskilled regarding its meaning and the states of calming and insight, so that no question naturally arises; and being unskilled regarding both together, so that nothing about the whole domain feels interesting enough to inquire into. Three causes prevent listening closely: a mind scattered by excessive unreasoned thought; being occupied with too many affairs; and a mind entangled and defiled by the hindrances, desire, ill will, dullness, agitation, remorse, and drowsiness. And three causes prevent someone from firmly retaining what they hear: never listening at all; listening badly, whether with aversion or to mistaken teachings; and having wisdom either covered over by ignorance, so that knowing cannot come forward, or overpowered by affliction, so that wisdom is left incapable, the traditional images being a bowl already lidded, which no food can enter, and grass piled on the knees, which scatters the moment one stands.

Three causes prevent close listening: excessive unreasoned thought; too many affairs; and a mind entangled by the hindrances. Three causes prevent firm retention: not listening; listening badly; and wisdom either covered over or overpowered.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi relays the commentarial images for a dull practitioner's failure to retain, a bowl with its lid on receiving nothing however much is poured, and grass resting on the knees that scatters the instant one rises, someone who seemed to be following the teaching while seated having forgotten all of it by the time they stand.

60. Section: Why Some Never Examine, Never Practice, and Never Work for Others, Closing the Differences in Entering

Three causes prevent someone from examining phenomena: delighting in idle talk, whether pointless speech or amusement; loving crowded, noisy company and gathering there rather than studying; and simply not having developed the wisdom that examines carefully, the wisdom born of reflection. Three causes prevent someone from practicing in accordance with the Dharma: clinging to one's own body and life, spending so much effort on maintaining it and fearing exhaustion that little practice actually happens; then, driven by that same clinging, becoming attached to gain and support; and becoming attached to being treated with respect, leaving no time for genuine practice. And three causes leave someone unwilling to work for others' benefit: an inferior disposition, wanting only one's own benefit, as with the śrāvaka lineage; a compassion too thin to be moved to help, as with the solitary-realizer lineage; and simply lacking the capacity, as with one of bodhisattva lineage who has not yet developed the strength. These ten together, thirty causes in all, are the dark side, the reasons a practitioner fails to gain from the teaching; the bright side is to be understood simply as the reverse of everything just described, a practitioner who avoids these thirty faults thereby accomplishing the corresponding virtues. This closes the discussion of the differences in entering the path.

Three causes prevent practicing in accordance with the Dharma: clinging to body and life; through that same force, attachment to gain; and attachment to being respected. Three causes leave one unwilling to work for others: an inferior disposition; thin compassion; and lacking capacity. The bright side is the reverse of all the above, and its characteristics should be understood accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes how the ninth cause traces a single root through three stages, clinging to one's own body and life generating attachment to material support and then to being honored, so that what begins as ordinary self-care ends by consuming the very time practice requires, closing the ten dark-side causes and, with the brief note that the bright side simply reverses them, the whole discussion of entering the path.

61. Section: Why a Great Disciple with Supernatural Power Would Visit the Realm of Hungry Ghosts

A further topic then opens: an explanation of touring the realm of hungry ghosts. The Buddha's noble disciples are recorded as making many kinds of tours, along riverbanks, through mountain valleys, and into the ghost realm itself, and a question is raised about the last of these. For what reason would great śrāvakas who have already attained supernatural power go to the realm of hungry ghosts to question its inhabitants about the past deeds that produced their present state? Was it to resolve doubts of their own, or to benefit beings? If it was to resolve their own doubts, that makes no sense, since they had already attained supernatural power and could simply know. And if it was to benefit beings, then it should be explained exactly how such a visit benefits anyone. The answer given is that it was indeed for beings' benefit, both for the hungry ghosts themselves and for others besides.

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies Maudgalyāyana as the disciple chiefly associated with these tours, and treats the question itself as a genuinely sharp one, since an arhat's own supernatural knowledge would already answer any question about karmic causation, which is exactly why the answer must lie somewhere other than in the questioner's own need to know.

62. Section: How Such a Visit Benefits the Ghosts Themselves and Everyone Who Hears of It

How does such a visit benefit the hungry ghosts? Through the noble one's own supernatural power, the ghosts are enabled to recollect their former lives and to state for themselves what unwholesome deeds they once committed, whereupon deep remorse and revulsion toward those deeds arises; and it is precisely when that conscience has been roused that the noble one teaches them the Dharma, which they are then able to take in. Through this, they are able to leave the ghost realm swiftly, and this is what is meant by benefiting the hungry ghosts. And how does it benefit everyone else? Those great śrāvakas who possess supernatural power, having heard from the ghosts themselves both the unpleasant results they now undergo and the unwholesome deeds of their former lives, return to the human world and pass the account on, so that whoever hears it grows weary of the faults of unwholesome action, actively abandons what is unwholesome, and cultivates what is wholesome. This is what is meant by benefiting other beings.

Ru-Yi Translation

Why? Because through supernatural power the hungry ghosts are made to recollect former lives and state for themselves the unwholesome deeds they once did, whereupon deep remorse arises; the Dharma is then taught and they are able to take it in, and through this they swiftly leave the ghost realm. Great śrāvakas possessing supernatural power, having heard of the unpleasant results and former unwholesome deeds, return to the human world and pass the account on, so that those who hear grow weary of wrongdoing, abandon the unwholesome, and cultivate the wholesome.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws attention to the sequence within the first benefit, that the ghosts are not simply told what they did but are enabled to recollect and state it themselves, remorse arising from their own recognition rather than from an outside accusation, and only then is the teaching given, at the one moment it can actually be received.

63. Section: Six Marks Making Desire-Realm Form Coarse, and the Close of the Śrāvaka Ground's Determination

A final topic then opens: why the form belonging to the desire realm is called coarse, known through six marks. It is coarse through multiplicity, since the desire realm holds countless kinds of visible form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, and the faculties of both sexes besides. It is coarse through heaviness, being weighty and conspicuous. It is coarse through impurity. It is coarse through hardness, being solid and unyielding rather than pliant. It is coarse through liability to damage, since even humans and gods of this realm may lose a hand or a foot. And it is coarse through not following the mind, since the power of concentration here is shallow and neither the body nor any other form can be made to change freely at will. With this the Determination's treatment of the Śrāvaka Ground reaches its end, no further text of decisive analysis appearing for this ground; and the Solitary-Realizer Ground likewise receives no separate treatment here, since what was already set out in the Basic Section is clear enough on its own and needs no further determining. The source lecture-transcript comes to rest at exactly this point.

Through six marks the form belonging to the desire realm should be known as coarse. Which six? Coarse through multiplicity; through heaviness; through impurity; through hardness; through liability to damage; and through not following the mind. No further text of decisive analysis appears for this ground; and no text of decisive analysis appears for the Solitary-Realizer Ground either.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the closing note on the Solitary-Realizer Ground plainly: the Basic Section's own account of it is already sufficiently explicit, so the Compendium of Determinations passes over it rather than repeating what needs no further clarification, and with that the whole Determination of the Śrāvaka Ground, carried across fascicles 67 through 71, comes to its close.
