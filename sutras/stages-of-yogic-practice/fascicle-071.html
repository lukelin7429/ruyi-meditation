Fascicle 71 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十一 · The Śrāvaka Ground, Continued

Seven Kinds of Meaning, Seven Kinds of Joy

Continuing directly from fascicle 70, Maitreya opens a fourteen-part discussion of assorted categories of meaning within the eighth of the śrāvaka canon's own thirteen topics, beginning with seven kinds of meaning a practitioner should master, the seven kinds of joy each one brings in turn, five kinds of Dharma, and three archery virtues standing for the precepts, the concentration, and the wisdom a practitioner needs to hit the target true.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 71 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Seven Kinds of Meaning a Practitioner Should Master

This fifth part of the Śrāvaka Ground's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 70, taking up a fourteen-part discussion of assorted categories of meaning still gathered under the eighth of the śrāvaka canon's own thirteen topics, its own explanation of miscellaneous meanings. The topic opens with seven kinds of meaning a practitioner should recognize within the Buddha's own teaching. The first, meaning to be investigated, refers to the teaching itself, the aggregates, sense-fields, elements, noble truths, and dependent origination among the specific topics whose own essential nature, distinctions, specific characteristics, and shared characteristics a practitioner should seek to understand. The second, meaning to be grasped, refers to whatever doctrine that same teaching actually expresses, impermanence, non-self, the peace of nirvana, emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness among the specific doctrines a practitioner should take hold of and genuinely comprehend. The third, meaning to be avoided, refers to incorrect views and teachings, unwholesome friends, and unwholesome circumstances, all of which should be kept at a distance so that mistaken thinking and mistaken conduct never take root. The fourth, meaning to be drawn forth, refers to the virtues cultivated within concentration, precepts, concentration, and wisdom together, which a practitioner should deliberately bring forth through sustained practice. The fifth, meaning to be restrained, refers to whatever unwholesome conduct of body, speech, or mind, together with unwholesome habits and unwholesome views, should be actively checked. The sixth, meaning to be upheld, refers to the practices of precepts, concentration, and wisdom benefiting oneself, together with the very meaning of nirvana, which should be firmly maintained. And the seventh, meaning to be developed, refers to how precepts give rise to concentration and concentration gives rise to wisdom in turn, this same development extending from benefiting oneself all the way to the great practice of a bodhisattva benefiting others as well. A practitioner who genuinely penetrates all seven of these meanings, from investigation through development, thereby gives rise to seven kinds of genuine joy.

Chinese Source

復次，有七種義。一、應推義，二、應攝義，三、應避義，四、應引義，五、應遮義，六、應持義，七、應發義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seven kinds of meaning exist: meaning to be investigated, meaning to be grasped, meaning to be avoided, meaning to be drawn forth, meaning to be restrained, meaning to be upheld, and meaning to be developed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Notes on the Yogācārabhūmi's own gloss on each of these seven meanings, noting that "meaning to be drawn forth" applies broadly to every virtue cultivated within concentration, precepts, concentration, and wisdom included, while "meaning to be developed" carries the discussion all the way from a practitioner's own benefit to the bodhisattva's own turn toward benefiting others as well, this final meaning quietly opening the door toward the Mahāyāna path still further ahead.

2. Section: Seven Kinds of Joy Answering the Seven Kinds of Meaning

Seven kinds of joy then answer these same seven kinds of meaning in turn. The first, joy drawn from hearing, arises from the wisdom born of hearing as a practitioner investigates and penetrates the teaching itself, corresponding to meaning to be investigated. The second, joy drawn from reflection, arises from the wisdom born of reflection as a practitioner grasps the doctrine actually taught and avoids unwholesome friends, circumstances, and conduct in turn, corresponding to both meaning to be grasped and meaning to be avoided. The third, joy drawn from cultivation, arises from the wisdom born of cultivation as a practitioner draws forth the virtues of precepts, concentration, and wisdom, corresponding to meaning to be drawn forth. The fourth, joy drawn from freedom from the hindrances, arises as that same cultivation frees a practitioner from desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt, corresponding to meaning to be restrained understood inwardly. The fifth, joy drawn from debate, arises as a practitioner subdues non-Buddhist arguments and checks unwholesome teaching from without, corresponding to meaning to be restrained understood outwardly. The sixth, joy drawn from recollecting one's own virtue, arises as a practitioner reflects on precepts kept, concentration cultivated, and wisdom developed for one's own genuine benefit, corresponding to meaning to be upheld. And the seventh, joy drawn from never resting content with what is inferior, arises as a practitioner, recognizing that whatever has been achieved so far still falls short, presses on toward further virtue and toward benefiting others as well, corresponding to meaning to be developed.

Chinese Source

復次，有七種喜。一、聞所引喜，二、思所引喜，三、修所引喜，四、離蓋所引喜，五、議論所引喜，六、念自功德所引喜，七、於諸下劣不生知足所引喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seven kinds of joy answer these seven kinds of meaning: joy drawn from hearing, from reflection, from cultivation, from freedom from the hindrances, from debate, from recollecting one's own virtue, and from never resting content with what is inferior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws this correspondence from several commentators cited in the Notes on the Yogācārabhūmi, Master Jǐng and Master Shéntài among them, and highlights the final joy's own quiet ambition, a practitioner genuinely satisfied with precepts, concentration, and wisdom already cultivated still recognizing that a bodhisattva's own path requires benefiting others as well, self-benefit alone never quite enough to rest content with.

3. Section: Five Kinds of Dharma

A further topic then opens: five kinds of Dharma. The first, the taught Dharma, comprises the scriptures, the discipline, and the fundamental treatises the Buddha himself expounded. The second, the practiced Dharma, comprises the actual practice of precepts and related conduct. The third, the Dharma to be embraced, comprises both material and spiritual support a practitioner gathers in. The fourth, the Dharma to be enjoyed, likewise comprises both material and spiritual benefit a practitioner makes genuine use of. And the fifth, the realized Dharma, comprises the principle and the specific reality a practitioner ultimately comes to realize. A related passage compares the renunciant and the Brahmin through this same fivefold structure, teaching, precept, embracing, enjoyment, and realization, while an earlier fascicle of this same treatise compressed embracing and enjoyment together, describing only three kinds of Dharma, teaching, practice, and ultimate realization.

Chinese Source

復次，有五種法。一、教法，二、行法，三、攝受法，四、受用法，五、證法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Five kinds of Dharma exist: the taught Dharma, the practiced Dharma, the Dharma to be embraced, the Dharma to be enjoyed, and the realized Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references fascicle 70's own threefold account of what makes the Dharma uncommon among non-Buddhist teachings, teaching, practice, and ultimate realization, noting that this fivefold version simply unfolds "practice" there into embracing and enjoyment here, the same underlying structure examined at two different levels of detail.

4. Section: A Bow's Own Strength and an Arrow's Own Aim, Two Archery Virtues Standing for Precepts and Concentration

A further topic then opens: three virtues of skillful archery, offered here as a sustained image for the precepts, the concentration, and the wisdom a practitioner needs. The first, the bow's own virtue, stands for the precepts, since an arrow depends entirely on the bow that launches it, exactly as concentration depends entirely on precepts kept purely beforehand. A bow possesses two virtues of its own: a genuinely sturdy, durable nature, standing for how precepts, once firmly established, resist being broken down by outside temptation; and the capacity to bring the entire task to completion, since without a bow, an arrow has nothing to rely on and can never be loosed at all, exactly as concentration can never arise, and wisdom never be drawn forth, without pure precepts as their own foundation. The second, the arrow's own virtue, stands for concentration, an arrow possessing a single virtue of its own, likewise the capacity to bring the task to completion, since it is the arrow, resting on the bow, that actually flies toward the target, exactly as it is concentration, sustained through continuous, focused contemplation, that actually gives rise to the insight capable of penetrating reality.

Chinese Source

復次，具三種德，方能善射。一、由弓德，二、由箭德，三、由中的德。弓有二德。一、其性堅牢，二、善作究竟。箭有一德。善作究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Three virtues together make for skillful archery: the bow's own virtue, the arrow's own virtue, and the virtue of striking the target's center. The bow possesses two virtues: a genuinely sturdy nature, and the capacity to bring the task to completion. The arrow possesses one virtue: the capacity to bring the task to completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this archery image as a genuinely elegant way of showing precepts, concentration, and wisdom as a single continuous action rather than three separate achievements, a bow's sturdiness meaningless without an arrow to launch, and an arrow meaningless without a bow to launch it from, exactly as precepts without concentration, or concentration without the wisdom it makes possible, remain similarly incomplete.

5. Section: Three Kinds of Skill Behind Striking the Target's Center, Closing the Archery Image

The third virtue, striking the target's own center, stands for wisdom, and comprises three further kinds of skill. The first, ultimate skill, describes an archer so thoroughly trained that every single shot strikes true, standing for the wisdom of an arhat beyond further learning, whose knowledge that affliction is exhausted and that no further rebirth awaits is itself fully and finally accomplished. The second, skill through sustained practice, describes how repeated practice alone brings an archer to strike the center reliably, standing for the wisdom cultivated along the path of cultivation, a practitioner who has already seen the truth directly still needing to continue that same cultivation in order to sever ever subtler affliction and allow wisdom to keep maturing. And the third, skill through a teacher's instruction, describes how a beginning archer must first learn the fundamental method and technique from an instructor before ever striking the center at all, standing for the wisdom belonging to the path of seeing, a practitioner who studies the Buddha's own teaching, penetrates its essential principles, and cultivates precepts, concentration, and wisdom in order to first attain that direct seeing of the truth. This closes the sustained image of the bow, the arrow, and the target's center standing for the precepts, the concentration, and the wisdom a practitioner needs.

Chinese Source

中的有三德。一、究竟工巧，二、串習工巧，三、師學工巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Striking the target's center comprises three kinds of skill: ultimate skill, skill through sustained practice, and skill through a teacher's instruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three kinds of skill as tracing wisdom's own full arc across the path, a beginner's skill first learned from a teacher at the path of seeing, refined through sustained practice along the path of cultivation, and only fully perfected at the path beyond further learning, no shortcut available from beginner's instruction straight to an arhat's own effortless accuracy, closing this sustained archery image for precepts, concentration, and wisdom together.

6. Section: Three Kinds of Teaching, Three Kinds of Underlying Approach

A fifth topic then opens: three kinds of teaching the Buddha employed. The first, the gradual teaching, describes the step-by-step guidance given to śrāvaka practitioners over an extended period. The second, the uninterrupted teaching, describes the sudden, single-vehicle teaching delivered all at once, as when the Buddha, newly awakened, expounded the Flower Adornment Sūtra in a single unbroken session. And the third, the teaching never repeated, describes a final, fully explicit teaching requiring no later restatement in verse, as with the Nirvana Sūtra delivered just before the Buddha's own passing, or the Diamond Sūtra, consisting entirely of prose with no repeated verses at all. A further threefold structure then reduces the Buddha's own boundless teaching to three underlying approaches: an approach distinguished by doctrine, since a given teaching may emphasize emptiness in one passage and existence in another; an approach distinguished by wording, since the same doctrine may be expressed through entirely different phrasing; and an approach distinguished by the difficulty of a given passage's own meaning, since some portions of the teaching require considerably more explanation than others.

Chinese Source

復次，如來教有三種。一、長時教，二、無間教，三、不重說教。復次，如來無量法教，皆由三種理趣。一、由義差別理趣，二、由文差別理趣，三、由難釋差別理趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Buddha's teaching is of three kinds: the gradual teaching, the uninterrupted teaching, and the teaching never repeated. The Buddha's boundless teaching, moreover, is expressed through three underlying approaches: an approach distinguished by doctrine, one distinguished by wording, and one distinguished by the difficulty of a given passage's own meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Determination's own considerably more elaborate account of the same underlying territory, six gateways of principle and, more expansively still, sixteen distinct underlying intentions said to run throughout the Buddha's own teaching, together with a further passage describing two conditions for genuinely entering that teaching, penetrating it through the wisdom that abides in things as they are, and settling it through the wisdom that directly realizes the truth, this brief threefold structure standing here as a compact summary of considerably more extensive material found elsewhere in the treatise.

7. Section: Five Antidotes for the Journey from Birth and Death to Nirvana

A sixth topic then opens: an extended image of a traveler on the road, offered here for the five antidotes a practitioner needs on the journey from birth and death to nirvana. Just as a traveler shields against blazing sunlight with a parasol, a practitioner counters wrong view and wrong wisdom with the correct view gained through the Buddha's own teaching. Just as a traveler counters bandits and danger along a difficult road with weapons, a practitioner counters the risk of falling into a lower rebirth by keeping the precepts. Just as a traveler crosses a river by boat or by bridge, a practitioner crosses the flooding waters of craving by relying on the wisdom of prajñā, the only vessel capable of carrying anyone across an ocean of suffering and endless waves of attachment. Just as a traveler relieves thirst brought on by a long, sun-scorched road with water, a practitioner counters a scattered mind with the water of concentration. And just as a traveler relieves bodily fatigue through massage, a practitioner counters the coarseness that settles into body and mind with the pliancy that concentration itself brings.

Chinese Source

復次，夫涉道者，須五對治。一、炎光對治，二、艱險對治，三、江河對治，四、枯竭對治，五、身勞對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

A traveler on the road needs five antidotes: against blazing sunlight, against danger, against a river to cross, against thirst, and against bodily fatigue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this traveler's-road image as a genuinely vivid way of mapping precepts, concentration, and wisdom onto a journey's own ordinary hazards, correct view shielding against wrong belief exactly as a parasol shields against the sun, and prajñā ferrying a practitioner across craving's own flood exactly as a boat ferries a traveler across a river too wide to wade.

8. Section: Two Marks of Precept-Keeping That Lead to a Favorable Rebirth

A seventh topic then opens: two marks of precept-keeping capable of leading a practitioner to a favorable rebirth among humans or gods. The first, receiving the precepts, means first taking on precepts at all, whether the five precepts or the bodhisattva precepts for a lay practitioner, or the full monastic precepts, together with the bodhisattva precepts' own four grave and forty-eight lesser rules, for one who has gone forth. And the second, keeping the precepts without any gap, means genuinely guarding those same precepts once received, free of any violation. Receiving the precepts and then keeping them purely are together the two paths that lead a practitioner toward a favorable rebirth, and this same treatise's own fascicle 70 already described four further ways of observing exactly how well a given practitioner has actually kept them, through shared dwelling, through hardship, through ordinary daily affairs, and through discussion and skillful discernment.

Chinese Source

復次，尸羅有二種相，能生善趣。一、攝受尸羅，二、不闕尸羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precept-keeping bears two marks capable of leading a practitioner to a favorable rebirth: receiving the precepts, and keeping them without any gap.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references fascicle 70's own fourfold set of criteria for observing precept-keeping, through shared dwelling, hardship, daily affairs, and discussion, as the natural companion to this simpler twofold structure, receiving the precepts and keeping them without gap describing what genuine precept-keeping consists of, and the earlier fourfold set describing how another observer might actually verify it.

9. Section: Three Stages Behind Enjoying Desire in This Very Life

An eighth topic then opens: three specific undertakings involved whenever an ordinary being enjoys the pleasures of desire in this present life, all of which depend on material means. The first is seeking out wealth, the work and effort needed to actually acquire it through one means or another. The second is guarding that wealth once acquired, protecting it from loss or ruin. And the third is indulging in its enjoyment, growing attached to the pleasure that wealth makes possible, the mind defiled by craving in the process.

Chinese Source

復次，諸受欲者，於現法中有三種義。一、追尋財寶，二、守護財寶，三、耽著受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Enjoying the pleasures of desire in this present life involves three undertakings: seeking out wealth, guarding it once acquired, and indulging in its enjoyment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this brief threefold analysis as a plain, unsentimental description of what pursuing worldly pleasure actually entails from beginning to end, seeking, guarding, and indulging together forming a cycle that, left unexamined, simply repeats itself without ever genuinely satisfying the craving that drives it.

10. Section: Coarse and Subtle Decay Across a Lifespan

A ninth topic then opens: the decay of a lifespan considered through two further pairs of distinctions. Considered by its own timescale, decay is twofold: coarse decay, the death that finally arrives once an entire lifespan has run its course, and subtle decay, the moment-to-moment arising and ceasing of body and mind that means every being is, in truth, dying continuously with each passing instant. Considered instead by its own stage, that same decay is likewise twofold: a coarse stage, the visible signs of aging, wrinkled skin, whitened hair, loosening teeth, and clouded vision among them, and a subtle stage, invisible to the naked eye, such as cellular renewal slowing, the senses dulling, and function gradually declining beneath the surface.

Chinese Source

復次，壽命變壞有二種。一、麤變壞，二、細變壞。變壞分位亦有二種。一、麤，二、細。

Ru-Yi Translation

The decay of a lifespan is twofold, considered by timescale: coarse decay, the death that finally arrives, and subtle decay, the moment-to-moment arising and ceasing of body and mind. Considered by stage, decay is likewise twofold: a coarse, visible stage, and a subtle, invisible one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats subtle decay, both the moment-to-moment arising and ceasing of body and mind and its invisible cellular counterpart, as this passage's own quiet reminder that impermanence is never confined to the dramatic, visible changes old age eventually brings, death already fully underway, in a genuine sense, at every single instant of a life still considered young.
