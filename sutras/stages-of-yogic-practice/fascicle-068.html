Fascicle 68 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十八 · The Śrāvaka Ground, Continued

Three Kinds of Cessation, and Only One That Is Truly Nirvana

Continuing directly from fascicle 67, Maitreya resumes the Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discernment at the truth of cessation, the third of the four noble truths, opening with a careful distinction between the analytical cessation that wisdom's own discernment actually attains and two lesser kinds of ceasing, a sound simply fading into silence and a sense faculty simply failing to meet its object, neither of which deserves the name of genuine nirvana.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 68 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Only Wisdom's Own Cessation Deserves the Name Nirvana

This second part of the Śrāvaka Ground's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 67, taking up the third of the four noble truths where the prior fascicle's own transcript left off: the truth of cessation. A question opens this new topic: is the mere ceasing of every conditioned process the same thing as the truth of cessation, and does the truth of cessation, conversely, always involve every conditioned process ceasing? Whatever counts as the truth of cessation, it is answered, does indeed involve conditioned processes ceasing, since cessation itself is unconditioned, and a conditioned process must first be brought to an end before anything unconditioned can be realized in its place; here, cessation names specifically the exhaustion of suffering and its origin, the state a noble one's own outflow-free wisdom actually attains by discerning and extinguishing affliction, karma, and suffering together, arriving at genuine, peaceful, and secure cessation. But not every instance of a conditioned process ceasing deserves to be called the truth of cessation in turn. Some ceasing occurs simply through impermanence, as when a sound fades into silence once its causes are spent, and some occurs simply because the conditions for something to arise happen to be absent altogether, as when one sense faculty naturally fails to register its object because attention lies elsewhere; neither kind of ceasing results from wisdom's own deliberate discernment severing affliction at its root, and neither, therefore, may rightly be called the truth of cessation, which belongs to analytical cessation alone, the peace genuinely won through insight rather than merely happened upon by default.

Chinese Source

問：諸行寂滅，是滅諦耶？設是滅諦，諸行寂滅耶？答：若是滅諦，亦諸行寂滅。或諸行寂滅，然非滅諦。謂由無常滅故、非擇滅故，諸行寂滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is the ceasing of every conditioned process the truth of cessation, and does the truth of cessation always involve such ceasing? Whatever is the truth of cessation does involve conditioned processes ceasing. But some ceasing is not the truth of cessation, namely ceasing through simple impermanence or through the mere absence of conditions for arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening distinction, between cessation genuinely won through wisdom's own discernment and cessation that merely happens by default when a cause runs out or a condition never arises, as this whole topic's own essential foundation, since conflating the two would reduce nirvana to something no more significant than a sound simply fading away. He highlights the case of a sense faculty failing to register its object purely because attention lies elsewhere as a particularly instructive example, since it shows that even something resembling meditative absorption from the outside, one sense door apparently stilled, carries no genuine liberating value at all unless it results from wisdom actually uprooting affliction rather than mere circumstantial inattention.

2. Section: What Exactly Is Extinguished — Affliction Itself, or Its Very Basis

A further question then asks precisely what must cease for something to be rightly called the truth of cessation at all. Briefly, it is answered, two things: the cessation of affliction itself, and the cessation of affliction's own underlying basis. The cessation of affliction refers to permanently emerging from every entangling affliction and permanently casting off every latent tendency toward it, affliction already countered at the path of seeing and again at the path of cultivation, so that its future retributive suffering will never arise again and its present, afflictive distress no longer manifests even now, this being the liberation a noble one attains while still embodied, nirvana together with a remainder. The cessation of the basis refers, more radically still, to the eventual and complete vanishing of every remaining aggregate itself, the very body and mind produced by past karma and affliction now finally exhausted and extinguished, leaving only pure suchness itself, free of every characteristic, utterly still, as one's sole support, every other basis having permanently ceased, this being nirvana entirely without remainder.

Chinese Source

問：何等法滅故名滅諦耶？答：略有二種。一、煩惱滅故；二、依滅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What must cease for something to be called the truth of cessation? Briefly, two things: the cessation of affliction itself, and the cessation of affliction's own underlying basis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twofold distinction, nirvana still embodied versus nirvana entirely without remainder, as this treatise's own careful way of honoring both a living arhat's genuine and complete freedom from affliction and the further, final cessation that awaits even that same arhat's own body and mind once their natural course finally runs out. He notes that this same distinction, addressed already in earlier fascicles of this decisive discernment, is being revisited here specifically within the context of the four noble truths, showing how consistently this treatise circles back to its most important teachings from one fresh angle after another.

3. Section: Nirvana with a Remainder, Nirvana without One

How each of these two kinds of cessation is actually attained is then explained. Through the cessation of affliction alone, one attains nirvana with a remainder, since the six root afflictions, craving, aversion, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong view, have already been permanently exhausted and the very cause of continued birth and death forever severed, even while a residual, outflow-laden body still remains, exactly as with a living arhat who has not yet passed away. And through the cessation of the very basis that supports affliction, one attains nirvana without any remainder at all, the cause of future arising permanently cut off, the fruit of past causes fully exhausted, and the presently remaining aggregates simply and naturally dissolving away of their own accord, every form of suffering thereby stilled forever and the three realms left permanently behind. Various schools of thought differ over precisely what this cessation encompasses, some holding that only the craving capable of drawing forth a future rebirth truly counts as the cessation-truth proper, others extending the same name to the complete exhaustion of the five aggregates themselves, whether one's own or another's; still another tradition distinguishes the cessation of craving as the cause's own exhaustion from the cessation of suffering as the fruit's own exhaustion, comparing the two to a lamp's own flame going out and the very light it once gave off vanishing along with it. Framed instead through the three natures already established elsewhere in this treatise, cessation may equally be explained as threefold: the cessation of the imagined nature, which was never truly arisen to begin with; the cessation of the two forms of grasping, subject and object, belonging to the dependent nature, since neither ever truly existed as it appeared to; and the cessation revealed in the perfected nature's own intrinsically still and peaceful essence.

Chinese Source

煩惱滅故，得有餘依滅諦；依滅故，得無餘依滅諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the cessation of affliction alone, one attains nirvana with a remainder. Through the cessation of the very basis supporting affliction, one attains nirvana entirely without remainder.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the lamp-and-light image, the cause's own flame extinguished and the fruit's own light vanishing along with it, as an unusually clear way of distinguishing why two genuinely different moments, an arhat's living liberation and an arhat's final passing, both deserve to be called cessation, one addressing the cause of suffering directly and the other addressing suffering's own last remaining trace. He highlights the three-natures framework's own final formulation, cessation as the imagined nature's own never having truly arisen at all, as this treatise's own deepest way of putting the matter, suggesting that liberation is ultimately less about eliminating something real than about finally seeing clearly that what was taken to be real never actually was.

4. Section: Why Cessation Was Never Absent to Begin With

A difficult question is then raised: if the truth of cessation did not exist before a practitioner attained it and only came to exist afterward, how can it rightly be called permanent? And if it exists permanently at all times, why do not all sentient beings attain complete nirvana at every single moment? The first half of this question is answered by pointing to cessation's own defining marks: never transmigrating through the destinies at all, and never actually manifesting as any active process whatsoever, these very marks defining what the truth of cessation is. How, then, could something marked by never transmigrating and never manifesting possibly be said to have been absent before and present only afterward? A conditioned dharma alone properly admits of such a before-and-after distinction, arising where it once did not exist and passing away once its causes are spent; but cessation itself carries none of the three characteristic marks proper to conditioned things, arising, perishing, or change while persisting, and for this very reason is called unconditioned, so that no genuine transmigration or manifestation was ever present to begin with, and speaking of it as first absent and only later present simply fails to apply at all. As one further passage confirms, even the transformed basis attained upon entering nirvana without remainder should be said to genuinely exist, precisely because this very nature remains, in both nirvana with a remainder and without one alike, an entirely unmoving reality, and something unmoving in this way could never coherently be said to have first existed and then ceased to exist.

Chinese Source

問：若此滅諦先無後有，云何是常？若常時有，何不一切有情於一切時般涅槃耶？答：不流轉相、不現行相是滅諦相，此諦云何先無後有。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the truth of cessation did not exist before and exists only after, how can it be permanent? And if it exists permanently, why do not all beings attain nirvana at every moment? Answer: never transmigrating and never manifesting are cessation's own defining marks — how, then, could it have been first absent and only later present?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this resolution as this treatise's own careful dissolving of a question that only seems coherent from within the conditioned world's own familiar categories of arising and ceasing, showing that the very framework of before-and-after was never actually applicable to something unconditioned in the first place, rather than offering some special exception to that framework. He highlights the citation confirming the transformed basis as genuinely existing precisely because it is unmoving as a fitting reminder that permanence, in this treatise's own careful usage, names something considerably subtler than mere unbroken duration through time.

5. Section: Nirvana Is Revealed by Realization, Never Produced by Arising

Cessation is further shown to be permanent for a second reason: having no mark of arising and no mark of perishing is itself precisely what permanence means, and cessation carries neither mark at all, and so is rightly called permanent on this ground as well. The second half of the original question, why sentient beings do not all attain nirvana at every moment if cessation is indeed permanent, is then answered by distinguishing the practitioner rather than the truth itself: whoever has genuinely attained the permanent stilling of every coarse residue of affliction does indeed enter complete nirvana, while whoever has not yet attained this does not, the difference lying entirely in what has or has not actually been realized rather than in any change within cessation's own permanent nature. Because the truth of cessation exists, certain beings do come to realize it, and it is precisely this realization that reveals nirvana, nirvana never having been produced or newly generated by that realization in the first place. Nirvana, understood in this way, is the very emptiness underlying all conditioned dharmas, ownerless and self-less, disclosed through the wisdom that observes dependent origination through to its own necessary conclusion; genuine practice, accordingly, aims not at acquiring some new possession but at releasing every mistaken grasping, until what was always already so is finally and simply confirmed.

Chinese Source

又無生相、無滅相是常相，滅諦亦爾，是故名常。若有證得一切麤重永息滅者，彼般涅槃；若未證得者，彼不般涅槃。有滅諦故，諸有情類有證得者，是故涅槃是證所顯，非生所顯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having no mark of arising and no mark of perishing is itself the mark of permanence, and cessation carries neither. Whoever has attained the permanent stilling of every coarse affliction enters nirvana; whoever has not, does not. Nirvana is revealed by realization, never produced by arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the teaching that nirvana is revealed rather than produced as among this whole section's most quietly transformative points, since it reframes the entire path away from acquisition and toward release, a practitioner ultimately uncovering rather than constructing the very peace being sought. He highlights the elsewhere-cited fourfold account of nirvana, intrinsically pure and shared equally by every being, with remainder, without remainder, and the unfixed abode unique to a buddha who dwells in neither samsara nor nirvana exclusively for the sake of all beings, as showing that even the humblest sentient being already possesses, in the first of these four senses, a share in nirvana's own intrinsic purity long before any formal attainment ever actually occurs.

6. Section: Four Ways to Misspeak Cessation, and Why Saṃsāra Itself Is Nirvana

Whoever explains the truth of cessation correctly, it is further taught, must avoid four distinct faults. The first is the fault of exaggeration, claiming that conditioned processes and their cessation are simply different things, as though cessation were some separate entity existing quite apart from conditioned existence altogether, a claim that would sever cessation from the very conditioned processes whose extinguishing it actually names, and would therefore fail to represent conditioned existence's own real nature at all. The second is the fault of wrongly characterizing cessation's own nature, claiming instead that conditioned processes and their cessation are simply identical, since conditioned processes are defiled while cessation itself remains genuinely pure, and treating the two as flatly the same would wrongly attribute conditioned existence's own defilement to cessation as well. The third is the fault of confusion, claiming that they are both different and not different at once, a position that immediately falls into both of the preceding errors together and thereby contradicts itself. And the fourth is the fault of nihilistic denial, claiming that they are neither different nor not different, which amounts to denying that cessation possesses any nature or reality whatsoever, treating the utter stilling of conditioned existence as mere annihilation rather than recognizing that this same still, peaceful nature is none other than suchness itself, genuinely real and never a mere nothingness. Cessation's own true nature, avoiding every one of these four extremes, can only ever be personally realized through direct inner experience, never adequately captured by declaring it simply the same as, different from, both, or neither in relation to conditioned existence; and it is for this very reason that the Compendium of the Great Vehicle itself teaches that when equal wisdom regarding saṃsāra and nirvana genuinely arises, one thereby realizes, in that very moment, that saṃsāra is nirvana itself, neither abandoned nor not abandoned, nirvana itself neither newly attained nor left unattained. With this the truth of cessation's own full explanation comes to its close.

Chinese Source

復次，若有遠離四種過失說滅諦者，是名正說。何等名為四種過失？一、增益過失，二、自相邪分別過失，三、相雜亂過失，四、損減過失。若言諸行、盡滅有異者，是增益過失；言無異者，是自相邪分別過失；言亦有異亦無異者，是相雜亂過失；言非有異非無異者，是損減過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever speaks correctly of cessation avoids four faults. Claiming conditioned existence and its cessation are different is exaggeration; claiming they are identical is wrong characterization; claiming both is confusion; claiming neither is nihilistic denial. Cessation's true nature can only be personally realized, never adequately captured by any of these four positions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold structure as this treatise's own careful application of a classic pattern found throughout Buddhist philosophy, systematically ruling out every possible logical relationship a concept might bear to its own basis in order to point toward a reality that ultimately exceeds every such relationship altogether. He highlights the closing citation, that saṃsāra is nirvana the moment equal wisdom regarding both genuinely arises, as this whole section's own most striking conclusion, suggesting that liberation is finally less a matter of traveling from one place to another than of seeing the single place one has always already been standing with eyes no longer clouded by distortion.

7. Section: Why the Eightfold Path Alone Represents the Truth of the Path

A further topic then opens: the fourth and final noble truth, the truth of the path. A question is first raised: if every outflow-free aggregate belonging to the transcendent path, taken together, is truly what constitutes the truth of the path, why does the Buddha speak of the truth of the path specifically as the noble eightfold path, right view leading the rest? The answer given rests on the three trainings themselves: it is precisely because a learner, again and again, trains in the higher training in precepts, again and again trains in the higher training of mind, and again and again trains in the higher training of wisdom, relying on precepts to establish concentration and relying on concentration to accomplish wisdom, wisdom itself finally accomplishing genuine liberation, that the eightfold path is named the truth of the path, this same eightfold path being nothing other than these three trainings unfolded into eight distinct limbs. The higher training in precepts establishes the very foundation, dwelling in complete precepts, guarding the code of individual liberation, one's conduct and bearing fully accomplished, regarding even the smallest fault with genuine dread, and training diligently in every precept undertaken. The higher training in mind then rests upon this foundation, becoming free of unwholesome desire through the four absorptions in turn, until the mind attains one-pointed concentration and thereby gains genuine, direct knowledge and vision of whatever object is known. And the higher training in wisdom finally rests upon this same concentration, genuine knowledge and vision arising with respect to the four noble truths and the twelve links of dependent origination, until, through this thoroughly purified knowledge and vision, every affliction is at last completely and permanently severed, this very severing itself being the supreme benefit beyond which no greater benefit remains to be accomplished.

Chinese Source

問：若唯一切出世間五非取蘊皆道諦攝，何因緣故，唯說正見為先八聖支道而為道諦？答：依三學故作如是說。由有學者，於時時間依增上戒學而起修學，於時時間依增上心學而起修學，於時時間依增上慧學而起修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

If every outflow-free aggregate belongs to the truth of the path, why is the path named specifically as the noble eightfold path? Answer: because of the three trainings — a learner trains again and again in higher precepts, higher mind, and higher wisdom, relying on precepts to establish concentration and on concentration to accomplish the wisdom that finally severs affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the naming of the eightfold path as this treatise's own practical distillation of an otherwise vast and potentially unwieldy category, every outflow-free dharma belonging technically to the truth of the path, into eight concrete, actionable limbs a practitioner can genuinely take up moment by moment. He highlights the precise sequence given, precepts establishing concentration and concentration accomplishing wisdom, as this treatise's own consistent architecture for the entire path, a structure repeated so often throughout this work that it functions almost as its own organizing principle.

8. Section: Three Aggregates, Eight Limbs, One Path

A second reason for naming the path specifically as the eightfold path is then given: this same eightfold path is fully subsumed within three aggregates, precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and it is for this reason too that only this eightfold path is called the truth of the path. Right speech, right action, and right livelihood together constitute the cultivation of precepts, right speech naming correct and truthful expression, right action naming bodily, verbal, and mental conduct carried out with clear awareness, and right livelihood naming a rightful, lawful means of sustaining life. Right mindfulness and right concentration together constitute the cultivation of concentration, right mindfulness holding one's object of attention clearly and without forgetting, thereby serving as the very support on which right concentration itself depends. And right view, right intention, and right effort together constitute the cultivation of wisdom, right view providing a correct and accurate understanding, right intention reflecting rightly on the basis of that same view, and right effort applying sustained diligence to the true teaching accordingly. Precepts thus provide the very foundation on which concentration is built, concentration in turn provides the basis on which wisdom is cultivated, and wisdom finally brings every affliction to its complete and final severing, this same threefold structure of precepts, concentration, and wisdom accounting for the eightfold path's own full function from beginning to end.

Chinese Source

又此八聖支道三蘊所攝，是故唯此說名道諦。正語、正業、正命，名為修戒；正念、正定，名為修定；正見、正思惟、正精進，名為修慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

This eightfold path is fully subsumed within three aggregates: right speech, action, and livelihood constitute the cultivation of precepts; right mindfulness and concentration constitute the cultivation of concentration; and right view, intention, and effort constitute the cultivation of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold sorting of the eight limbs as offering two complementary ways of understanding one and the same path, the three trainings explained in the prior section showing the path's own dynamic, sequential unfolding across time, while this threefold aggregate division shows how the very same eight limbs sort naturally into three simultaneous dimensions of practice, precepts governing outward conduct, concentration governing inward stability, and wisdom governing genuine understanding, all three needing to be cultivated together rather than merely one after another in strict succession.

9. Section: Right Speech and Livelihood Reveal a Mind Already Purified

A question then asks why right speech, right action, and right livelihood in particular are said to constitute the aggregate of precepts. Two reasons are given: relying on the proper use of the Dharma itself, and relying on the proper use of material wealth. Right speech, truthful and free of divisive, harsh, or frivolous talk, and right action, free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, together form the very root and foundation on which a practitioner is able to genuinely receive and make use of the true teaching at all, and are therefore said to rely on the proper use of the Dharma. Right livelihood, sustaining life through rightful, lawful means rather than through any of the deceptive methods, feigned spiritual attainment, false speech, manufactured hardship, or manipulative gain-seeking, by which requisites might otherwise be wrongly obtained, forms the very root and foundation for rightly making use of material wealth, and is therefore said to rely on the proper use of wealth. The Buddha further declared that precisely in this domain of right speech, action, and livelihood, a supremely pure mental conduct becomes visible: whenever the intention to break a precept, arising from craving or some other affliction, and the intention to pursue requisites through deceptive means alike are both genuinely absent, this very absence itself constitutes that supremely pure mental conduct, revealing, through right speech, a corresponding purity of view, through right action, a corresponding purity of precepts, and through right livelihood, a corresponding purity of life itself.

Chinese Source

問：何因緣故，正語、正業、正命說為戒蘊？答：二因緣故。一、依正受用法故；二、依正受用財故。此中依止貪等，起犯戒思；依止矯詐等，起邪追求衣服等思。若離此事，應知是名增上清淨意現行性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are right speech, action, and livelihood called the aggregate of precepts? Two reasons: relying on the proper use of the Dharma, and relying on the proper use of wealth. Where the intention to break precepts and the intention to pursue requisites deceptively are both absent, this absence itself is a supremely pure mental conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the identification of purity with a genuine absence, no precept-breaking intention and no deceptive livelihood-seeking intention arising in the first place, as this treatise's own consistent preference for locating virtue in what does not happen rather than only in some further, additional achievement. He highlights the mapping of right speech, action, and livelihood onto view, precept, and life purity respectively as a fittingly precise correspondence, each of the three outward practices serving as visible evidence of a corresponding inward clarity that would otherwise remain entirely hidden from an outside observer.

10. Section: Three Functions of Wisdom, from Penetrating Truth to Severing the Remaining Fetters

A further question then asks why right view, right intention, and right effort in particular are said to constitute the aggregate of wisdom. This aggregate, it is answered, carries out three distinct functions, and only through the completion of all three together does wisdom itself finally reach its own genuine fulfillment. The first function is penetrating the true meaning of every dharma, right view directly realizing, through outflow-free non-conceptual wisdom, the very truth of the four noble truths. The second function, once this true meaning has already been penetrated, is skillfully explaining, establishing, and clarifying that same truth for the sake of other beings, right intention providing the reflective basis on which the true teaching can then be set forth in whatever way best suits its listeners, until they too come to understand. And the third function is severing whatever further affliction still remains, right effort sustaining continued practice of the eightfold path itself in order to eliminate the innate, jointly arisen afflictions still remaining even after a first breakthrough into the truth, exactly as one who has already attained the first fruit of the path, having permanently severed self-view, wrongly held precepts and vows, and doubt, the very three fetters whose severing establishes that first fruit, must nonetheless continue practicing the path still further in order to sever whatever remaining fetters persist on the way toward complete and final liberation. Nirvana alone, one commentary notes, is properly called the Dharma itself, since it alone stands supreme among all things, untouched by birth, aging, sickness, or death, while the eightfold path, following after it and according with it, is rightly called what accords with the Dharma, in the same relation a great minister bears to a sovereign king.

Chinese Source

問：何因緣故，正見、正思惟、正精進說為慧蘊？答：由此慧蘊略有三種作業，因此三法方得究竟。謂通達諸法真義，是初業。通達諸法真義已，即於真義為他宣說、施設、建立、分別、開示，令其易了，是第二業。為斷餘結，法隨法行，是第三業。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are right view, intention, and effort called the aggregate of wisdom? This aggregate carries three functions: penetrating the true meaning of every dharma is the first; explaining that truth to others is the second; and severing whatever affliction still remains through continued practice of the path is the third.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three fetters severed at the first fruit, self-view, wrongly held precepts, and doubt, as this treatise's own concise summary standing in for the fuller list of afflictions a beginning noble one has already overcome, chosen precisely because they represent the most serious obstacles blocking entry onto the transcendent path in the first place. He highlights the analogy of a sovereign king and a great minister, nirvana alone bearing the name of Dharma while the eightfold path merely accords with it, as a fitting reminder that the path itself, however indispensable, always remains a means toward nirvana rather than nirvana's own equal.

11. Section: How Mindfulness Itself Becomes Concentration's Own Support

A question then asks why right mindfulness and right concentration in particular are said to constitute the aggregate of concentration. Two reasons are given: concentration's own intrinsic nature, and what concentration depends upon as its support. Regarding intrinsic nature, concentration itself simply is single-pointed absorption, the mind resting spontaneously and continuously upon its object without any distraction at all, attained only through patient, undiscouraged practice across three successive stages, first attaining access concentration, then bringing the four fundamental absorptions themselves to full completion, and finally attaining complete mastery, freely entering, dwelling in, and emerging from them at will, every trace of craving, wrong view, conceit, and ignorance those absorptions might otherwise still carry now removed as well. Regarding its support, mindfulness serves concentration through four distinct functions. First, mindfulness binds the mind to its object, holding attention fixed upon body, feeling, mind, and mental objects throughout the four foundations of mindfulness, without which attention inevitably wanders and concentration can never actually take hold. Second, mindfulness accords with concentration, guarding the sense faculties from wandering toward their objects, maintaining clear awareness of one's own present conduct, and reflecting with genuine joy on whatever inspires confidence in the path. Third, mindfulness severs the five hindrances, sensory craving overcome through the contemplation of impurity, ill will through loving-kindness, dullness and drowsiness through the perception of brightness, restlessness and regret through the mindful counting of breath, and doubt through the contemplation of dependent origination, each hindrance's own complete severance occurring at its own appropriate stage of the path. And fourth, mindfulness sustains extensive, uninterrupted, and earnest cultivation of the signs of calming, uplifting, and equanimity, skillfully guiding the mind through whichever of these three postures the moment actually requires. It is precisely for these reasons that mindfulness serves as concentration's own genuine support, and with this the full explanation of why the eightfold path represents the truth of the path, through both the three trainings and the three aggregates together, comes to its close.

Chinese Source

問：何因緣故，正念、正定說為定蘊？答：二因緣故。一、由自性故；二、由所依故。一、繫所緣故。二、隨順定故。三、能斷蓋故。四、極多修習相作意故。是故此念為定所依。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are right mindfulness and concentration called the aggregate of concentration? Two reasons: intrinsic nature, and what serves as support. Mindfulness supports concentration through four functions: binding the mind to its object, according with concentration, severing the five hindrances, and sustaining extensive cultivation of calming, uplifting, and equanimity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise mapping of each hindrance to its own antidote and to the exact stage of the path at which it is finally and completely severed, doubt already gone at the very first fruit, craving and ill will not fully uprooted until the third, and dullness and restlessness lingering all the way until arhatship itself, as a genuinely useful practical map for any meditator wondering which hindrance is likely to prove most stubborn and how far along the path its complete removal can actually be expected. He highlights the four functions of mindfulness together as this treatise's own comprehensive account of concentration's own foundation, mindfulness never merely one ingredient among several but the very thread holding every other element of meditative practice together.

12. Section: Eleven Ways of Seeing Rightly, from a Disease to Freedom Itself

A further topic then opens: an extensive explanation of right view itself, beginning with its own eleven distinct varieties. Briefly named, these are the view of conditioned existence as a disease, the view of it as a festering boil, the view of it as a dart lodged within the body, the view of it as a genuine calamity, the view of it as impermanent, the view of it as suffering, the view of it as empty, the view of it as without any real self, the view of whatever constitutes a fetter binding a being to continued existence, the view of freedom from every such binding fetter, and the view of whatever is actually capable of bringing that same freedom about. Each of these eleven, taken together, offers its own distinct angle from which a practitioner may come to see conditioned existence exactly as it truly is, the physical and psychological images, disease, boil, and dart, addressing the felt experience of suffering directly, the more abstract marks of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness addressing its underlying nature, and the final three, fetter, freedom from fetter, and the means of achieving that freedom, addressing the very practical question of how a practitioner actually moves from bondage toward liberation itself.

Chinese Source

復次，正見差別略有十一。謂如病見、如癰見，乃至無我見、結見、離繫見、能離結見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Right view, briefly, has eleven distinct varieties: the view of conditioned existence as a disease, as a boil, and so on through the view of it as without a real self, the view of what constitutes a fetter, the view of freedom from that fetter, and the view of what is capable of achieving that freedom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the progression from visceral, almost physical images like disease and a festering boil toward the more abstract marks of impermanence and selflessness, and finally toward the practical vocabulary of bondage and release, as this elevenfold list's own carefully designed pedagogical arc, meeting a practitioner first at the level of felt experience before gradually leading them toward both philosophical understanding and concrete practice. He notes that each of these eleven varieties awaits its own fuller explanation in what follows, this section offering only the complete list as a roadmap for the more detailed treatment still to come.
