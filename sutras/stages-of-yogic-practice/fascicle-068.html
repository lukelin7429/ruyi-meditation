Fascicle 68 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十八 · The Śrāvaka Ground, Continued

Three Kinds of Cessation, and Only One That Is Truly Nirvana

Continuing directly from fascicle 67, Maitreya resumes the Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discernment at the truth of cessation, closing the entire four noble truths and a warning against flattery and coarse speech, before opening the vinaya's own thirteen divisions — the four obstacles blocking a genuine going forth, the marks of heavy and light offenses, the sevenfold dependence a diligent monastic relies upon, the five ways of rightly receiving, and finally what taints a monastic's actual use of robes, food, dwelling, and medicine — the transcript coming to rest with a monastic's mind untroubled by discriminating thought even while drinking a single bowl of water.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 68 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Only Wisdom's Own Cessation Deserves the Name Nirvana

This second part of the Śrāvaka Ground's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 67, taking up the third of the four noble truths where the prior fascicle's own transcript left off: the truth of cessation. A question opens this new topic: is the mere ceasing of every conditioned process the same thing as the truth of cessation, and does the truth of cessation, conversely, always involve every conditioned process ceasing? Whatever counts as the truth of cessation, it is answered, does indeed involve conditioned processes ceasing, since cessation itself is unconditioned, and a conditioned process must first be brought to an end before anything unconditioned can be realized in its place; here, cessation names specifically the exhaustion of suffering and its origin, the state a noble one's own outflow-free wisdom actually attains by discerning and extinguishing affliction, karma, and suffering together, arriving at genuine, peaceful, and secure cessation. But not every instance of a conditioned process ceasing deserves to be called the truth of cessation in turn. Some ceasing occurs simply through impermanence, as when a sound fades into silence once its causes are spent, and some occurs simply because the conditions for something to arise happen to be absent altogether, as when one sense faculty naturally fails to register its object because attention lies elsewhere; neither kind of ceasing results from wisdom's own deliberate discernment severing affliction at its root, and neither, therefore, may rightly be called the truth of cessation, which belongs to analytical cessation alone, the peace genuinely won through insight rather than merely happened upon by default.

Chinese Source

問：諸行寂滅，是滅諦耶？設是滅諦，諸行寂滅耶？答：若是滅諦，亦諸行寂滅。或諸行寂滅，然非滅諦。謂由無常滅故、非擇滅故，諸行寂滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Is the ceasing of every conditioned process the truth of cessation, and does the truth of cessation always involve such ceasing? Whatever is the truth of cessation does involve conditioned processes ceasing. But some ceasing is not the truth of cessation, namely ceasing through simple impermanence or through the mere absence of conditions for arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening distinction, between cessation genuinely won through wisdom's own discernment and cessation that merely happens by default when a cause runs out or a condition never arises, as this whole topic's own essential foundation, since conflating the two would reduce nirvana to something no more significant than a sound simply fading away. He highlights the case of a sense faculty failing to register its object purely because attention lies elsewhere as a particularly instructive example, since it shows that even something resembling meditative absorption from the outside, one sense door apparently stilled, carries no genuine liberating value at all unless it results from wisdom actually uprooting affliction rather than mere circumstantial inattention.

2. Section: What Exactly Is Extinguished — Affliction Itself, or Its Very Basis

A further question then asks precisely what must cease for something to be rightly called the truth of cessation at all. Briefly, it is answered, two things: the cessation of affliction itself, and the cessation of affliction's own underlying basis. The cessation of affliction refers to permanently emerging from every entangling affliction and permanently casting off every latent tendency toward it, affliction already countered at the path of seeing and again at the path of cultivation, so that its future retributive suffering will never arise again and its present, afflictive distress no longer manifests even now, this being the liberation a noble one attains while still embodied, nirvana together with a remainder. The cessation of the basis refers, more radically still, to the eventual and complete vanishing of every remaining aggregate itself, the very body and mind produced by past karma and affliction now finally exhausted and extinguished, leaving only pure suchness itself, free of every characteristic, utterly still, as one's sole support, every other basis having permanently ceased, this being nirvana entirely without remainder.

Chinese Source

問：何等法滅故名滅諦耶？答：略有二種。一、煩惱滅故；二、依滅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What must cease for something to be called the truth of cessation? Briefly, two things: the cessation of affliction itself, and the cessation of affliction's own underlying basis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twofold distinction, nirvana still embodied versus nirvana entirely without remainder, as this treatise's own careful way of honoring both a living arhat's genuine and complete freedom from affliction and the further, final cessation that awaits even that same arhat's own body and mind once their natural course finally runs out. He notes that this same distinction, addressed already in earlier fascicles of this decisive discernment, is being revisited here specifically within the context of the four noble truths, showing how consistently this treatise circles back to its most important teachings from one fresh angle after another.

3. Section: Nirvana with a Remainder, Nirvana without One

How each of these two kinds of cessation is actually attained is then explained. Through the cessation of affliction alone, one attains nirvana with a remainder, since the six root afflictions, craving, aversion, delusion, conceit, doubt, and wrong view, have already been permanently exhausted and the very cause of continued birth and death forever severed, even while a residual, outflow-laden body still remains, exactly as with a living arhat who has not yet passed away. And through the cessation of the very basis that supports affliction, one attains nirvana without any remainder at all, the cause of future arising permanently cut off, the fruit of past causes fully exhausted, and the presently remaining aggregates simply and naturally dissolving away of their own accord, every form of suffering thereby stilled forever and the three realms left permanently behind. Various schools of thought differ over precisely what this cessation encompasses, some holding that only the craving capable of drawing forth a future rebirth truly counts as the cessation-truth proper, others extending the same name to the complete exhaustion of the five aggregates themselves, whether one's own or another's; still another tradition distinguishes the cessation of craving as the cause's own exhaustion from the cessation of suffering as the fruit's own exhaustion, comparing the two to a lamp's own flame going out and the very light it once gave off vanishing along with it. Framed instead through the three natures already established elsewhere in this treatise, cessation may equally be explained as threefold: the cessation of the imagined nature, which was never truly arisen to begin with; the cessation of the two forms of grasping, subject and object, belonging to the dependent nature, since neither ever truly existed as it appeared to; and the cessation revealed in the perfected nature's own intrinsically still and peaceful essence.

Chinese Source

煩惱滅故，得有餘依滅諦；依滅故，得無餘依滅諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the cessation of affliction alone, one attains nirvana with a remainder. Through the cessation of the very basis supporting affliction, one attains nirvana entirely without remainder.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the lamp-and-light image, the cause's own flame extinguished and the fruit's own light vanishing along with it, as an unusually clear way of distinguishing why two genuinely different moments, an arhat's living liberation and an arhat's final passing, both deserve to be called cessation, one addressing the cause of suffering directly and the other addressing suffering's own last remaining trace. He highlights the three-natures framework's own final formulation, cessation as the imagined nature's own never having truly arisen at all, as this treatise's own deepest way of putting the matter, suggesting that liberation is ultimately less about eliminating something real than about finally seeing clearly that what was taken to be real never actually was.

4. Section: Why Cessation Was Never Absent to Begin With

A difficult question is then raised: if the truth of cessation did not exist before a practitioner attained it and only came to exist afterward, how can it rightly be called permanent? And if it exists permanently at all times, why do not all sentient beings attain complete nirvana at every single moment? The first half of this question is answered by pointing to cessation's own defining marks: never transmigrating through the destinies at all, and never actually manifesting as any active process whatsoever, these very marks defining what the truth of cessation is. How, then, could something marked by never transmigrating and never manifesting possibly be said to have been absent before and present only afterward? A conditioned dharma alone properly admits of such a before-and-after distinction, arising where it once did not exist and passing away once its causes are spent; but cessation itself carries none of the three characteristic marks proper to conditioned things, arising, perishing, or change while persisting, and for this very reason is called unconditioned, so that no genuine transmigration or manifestation was ever present to begin with, and speaking of it as first absent and only later present simply fails to apply at all. As one further passage confirms, even the transformed basis attained upon entering nirvana without remainder should be said to genuinely exist, precisely because this very nature remains, in both nirvana with a remainder and without one alike, an entirely unmoving reality, and something unmoving in this way could never coherently be said to have first existed and then ceased to exist.

Chinese Source

問：若此滅諦先無後有，云何是常？若常時有，何不一切有情於一切時般涅槃耶？答：不流轉相、不現行相是滅諦相，此諦云何先無後有。

Ru-Yi Translation

If the truth of cessation did not exist before and exists only after, how can it be permanent? And if it exists permanently, why do not all beings attain nirvana at every moment? Answer: never transmigrating and never manifesting are cessation's own defining marks — how, then, could it have been first absent and only later present?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this resolution as this treatise's own careful dissolving of a question that only seems coherent from within the conditioned world's own familiar categories of arising and ceasing, showing that the very framework of before-and-after was never actually applicable to something unconditioned in the first place, rather than offering some special exception to that framework. He highlights the citation confirming the transformed basis as genuinely existing precisely because it is unmoving as a fitting reminder that permanence, in this treatise's own careful usage, names something considerably subtler than mere unbroken duration through time.

5. Section: Nirvana Is Revealed by Realization, Never Produced by Arising

Cessation is further shown to be permanent for a second reason: having no mark of arising and no mark of perishing is itself precisely what permanence means, and cessation carries neither mark at all, and so is rightly called permanent on this ground as well. The second half of the original question, why sentient beings do not all attain nirvana at every moment if cessation is indeed permanent, is then answered by distinguishing the practitioner rather than the truth itself: whoever has genuinely attained the permanent stilling of every coarse residue of affliction does indeed enter complete nirvana, while whoever has not yet attained this does not, the difference lying entirely in what has or has not actually been realized rather than in any change within cessation's own permanent nature. Because the truth of cessation exists, certain beings do come to realize it, and it is precisely this realization that reveals nirvana, nirvana never having been produced or newly generated by that realization in the first place. Nirvana, understood in this way, is the very emptiness underlying all conditioned dharmas, ownerless and self-less, disclosed through the wisdom that observes dependent origination through to its own necessary conclusion; genuine practice, accordingly, aims not at acquiring some new possession but at releasing every mistaken grasping, until what was always already so is finally and simply confirmed.

Chinese Source

又無生相、無滅相是常相，滅諦亦爾，是故名常。若有證得一切麤重永息滅者，彼般涅槃；若未證得者，彼不般涅槃。有滅諦故，諸有情類有證得者，是故涅槃是證所顯，非生所顯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having no mark of arising and no mark of perishing is itself the mark of permanence, and cessation carries neither. Whoever has attained the permanent stilling of every coarse affliction enters nirvana; whoever has not, does not. Nirvana is revealed by realization, never produced by arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the teaching that nirvana is revealed rather than produced as among this whole section's most quietly transformative points, since it reframes the entire path away from acquisition and toward release, a practitioner ultimately uncovering rather than constructing the very peace being sought. He highlights the elsewhere-cited fourfold account of nirvana, intrinsically pure and shared equally by every being, with remainder, without remainder, and the unfixed abode unique to a buddha who dwells in neither samsara nor nirvana exclusively for the sake of all beings, as showing that even the humblest sentient being already possesses, in the first of these four senses, a share in nirvana's own intrinsic purity long before any formal attainment ever actually occurs.

6. Section: Four Ways to Misspeak Cessation, and Why Saṃsāra Itself Is Nirvana

Whoever explains the truth of cessation correctly, it is further taught, must avoid four distinct faults. The first is the fault of exaggeration, claiming that conditioned processes and their cessation are simply different things, as though cessation were some separate entity existing quite apart from conditioned existence altogether, a claim that would sever cessation from the very conditioned processes whose extinguishing it actually names, and would therefore fail to represent conditioned existence's own real nature at all. The second is the fault of wrongly characterizing cessation's own nature, claiming instead that conditioned processes and their cessation are simply identical, since conditioned processes are defiled while cessation itself remains genuinely pure, and treating the two as flatly the same would wrongly attribute conditioned existence's own defilement to cessation as well. The third is the fault of confusion, claiming that they are both different and not different at once, a position that immediately falls into both of the preceding errors together and thereby contradicts itself. And the fourth is the fault of nihilistic denial, claiming that they are neither different nor not different, which amounts to denying that cessation possesses any nature or reality whatsoever, treating the utter stilling of conditioned existence as mere annihilation rather than recognizing that this same still, peaceful nature is none other than suchness itself, genuinely real and never a mere nothingness. Cessation's own true nature, avoiding every one of these four extremes, can only ever be personally realized through direct inner experience, never adequately captured by declaring it simply the same as, different from, both, or neither in relation to conditioned existence; and it is for this very reason that the Compendium of the Great Vehicle itself teaches that when equal wisdom regarding saṃsāra and nirvana genuinely arises, one thereby realizes, in that very moment, that saṃsāra is nirvana itself, neither abandoned nor not abandoned, nirvana itself neither newly attained nor left unattained. With this the truth of cessation's own full explanation comes to its close.

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever speaks correctly of cessation avoids four faults. Claiming conditioned existence and its cessation are different is exaggeration; claiming they are identical is wrong characterization; claiming both is confusion; claiming neither is nihilistic denial. Cessation's true nature can only be personally realized, never adequately captured by any of these four positions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold structure as this treatise's own careful application of a classic pattern found throughout Buddhist philosophy, systematically ruling out every possible logical relationship a concept might bear to its own basis in order to point toward a reality that ultimately exceeds every such relationship altogether. He highlights the closing citation, that saṃsāra is nirvana the moment equal wisdom regarding both genuinely arises, as this whole section's own most striking conclusion, suggesting that liberation is finally less a matter of traveling from one place to another than of seeing the single place one has always already been standing with eyes no longer clouded by distortion.

7. Section: Why the Eightfold Path Alone Represents the Truth of the Path

A further topic then opens: the fourth and final noble truth, the truth of the path. A question is first raised: if every outflow-free aggregate belonging to the transcendent path, taken together, is truly what constitutes the truth of the path, why does the Buddha speak of the truth of the path specifically as the noble eightfold path, right view leading the rest? The answer given rests on the three trainings themselves: it is precisely because a learner, again and again, trains in the higher training in precepts, again and again trains in the higher training of mind, and again and again trains in the higher training of wisdom, relying on precepts to establish concentration and relying on concentration to accomplish wisdom, wisdom itself finally accomplishing genuine liberation, that the eightfold path is named the truth of the path, this same eightfold path being nothing other than these three trainings unfolded into eight distinct limbs. The higher training in precepts establishes the very foundation, dwelling in complete precepts, guarding the code of individual liberation, one's conduct and bearing fully accomplished, regarding even the smallest fault with genuine dread, and training diligently in every precept undertaken. The higher training in mind then rests upon this foundation, becoming free of unwholesome desire through the four absorptions in turn, until the mind attains one-pointed concentration and thereby gains genuine, direct knowledge and vision of whatever object is known. And the higher training in wisdom finally rests upon this same concentration, genuine knowledge and vision arising with respect to the four noble truths and the twelve links of dependent origination, until, through this thoroughly purified knowledge and vision, every affliction is at last completely and permanently severed, this very severing itself being the supreme benefit beyond which no greater benefit remains to be accomplished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the naming of the eightfold path as this treatise's own practical distillation of an otherwise vast and potentially unwieldy category, every outflow-free dharma belonging technically to the truth of the path, into eight concrete, actionable limbs a practitioner can genuinely take up moment by moment. He highlights the precise sequence given, precepts establishing concentration and concentration accomplishing wisdom, as this treatise's own consistent architecture for the entire path, a structure repeated so often throughout this work that it functions almost as its own organizing principle.

8. Section: Three Aggregates, Eight Limbs, One Path

A second reason for naming the path specifically as the eightfold path is then given: this same eightfold path is fully subsumed within three aggregates, precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and it is for this reason too that only this eightfold path is called the truth of the path. Right speech, right action, and right livelihood together constitute the cultivation of precepts, right speech naming correct and truthful expression, right action naming bodily, verbal, and mental conduct carried out with clear awareness, and right livelihood naming a rightful, lawful means of sustaining life. Right mindfulness and right concentration together constitute the cultivation of concentration, right mindfulness holding one's object of attention clearly and without forgetting, thereby serving as the very support on which right concentration itself depends. And right view, right intention, and right effort together constitute the cultivation of wisdom, right view providing a correct and accurate understanding, right intention reflecting rightly on the basis of that same view, and right effort applying sustained diligence to the true teaching accordingly. Precepts thus provide the very foundation on which concentration is built, concentration in turn provides the basis on which wisdom is cultivated, and wisdom finally brings every affliction to its complete and final severing, this same threefold structure of precepts, concentration, and wisdom accounting for the eightfold path's own full function from beginning to end.

Ru-Yi Translation

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold sorting of the eight limbs as offering two complementary ways of understanding one and the same path, the three trainings explained in the prior section showing the path's own dynamic, sequential unfolding across time, while this threefold aggregate division shows how the very same eight limbs sort naturally into three simultaneous dimensions of practice, precepts governing outward conduct, concentration governing inward stability, and wisdom governing genuine understanding, all three needing to be cultivated together rather than merely one after another in strict succession.

9. Section: Right Speech and Livelihood Reveal a Mind Already Purified

A question then asks why right speech, right action, and right livelihood in particular are said to constitute the aggregate of precepts. Two reasons are given: relying on the proper use of the Dharma itself, and relying on the proper use of material wealth. Right speech, truthful and free of divisive, harsh, or frivolous talk, and right action, free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, together form the very root and foundation on which a practitioner is able to genuinely receive and make use of the true teaching at all, and are therefore said to rely on the proper use of the Dharma. Right livelihood, sustaining life through rightful, lawful means rather than through any of the deceptive methods, feigned spiritual attainment, false speech, manufactured hardship, or manipulative gain-seeking, by which requisites might otherwise be wrongly obtained, forms the very root and foundation for rightly making use of material wealth, and is therefore said to rely on the proper use of wealth. The Buddha further declared that precisely in this domain of right speech, action, and livelihood, a supremely pure mental conduct becomes visible: whenever the intention to break a precept, arising from craving or some other affliction, and the intention to pursue requisites through deceptive means alike are both genuinely absent, this very absence itself constitutes that supremely pure mental conduct, revealing, through right speech, a corresponding purity of view, through right action, a corresponding purity of precepts, and through right livelihood, a corresponding purity of life itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are right speech, action, and livelihood called the aggregate of precepts? Two reasons: relying on the proper use of the Dharma, and relying on the proper use of wealth. Where the intention to break precepts and the intention to pursue requisites deceptively are both absent, this absence itself is a supremely pure mental conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the identification of purity with a genuine absence, no precept-breaking intention and no deceptive livelihood-seeking intention arising in the first place, as this treatise's own consistent preference for locating virtue in what does not happen rather than only in some further, additional achievement. He highlights the mapping of right speech, action, and livelihood onto view, precept, and life purity respectively as a fittingly precise correspondence, each of the three outward practices serving as visible evidence of a corresponding inward clarity that would otherwise remain entirely hidden from an outside observer.

10. Section: Three Functions of Wisdom, from Penetrating Truth to Severing the Remaining Fetters

A further question then asks why right view, right intention, and right effort in particular are said to constitute the aggregate of wisdom. This aggregate, it is answered, carries out three distinct functions, and only through the completion of all three together does wisdom itself finally reach its own genuine fulfillment. The first function is penetrating the true meaning of every dharma, right view directly realizing, through outflow-free non-conceptual wisdom, the very truth of the four noble truths. The second function, once this true meaning has already been penetrated, is skillfully explaining, establishing, and clarifying that same truth for the sake of other beings, right intention providing the reflective basis on which the true teaching can then be set forth in whatever way best suits its listeners, until they too come to understand. And the third function is severing whatever further affliction still remains, right effort sustaining continued practice of the eightfold path itself in order to eliminate the innate, jointly arisen afflictions still remaining even after a first breakthrough into the truth, exactly as one who has already attained the first fruit of the path, having permanently severed self-view, wrongly held precepts and vows, and doubt, the very three fetters whose severing establishes that first fruit, must nonetheless continue practicing the path still further in order to sever whatever remaining fetters persist on the way toward complete and final liberation. Nirvana alone, one commentary notes, is properly called the Dharma itself, since it alone stands supreme among all things, untouched by birth, aging, sickness, or death, while the eightfold path, following after it and according with it, is rightly called what accords with the Dharma, in the same relation a great minister bears to a sovereign king.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three fetters severed at the first fruit, self-view, wrongly held precepts, and doubt, as this treatise's own concise summary standing in for the fuller list of afflictions a beginning noble one has already overcome, chosen precisely because they represent the most serious obstacles blocking entry onto the transcendent path in the first place. He highlights the analogy of a sovereign king and a great minister, nirvana alone bearing the name of Dharma while the eightfold path merely accords with it, as a fitting reminder that the path itself, however indispensable, always remains a means toward nirvana rather than nirvana's own equal.

11. Section: How Mindfulness Itself Becomes Concentration's Own Support

A question then asks why right mindfulness and right concentration in particular are said to constitute the aggregate of concentration. Two reasons are given: concentration's own intrinsic nature, and what concentration depends upon as its support. Regarding intrinsic nature, concentration itself simply is single-pointed absorption, the mind resting spontaneously and continuously upon its object without any distraction at all, attained only through patient, undiscouraged practice across three successive stages, first attaining access concentration, then bringing the four fundamental absorptions themselves to full completion, and finally attaining complete mastery, freely entering, dwelling in, and emerging from them at will, every trace of craving, wrong view, conceit, and ignorance those absorptions might otherwise still carry now removed as well. Regarding its support, mindfulness serves concentration through four distinct functions. First, mindfulness binds the mind to its object, holding attention fixed upon body, feeling, mind, and mental objects throughout the four foundations of mindfulness, without which attention inevitably wanders and concentration can never actually take hold. Second, mindfulness accords with concentration, guarding the sense faculties from wandering toward their objects, maintaining clear awareness of one's own present conduct, and reflecting with genuine joy on whatever inspires confidence in the path. Third, mindfulness severs the five hindrances, sensory craving overcome through the contemplation of impurity, ill will through loving-kindness, dullness and drowsiness through the perception of brightness, restlessness and regret through the mindful counting of breath, and doubt through the contemplation of dependent origination, each hindrance's own complete severance occurring at its own appropriate stage of the path. And fourth, mindfulness sustains extensive, uninterrupted, and earnest cultivation of the signs of calming, uplifting, and equanimity, skillfully guiding the mind through whichever of these three postures the moment actually requires. It is precisely for these reasons that mindfulness serves as concentration's own genuine support, and with this the full explanation of why the eightfold path represents the truth of the path, through both the three trainings and the three aggregates together, comes to its close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise mapping of each hindrance to its own antidote and to the exact stage of the path at which it is finally and completely severed, doubt already gone at the very first fruit, craving and ill will not fully uprooted until the third, and dullness and restlessness lingering all the way until arhatship itself, as a genuinely useful practical map for any meditator wondering which hindrance is likely to prove most stubborn and how far along the path its complete removal can actually be expected. He highlights the four functions of mindfulness together as this treatise's own comprehensive account of concentration's own foundation, mindfulness never merely one ingredient among several but the very thread holding every other element of meditative practice together.

12. Section: Eleven Ways of Seeing Rightly, from a Disease to Freedom Itself

A further topic then opens: an extensive explanation of right view itself, beginning with its own eleven distinct varieties. Briefly named, these are the view of conditioned existence as a disease, the view of it as a festering boil, the view of it as a dart lodged within the body, the view of it as a genuine calamity, the view of it as impermanent, the view of it as suffering, the view of it as empty, the view of it as without any real self, the view of whatever constitutes a fetter binding a being to continued existence, the view of freedom from every such binding fetter, and the view of whatever is actually capable of bringing that same freedom about. Each of these eleven, taken together, offers its own distinct angle from which a practitioner may come to see conditioned existence exactly as it truly is, the physical and psychological images, disease, boil, and dart, addressing the felt experience of suffering directly, the more abstract marks of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness addressing its underlying nature, and the final three, fetter, freedom from fetter, and the means of achieving that freedom, addressing the very practical question of how a practitioner actually moves from bondage toward liberation itself.

Chinese Source

復次，正見差別略有十一。謂如病見、如癰見，乃至無我見、結見、離繫見、能離結見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Right view, briefly, has eleven distinct varieties: the view of conditioned existence as a disease, as a boil, and so on through the view of it as without a real self, the view of what constitutes a fetter, the view of freedom from that fetter, and the view of what is capable of achieving that freedom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the progression from visceral, almost physical images like disease and a festering boil toward the more abstract marks of impermanence and selflessness, and finally toward the practical vocabulary of bondage and release, as this elevenfold list's own carefully designed pedagogical arc, meeting a practitioner first at the level of felt experience before gradually leading them toward both philosophical understanding and concrete practice. He notes that each of these eleven varieties awaits its own fuller explanation in what follows, this section offering only the complete list as a roadmap for the more detailed treatment still to come.

13. Section: Disease, Boil, Dart, and Obstruction — Four Ways the Aggregates Wound

The eleven varieties of right view are now matched to their corresponding truth, beginning with the eight belonging to the truth of suffering, the first four corresponding to four distinct conditions in which the five aggregates may be found. Whenever the aggregates are actively entangled with some affliction currently manifesting, craving, aversion, or delusion stirred up respectively by pleasant, painful, or neutral feeling, they should be observed as like a disease, the body and mind thereby thrown out of their own natural ease. Whenever the aggregates are instead bound up with the mere latent seed of affliction, not currently manifesting yet still quietly present, they should be observed as like a festering boil, outwardly unremarkable for the moment yet already carrying a serious hidden trouble certain to erupt again given the right condition. Whenever the aggregates are bound up with craving's own relish for some pleasing object, they should be observed as like an arrow already lodged within the heart, an affliction that follows a being everywhere, bringing suffering now and promising more suffering still to come. And whenever the aggregates are bound up with genuine fault, the obstruction of affliction, of karma, or of retribution itself standing in the way, they should be observed as like an obstruction outright, blocking a practitioner from ever accomplishing the wholesome qualities, worldly or transcendent, that would otherwise lead toward genuine liberation.

Chinese Source

謂於諸煩惱纏俱行行中，於煩惱隨眠俱行行中，於愛味俱行行中，於過患俱行行中，次第觀為如病、如癰、如箭、如障。

Ru-Yi Translation

The aggregates actively entangled with a manifesting affliction should be observed as like a disease; bound to affliction's mere latent seed, as like a boil; bound to craving's relish for a pleasing object, as like an arrow lodged in the heart; and bound to genuine fault obstructing wholesome accomplishment, as like an obstruction itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the progression from a currently active disease through a merely latent boil to an already-lodged arrow and finally to an outright obstruction as tracing four genuinely different depths at which affliction can be found within the very same five aggregates, from what is presently flaring up to what merely waits its turn to what has already done its damage to what now actively blocks any further progress. He highlights the boil image especially as a fittingly unsettling reminder that the mere absence of currently manifesting affliction offers no real safety at all, since a seed left undisturbed remains just as capable of erupting again the moment conditions allow.

14. Section: Impermanent, Suffering, Empty, and without a Real Self

The remaining four views belonging to the truth of suffering are then explained. Observing the aggregates as arising and perishing moment by moment is called the view of impermanence, since a single lifetime's own aggregates, however continuous they may appear on the surface, are in truth never anything but a rapid, unbroken succession of fresh moments, never actually persisting unchanged for even an instant. Observing the aggregates as constantly pursued by the three kinds of suffering, the suffering of pain, of change, and the suffering pervading conditioned existence itself, is called the view of suffering, the aggregates themselves being nothing other than suffering's own constant dwelling place. Observing the aggregates as genuinely free of any further self or self's possession beyond the aggregates themselves is called the view of emptiness, since no constant, autonomous self can ever actually be found dwelling somewhere among them. And observing the aggregates' own intrinsic nature as carrying no real function of a self or of what belongs to a self is called the view of selflessness, since every conditioned process, arising only through dependence on conditions, thereby possesses no genuine autonomous power of its own at all. As one commentary distinguishes these last two: emptiness addresses whether a self's own substance can be found at all, while selflessness addresses whether a self's own function can be found once its substance has already been ruled out; grasping at some substance is countered by emptiness, and grasping at some remaining function is countered by selflessness in turn. With this the full eight views belonging to the truth of suffering are now complete.

Chinese Source

若於諸行觀為生滅，名無常見。觀為三苦之所隨逐，名為苦見。觀彼遠離餘我我所，名為空見。觀彼諸行體性非我及我所相，名無我見。如是八種，是緣苦諦正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Observing the aggregates as arising and perishing is the view of impermanence. Observing them as pursued by the three sufferings is the view of suffering. Observing them as free of any self or self's possession is the view of emptiness. Observing their own nature as carrying no function of a self is the view of selflessness. These eight together are right view concerning the truth of suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between emptiness as ruling out a self's own substance and selflessness as ruling out a self's own function as a genuinely fine but important refinement, since a practitioner might intellectually accept that no fixed self-substance exists while still, in practice, continuing to act as though some autonomous controlling function remained, and only the second view fully closes that remaining gap. He highlights the overall structure of these eight views, the first four addressing the aggregates' own felt, almost visceral condition and the second four addressing their underlying philosophical nature, as this treatise's own way of ensuring that right view engages both direct experience and genuine understanding together, neither alone being sufficient.

15. Section: The Fetter That Binds, and the First Steps toward Freedom from It

The truth of origin's own four views are then explained together as a single view, the view of what constitutes a fetter, corresponding to the traditional four aspects of cause, accumulation, arising, and condition. Observing origin as cause means recognizing craving for continued existence itself as the very generating cause of a future rebirth. Observing it as accumulation means recognizing that this same craving for continued existence actively accumulates and draws together the aggregates of a life yet to come. Observing it as arising means recognizing that delight-craving toward an already obtained, pleasing object gives rise, in turn, to a future life's own continued unfolding. And observing it as condition means recognizing that craving for delight in what is not yet obtained serves as the further condition sustaining that same unfolding still. All four aspects, taken together, are simply craving in its different guises, and are called the view of a fetter precisely because craving is what actually binds a being to suffering, entangling the aggregates with affliction in the present and thereby generating further defilement in a life yet to come. A further topic then opens: the truth of cessation's own four views, together called the view of freedom from bondage, the first two of these four aspects being cessation itself, whereby origin's craving is entirely and completely extinguished, and peace, whereby a practitioner who has actually realized cessation eventually enters, at life's own natural end, nirvana without any remaining trace of the three realms' own suffering at all.

Chinese Source

若於集諦觀為因、集、生、緣，名為結見。由彼集諦，於苦諦中起雜染結故。若於滅諦觀滅、靜、妙、離，名離繫見。由彼滅諦，一切煩惱及依離繫所顯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Observing origin as cause, accumulation, arising, and condition is called the view of a fetter, since origin generates defilement binding the aggregates to suffering. Observing cessation as extinguishing origin and as the peace of nirvana without remainder, among its four aspects, is called the view of freedom from bondage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the collapsing of origin's own four traditional aspects into a single unified view, the fetter, as a fitting recognition that cause, accumulation, arising, and condition are ultimately just craving observed from four different angles rather than four genuinely separate realities, exactly paralleling craving's own threefold analysis already given in the prior fascicle. He highlights the term fetter itself as this whole view's own most vivid image, craving pictured not as some abstract mental event but as an actual binding cord tying a being fast to suffering, a picture worth holding in mind as the remaining two aspects of freedom from that same fetter are explained in what follows.

16. Section: Wonderful, Liberating, and the Path That Leads There

The remaining two aspects of freedom from bondage are then explained, completing the truth of cessation's own fourfold view. Cessation is wonderful, since it stands as the most excellent, the very foremost, and the most thoroughly pure of all things, an unsurpassed and supremely peaceful dwelling beyond which nothing greater remains to be found. And cessation is liberation itself, since through the extinguishing of suffering and its origin together, a being permanently departs the three realms once and for all, abiding thereafter in an unchanging state from which no further regression is ever possible. A further and final view then completes the full set of eleven: the truth of the path's own view, called the view capable of achieving freedom from every fetter, corresponding to the aspects of path, suchness, practice, and emergence, since the truth of the path alone is what ultimately reveals a practitioner's complete freedom from every binding fetter at last. Path names the very passage a practitioner must travel, awakening to the genuine antidote capable of thoroughly examining the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements until their real, unchanging nature is finally uncovered. Suchness names the unchanging reality actually reached at the end of that passage, non-conceptual wisdom directly realizing what has been sought all along. Practice names the actual method itself, applying the four truths to each link of the twelvefold chain of dependent origination in turn, and applying that same fourfold framework to the impermanent, suffering, empty, and selfless nature of the very cycle of rebirth across the three realms. And emergence names the successful result, actually departing affliction's own grip and arriving, at last, at nirvana itself. With these eleven views now fully matched to their four respective truths, disease, boil, dart, and obstruction together with impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness under suffering, the fetter under origin, freedom from bondage under cessation, and this final view under the path, the full explanation of right view's own eleven varieties comes to its close.

Chinese Source

若於道諦觀道、如、行、出，名能離結見。由彼道諦，究竟能離結縛所顯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Observing the path as path, suchness, practice, and emergence is called the view capable of achieving freedom from every fetter, since the truth of the path alone ultimately reveals complete freedom from every binding fetter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the completed set of eleven views, now systematically matched across all four truths, as this treatise's own thorough demonstration that right view is never a single, monolithic insight but a carefully differentiated instrument suited to each distinct aspect of reality it must illuminate, suffering demanding one kind of scrutiny, its origin another, and cessation and the path still others again. He highlights the final aspect of the path, emergence, as a fitting closing note for this entire elevenfold structure, every prior view ultimately in service of this one practical outcome, a genuine and complete departure from affliction's own long-standing grip.

17. Section: Why Disease and Boil Come before Impermanence for a Noble One

A question then asks why, if a practitioner ordinarily first cultivates the views of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness before then cultivating the views of disease, boil, dart, and obstruction, this section's own explanation reversed that order, presenting the latter four first. This ordering, it is answered, describes specifically a being who has already attained the noble path. A noble learner, already possessing a genuinely powerful aspiration toward nirvana, first observes the very aggregates proper to human and heavenly rebirth as like a disease, a boil, a dart, and an obstruction, generating real revulsion toward attachment to them, and only then, building on this revulsion, gives rise to a still higher, more refined, and more thoroughly purified round of the views of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, precisely in order to sever whatever innate affliction still remains even after the breakthrough of noble realization has already occurred. An ordinary being not yet on the path, by contrast, must first cultivate impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness as the very entry point leading toward the path in the first place, only afterward, once already on the path, coming to cultivate the views of disease and the rest.

Chinese Source

問：若先起無常、苦、空、無我見，後方起如病、如癰、如箭、如障見，何緣此中先說如病等見？答：此中依已得道補特伽羅，說彼為先。何以故？已得聖道諸有學者，由增上意樂，於諸取蘊觀為如病，乃至如障；如是觀已，為斷餘結，復起上位清淨無常見，乃至無我見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why does this section present disease, boil, dart, and obstruction before impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, when practice ordinarily proceeds the other way? Answer: this describes one who has already attained the path. A noble learner first cultivates revulsion through the former four, then a still more refined round of the latter four to sever whatever affliction still remains.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this reconciliation as a genuinely important reminder that the very same set of contemplative views can serve two different purposes depending entirely on where a practitioner already stands, functioning as the initial gateway onto the path for someone not yet arrived and as a still deeper refinement for someone already there, the same words carrying different practical weight at different stages. He highlights the noble learner's own motivation, a genuinely powerful aspiration toward nirvana rather than mere doctrinal repetition, as what actually makes this second, more refined round of contemplation effective where a first, cruder pass already proved insufficient to sever every remaining affliction.

18. Section: An Impure View That Leads to the Path, a Pure View That Deepens It

One should further know that the views of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness themselves unfold in two distinct ways. The first is an impure view, still outflow-laden and not yet free of affliction, on which the views of disease, boil, dart, and obstruction themselves depend as their own foundation, this impure view serving as the very guide leading a practitioner toward whatever has not yet been attained, namely the noble path itself, a way of contemplation proper to an ordinary being not yet arrived there. The second is instead a pure view, genuinely outflow-free, which itself depends upon the views of disease, boil, dart, and obstruction already cultivated, this pure view growing ever further in strength through such continued cultivation until it brings about the mind's own genuine and complete liberation from whatever affliction still remains, a way of contemplation proper instead to a noble one who has already attained the path and now presses on toward its final completion.

Chinese Source

當知此中，略有二種無常等見。一、是如病等見所依不清淨見；以此為先，此為引導，為欲獲得所未得故。二、是如病等見能依清淨見；已得如病等見，復令增長，及為得心善解脫故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The views of impermanence and the rest unfold in two ways: an impure view, on which disease and the rest depend, guiding a practitioner toward what has not yet been attained; and a pure view, which depends on disease and the rest already cultivated, growing further to bring about the mind's genuine liberation from what still remains.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this doubling of the same four views into an impure, guiding form and a pure, deepening form as this treatise's own precise account of spiritual development as a genuine spiral rather than a simple straight line, a practitioner returning to what looks like the same insight twice yet actually encountering it each time at a meaningfully different depth. He highlights the phrase "the mind's genuine liberation" as this whole progression's own intended destination, the entire elevenfold structure of right view ultimately existing to serve this one concrete transformation rather than remaining merely theoretical.

19. Section: Three Gates to Liberation, and the Four Truths' Universal Antidote

These eleven views may further be sorted according to the three gates of liberation. The views of emptiness and selflessness together constitute the emptiness gate, both alike taking the absence of any real self or self's possession as their shared object. The truth of cessation's own view of freedom from bondage constitutes the signless gate, cessation itself being free of every characteristic mark whatsoever. And every remaining view, the eight views belonging to suffering apart from emptiness and selflessness, together with the fetter view belonging to origin and the final view belonging to the path, constitutes the wishless gate. A further topic then opens: in the very course of the path of cultivation, every transcendent path without exception takes the four noble truths together as its object, and one should know that doing so is capable of countering every affliction found across all three realms at once.

Chinese Source

如是十一正見中，空行、無我行見名為空行，餘行見名無願行，一行見名無相行。謂於滅諦離繫行見。復次，於修道中，一切出世間道緣四諦為境，當知皆能對治三界一切煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every transcendent path in the course of cultivation takes the four truths together as its object, capable of countering every affliction across all three realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this mapping of the eleven views onto the three gates of liberation as this treatise's own demonstration that its various analytical frameworks are never merely parallel systems but genuinely interlocking ones, the same set of contemplations capable of being organized by truth, by liberation-gate, or by practitioner-stage depending entirely on which aspect of the path a given passage means to illuminate. He highlights the claim that the four truths together counter every affliction across all three realms at once as a fittingly expansive closing statement, suggesting that this single, unified object of contemplation, rightly cultivated, leaves no remaining affliction anywhere within conditioned existence genuinely untouched.

20. Section: All Three Realms Merged into One Contemplation, Closing the Four Truths

Why should this be so? A noble learner who has already glimpsed the noble path's own footprint first attains, through direct dharma-wisdom, realization of the desire realm's own four truths as they are actually and presently seen, and then, through corresponding class-wisdom, extends that same realization by inference to the form and formless realms not presently seen at all; but once established on the path of cultivation itself, such a practitioner no longer contemplates the three realms separately, instead gathering every realm's aggregates together into a single mass, a single portion, and a single heap, observing impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness across the whole without any longer distinguishing desire-realm processes from form-realm or formless-realm processes, or presently seen processes from those not presently seen at all. One should therefore know that in the course of cultivation, the transcendent path is capable of countering, all at once and without distinction, every affliction belonging to the lower and higher realms together. A noble one, moreover, having already advanced this far along the path of cultivation, mostly turns to signless contemplation, dwelling again and again in mental engagement free of every characteristic mark, since this same signless engagement proves most effective at inducing genuine ease in the very midst of present life while severing affliction at the same time. The truth of the path further subsumes every excellent quality a noble one comes to possess, the five supernatural powers, the concentration of non-contention, the concentration of wish-fulfilling wisdom, the four kinds of unobstructed understanding, and, for a buddha alone, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, and every other quality belonging exclusively to a fully awakened one, since all of these are attained only after the path itself, are intrinsically pure in their own nature, and are established entirely by the path's own power, and for this reason all such qualities are likewise gathered under the truth of the path. With this, the truth of the path's own fourfold treatment, the eightfold path's own designation, the universal antidote reaching across all three realms, the preference for signless contemplation, and the subsuming of every noble quality, is complete, and with it the entire explanation of the four noble truths comes to its close.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The five supernatural powers, non-contention, wish-fulfilling wisdom, the unobstructed understandings, and every quality unique to a buddha are all attained after the path, intrinsically pure, and established by the path, and are therefore likewise gathered under the truth of the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing gathering of every noble quality, from ordinary supernatural powers all the way up to a buddha's own exclusive qualities, under this single truth of the path as this whole four-noble-truths explanation's own generous final gesture, insisting that nothing genuinely excellent a practitioner ever comes to possess along the way stands outside the path that actually produced it. He highlights the shift from separately contemplating each of the three realms to merging them into a single unified object of contemplation as a vivid illustration of how a practitioner's own scope of vision itself matures over the course of practice, early effort necessarily proceeding piece by piece before a more advanced stage can finally take in the whole picture at once.

21. Section: Why Only Suffering Demands to Be Fully and Completely Known

A further topic then opens, gathering together everything said so far about all four truths at once. A question is raised: if every one of the four truths alike calls for complete understanding, why does the Buddha speak of complete understanding specifically in connection with the truth of suffering alone? The truth of suffering, it is answered, calls for understanding in two distinct respects, both its own particular characteristic and the characteristics it shares with the other truths. Suffering's own particular characteristic is simply being suffering itself, while impermanence stands as a characteristic shared by suffering, origin, and the path together, and emptiness and selflessness stand as characteristics shared by all four truths without exception; understanding suffering fully therefore requires taking in all four aspects together, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, and it is precisely this fourfold, comprehensive scope that earns suffering alone the name of complete understanding. Each of the remaining three truths, by contrast, need only be understood through its own particular characteristic: origin through the aspects of cause, accumulation, arising, and condition; cessation through the aspects of cessation, peace, wonder, and liberation; and the path through the aspects of path, suchness, practice, and emergence. It is for this reason that only the truth of suffering is said to be that which calls for complete understanding.

Chinese Source

問：一切四諦皆應遍知，何緣但說苦諦是應遍知？答：由於苦諦以二種相應遍知故，所謂自相及與共相。於所餘諦但知自相。謂以因等行知集諦自相，以滅等行知滅諦自相，以道等行知道諦自相。是故唯說苦諦是應遍知。

Ru-Yi Translation

If all four truths call for complete understanding, why is this said of suffering alone? Answer: suffering must be understood through both its own particular characteristic and the characteristics shared with the others, while each remaining truth need only be understood through its own particular characteristic alone. It is for this reason that suffering alone is called that which calls for complete understanding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this distinction between a particular characteristic and a shared one as this section's own elegant explanation for a genuinely puzzling asymmetry in how the four truths are traditionally described, showing that suffering was never actually privileged over the others in importance but simply carries a broader descriptive burden, since impermanence, emptiness, and selflessness all run through it as well as through its own unique nature as suffering itself. He highlights the neat correspondence between this fourfold understanding of suffering and the eleven views already explained at such length, the very same framework of shared and particular characteristics quietly organizing everything this whole section has covered so far.

22. Section: Severing the Cause Already Reveals the Effect's Own Severing

A further question then asks why, if the truth of suffering should likewise be permanently ended, only the truth of origin is said to call for permanent severing. Because permanently severing origin, it is answered, already reveals suffering's own permanent ending as well, only origin is spoken of in this way, since origin serves as suffering's own cause and suffering as origin's own effect, and once a cause has genuinely been cut off, its effect is thereby shown to have ended along with it, with no further need to speak separately of ending the effect itself. This is the very reasoning behind the classic teaching that suffering is to be fully known, origin permanently severed, cessation permanently realized, and the path permanently cultivated. A further topic then opens: direct realization itself, briefly divided into two kinds, the direct realization of wisdom and the direct realization of severance.

Chinese Source

問：苦諦亦應永斷，何緣唯說集諦是應永斷？答：由集諦永斷能顯苦諦永斷，是故唯說集諦是應永斷。復次，於諸諦中，略有二種現觀。一、智現觀，二、斷現觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

If suffering should likewise be permanently ended, why is only origin said to call for permanent severing? Answer: because severing origin already reveals suffering's own ending as well, origin alone is spoken of this way, being suffering's cause. A further topic opens: direct realization, briefly two kinds — of wisdom, and of severance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this explanation, that severing a cause already reveals its effect's own ending without any further separate action required, as a fitting piece of practical economy running throughout this entire section, this treatise consistently preferring to name only what a practitioner must actually do, severing craving at its root, over cataloguing every downstream consequence that follows automatically once that root work is genuinely accomplished. He notes that the classic fourfold formula, knowing suffering, severing origin, realizing cessation, and cultivating the path, now stands fully justified by everything this section has explained, each of its four verbs matched precisely to what that particular truth alone actually requires.

23. Section: The Wisdom That Realizes, and Five Causes of Genuine Diligence

Direct realization itself, briefly, comprises two kinds. The direct realization of wisdom refers to the particular wisdom arising, in strict sequence, with respect to each of the sixteen aspects belonging to the four truths in turn, the patient acceptance and full wisdom proper to suffering, then to origin, then to cessation, and then to the path, first with respect to the desire realm directly seen and then, by inference, with respect to the form and formless realms as well. And the direct realization of severance refers to the actual extinguishing of affliction that results, relying on this very same undistorted wisdom as it arises in that same sequence, wisdom and severance thus naming two inseparable sides of one and the same breakthrough. A further topic then opens: five causes, briefly, that give rise to genuine diligence. The first is expounding the true teaching, whether by actually teaching it oneself, feeling the genuine humility that comes from measuring one's own incomplete understanding against a noble one's own full realization, or by attentively hearing it taught by another, an effort in itself for anyone whose own capacity is not yet equal to the teaching's full depth. The second is dwelling together with fellow practitioners, mutual encouragement and example proving far more sustaining than solitary effort ever could. The third is observing laziness's own genuine faults and diligence's own genuine merits, this clear-eyed comparison itself spurring continued effort. The fourth is the maturing power of sustained reflection, a mind that keeps turning the true teaching over again and again eventually finding diligence arising almost on its own. And the fifth is recognizing the real difference between what one has already attained and what remains yet to be attained, this same recognized progress itself becoming the very engine driving a practitioner further still.

Chinese Source

智現觀者，謂隨次第，於諸諦中別相智生。斷現觀者，謂隨次第，無倒智生為依止故，證得所有煩惱斷滅。復次，略有五種發起正精進因緣。一、宣說正法；二、修行共住；三、察懈怠過失，見精進功德；四、由思擇力相續成熟；五、知所證得前後差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Direct realization of wisdom is the particular wisdom arising with respect to each of the sixteen aspects in sequence. Direct realization of severance is affliction's own extinguishing, relying on that same undistorted wisdom. Five causes give rise to genuine diligence: expounding the teaching, dwelling with fellow practitioners, observing laziness's faults and diligence's merits, sustained reflection's maturing power, and recognizing one's own progress.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the inseparability of wisdom-realization and severance-realization as this section's own important clarification that genuine insight and the actual removal of affliction are never two separate achievements requiring two separate efforts, the same undistorted wisdom accomplishing both at once. He highlights the fifth cause of diligence, recognizing one's own real progress, as a genuinely encouraging teaching for any long-term student, suggesting that even simply noticing how much easier a familiar teaching has become to follow over repeated study is itself a legitimate and effective source of continued motivation.

24. Section: Passing through a Hundred Flowers, Not a Single Petal Clings

A further topic then opens: two distinct kinds of realized severance. The first is realized suppression of a seed, affliction's seed merely held in check for a time through the power of concentration without being permanently uprooted, a capacity common to noble ones and ordinary practitioners alike. The second is realized permanent severing of a seed, the very seed itself, and with it every future possibility of that affliction ever arising again, entirely and permanently eliminated, an attainment belonging to noble ones alone. Freedom from bondage, one should further know, likewise comprises two kinds. The first is freedom from bondage with respect to each particular category of affliction, severing craving, wrong view, and every other distinct kind of affliction one by one according to its own type. And the second is freedom from bondage with respect to whatever matter affliction actually attaches itself to, whether the sentient or insentient objects that afflictions take as their occasion, or the very bodily, verbal, and mental conduct bound up with them, a noble one who has attained this second freedom no longer forming any real attachment even while directly encountering such objects, exactly as one classic image describes a mind passing straight through a hundred flowers without a single petal ever managing to cling. With this the entire explanation of the four noble truths has now been given in full.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Realized severance comprises two kinds: realized suppression of a seed, common to ordinary and noble alike, and realized permanent severing of a seed, belonging to noble ones alone. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the classic image of passing through a hundred flowers without a single petal clinging as one of this treatise's most beloved and immediately graspable illustrations of what freedom from bondage with respect to an object actually looks like from the inside, not avoidance of experience but full, unobstructed contact with it that simply leaves no residue behind. He highlights the distinction between merely suppressing an affliction's seed and permanently severing it as this whole extensive explanation's own final and most practically important reminder, since a practitioner who mistakes temporary calm, achieved through concentration alone, for genuine and complete liberation risks discovering the difference only when that same seed eventually manifests again.

25. Section: Twelve Faults the Buddha Saw in Flattery and Deceit

A further topic then opens: the fifth of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, explaining the fault of flattery and deceit. As the World-Honored One himself taught, monastics should never engage in flattery or deceit. A question is raised: how many faults did the Tathāgata himself observe in such crooked, flattering conduct, that he should caution his monastic disciples against it so directly? Twelve such faults, briefly, are given in answer. The first is that flattery prevents a practitioner from ever attaining genuine wisdom. The second is the loss of one's own good reputation. The third is the loss of others' trust. The fourth is the failure of one's own merit to continue growing. The fifth is losing access to hearing the true teaching and receiving genuine instruction from the wise, since a teacher deceived can no longer be relied upon to teach in turn. The sixth is the growth of every kind of unwholesome quality. The seventh is a mind that gradually drifts ever further from wholesome states the longer such flattery continues. The eighth is a mind thrown off balance and genuinely harmed by its own crookedness, left in constant, unsettled distress. The ninth is anxious worry over the suffering such conduct will bring in a future life. The tenth is becoming altogether unfit to serve as a vessel for the noble teaching. The eleventh is bitter regret arising at the very moment of death. And the twelfth is that, once the body itself has broken apart, such a being falls into a miserable destiny, born into the hells themselves.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The World-Honored One taught monastics never to engage in flattery or deceit. How many faults did the Tathāgata observe in it? Twelve: it prevents genuine wisdom, loses reputation and others' trust, halts merit's growth, loses access to the wise, grows unwholesome states, drifts from wholesome ones, brings inner turmoil, future worry, unfitness for the teaching, deathbed regret, and rebirth in the hells.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twelvefold catalogue as this treatise's own remarkably comprehensive case against a fault often dismissed as merely a minor social failing, tracing its consequences from the immediately practical, lost trust and lost access to genuine teaching, through the psychological, constant inner unease, all the way to the ultimate, rebirth in the hells themselves. He highlights the fifth fault especially, losing access to a wise teacher's own instruction, as this whole list's own most self-defeating consequence, since flattery aimed at winning a teacher's favor in the short term ultimately forfeits the very relationship it was meant to secure.

26. Section: Eight Conducts and Seven Matters of Flattery in the Desire Realm

Flattery within the desire realm, it is further explained, unfolds through eight distinct conducts arising in connection with seven distinct matters. The eight conducts are named as follows: confusing conduct, presenting what is contrary to the true teaching as though it accorded with it, or the reverse, in order to mislead another; concealing conduct, hiding the real fault behind one's own wrongdoing; false-display conduct, presenting some genuine virtue one does not actually possess; faction-forming conduct, gathering allies during a dispute in order to overwhelm an opponent and deflect blame; submissive conduct, feigning humble deference toward someone feared for their power or violence; scheming conduct, presenting a false appearance of trustworthiness in order to exploit someone genuinely honest and unsuspecting; shirking conduct, feigning eager partnership in a friend's future undertaking only to invent excuses when the actual labor finally arrives; and grief-feigning conduct, exaggerating one's own suffering before an adversary far beyond what is genuinely felt. The seven matters in connection with which these conducts arise are named in turn: the matter of speech itself; the matter of being questioned or challenged; the matter of open dispute; the matter of presenting oneself as a friend; the matter of presenting oneself as trustworthy; the matter of finding a pretext to avoid some undertaking; and the matter of hardship genuinely or falsely endured.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The eight conducts: confusing, concealing, false-display, faction-forming, submissive, scheming, shirking, and grief-feigning. The seven matters: speech, being questioned, open dispute, presenting friendship, presenting trustworthiness, finding a pretext, and hardship.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this eightfold and sevenfold cataloguing as this treatise's own remarkably fine-grained taxonomy of a fault often lumped together as simple insincerity, distinguishing conducts that mislead through speech from conducts that mislead through concealment, display, alliance, submission, scheming, evasion, and exaggerated emotion as genuinely distinct strategies each requiring its own particular vigilance to recognize. He notes that the fuller explanation matching each conduct to its corresponding matter still remains to be read, this section offering only the two complete lists as a foundation for that more detailed pairing.

27. Section: The Monk Who Would Not Tell a Killer That Killing Was Wrong

The matter of speech itself is now matched to confusing conduct. Whatever a flattering person of the desire realm actually says to the world at large, such a person may present what is contrary to the true teaching as though it accorded with it, thereby misleading the listener, or, just as easily, present what genuinely accords with the true teaching as though it did not; and the same pattern holds equally for presenting what genuinely exists as though it did not exist, or the reverse. As one classic story illustrates this very fault, a lay follower whose own livelihood depended on killing once came to a monastery to pay his respects, and the resident monk, unwilling to offend so generous a donor, never told him plainly that killing carried genuine karmic fault. When this same follower later died and fell into the hells, and afterward became a hungry ghost, he returned in anger to trouble that very monk, demanding to know why he had never once been warned. A teacher who withholds the true teaching purely to flatter a donor, seeking only continued support rather than the donor's own genuine welfare, thereby fails at the very heart of a teacher's own responsibility; and the same holds for any teacher facing a student's own fault, since remaining silent out of misplaced kindness betrays the Buddha's own teaching even if the student, once told, still fails to change, the telling itself being what genuine responsibility actually requires, timed wisely to when it can actually be heard, but never simply withheld out of a desire to please.

Chinese Source

由初事故，其諂曲者與諸世間隨起言說，於非義中示現為義，以相迷惑；或於義中示現非義。如於義非義，於有非有，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through this first matter, a flattering person, speaking with the world, presents what is contrary to the true teaching as though it accorded with it, thereby misleading the listener, or presents what genuinely accords with it as though it did not. The same holds for presenting what exists as though it did not, and the reverse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this story's own uncomfortable honesty about clerical failure, a monk's own well-meaning desire not to offend a donor directly costing that same donor a warning that might have spared him genuine suffering, as this whole section's own most memorable and cautionary illustration, a reminder that flattery's harm reaches well beyond the flatterer's own conscience into the very future of the person being flattered. He highlights the closing instruction, that a teacher's genuine duty is simply to speak the truth wisely timed rather than to guarantee its reception, as an unusually practical piece of pastoral guidance, freeing a conscientious teacher from the impossible burden of controlling another's response while still holding them fully responsible for their own honesty.

28. Section: Six More Faces of Flattery, from Concealed Faults to Feigned Grief

The remaining six matters are then matched to their own corresponding conducts in turn. The matter of being questioned corresponds to both concealing and false-display conduct together: whenever another challenges the very wrongdoing a flattering person's own crooked behavior has produced, such a person conceals the genuine fault involved and, going further still, presents an entirely false appearance of virtue in its place. The matter of open dispute corresponds to faction-forming conduct: when a dispute threatens to expose genuine merit and fault alike, a flattering person gathers allies into a faction, using sheer numbers to overwhelm and silence whoever stands weaker, rather than allowing the plain truth to come to light. The matter of presenting friendship corresponds to submissive conduct: encountering an opponent feared for real power or violence, a flattering person, gripped by genuine terror, adopts an outwardly humble bearing and feigns warm friendship purely to appease that same feared opponent. The matter of presenting trustworthiness corresponds to scheming conduct: spotting someone genuinely gentle and honest, ripe for exploitation, a flattering person, though genuinely untrustworthy within, carefully cultivates an outward appearance of impeccable, reliable virtue precisely in order to win that same person's trust and later profit unlawfully from it. The matter of finding a pretext corresponds to shirking conduct: eagerly volunteering as a partner in a close friend's future undertaking, a flattering person later invents whatever excuse proves necessary the moment real labor is actually required, purely to spare personal effort. And the matter of hardship corresponds to grief-feigning conduct: upon encountering some genuine misfortune, a flattering person, before whoever caused that misfortune, displays far greater sorrow and distress than is actually felt, sighing, lamenting, and even collapsing outright, precisely in order to unsettle the very person being addressed.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

When questioned about wrongdoing, a flattering person conceals the real fault and displays a false virtue instead. When a dispute threatens to expose merit and fault, such a person gathers allies into a faction. Facing a feared, powerful opponent, such a person adopts a humble bearing and feigns warm friendship out of genuine terror.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the scheming conduct aimed specifically at someone genuinely gentle and honest as this whole catalogue's own most morally troubling entry, since it deliberately targets precisely the trust a good-hearted person extends most readily, turning their own decency into the very vulnerability being exploited. He highlights the closing advice implicit in the matter of hardship, that a listener hearing only one side of a dispute should hesitate before fully believing exaggerated grief, as a practical caution against being manipulated by exactly this same conduct oneself, a fitting close to a section devoted to recognizing flattery's own many disguises.

29. Section: Not Even Fit to Receive the Teaching — Opening the Coarse-Spoken Śrāvaka

Gathering together everything just explained, the eight conducts match the seven matters in turn: confusing conduct arises in the matter of speech; concealing conduct in the matter of being questioned; false-display conduct as simply praising oneself for virtue never actually attained; faction-forming conduct in the matter of open dispute, gathering allies the moment opinions diverge; submissive conduct in the matter of presenting friendship, feigned out of fear of another's power; scheming conduct in the matter of presenting trustworthiness, in order to profit from another's own genuine goodwill; shirking conduct in the matter of finding a pretext, evading whatever labor was once promised; and grief-feigning conduct in the matter of hardship, exaggerating one's own suffering when reporting it to others. With this the full explanation of flattery and deceit's own faults, the fifth of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, comes to its close. A further topic then opens: the sixth topic, explaining the coarse-spoken śrāvaka. Such a practitioner, it is bluntly stated at the outset, does not even deserve to receive instruction and training in the four noble truths at all, let alone actually attain direct realization of the truths or any genuine purity of mind. Nine distinct marks, briefly, characterize such coarse speech, and whoever is found to possess them should be known as a śrāvaka genuinely unfit to be spoken with or taught at all. The first mark appears when someone rightly charges such a person with wrongdoing: rather than answering honestly, the coarse-spoken one replies with harsh, unpleasant words, or with an answer that fails to actually address the charge, or with an answer so unclear that it cannot even be understood, or simply changes the subject entirely, seizing on some unrelated matter to avoid the question altogether. And the second mark is resorting to some absurd, manifestly false claim as a further pretext for evading the very charge being pressed.

Chinese Source

復次，麤語聲聞，尚不應得於諸諦中教誡教授，況當能得真諦現觀，或復清淨。又有九種麤語聲聞麤語之相。謂能舉罪補特伽羅正詰問時，以不美言假合而答，或不相應、或不圓滿、或託餘事方便而答。是名初相。又以謬言假設餘論，方便推遣所詰問事。是第二相。

Ru-Yi Translation

A coarse-spoken śrāvaka does not even deserve instruction in the four noble truths, let alone genuine realization or purity. Nine marks characterize such speech. The first: when rightly challenged, answering with unpleasant words, an irrelevant or unclear answer, or changing the subject. The second: resorting to an absurd, false claim to evade the charge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the blunt opening judgment, that a coarse-spoken practitioner does not even deserve to hear the four noble truths taught at all, as this treatise's own unusually severe warning about how a defensive, evasive response to legitimate correction can actually forfeit access to the very teaching capable of helping most. He highlights the first two marks together as tracing a single, recognizable pattern many people will find uncomfortably familiar, deflecting a fair challenge first through vague or hostile evasion and then, if that fails, through outright fabrication rather than simply admitting fault.

30. Section: Anger, Concealment, and the Grudge That Never Lets Go

The remaining marks of coarse speech are then explained in turn. The third mark is a mind genuinely disturbed by the entanglement of anger, thrown into confusion and turbid disorder the moment a fault is named. The fourth mark is that same entangling anger giving rise outright to resentful complaint, abusive cursing, and vulgar, degrading language. The fifth mark is a mind swollen with arrogance, reasoning that since another has already accused oneself of wrongdoing, one is equally entitled to accuse that same person in return. The sixth mark is stubbornly and firmly concealing one's own actual wrongdoing rather than ever genuinely acknowledging it. The seventh mark is harboring a resentful grudge that persists without ever being released, nursing the very enmity a fair challenge provoked rather than reflecting honestly on one's own fault. And the eighth mark is repeatedly giving rise to an outright wish for revenge against whoever first raised the challenge.

Ru-Yi Translation

The third mark: anger disturbing and confusing the mind. The fourth: anger giving rise to cursing and vulgar language. The fifth: arrogantly reasoning one may accuse in return. The sixth: stubbornly concealing one's own wrongdoing. The seventh: a grudge that never lets go. The eighth: repeatedly wishing for revenge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this escalating sequence, from an initially disturbed mind through outright cursing, tit-for-tat reasoning, stubborn concealment, and finally a lingering grudge ripening into an active wish for revenge, as tracing a single fault's own natural progression when left completely unchecked at every earlier stage. He highlights the fifth mark's own reasoning, that being accused somehow licenses accusing in return, as this whole list's own clearest example of a self-justifying logic that feels intuitively fair in the moment yet leads a practitioner steadily further from the honest self-examination genuine correction actually requires.

31. Section: Refusing to Believe Even Genuine Virtue Closes the Ninth Mark

The ninth and final mark of coarse speech is then given: when another plainly points out some genuine or even doubtful virtue belonging to the very person who first raised the charge, the coarse-spoken one refuses to accept it at all, instead slandering, denying, and reviling whatever virtue that accuser actually possesses. With these nine marks complete, one should know two distinct kinds of accuser stand behind this whole ninefold pattern: one genuinely accomplished and respected by the community at large, and one not accomplished in this same way, lacking the community's shared respect. The first two marks, still answering however evasively when challenged, belong specifically to an exchange with the first, respected kind of accuser, since even a genuinely displeased practitioner still musters some reply before someone truly worthy of respect; but the remaining seven marks, from disturbed anger through outright slander, belong specifically to an exchange with the second kind, an accuser not universally respected, whose own imperfections make the charge easier to resent and resist. For this reason, the Buddha taught that whoever raises a charge against another should possess five qualities in turn: knowing the right time, speaking only what is genuinely true, acting from real benefit to the other, speaking gently, and holding a compassionate mind throughout, since a charge raised without these same qualities all too easily provokes exactly this kind of resentful, defensive reaction rather than genuine correction. As one great modern teacher accordingly counseled his own students, it is wiser to regard every other person as a bodhisattva and oneself alone as the ordinary being still needing correction, learning from whatever another does rightly and, upon seeing another's fault, examining first whether that same fault lies hidden within oneself as well. With this the coarse-spoken śrāvaka, the sixth of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, comes to its close.

Ru-Yi Translation

The ninth mark: refusing to believe another's genuine or doubtful virtue, slandering it instead. Two kinds of accuser stand behind these marks: one respected by the community, and one not. The first two marks belong to an exchange with the respected accuser; the remaining seven, to an exchange with the accuser not universally respected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of the accused's ninefold pattern with the accuser's own fivefold requirement, timing, truth, benefit, gentleness, and compassion, as this whole topic's own balanced final teaching, refusing to let either party off the hook entirely, a hostile response is never justified yet a poorly delivered accusation genuinely invites it. He highlights the closing counsel to regard every other person as a bodhisattva and oneself alone as the one needing correction as an unusually practical antidote to precisely the defensive spiral this entire ninefold list has just traced, redirecting attention from another's fault back toward one's own growth before resentment ever has the chance to take root.

32. Section: Opening the Vinaya's Own Characteristics, Seven Categories in All

A further topic then opens: the seventh of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, explaining the vinaya's own many characteristics, a topic substantial enough to unfold across thirteen further divisions of its own, summarized in a single memorial verse covering encompassing categories, formal establishment, the moral discipline itself, heedfulness, obstruction, training, contemplation, dependence, acceptance, actual use, its own profound depth, its manner of teaching, and illustrative example. The first of these thirteen divisions explains the seven categories that together encompass the entire vinaya and the code of individual liberation alike: general exhortation, explicit permission, explicit prohibition, the very locus where a violation occurs, what actually constitutes a violation, what does not, and how a committed violation is confessed and purified. General exhortation refers to the World-Honored One's own broad instruction within the vinaya, that monastics should abandon every unwholesome quality and cultivate every wholesome one, this same brief teaching, however simply stated, carrying boundless depth once fully unfolded. And explicit permission refers to whatever the World-Honored One openly allows within the vinaya, namely making use of robes, food, lodging, and medicine and other genuine requisites for living, whenever real need calls for them, so long as the mind receiving them remains entirely free of defilement.

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly, seven categories encompass the vinaya and the code of individual liberation: exhortation, permission, prohibition, the locus of violation, what constitutes violation, what does not, and confession. Exhortation is the instruction to abandon the unwholesome and cultivate the wholesome. Permission is making genuine use of requisites without any defiled mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this topic's own scale, a full thirteen further divisions summarized in a single memorial verse, as fair warning that this treatise is about to give the vinaya a treatment as careful and systematic as anything it has offered any other subject, despite this whole work's own primary emphasis lying elsewhere, in concentration and wisdom. He highlights the qualifier attached to permission, that requisites may be used only with a mind entirely free of defilement, as a fitting reminder that even explicitly sanctioned conduct still depends, in this treatise's own consistent view, on the inner quality of the mind engaging in it rather than on outward rule-following alone.

33. Section: Faults Wrong by Nature, and Faults the Buddha Chose to Forbid

Explicit prohibition is then explained: whatever the World-Honored One prohibits within the vinaya, both wrongdoing that is intrinsically wrong by its own very nature and conduct that, while not intrinsically wrong in itself, still runs contrary to wholesome practice. A fault intrinsically wrong by nature, such as killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, or lying, remains genuinely wrong whether or not the Buddha ever specifically prohibited it at all, its own wrongness inherent to the deed itself; a fault that instead runs contrary to wholesome practice, such as the precept against alcohol, carries no such inherent wrongness in itself, yet the Buddha nonetheless prohibited it precisely because indulging in it tends to weaken a practitioner's guard over every other precept, making unintentional violation of graver matters considerably more likely, so that violating even this second kind of precept still constitutes a genuine transgression once the Buddha has explicitly forbidden it.

Chinese Source

云何制止？謂佛世尊毗奈耶中，制止一切自性罪法、違無罪法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is explicit prohibition? It refers to whatever the World-Honored One prohibits within the vinaya, both what is intrinsically wrong by its own nature and conduct that, while not intrinsically wrong, still runs contrary to wholesome practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between intrinsically wrong conduct and conduct merely prohibited for its own protective value as a genuinely useful piece of ethical clarity, explaining why breaking the alcohol precept and breaking the precept against killing carry such different weight even though both count as genuine violations once the Buddha has spoken. He highlights the underlying logic of the alcohol precept especially, prohibited not for any wrongness in itself but for how reliably it erodes the very vigilance every other precept depends upon, as a fitting example of this treatise's own consistently practical, psychologically astute approach to monastic discipline.

34. Section: Eighteen Places a Violation Can Occur, from the Bowl to a Goose's Own Life

The locus where a violation actually occurs is then explained, briefly eighteen in number. These are: what is intrinsically unwholesome; what runs contrary to wholesome conduct; violation through bodily, verbal, or mental action; the ruin of precepts themselves; the ruin of right view, mistaking bare practice for a substitute for genuine precept-keeping; the ruin of proper deportment; the ruin of right livelihood; violation committed while accommodating another's own feelings, as when a monk once yielded to his own mother's and former wife's wishes before the precept against sexual conduct had even been formally established; violation committed while protecting another being from harm, as when a monk once denied having seen a goose swallow a jewel purely to protect that same goose's life, thereby breaking the precept against false speech instead; violation arising from genuine doubt in an ambiguous situation, not yet having studied the precepts thoroughly enough to know what is actually permitted or forbidden; violations connected with sexual matters more broadly, beyond the root offense itself; and violations connected, in turn, with the bowl, the robe, food, bedding, and medicine, the very ordinary requisites of daily monastic life through which craving, aversion, and delusion so easily find their opening. Every violation belonging to bodily, verbal, or mental action, together with every one of the remaining, more particular loci just described, should further be understood as falling somewhere among the five categories into which all offenses are gathered, the unpardonable category, the serious yet pardonable category, the category leading to a miserable rebirth, the category confessable individually, and the category of a minor misdeed confessable directly.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the monk who broke his precept against false speech purely to save a goose from being killed for the jewel it had swallowed as a genuinely poignant illustration of how even a well-intentioned, compassionate act can still constitute a real violation, this treatise refusing to soften the letter of the precept even where the underlying motive was entirely good. He highlights the ruin of right view, mistaking mere practice for a substitute for actually keeping the precepts, as a fault with particular relevance beyond the monastic context as well, a reminder that meditative or contemplative effort was never meant to excuse a practitioner from ordinary ethical discipline.

35. Section: Five Categories Gathering Every Possible Offense

Every violation among these eighteen loci, whether arising through the body, speech, or mind, or through any of the remaining, more particular circumstances already described, is further sorted among five categories gathering all offenses without exception. The unpardonable category names an offense so grave that a practitioner, already overcome by their own affliction, permanently forfeits monastic standing, admitting of no path back to purity at all. The serious yet pardonable category names an offense grave enough to resemble someone gravely wounded yet not beyond all hope, still capable, through the assembly's own formal proceedings, of being purified and restored. The category leading to a miserable rebirth names an offense whose committing draws a being toward the hells themselves. The individually confessable category names an offense purified simply by confessing it, one by one, to a fellow practitioner. And the category of a minor misdeed confessable directly names the lightest offenses, purified merely by an honest, direct acknowledgment. With this the locus of violation has now been fully explained. A further topic then opens: what actually constitutes a violation, briefly gathered, once again, into these same five categories of offense.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodily, verbal, and mental violations, and every other locus described, should be understood as falling among the five categories of offense as appropriate. What constitutes violation? Briefly, it too is gathered into these same five categories.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold classification of offenses, from the single unpardonable category down to the lightest misdeed confessable directly, as this treatise's own consistent and compassionate architecture running throughout its entire treatment of monastic discipline, offering a genuine path back to purity for every offense except the very gravest, a structure already encountered in earlier fascicles now applied specifically to this eighteenfold map of where violations actually tend to occur. He notes that the same fivefold categories will now be used again to explain precisely what counts as a genuine violation in the first place, this treatise consistently reusing its own established frameworks rather than introducing an entirely new system for each fresh question.

36. Section: Five Causes of Violation, and Nine Kinds of Person Who Can Commit One

Why do violations actually occur among these five categories of offense at all? Briefly, through five causes: the underlying motivating condition, whether craving, aversion, or delusion, that first inclines a practitioner toward wrongdoing; the actual initiation of the deed through bodily or verbal action; the particular matter involved, whether a sentient being or some insentient object; the very method or approach by which the offense is carried out; and its full completion, the deed brought all the way to its own intended conclusion. A fuller analysis of this same fivefold distinction, examined through the causal condition, the arising, the actual matter transgressed, the applied effort, and the completion together, is given at greater length elsewhere in this treatise's own further discussion of monastic discipline. A further topic then opens: violation considered by way of the person committing it, nine kinds of practitioner in all, distinguished by which precepts each has actually undertaken. These are the lay man, the lay woman, the male novice, the female novice, the female probationer, the fully ordained nun, the fully ordained monk, and, cutting finally across ordinary and noble alike, the ordinary unawakened being and the noble learner still on the path.

Chinese Source

謂五因故。一、因緣故；二、發起故；三、事故；四、方便故；五、究竟故。復有九種犯。一、近事男犯，二、近事女犯，三、勤策男犯，四、勤策女犯，五、正學犯，六、苾芻尼犯，七、苾芻犯，八、異生犯，九、有學犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Violations arise through five causes: the underlying motivating condition, the actual initiation through body or speech, the particular matter involved, the method employed, and the deed's own completion. A further nine kinds of person can commit a violation: the lay man and woman, the male and female novice, the female probationer, the nun, the monk, and, across ordinary and noble alike, the unawakened being and the noble learner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold analysis of how a violation actually unfolds, motivation, initiation, matter, method, and completion, as this treatise's own careful reminder that a genuine offense requires the full sequence to actually play out, a stray thought or an interrupted attempt alone not yet constituting the complete violation these categories describe. He highlights the inclusion of the noble learner alongside the ordinary being in this ninefold list as a striking claim worth watching closely, since it suggests that even someone who has already broken through to the truth remains capable, in some sense, of violation, a claim the text goes on to illustrate with a remarkable story in what follows.

37. Section: The Arhat Wrongly Imprisoned, and a Single Thought That Moved the Spirits

A story preserved in the great Abhidharma commentaries illustrates precisely how even a noble learner, not yet an arhat, can still commit a genuine violation through nothing more than an unexpressed thought. An elderly arhat monk was once dyeing robes beneath a tree, the dye simmering in a pot nearby, when a farmer searching for a lost cow happened upon him and mistook the contents of that same pot for the butchered remains of his missing animal. Enraged, and without pausing to investigate further, the farmer reported the monk to the king, who, through the working of some past karma, had him imprisoned without any inquiry at all; the arhat himself, though genuinely possessing supernatural powers capable of proving his own innocence at once, simply forgot, through that same karmic working, to ever use them, and his own fellow monastics, equally affected, never so much as noticed his absence for several years. When his community finally realized the old monk had vanished and eventually traced him to the king's own prison, they explained to the king that a genuine arhat could never possibly commit such a deed, and the king, himself a sincere follower of the Buddha's teaching, immediately released him, restoring his supernatural powers along with his freedom and offering, in genuine remorse, to personally support him from then on, an offer the arhat graciously accepted. At this very moment, however, a noble learner nearby, not yet an arhat, thought to himself how wrong the king had been to imprison so genuine an arhat through such careless negligence, and the very spirits who protect noble ones, moved by this same learner's own unspoken indignation, went on to punish the king accordingly, entirely apart from anything the learner himself had actually said or done. This is precisely what is meant by a noble learner's own violation, a genuine ripple set in motion by nothing more than a single unexpressed thought, however righteous its underlying intention may have felt in the moment, a subtlety of consequence an arhat, having permanently exhausted every trace of reactive affliction, could never produce at all.

Chinese Source

（如《大毘婆沙論》所載阿羅漢被誣入獄，及有學聖者因一念不平，令護法諸鬼神代為降罰國王之公案，說明有學犯之相。）

Ru-Yi Translation

An arhat wrongly imprisoned for a farmer's lost cow, later freed and restored once his innocence became clear, illustrates that even a noble learner nearby, merely thinking the king's negligence unjust, could set protective spirits in motion to punish the king, purely through an unspoken thought — the very subtlety that constitutes a learner's own kind of violation, one an arhat could never produce.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this story's own quiet insistence, that even a righteously indignant thought on someone else's behalf still counts as a kind of violation for a noble learner, as one of this treatise's most humbling teachings, undercutting the ordinary confidence that taking someone's side against an obvious wrong is simply and straightforwardly good. He highlights the sheer subtlety of the mechanism involved, protective spirits acting on an unspoken thought rather than any word or deed at all, as a vivid illustration of just how much a practitioner's own inner life continues to matter long after outward conduct has already become impeccable, the noble learner's own remaining task lying entirely within the territory of thought itself.

38. Section: Why an Arhat Can Never Truly Violate a Precept

No arhat, by contrast, ever commits a genuinely defiled violation at all. Why is this so? Because an arhat has nothing further left to accomplish, every task of severing affliction already completed, such a being naturally and spontaneously ceases to violate even the smallest and most minor precepts, let alone anything graver, and most certainly never commits any defiled offense whatsoever. As one passage elsewhere confirms, a monk whose every outflow has been permanently exhausted can never again approach five particular things: deliberately taking the life of any sentient being, taking what has not been freely given, engaging in any act of sexual conduct, knowingly speaking falsely, particularly regarding one's own spiritual attainment, and hoarding sensory requisites for personal indulgence.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

No arhat commits a genuinely defiled violation, since with nothing further to accomplish, even the smallest precepts naturally cease to be violated. A monk whose outflows are exhausted can never approach five things: deliberately taking life, taking what is not given, sexual conduct, knowingly speaking falsely, or hoarding requisites.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the phrase "nothing further to accomplish" as this section's own precise explanation for why an arhat's own precept-keeping requires no ongoing vigilance at all, moral effort itself belonging properly to the path still being traveled rather than to a destination already and permanently reached. He highlights the direct contrast with the noble learner's own subtler violation just described as this whole passage's own carefully constructed spectrum, from an ordinary being's outright transgression, through a learner's still-lingering reactive thought, to an arhat's own complete and effortless freedom from every one of these possibilities at once.

39. Section: Four Situations That Never Count as a Genuine Violation

An arhat, finally, never hoards sensory requisites purely for personal indulgence, keeping no surplus provisions set aside out of any lingering worldly concern at all. What does not count as a genuine violation is then explained, briefly four situations in all. The first is a first offense, committed by the very first person to do so before the Buddha had yet established the corresponding precept at all, since no rule could possibly be broken before it existed, this same first instance sometimes even deliberately enacted by certain especially skillful disciples precisely in order to prompt the Buddha into formally establishing a needed rule, itself a kind of teaching rather than a genuine fault. The second is genuine insanity, an offense committed while the mind itself is diseased and no longer functioning as it should. The third is a mind thrown into complete disorder, so overwhelmed by affliction in the moment that no real culpability attaches to whatever results. And the fourth is conduct compelled by overwhelming physical pain, a practitioner driven to violate a precept purely because genuinely unbearable suffering left no other option available at all. Though each of these four situations does involve conduct that would otherwise constitute a violation, none of them, for the specific reasons just given, is ever actually counted as one.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

An arhat never hoards sensory requisites for indulgence. What does not count as genuine violation? Briefly, four situations: a first offense before the precept existed, genuine insanity, a mind thrown into complete disorder, and conduct compelled by overwhelming pain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first situation, an offense committed before the corresponding precept even existed, as a genuinely elegant piece of legal reasoning, a rule simply cannot be broken before it has been established, and notes with real appreciation the tradition that certain disciples deliberately modeled such first offenses precisely so the Buddha would then establish the very rule the community needed, turning what looks superficially like misconduct into a genuine act of service. He highlights the four situations together as this treatise's own careful boundary around genuine moral responsibility, distinguishing conduct that merely resembles a violation on the surface from conduct that actually carries the culpability the term implies.

40. Section: Confessing Alone, Confessing before Another, and a Noble One's Own Small Duty

How a committed violation is confessed and thereby purified is then explained, briefly through five modes. Confessing through oneself alone applies to sufficiently minor offenses, purified simply through quietly acknowledging the fault to oneself and resolving never to repeat it. Confessing through another applies, more surprisingly, even to a noble one who has already directly realized the truths, since such a being, even when committing some entirely undefiled violation, perhaps pulling weeds in front of an image of the Buddha purely out of a genuinely good intention to keep the shrine clean, an act the precepts nonetheless forbid, still confesses this same violation before a fellow practitioner rather than merely settling the matter internally, precisely because such a noble one's own sense of conscience remains so acute that even a wholly well-intentioned technical infraction still calls for open acknowledgment. Confessing through both oneself and another applies to an ordinary being's own violations, whether defiled or undefiled, first quietly acknowledging the fault internally and then, depending on its severity, confessing it openly before anywhere from a single fellow practitioner to the entire assembled community, exactly as one classic teaching promises that a person who has committed some grave offense, having deeply reproached themselves and resolved never to repeat it, can thereby uproot even that offense's own underlying karmic root.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

How is a violation confessed and purified? Briefly, five modes. Confessing through oneself alone applies to minor offenses. Confessing through another applies even to a noble one's own undefiled violation. Confessing through both oneself and another applies to an ordinary being's defiled or undefiled offenses alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the example of a noble one pulling weeds before a Buddha image purely out of genuine care for the shrine's own cleanliness, and still confessing this same technically forbidden act before another, as a genuinely moving illustration of conscience operating at a level well beyond mere rule-following, a heart so thoroughly purified that even a well-intentioned technical lapse still feels worth openly acknowledging rather than quietly excusing. He highlights the promise that sincere self-reproach can uproot even a grave offense's own underlying karmic root as this whole section's own most hopeful teaching, confession here functioning not as mere ritual but as a genuinely transformative act capable of reaching all the way to a fault's own deepest cause.

41. Section: When a Monk Becomes a Nun Overnight, and Death Itself Dissolves All Fault

Confession through the transformation of one's own basis applies to the rare case in which a monk's own basis is suddenly relinquished and a nun's own basis spontaneously assumed instead, or the reverse, an occurrence the ancient monastic codes themselves record as having genuinely happened from time to time; whatever offenses had been committed under the former basis are thereby resolved according to the newly assumed basis, the same practitioner now simply transferred to the corresponding community and continuing to count their years of ordination from the very same original date, though should the transformation instead produce both sexual characteristics at once or neither at all, such a being can no longer remain within either community and must return to lay life altogether. And confession through relinquishing one's basis entirely applies simply to death itself, the very basis on which the precepts once rested now permanently given up, so that whatever unresolved offense still remained simply ceases, along with everything else, to continue at all. With this the seven categories together, exhortation, permission, prohibition, the locus of violation, what constitutes violation, what does not, and confession, have now fully explained everything the vinaya and the code of individual liberation together encompass, and the first of the vinaya's own thirteen further divisions comes to its close.

Ru-Yi Translation

Confession through transformation of basis applies when a monk's basis is relinquished and a nun's basis assumed, or the reverse, prior offenses resolved according to the newly assumed basis. Confession through relinquishing one's basis entirely applies simply to death itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the ancient records of monks spontaneously becoming nuns and nuns becoming monks as a genuinely striking piece of monastic legal history, the tradition's own willingness to actually legislate for so unusual an occurrence, preserving ordination seniority across the transition and reclassifying prior offenses according to the newly assumed basis, revealing a surprisingly practical and non-punitive spirit beneath what might otherwise seem like rigid rule-keeping. He highlights death itself as the simplest and most universal mode of resolution among all five, every remaining unresolved fault dissolving along with the very basis that once made fault possible at all, a fitting note on which this first of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions comes to rest.

42. Section: Ten Kinds of Purity the Precepts Establish, and Ten Genuine Benefits

A further topic then opens: the second of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining why the precepts were formally established in the first place, prompted, as tradition records, by Śāriputra's own observation, reached in deep meditation, that a teaching left without detailed precepts tends to fade from the world far sooner than one properly supported by them. Briefly, ten kinds of purity result from establishing the code of training: purity of underlying reason, since each precept answers a genuine occasion of misconduct; purity of fruit, since precepts support concentration, concentration supports wisdom, and wisdom brings the very fruit of the path; purity of unity, gathering the whole community under one shared standard the way traffic signals prevent collision; purity in the eyes of others, since visibly disciplined conduct inspires outsiders' own genuine respect; inward purity, since obedience to the precepts tames the senses and settles the mind; complete purity, whether of the precepts themselves, kept without partiality between grave and light, or of the community kept harmonious by their shared observance; purity of affection and respect, since mutual precept-keeping dissolves occasion for quarrel; purity through suppressing affliction, since precepts curb affliction's own manifestation; purity through severing its latent seeds, since precepts support the very concentration and wisdom capable of uprooting affliction entirely; and purity of unbroken continuity, since precepts sustain the true teaching's own survival across time. Ten genuine benefits flow from this same establishment in turn: gathering the community together; freeing genuinely earnest practitioners from excessive indulgence in the five sense pleasures; granting the community real ease by steering clear of pointless self-mortification as well; inspiring faith in those without it and drawing the yet-unconverted toward the teaching; deepening the faith of those who already believe; bringing even the most difficult to discipline under control, expelling only when genuinely necessary; granting the conscientious real peace of mind through confession; guarding against affliction's own present manifestation; uprooting affliction's future seed entirely; and, above all, ensuring that pure practice endures and spreads for the many, sustaining the true teaching itself across a long, unbroken span of time.

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly, ten kinds of purity result from establishing the training precepts: purity of underlying reason, of fruit, of unity, in others' eyes, inward purity, complete purity, of affection, through suppressing affliction, through severing its seeds, and of unbroken continuity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Śāriputra's own observation, that a teaching without detailed precepts tends to fade from the world far sooner, as this whole topic's own founding insight, explaining why so much of this treatise's remaining attention now turns to a subject, monastic discipline, that might otherwise seem secondary to concentration and wisdom. He highlights the final benefit, sustaining the true teaching across a long, unbroken span of time, as the thread quietly running through all nine benefits before it, every other purpose the precepts serve ultimately in service of this same larger, historical aim.

43. Section: Ten Faults of Broken Discipline, Ten Virtues of Discipline Kept

A further topic then opens: the third of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining the precepts' own faults when broken and virtues when genuinely kept, best understood together as ten direct opposites. Failing to keep the training brings the fault of a community unable to correct one another, while communal harmony brings the corresponding virtue of unity. Taking no genuine pleasure in pure precepts brings the fault of inconsistent practice, on again and off again, while genuine delight in them brings the virtue of unbroken observance sustained from ordination to death. Discord with one's own companions in practice brings the fault of enmity, while shared observance brings the virtue of freedom from hostility altogether. Betraying the very aspiration toward awakening first made at ordination brings the fault of an unfulfilled vow, while faithful practice brings the virtue of a vow left undamaged. Indulging one's own craving, aversion, and delusion brings the fault of heedlessness, while steady, diligent protection of the precepts brings the virtue of genuine firmness. Remaining ignorant of what actually violates a precept while believing oneself already accomplished brings the fault of false pride, while genuine freedom from that same self-deception brings the virtue of true release. Failing to cultivate precepts, concentration, and wisdom together leaves affliction's own latent seeds untouched, an impure fault, while their genuine cultivation overcomes exactly what needed to be overcome, a virtue in its own right. A mind still secretly attached to sensory pleasure brings the fault of impure intention, while a mind that never regresses from its own resolve brings the virtue of steadfastness. Never confessing a violation, or clinging to views no different from those outside the teaching, brings the fault of failing to actually depart from bondage, while genuine practice unique to this teaching brings the virtue of an unsurpassed path shared with no other tradition, one capable of reaching all the way to arhatship and beyond.

Ru-Yi Translation

Ten virtues of discipline kept answer each in turn: unity, unbroken observance, freedom from hostility, and so on through an unsurpassed path shared with no other tradition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sixth pairing, false pride mistaking mere lax behavior for genuine attainment, answered by the virtue of true release from exactly that same self-deception, as this list's own most quietly dangerous fault, since it disguises itself as spiritual confidence rather than announcing itself as failure the way the other nine more obviously do. He highlights the tenth and final pairing, clinging to views indistinguishable from those outside the teaching answered by an unsurpassed path found nowhere else, as this whole comparison's own fitting climax, precepts genuinely kept culminating not merely in good behavior but in a path capable of reaching all the way to liberation itself.

44. Section: Ten Occasions for Heedfulness, Beginning at the Very Edge of Violation

A further topic then opens: the fourth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining ten distinct occasions on which a monastic diligently training in the discipline should remain genuinely heedful. These are: at the very moment a violation might occur; during meditation itself; at the moment of applying correct effort as death approaches; while out on the almsround; while engaged in ordinary work; while receiving offerings and reverence; while seeking to learn the teaching more broadly; while reflecting on the true teaching already learned; during genuine seclusion of body and mind; and while contemplating reality's own ultimate nature through calm-abiding and insight together. Regarding the first of these, why should a monastic remain heedful at the very moment a violation might occur? Because such a monastic has already brought to completion a fivefold heedfulness spanning past, present, and future together, never having violated the precepts in the past, not violating them now, and resolved never to violate them in the future, this same resolve first taken up as a deliberate vow before any temptation ever actually arises and then genuinely carried out, moment by moment, exactly as that vow first intended. It is precisely this fivefold heedfulness, spanning every moment of time together with the vow that first set it in motion and the ongoing practice that continues to fulfill it, that constitutes remaining heedful at this very first of the ten occasions.

Chinese Source

復次，於毗奈耶勤學苾芻，依於十時，應不放逸。何等為十？一、依犯時，二、依定時，三、依正時，四、依乞食時，五、依所作時，六、依受用利養恭敬時，七、依求多聞時，八、依思正法時，九、依遠離時，十、依思通達究竟時。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monastic diligently training in the discipline should remain heedful on ten occasions: at the moment of potential violation, during meditation, at death, during almsround, during work, while receiving offerings, while seeking learning, while reflecting on the teaching, during seclusion, and while contemplating ultimate reality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this list's own remarkable range, spanning moments as different as almsround, receiving honor, and the very approach of death itself, as this treatise's own reminder that genuine heedfulness can never be confined to a single dramatic moment of temptation but must instead accompany a practitioner through the entire, undramatic texture of ordinary monastic life. He highlights the fivefold structure given for the very first occasion, a vow taken before temptation arises and then genuinely carried out moment by moment thereafter, as a practical template a practitioner can apply to each of the remaining nine occasions in turn.

45. Section: Calming, Uplifting, and Equanimity — Heedfulness during Meditation

Why should a monastic remain heedful during meditation itself, the second of the ten occasions? Whether dwelling in a solitary clearing or beneath a tree, such a monastic, upon noticing any of the five hindrances arise, sensory craving and the rest, refuses to simply tolerate their presence, instead abandoning them, dispelling them, and expelling them entirely, since these same five hindrances defile the mind and, left unchecked, ultimately prevent nirvana itself from ever being attained. In order to sever them, a monastic cultivates, as each moment actually requires, the sign of calming, the sign of uplifting, and the sign of equanimity in turn. Once genuinely skilled in all three signs, a monastic who senses the mind growing dull and worries it may sink into dullness applies the uplifting sign directly; one who senses the mind growing agitated and worries it may scatter into distraction applies the calming sign instead; and once the mind has settled into genuine balance, neither dull nor agitated, the still higher equanimity is then cultivated in its place. And even once genuine concentration has actually been attained, whether the access concentration bordering the desire realm or any of the four fundamental absorptions themselves, a monastic gives rise to no craving or relish for it at all, harboring no wistful attachment, no lingering defilement, dwelling instead entirely without clinging even to this same hard-won attainment. This fourfold practice, removing the five hindrances, skillfully applying the three signs, refining that same application as the moment requires, and remaining utterly unattached even to genuine concentration once attained, together constitutes remaining heedful during meditation itself.

謂有苾芻，或住空閑、或居樹下，生貪欲蓋乃至疑蓋，終不安忍，則便棄捨、除遣、變吐。於時時間應修止相，於時時間應修舉相，於時時間應修捨相。又於已得三摩地中，不生愛味，不起顧戀，無有貪染，無著而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monastic, noticing the five hindrances arise, refuses to tolerate them, cultivating the signs of calming, uplifting, and equanimity as each moment requires. And even once genuine concentration is attained, such a monastic gives rise to no craving or attachment toward it at all, dwelling entirely without clinging.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise, moment-by-moment discernment required here, correctly diagnosing whether the mind currently needs uplifting, calming, or simple equanimity, as a genuinely demanding skill in its own right, meditation instruction here refusing any one-size-fits-all technique in favor of continuous, responsive adjustment. He highlights the final requirement, remaining entirely unattached even to genuine concentration once actually attained, as this whole fourfold practice's own most advanced and easily overlooked stage, since the very success of calming the mind can itself become a subtle new object of craving unless this last, further vigilance is maintained.

46. Section: A Wholesome Death, and Heedfulness on the Almsround

Why should a monastic remain heedful at the very moment of death, the third occasion? Such a monastic, as death draws near, generates a genuinely forceful resolve, thinking, I should now die with mindfulness fixed upon the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, or die with a wholesome mind fixed upon whatever good I myself have done. Carrying this resolve through, such a monastic carefully guards exactly this same mindfulness as it remains present, dying with mindfulness fixed on the three jewels or with a wholesome mind fixed on genuine goodness, and precisely because of this is said to die a wholesome death, to pass away in a wholesome manner, and to proceed toward a wholesome future life, three different names for one and the same outcome. As one classic verse describes this very death, pure conduct beautifully established, the noble path already well cultivated, one rejoices as life itself comes to its end, setting it aside the way one gladly sets aside a container once filled with poison. Why should a monastic remain heedful during the almsround as well, the fourth occasion? Dwelling near some village or settlement, such a monastic goes about begging exactly as the classic teaching on purifying the almsround describes, moving from house to house in fair and orderly sequence rather than favoring whichever household happens to give the finer offering, the very same wholesome qualities described in the Moon-Simile Sūtra steadily increasing all the while.

謂有苾芻臨命終時，其心猛利，發起如是正加行心：謂我今者應以緣佛、緣法、緣僧正念而死，應以緣善善心而死。彼由緣佛、緣法、緣僧所有正念，及由緣善所有善心而命終故，名賢善死、賢善夭沒，亦名賢善趣於後世。

Ru-Yi Translation

As death draws near, a monastic resolves to die with mindfulness fixed on the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha, or on genuine goodness. Carrying this mindfulness through to the end, such a monastic dies a wholesome death, passes away in a wholesome manner, and proceeds toward a wholesome future life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the image of setting death aside the way one gladly sets aside a container once filled with poison as one of this treatise's most striking pictures of what a genuinely well-prepared death can actually feel like, relief and even joy rather than mere resignation, precisely because a lifetime of pure conduct has already removed whatever there might have been to fear. He highlights the almsround's own simple fairness, refusing to favor a generous household over a stingy one, as a fittingly modest illustration that heedfulness belongs just as much to the most ordinary daily routine as to the dramatic moment of death itself.

47. Section: Twelve Ways a Monastic's Own Work Stays Free of Negligence

Why should a monastic remain heedful while engaged in ordinary work as well, the fifth occasion? Whether occupied with the bowl, with robes, with attending upon some wise fellow practitioner, or with any other task whatsoever, such a monastic carries it out entirely without indulgent negligence. Being free of such negligence means the work is never agitated but carried out calmly, never sloppy but carried out with real care, never allowed to generate further fault but kept genuinely beneficial, never left to fall into disrepair but properly maintained, never left incomplete but brought fully to its own conclusion, never allowed to exceed its own proper scope, never treated with contempt however humble the task itself, whether sweeping a floor or cleaning a latrine, never resented but genuinely cared for, never rushed through in careless haste, never needlessly delayed either, never allowed to scatter into distraction but kept genuinely focused, and never carried out with any lingering attachment or greed, according throughout with both ordinary custom and the vinaya's own established norms. This same careful attentiveness to even the smallest task constitutes remaining heedful during work itself.

Chinese Source

謂有苾芻，於鉢作業、於衣作業、於諸智者同梵行所看侍作業，或復於餘所有作業，皆無縱逸。無縱逸故，不焦、不爛、不失、不壞，亦不零落，不過所作、不鄙所作、不惡所作、不急所作、不緩所作、不掉所作、不染所作，隨順世間，順毗奈耶所有軌則。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether working on the bowl, robes, attending a fellow practitioner, or any other task, a monastic remains free of negligence: never agitated, sloppy, fault-generating, neglected, incomplete, excessive, contemptuous of small tasks, resentful, rushed, delayed, distracted, or attached, according with both custom and the vinaya's norms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twelvefold list's own inclusion of humble tasks like sweeping and cleaning alongside more obviously meaningful monastic duties as a genuinely important democratizing move, insisting that heedfulness admits no hierarchy of worthy versus unworthy labor, the same careful attentiveness owed to the most menial chore as to any more visibly spiritual undertaking. He highlights the final requirement, freedom from any lingering attachment or greed even while working, as this list's own reminder that inner quality matters just as much as outward diligence, a task competently finished while the mind remains subtly grasping still falling short of genuine heedfulness.

48. Section: A Debt to Be Repaid, and the Pig Who Once Stole from His Father

Why should a monastic remain heedful when receiving offerings and reverence as well, the sixth occasion? Whatever offerings and reverence such a monastic happens to receive, they are received without any defiled attachment, without craving, without indulgent excess, without becoming lost in them, without confusion, without possessive grasping, and without treasuring or admiring them as personal ornaments, but rather by genuinely seeing the real dangers hidden within them and skillfully knowing how to remain free of exactly this kind of attachment even while making use of them. As one revered teacher once illustrated with a simple story, a child who secretly stole money from his own father, and was never once corrected for it, was later reborn, upon dying, as a pig within that very same household, precisely in order to repay through servitude the debt that theft, even from one's own father, still genuinely incurred. Receiving an offering, in just the same way, is itself a kind of debt for an ordinary practitioner not yet free of affliction, a debt that must eventually be repaid through genuine practice, and such a monastic should silently reflect, upon receiving anything at all, that this very feeling of gratification is itself already a subtle form of suffering, since it marks a real and serious trap capable of drawing a careless practitioner toward a miserable rebirth if the debt is never actually repaid; a noble one who has attained the second or third fruit, by contrast, receives offerings the way a son makes free use of what already belongs to his own father, owing no debt yet still offering simple thanks, while an arhat alone is genuinely worthy of offering outright, owing nothing at all and needing not even to offer thanks in return.

Chinese Source

謂有苾芻，隨所獲得利養恭敬，於所獲得不染、不著、不耽、不湎、不悶、不執，亦不保玩而受用之；深見過患，善知出離而受用之。隨其所得利養恭敬，能自制伏；不因所得利養恭敬，心住憍傲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever offerings and reverence a monastic receives are received without defiled attachment, craving, or possessive grasping, but by genuinely seeing the real dangers within them, skillfully remaining free of attachment while still making use of them, and never growing arrogant on account of whatever is received, however much greater it may be than what another has received.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the child reborn as a pig purely to repay a debt incurred even from his own father as a genuinely startling and effective teaching precisely because it refuses to let intimacy or good intention soften karma's own exacting arithmetic, a debt remaining a debt regardless of who it was taken from. He highlights the threefold distinction between an ordinary being's debt-laden reception, a noble learner's debt-free yet still gracious reception, and an arhat's own effortless worthiness to receive at all as a fitting reminder that even something as simple as accepting a gift reveals, with real precision, exactly how far a given practitioner has actually traveled along the path.

49. Section: Ten Marks of Genuine Learning, and Why Learning Alone Can Harm

Why should a monastic remain heedful while seeking to learn the teaching more broadly, the seventh occasion? Genuine, complete learning, one classic passage explains, unfolds through ten distinct marks, five belonging to the one who teaches and five to the one who listens, both sides needing to be genuinely fulfilled before real learning can actually occur. On the teacher's side, the content taught must be the Buddha's own genuine teaching rather than worldly literature or heterodox doctrine, delivered with nirvana itself as its ultimate aim, in clear and skillful language suited to its listeners, addressing both the full particulars and the underlying true nature of whatever is taught, offered as a genuine means toward worldly and transcendent ease alike, and aimed throughout at freeing beings from suffering. On the listener's side, one must listen without hunting for fault in the teaching or the teacher, listen only in genuine pursuit of nirvana, listen with a pure, attentive, and respectful mind free of factional bias, listen carefully enough to actually absorb the teacher's own meaning, and listen closely enough to examine each and every phrase and word with real, sincere attention. As one classic verse summarizes the benefits that follow, broad learning brings knowledge of the true teaching, broad learning leads one away from wrongdoing, broad learning lets go of whatever carries no genuine value, and broad learning brings nirvana itself. Yet one further scripture equally warns that beings in a declining age who seek only outward learning, hoping thereby to inflate their own sense of self rather than genuinely seeking realization, actually turn this same broad learning into a source of harm rather than benefit.

Chinese Source

云何於毗奈耶勤學苾芻，依求多聞第七時中，應不放逸？多聞能知法，多聞能遠惡，多聞捨無義，多聞得涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why should a monastic remain heedful while seeking to learn the teaching more broadly? Broad learning brings knowledge of the true teaching, broad learning leads one away from wrongdoing, broad learning lets go of whatever carries no genuine value, and broad learning brings nirvana itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the warning that learning pursued merely to inflate one's own sense of self can actually cause genuine harm as this whole section's own necessary counterweight to its otherwise glowing praise of broad learning, a reminder that the same activity can either liberate or entrench a practitioner depending entirely on the motivation behind it. He highlights the requirement that a listener attend without hunting for fault in either the teaching or the teacher as a genuinely demanding discipline in its own right, since the habit of critical fault-finding, however intellectually satisfying, tends to close off exactly the receptivity genuine learning actually requires.

50. Section: Learning Sought for Nothing but One's Own Taming and Peace

What, more precisely, should a monastic actually seek while pursuing this same heedful learning? Such a monastic sets aside every kind of worldly, ornately composed literature, embracing instead learning genuinely aligned with the true teaching, respectfully receiving and thoroughly mastering every scripture the Buddha taught, whether the profound teachings on emptiness found in the great vehicle's own definitive scriptures or the comparably profound teachings on dependent origination found across both forward and reverse sequence in the lesser vehicle's own texts, embracing every one of them, whether of provisional or ultimate meaning, until genuine mastery is finally reached. Yet reaching this same ultimate mastery is not a matter of seeking some personal gain or advantage from the teaching studied, nor a matter of seeking merely to prevail over an opponent in debate, since neither motivation constitutes genuine, ultimate learning at all; true and ultimate learning is sought neither for material support nor for others' esteem, but solely in order to tame oneself, to attain genuine inner peace for oneself, to attain nirvana for oneself, and to attain the very highest good a renunciant or a genuine seeker of truth could ever hope to reach, and it is for this reason alone that such a teaching is genuinely well heard, well received, thoroughly read and recited, and brought all the way to complete and thorough mastery.

Chinese Source

謂有苾芻，棄捨世間所有諷誦綺飾詞論、絢藻文章，隨順世間相應多聞；於佛所說所有甚深、相似甚深空性相應緣性緣起，若順、若逆一切經典，恭敬受持，令得究竟。非為利養、非為恭敬，乃至但為自得調伏、自得寂靜、自得涅槃、自得沙門若婆羅門最上義利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such a monastic sets aside worldly ornate literature, embracing every scripture the Buddha taught, profound or comparably profound, until genuine mastery is reached, seeking this not for gain or esteem but solely to tame oneself, attain genuine peace, attain nirvana, and reach the very highest good a genuine seeker of truth could hope for.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the requirement to embrace both the great vehicle's profound teaching on emptiness and the lesser vehicle's own teaching on dependent origination together, rather than favoring one tradition at the expense of the other, as this treatise's own consistently generous and comprehensive approach to the wider Buddhist canon. He highlights the final, repeated insistence that genuine learning seeks nothing beyond one's own taming and peace as this whole section's own clearest possible statement of what actually distinguishes true scholarship from mere accumulation, learning valuable exactly to the degree it serves liberation and not one degree further.

51. Section: Reflecting by Meaning, Not by the Bare Letter

Why should a monastic remain heedful while reflecting on the true teaching already learned, the eighth occasion? Dwelling alone in genuine solitude, such a monastic reflects on the very meaning of whatever has already been heard, received, and attained, weighing it carefully through direct experience, sound inference, and reliable testimony together, and observing closely which matters genuinely call for reflection, whatever purifies the mind, and which do not, matters like the ultimate nature of self, of other beings, of the world, of karmic result, or the exact inner experience of an advanced meditator or a buddha's own domain, reserving simple faith for whatever still lies beyond present capacity rather than forcing premature speculation. Regarding whatever genuinely does call for reflection, such a monastic reflects always according to its underlying meaning rather than clinging to the bare letter alone, correctly distinguishing provisional teaching, not yet fully revealing its own ultimate intent, from teaching of a truly great and definitive kind, reflecting with real firmness so that a settled, genuine conviction takes root, with real care so that whatever was not yet understood becomes genuinely clear, and with real continuity so that whatever has already been understood is never subsequently lost or forgotten, always carrying every reflection through to its own actual completion and never once giving way to discouragement or retreat partway through, however difficult the matter still under consideration may prove to be.

Chinese Source

謂有苾芻，獨處空閑，如其所聞、如其所受、如其所得所有諸法，思惟其義，稱量、觀察。凡所思惟，但依於義，不依於文。堅固思惟。審諦思惟。相續思惟。隨所思惟要當究竟，於其中間終無退屈。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dwelling alone in solitude, a monastic reflects on the meaning of whatever has been learned, weighing it through direct experience, inference, and testimony. Reflection follows the underlying meaning rather than the bare letter, carried out firmly, carefully, and continuously, always brought through to genuine completion without ever giving way to discouragement partway through.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the careful boundary drawn here, reserving simple faith for matters genuinely beyond present capacity rather than forcing speculation about a buddha's own inner domain, as a fittingly humble corrective to the tempting but ultimately fruitless habit of reasoning confidently about what remains, for now, simply unknowable. He highlights the instruction to reflect according to meaning rather than the bare letter as this treatise's own consistent hermeneutic principle running throughout its entire treatment of scripture, a teaching's own words always serving as a pointer toward genuine understanding rather than an end in themselves.

52. Section: Closing the Ten Occasions with Honest Self-Assessment

Why should a monastic remain heedful during genuine seclusion, the ninth occasion, comprising both bodily and mental seclusion together? Bodily seclusion means never mingling with lay or monastic company at all, never sharing in their joys or sorrows, and never once allowing oneself to be drawn all the way into whatever worldly affairs such mingling tends to generate; mental seclusion means dwelling instead in some genuinely solitary, forest-like place, content with the most minimal bedding, entirely apart from wherever beings congregate, free of distraction, free of laziness, and free of every secondary affliction capable of obstructing calm-abiding and insight alike. And why should a monastic remain heedful while contemplating reality's own ultimate nature, the tenth and final occasion? Such a monastic genuinely knows what exists to actually exist and what does not exist to genuinely not exist, knows what is merely surpassable, such as worldly meditative attainment, still to be exceeded by something higher, and knows what is truly unsurpassable, nirvana itself, beyond which nothing greater remains; and precisely because of this same accurate knowledge, such a monastic never mistakes what has not yet been attained for something already attained, nor what has not yet been directly realized for something already realized, remaining entirely free of false pride and reporting only what has genuinely been understood, honestly assessing one's own progress rather than ever overestimating it. With this the full explanation of the ten occasions for genuine heedfulness, the fourth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, comes to its close.

Chinese Source

謂有苾芻，於有如實知有，於非有如實知非有，於有上如實知有上，於無上如實知無上。由彼於有、非有、有上、無上如實知故，於所未得不生得想，於所未證不生證想，離增上慢，非增上慢所攝持故，自記所解。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monastic genuinely knows what exists and what does not, what remains surpassable and what is truly unsurpassable. Through this same accurate knowledge, such a monastic never mistakes the unattained for the attained, remaining entirely free of false pride and honestly reporting only what has genuinely been understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this final occasion's own emphasis on rigorous honesty about one's own actual attainment as a fitting capstone to the entire tenfold structure just completed, every earlier occasion's own heedfulness ultimately proving worthless if a practitioner cannot even accurately assess where that heedfulness has actually led. He highlights the careful distinction between the surpassable and the truly unsurpassable as this treatise's own reminder that even genuine meditative accomplishment still falls short of the path's own final destination, a caution against mistaking any intermediate milestone, however genuinely impressive, for the journey's actual end.

53. Section: Four Obstacles That Can Block a Genuine Going Forth

A further topic then opens: the fifth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining four distinct obstacles capable of blocking a genuine going forth into monastic life: an obstacle of motivation, an obstacle of one's own physical basis, an obstacle of past karma, and an obstacle of insufficient personal autonomy. The obstacle of motivation refers to going forth not from any genuine aspiration at all but purely under external compulsion, as when someone is coerced into ordination by a king's own authority, forced into hiding from violent bandits, fleeing an unpaid debt, driven by simple fear, or unable to sustain themselves any other way and seeking shelter in monastic life purely for survival; none of these motivations constitutes a genuine going forth at all, since the very aspiration toward a pure, unconditioned life remains entirely absent, present only the external pressure of circumstance.

Chinese Source

復次，有四種障能障出家。謂意樂障、所依障、業障、不自在障。意樂障者，謂或為王威所逼迫而求出家，如是等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four obstacles can block a genuine going forth: an obstacle of motivation, of one's physical basis, of past karma, and of insufficient autonomy. The obstacle of motivation refers to seeking ordination purely under external compulsion, such as a king's coercion, rather than from any genuine aspiration at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this catalogue of coerced motivations, royal pressure, fleeing bandits or debt, fear, and simple economic desperation, as this treatise's own remarkably realistic acknowledgment that monastic institutions have always attracted some who arrive for reasons having nothing to do with genuine spiritual aspiration. He highlights the underlying principle at stake, that ordination without authentic inner motivation fails to constitute genuine going forth at all regardless of its outward form, as a reminder that this treatise consistently locates the real substance of any practice in the quality of mind behind it rather than in ritual form alone.

54. Section: Why a Damaged Body Cannot Properly Repay a Donor's Faith

The obstacle of one's own physical basis is then explained: blindness, deafness, damaged male faculties of either of the two traditionally recognized kinds, or severe skin disease, paralysis, and similar serious afflictions afflicting the body. Such a physical basis obstructs genuine ordination not from any inherent unworthiness but for a genuinely practical reason: a person so afflicted, even once ordained, lacks the physical capacity to properly serve their own teachers, requiring instead to be served and cared for by them, and likewise lacks the capacity to make proper use of whatever robes, food, and other genuinely faithful offerings a sincere donor provides, offerings extremely difficult to properly repay through actual practice under such physical constraint; receiving them regardless risks depleting rather than building the very merit and wholesome qualities monastic life is meant to cultivate, and it is for this reason, not from any judgment of the person's own worth, that a damaged physical basis constitutes a genuine obstacle to full ordination.

Chinese Source

所依障者，謂或盲聾、或扇搋迦、或半擇迦、或為疥癩禿攣躄等種種惡疾逼切其身，如是等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The obstacle of one's physical basis refers to blindness, deafness, damaged male faculties, severe skin disease, paralysis, and similar serious afflictions, since a person so afflicted lacks the capacity to properly serve teachers or repay a sincere donor's offerings through actual practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the explanation given here, that this obstacle rests entirely on practical capacity rather than on any judgment of a disabled person's own inherent worth, as an important clarification worth holding onto against a more casually dismissive reading, this treatise itself framing the concern as protecting both the ordinand from depleting merit they cannot yet properly repay and the community from an imbalance neither side actually intends. He highlights the underlying logic, that receiving support one cannot yet properly reciprocate through genuine practice actually harms the receiver's own spiritual progress, as a distinctively Buddhist way of reasoning about obligation, framed entirely in terms of the ordinand's own long-term welfare rather than mere institutional convenience.

55. Section: Grave Karma and Lack of Personal Freedom, Closing the Four Obstacles to Going Forth

The obstacle of karma is explained next: grave prior misdeeds such as the five acts of immediate retribution, killing one's mother or father, killing an arhat, causing a schism within the monastic community, or drawing a buddha's blood, along with violating a nun, entering monastic life as an outsider merely to steal the teaching without genuine intent, or already having been set apart from the community or expelled outright for a serious prior offense; none of these people should be given full ordination, since such grave misdeeds arise from an extreme absence of shame and conscience, a defilement so heavy that it leaves whatever wholesome qualities of shame, faith, effort, mindfulness, and wisdom a person might otherwise cultivate extremely weak and thin. The obstacle of insufficient personal autonomy is explained last: a person who lacks the freedom to decide for themselves to go forth, as when parents have not given consent, or the person remains bound by an unfulfilled contract as someone else's servant, or is a minister who has not obtained the king's consent, or has been abducted or otherwise claimed by another, or remains entangled in unresolved litigation; the commentary adds that someone the king has come to resent, or who has acted against the king's own interests, or who remains held by a creditor likewise cannot be given full ordination, since allowing such a person to go forth risks bringing genuine trouble upon the monastic community itself. Any of these four, an obstacle of motivation, of physical basis, of karma, or of insufficient autonomy, is sufficient to block a genuine going forth.

Chinese Source

業障者，謂害母等諸重惡業。不自在障者，謂父母等所不聽許，若諸僮僕，若王大臣，他所劫掠，若他所得，若有辯答，如是等。

Ru-Yi Translation

The obstacle of karma refers to grave prior misdeeds such as killing one's mother. The obstacle of insufficient autonomy refers to lacking parental consent, being another's bound servant, a minister without royal consent, someone abducted or claimed by another, or someone entangled in unresolved litigation, among similar circumstances.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the explanation given for the karma obstacle, that grave prior misdeeds leave shame, conscience, and every other wholesome quality extremely thin, as this treatise's own consistent insistence that outward ritual admission into monastic life can never substitute for the actual state of a person's accumulated karma. He highlights the lack-of-autonomy obstacle's own quiet realism, a person bound by unfinished contracts, unresolved debts, or ongoing litigation simply cannot yet freely choose anything, monastic life included, as a reminder that this treatise consistently insists on genuine freedom of will as the very foundation any authentic spiritual choice requires. With this, the fourfold catalogue of obstacles to a genuine going forth, motivation, physical basis, karma, and autonomy, together with the fifth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions that first opened it, stands complete.

56. Section: Five Things Every Monastic Must Learn, Beginning with the Six Marks of a Grave Offense

A further topic then opens: the sixth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, briefly stating that there are five matters every renunciant must without fail learn concerning the vinaya, namely whether an act constitutes an offense or not, whether an offense is heavy or light, and the individually stated rules of the pratimoksha sutra only briefly summarized here. Since what does and does not constitute an offense has already been explained earlier, this discussion turns to distinguishing a heavy offense from a light one, beginning with six differences capable of making an offense heavy. The first, the difference of formal establishment, refers simply to how the precepts themselves were formally established, since among a nun's own precepts eight are treated as grave while the rest remain comparatively light, and any offense bound up with the intrinsic wrongs of killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, or false speech counts as heavy for this same reason. The second, the difference of the matter involved, notes that among the many minor transgressions a nun may commit, even though formally the same category of offense, those tied to killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, or false speech remain heavier than those tied merely to a prohibited matter such as food or clothing, since the underlying matter itself differs in gravity even where the formal offense-category does not.

Chinese Source

復次，略有五處，諸出家者於毗奈耶決定應學。何等為五？謂應學知有犯、無犯、若重、若輕，及略所說別解脫經。由六種差別，所犯成重。一、制立差別。謂於學處而制立故。二、事差別。謂雖同是波逸底迦，然殺生等所有性罪，於餘遮罪有差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are five matters every renunciant must learn concerning the vinaya: knowing what constitutes an offense or not, what is heavy or light, and the individually stated pratimoksha sutra. Six differences make an offense heavy. First, the difference of formal establishment, based on how the precept itself was set. Second, the difference of the matter involved, since even among the same formal offense-category, those bound up with killing and similar intrinsic wrongs differ from those bound up with a merely prohibited matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sixfold catalogue as this treatise's own careful refusal to let "heavy" and "light" collapse into a single flat notion, insisting instead that genuine gravity depends on several genuinely distinct factors working together rather than any one alone. He highlights the second difference's own quiet clarification, that offenses formally belonging to the same disciplinary category can still carry meaningfully different weight depending on the underlying matter involved, as a caution against a purely mechanical reading of the precepts that ignores what is actually at stake in each particular act.

57. Section: Little Grass Cannot Dam a Flood, Closing the Six Marks of Heavy and Light Offenses

Four further differences complete the sixfold catalogue and close this sub-topic entirely. The third, the difference of habitual perforation, refers to a person who repeatedly commits the same offense again and again, an offense that individually might be minor but grows heavy through sheer habitual repetition. The fourth, the difference of affliction, refers to an offense committed under intensely fierce craving, aversion, or delusion, the sheer force of the underlying affliction itself making the offense heavy. The fifth, the difference of wisdom, describes a person of little cultivated wholesomeness and narrow, inferior wisdom who, even committing exactly the same offense as someone of far greater wisdom under the same formal category, the same matter, the same habitual repetition, and the same fierce affliction, nonetheless finds that offense becoming an extremely grave obstruction, since such a person genuinely lacks the capacity to repent and purify it, whereas the wiser person, though committing the identical act, knows how to find genuine release from it. A vivid illustration follows: a small stream can be dammed by a mere handful of grass, that same grass never swept away by so gentle a current, while a great flood sweeps away even heaps of grass and timber piled together; wisdom is compared here to grass and the offense itself to the flowing water, so that a person of little wisdom, however small the offense, may still find it sweeping them under, while a person of great wisdom finds even a heavy offense something they know how to overcome. The sixth and final difference, the difference of time, refers to a person who fails to repent an offense swiftly as the Dharma prescribes, instead allowing it to become an entrenched habit over a long stretch of time before finally addressing it, this same delay itself making the offense heavy. Precisely the reverse of these same six differences is what makes an offense light, closing this entire discussion of the five things every monastic must learn.

Chinese Source

如小水流，少草能偃，於彼細草不能漂沒；如大水流，聚積草木亦不能偃。此中道理，當知亦爾。與此相違，應知所犯名為輕罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

As a small stream can be dammed by a little grass, that grass never swept away by so gentle a flow, while a great flood sweeps away even heaps of grass and timber, so should this same principle be understood here. Precisely the reverse of these differences is what makes an offense light.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the water-and-grass image as this treatise's own most memorable illustration of why identical acts can carry entirely different weight for different people, wisdom itself functioning as the very capacity to find release rather than as some separate reward for good behavior. He highlights the difference of wisdom as the catalogue's own deepest point, a lack of cultivated wholesomeness and narrow wisdom not merely failing to prevent an offense but actively preventing its own resolution afterward, urging that the sustained cultivation of discipline, concentration, and wisdom together remains the only genuine safeguard against an offense quietly compounding itself over time.

58. Section: Five Marks for Judging an Offense, Closing with a Caution Against Hasty Judgment

A further topic then opens and closes within this same span: the seventh of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, stating that all who uphold the vinaya should observe any alleged offense through five distinct aspects before actually rendering judgment. The first, being intrinsically defiled, and the second, being intrinsically never to be done, together concern the intrinsic wrongs themselves, offenses such as killing or stealing arising from underlying craving, aversion, or delusion that should always without exception be taught as never to be done, and should anyone actually commit such a wrong, it should be pointed out to them precisely as it warrants, so that they swiftly repent and purify the offense. The third, being established according to a particular locus, recalls that the Buddha established each prohibitory precept based on a specific person, place, and time in which some particular fault first arose; the fourth, observing whether the underlying fault has actually arisen or not, together with commentary, explains that once the Buddha established a precept in response to one person's fault, that same established remedy still applies whenever another person elsewhere or at another time commits the identical fault, so genuine care is needed to observe carefully whether an offense has truly occurred before showing anyone the remedy for it; and the fifth, not being universally in effect, closes the discussion with a caution running the other direction, that if a practitioner has genuinely not committed such a fault at all, one should neither judge them as having offended nor show them any remedy for an offense they never actually committed, a reminder to fellow practitioners never to render judgment carelessly until the matter has been genuinely clarified. **This closes the entire discussion of observing an alleged offense, the seventh of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, now fully complete.**

復次，諸持律者，應以五相觀察所犯，然後斷罪。何等為五？謂一向雜染故，一向不行故，制立依處故，觀彼過失生不生故，非一向現行故。若有不犯如是過失，不應於此斷其有犯，亦不應顯對治之法。如是名為總略宣說觀察所犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

All who uphold the vinaya should observe an alleged offense through five aspects before rendering judgment: being intrinsically defiled, being intrinsically never to be done, being established according to a particular locus, observing whether the underlying fault has truly arisen, and not being universally in effect. If someone has genuinely not committed such a fault, one should neither judge them as having offended nor show them any remedy. This is the summary teaching on observing an alleged offense.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifth aspect's own closing caution, that a person who has genuinely not offended should neither be judged as having offended nor be shown any remedy for a fault they never committed, as this treatise's own quietly firm safeguard against exactly the kind of careless accusation condemned earlier in this same fascicle's discussion of coarse speech. He highlights the fourth aspect's own practical realism, that a precept established for one person's fault still properly applies to another person's identical fault elsewhere, as showing how a body of law grounded in particular historical incidents can still function as a genuinely general standard without losing its connection to the concrete circumstances that first called it forth.

59. Section: Seven Places of Dependence and the Three Trainings a Diligent Monastic Relies Upon

A further topic then opens: the eighth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining that a monastic diligently trained in the vinaya depends on seven distinct loci and applies effort in six distinct areas. The seven loci are the teacher, meaning the Buddha himself; the preceptor, who instructs in concentration and wisdom; the instructing master, who guides the actual conduct of daily monastic life; and, beyond these three sources of instruction, the four remaining necessities of robes, alms food, bedding, and medicine for illness. The six areas of applied effort are the Dharma itself, the three trainings, formal instruction and admonition, meditative absorption, offering, and heedfulness. Regarding the Dharma, this refers to the pratimoksha sutra together with its full elaboration in the broader vinaya literature, both diligently studied and put into practice. Regarding the three trainings, this refers to the increased training in discipline, the increased training in mind, and the increased training in wisdom, each demanding sustained cultivation. Regarding formal instruction and admonition, this refers to receiving a preceptor's or instructing master's guidance in each of these same three trainings in turn, guidance elsewhere in this treatise elaborated at considerable length; the commentary elsewhere further distinguishes a wholesome from an unwholesome mode of receiving such instruction, a disciple unable to bear a teacher's corrective words, or a teacher who himself holds distorted views and practices wrongly, both belonging to the unwholesome mode, while their reverse constitutes the wholesome mode a diligent disciple should genuinely rely upon.

Chinese Source

此中法者，謂別解脫經及廣分別。學者，謂增上戒學、增上心學、增上慧學。教授教誡者，謂依增上戒學教授教誡、依增上心學教授教誡、依增上慧學教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Dharma here refers to the pratimoksha sutra together with its full elaboration. The trainings refer to the increased training in discipline, the increased training in mind, and the increased training in wisdom. Formal instruction and admonition refers to guidance received in each of these three trainings in turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sevenfold-loci, sixfold-effort structure as this treatise's own comprehensive map of everything a genuinely diligent monastic actually depends upon, spiritual instruction and material necessity held together as equally indispensable rather than the latter dismissed as a merely worldly concern beneath a serious practitioner's attention. He highlights the commentary's own black-and-white distinction, a disciple unable to bear correction and a teacher holding distorted views placed on the very same unwholesome side, as a reminder that genuine instruction always depends on both parties, a disciple's own receptivity mattering just as much as a teacher's own integrity.

60. Section: Nine Progressive Absorptions and the Single Bowl That Makes an Offering Genuinely Shared

Meditative absorption, the fourth of the six areas of applied effort, refers to the nine progressive absorptions, the four form-realm absorptions and four formless attainments together with cessation-attainment, already treated at considerable length elsewhere in this treatise; a monastic who respectfully relies on a genuine teacher, who follows that teacher's instruction faithfully, and whose material support is obtained entirely in accordance with the Dharma and the vinaya, applying calming and insight together under these same conditions, can in this way actually attain these nine progressive states. Offering, the fifth area, comes in two kinds, a material offering and a Dharma offering, corresponding to the six forms of harmonious concord monastics living together in community should cultivate. The material offering, according to commentary, refers to just one of these six forms of harmonious concord, namely sharing benefit equally: a monastic should share whatever food and requisites are lawfully and rightfully obtained together with fellow practitioners of genuine wisdom, holding back nothing in private, receiving only what has actually been placed into and thereby claimed by one's own alms bowl. The Dharma offering refers to all five remaining forms of harmonious concord together, shared bodily conduct, speech free of contention, a mind that rejoices together with others, shared cultivation of discipline, and a view held in common, every one of these likewise counting as a genuine offering in its own right.

Chinese Source

等持者，謂九次第定。供養者，謂財供養及法供養。財供養者，謂由一種可愛樂法。法供養者，謂由所餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Meditative absorption refers to the nine progressive absorptions. Offering comes in two kinds, material offering and Dharma offering. Material offering refers to just one of the forms of harmonious concord. Dharma offering refers to all the remaining forms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the single-bowl image, food received only once it has actually been placed into and thereby claimed by one's own alms bowl, as this treatise's own remarkably concrete way of ensuring that a community's shared material life leaves no private hoarding whatsoever, generosity made a literal bodily practice rather than merely a stated ideal. He highlights the pairing of material and Dharma offering as showing how six seemingly separate forms of harmonious concord actually reduce to a single underlying principle, a community bound together equally in what it shares materially and in how it lives, speaks, and understands together.

61. Section: Sustained Skill Closes the Eighth Vinaya Division, Then Five Ways of Rightly Receiving Open

Heedfulness, the sixth and final area of applied effort, refers to sustained, uninterrupted diligence in cultivating five distinct skills, skill regarding the aggregates, the elements, the sense-fields, dependent origination, and what is and is not a genuine cause and effect, a monastic who properly relies on a genuine teacher and whose daily necessities are all lawfully obtained thereby applying unbroken effort toward mastering these same five skills. **This closes the entire discussion of the seven loci and six areas of applied effort, the eighth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, now fully complete.** A further topic then opens: the ninth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining that the vinaya further recognizes five distinct ways a monastic may rightly receive something, receiving as one's own outright, receiving something meant to be passed further on, receiving something merely entrusted for safekeeping, receiving in order to give it away again, and receiving on another's behalf.

Chinese Source

不放逸者，謂於五種善巧，相續無間勤修加行。謂蘊善巧乃至處非處善巧。復次，毗奈耶中，有五種攝受。一、屬己受，二、承受受，三、委寄受，四、捨施受，五、為他受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Heedfulness refers to sustained, uninterrupted effort in five skills, skill in the aggregates through skill in cause and effect. The vinaya further recognizes five ways of rightly receiving: receiving as one's own, receiving to pass on, receiving for safekeeping, receiving to give away, and receiving on another's behalf.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the completed eighth division as this treatise's own demonstration that genuine diligence is never a single undifferentiated effort but always this specific, sevenfold-anchored, sixfold-directed structure, sustained across every dimension of a monastic's actual daily life. He highlights the fivefold catalogue of receiving that now opens as continuing this same practical spirit into an area easily overlooked, showing that even the simple act of accepting something involves genuinely distinct categories each carrying its own conditions for purity, a theme the coming sections will take up one by one.

62. Section: What Makes an Outright Gift or a Passed-On Gift Genuinely Pure

Two causes render an item received outright as one's own impure: arising attachment toward it, and intending to hoard it for future accumulation; the reverse of these same two causes, holding no attachment and harboring no intent to hoard, is what makes such receiving genuinely pure. Two further causes likewise render an item received for passing on to others impure: receiving it in a place where receiving is not proper, such as accepting food directly at a stupa or along a walking path meant for circumambulation, thereby obstructing others' own worship or passage, and receiving an excessive or improperly obtained amount from a donor; the reverse of these same two causes, receiving only in a proper place and only a proper, lawfully obtained amount that can genuinely be passed on to others in turn, is what makes this second way of receiving pure as well.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，令屬己受不得清淨。一、生染著故；二、擬蓄積故。與此相違，乃得清淨。由二因緣，令承受受不得清淨。一、非處受故；二、非量非法受故。與此相違，乃得清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Two causes make receiving as one's own impure: arising attachment, and intending to hoard. The reverse makes it pure. Two causes make receiving to pass on impure: receiving in an improper place, and receiving an excessive or improperly obtained amount. The reverse makes it pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the stupa-and-walking-path example as this treatise's own vivid instance of a broader principle running throughout its vinaya discussions, that even a lawful act performed in the wrong place or manner can obstruct others and thereby lose its own purity, location and circumstance mattering just as much as the bare legality of the act itself. He highlights the pairing of attachment and hoarding-intent as the deeper root beneath both causes of impurity in outright receiving, an inner grasping that the treatise consistently treats as more decisive than any outward measure of quantity.

63. Section: Declaring Surplus Items Pure Through a Three-Party Exchange Between Monastics

Two causes render an item merely entrusted for safekeeping impure: entrusting it to someone without first examining whether that person is actually fit to hold it, and remaining inwardly attached to an item one knows to be an unclean matter rather than genuinely one's own; the reverse of these same two causes, both examining the recipient's fitness beforehand and holding no inward attachment to what is merely held in trust, is what makes this third way of receiving pure. This entrusted receiving refers to the traditional practice of declaring surplus items pure before making further use of them: when a monastic has obtained robes or medicine beyond the three permitted robes and single bowl, that monastic first seeks out a fellow monastic of genuine discipline and learning to serve as a nominal owner for the surplus item, then formally declares the item as purified before a third monastic, who in turn returns the item back to its original holder, who may then freely use it precisely because it now belongs, in name, to another; this entire three-party exchange is called declaring an item pure, and its purpose is to prevent a monastic from ever forgetting that all such possessions genuinely belong to others and are merely borrowed for use, thereby guarding against the arising of any possessive greed.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，令委寄受不得清淨。一、不觀察人而委寄故；二、於不淨物心繫著故。與此相違，乃得清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Two causes make entrusted receiving impure: entrusting an item without examining the recipient's fitness, and remaining inwardly attached to an item known to be an unclean matter. The reverse makes it pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three-party exchange of declaring an item pure as this treatise's own ingeniously concrete solution to an otherwise abstract problem, translating the inward discipline of non-attachment into an actual, repeatable social procedure a community can reliably practice together. He highlights the stated purpose behind the entire procedure, preventing a monastic from ever forgetting that possessions genuinely belong to others and are merely borrowed for use, as showing once again that this treatise consistently locates a practice's real value in the state of mind it cultivates rather than in the outward ritual alone.

64. Section: The Three Genuine Fields of Merit, and Every Other Recipient a Regrettable One

Two causes render receiving something in order to give it away again impure: giving to an unworthy recipient, and giving with an expectation of some benefit in return rather than freely. An unworthy recipient is defined here by exclusion: apart from three genuine fields of merit, every other recipient counts as unworthy. The first genuine field is the field of merit itself, the Buddha, the Dharma, and the monastic community, whose boundless virtue benefits all beings and whose worship generates immeasurable merit in turn. The second is the field of compassion, beings genuinely suffering in poverty or hardship, toward whom giving likewise generates immeasurable merit. The third is the field of gratitude, one's own parents, teachers, preceptors, instructing masters, or anyone who has genuinely saved one's life or shown one kindness, a field toward which knowing, feeling, and repaying that kindness likewise generates genuine merit, much as a field of earth itself nourishes and grows whatever is planted in it. Giving to anyone outside these three fields, particularly giving with an ulterior motive or to someone who would merely use the gift toward further wrongdoing, cannot be considered pure; the reverse of both these causes, giving only to a genuine field of merit and giving freely without expectation of return, is what makes this fourth way of receiving pure.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，令捨施受不得清淨。一、於鄙惡田而捨施故；二、非無希望而捨施故。除三種田，當知所餘名鄙惡田。謂功德田、悲田、恩田。與此相違，乃得清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Two causes make giving-away impure: giving to an unworthy field, and giving with expectation of return. Apart from three genuine fields, the field of merit, the field of compassion, and the field of gratitude, every other recipient counts as unworthy. The reverse of both causes makes it pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the threefold field of merit, compassion, and gratitude as this treatise's own compact answer to a question every generous person eventually faces, where giving actually does the most genuine good, reverence toward what is already noble, mercy toward what genuinely suffers, and repayment toward what has already given freely to oneself, together covering the full range of worthy giving without needing any further category. He highlights the second impure cause, giving with an expectation of return, as a quieter but equally important warning, that even a gift directed toward a genuinely worthy field can still be corrupted by the giver's own hidden self-interest.

65. Section: Closing the Five Ways of Receiving, Then Opening Five Causes That Taint What a Monastic Uses

Two causes render receiving on another's behalf, as when a monastic accepts an invitation extended to the entire community, impure: accepting from someone unfamiliar, someone with whom one has no established prior relationship of trust, whose genuine sincerity remains unknown, and doing so without informing others rather than openly; and accepting while harboring a defiled mind, or accepting an invitation to a place, such as one filled with entertainment or sensual distraction, likely to give rise to such a defiled mind. The reverse of both these causes, accepting only from someone whose sincerity is genuinely known and accepting openly, and remaining entirely free of a defiled mind or of any place likely to produce one, is what makes this fifth and final way of receiving pure. **This closes the entire fivefold catalogue of rightly receiving, the ninth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, now fully complete.** A further topic then opens: the tenth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, explaining that the vinaya further recognizes five distinct causes capable of tainting the actual use a monastic makes of robes, food, dwelling, and similar necessities, an intrinsically wrongful source, an unbecoming character, an unusable condition, an improperly claimed ownership, and a failure to have been properly declared pure, each of these five now to be explained in turn.

Chinese Source

由二因緣，令為他受不得清淨。一、非宿交，又不告白，及性不識故；二、有染心故。與此相違，乃得清淨。復次，毗奈耶中，由五因緣，所受用事不得清淨。一、性有罪故；二、不端嚴故；三、不任用故；四、非攝屬故；五、不作淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Two causes make receiving on another's behalf impure: accepting from an unfamiliar, unvetted host without informing others, and accepting with a defiled mind. The reverse makes it pure. The vinaya further recognizes five causes tainting actual use: intrinsically wrongful source, unbecoming character, unusable condition, improper ownership, and failure to be declared pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the completed fivefold catalogue of rightly receiving, outright, passed-on, entrusted, given-away, and on-another's-behalf, as this treatise's own comprehensive account of essentially every way an object can pass into a monastic's hands, each category carrying its own distinct conditions rather than a single blanket rule covering all forms of receiving equally. He highlights the newly opened fivefold catalogue of tainted use as extending this same precision one step further, from the moment of receiving something to the ongoing question of whether it may actually be used at all, a distinction the coming sections will explore through several genuinely striking illustrations.

66. Section: A Winery Owner Turned Away, Since No Offering Earned by Wrongdoing Can Be Pure

The first of the five causes tainting a monastic's actual use of robes, food, and similar necessities, an intrinsically wrongful source, is explained first: receiving and making use of goods obtained through fraudulent weighing scales or measuring devices, cheating a buyer or seller through mismatched large and small measures; through forged documents used to deceive another out of property; through kidnapping or beating another into submission; through outright killing; or through theft, snatching or grabbing another's belongings by hand. Whenever a donor's own offering has been obtained through any such intrinsically wrongful means, accepting and making use of it renders that use impure, regardless of how sincerely the offering itself may be intended. Master Yìn Guāng, a modern exemplar of this same exacting standard, is remembered for once refusing an offering from a lay follower whose household ran a winery, declining to accept a single gift from that household unless the donor first genuinely closed the winery business altogether, precisely because wealth earned through such an occupation could not be received without compromising the very purity this teaching insists upon.

Chinese Source

性有罪者，謂依偽斗、偽稱、偽函、繫縛、撾打、若斫、若殺，及摣撮等所獲財物而受用之，名不清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

An intrinsically wrongful source refers to receiving and using goods obtained through fraudulent scales, fraudulent measures, forged documents, binding and beating, killing, or outright theft; making use of such goods is impure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Yìn Guāng episode as this treatise's own principle carried into vivid, lived practice, a teacher willing to forgo needed support altogether rather than accept it at the cost of complicity in a donor's own harmful livelihood. He highlights the catalogue's own progression, from mere fraudulent measurement through forgery, violence, and finally outright killing, as showing that even the mildest of these wrongs, cheating on a scale, already falls under the very same principle that governs the gravest, since what taints the offering is the wrongful means itself rather than the degree of harm alone.

67. Section: Land, Livestock, and Unhulled Grain — What a Monastic Cannot Fittingly Keep

The second cause, an unbecoming character, is explained next: receiving and making use of land or dwellings unsuited to monastic life, goods obtained through bribery, or livestock and animals such as elephants, horses, cattle, sheep, chickens, pigs, and dogs, all of which tend to give rise to attachment and affection unbecoming a renunciant, monastic vinaya specifically prohibiting the keeping of cats or dogs within a monastery; likewise receiving grown men, women, or children offered by donors who have no genuine intention of themselves renouncing worldly life, since too great a presence of lay persons within a monastic setting itself becomes unbecoming; likewise receiving bondservants or hired help offered by a lay donor, delegating one's own proper monastic duties entirely onto such servants; and likewise receiving gold, silver, jewels, or unhulled grain still requiring further processing before use, since a renunciant should never adorn themselves with gold or silver, and unhulled grain demands considerable time and labor to prepare, itself becoming an obstacle to genuine practice. Whatever fails to lend genuine adornment to one's own cultivation of discipline, concentration, and wisdom in this way renders such use impure.

Chinese Source

不端嚴者，謂受田宅及諸賄貨、象、馬、牛、羊、雞、豬、狗、犬、大男、大女、小男、小女、奴婢、作使、金銀、珍寶及生穀等而受用之，名不清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

An unbecoming character refers to receiving and using land, dwellings, bribery-derived goods, livestock of any kind, grown men and women or children, bondservants and hired help, or gold, silver, jewels, and unhulled grain; such use is impure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this lengthy catalogue as unified by a single underlying test rather than a mere arbitrary list, whether receiving and keeping a given thing genuinely adorns one's own discipline, concentration, and wisdom or instead quietly erodes it, land and livestock and unprocessed grain each failing this same test for their own distinct reasons. He highlights the inclusion of donated servants and hired help as a particularly pointed instance, since delegating one's own proper monastic labor entirely onto another person, however practically convenient, directly undercuts the very self-reliance and simplicity monastic life is meant to cultivate.

68. Section: A Vessel Once Soiled Should Never Again Hold Food

The third cause, an unusable condition, is explained next: any item soiled by excrement, urine, mucus, spittle, pus, blood, fat, or similar unclean matter, together with any further items of a similar unusable kind, renders such use impure should a monastic actually make use of them. The World-Honored One himself is cited directly on this very point: any vessel once used to hold urine, feces, or similar unclean matter is thereby rendered impure and should never again be received or used, even were it to be washed and reused to hold food afterward; a vessel that has once held such unclean matter remains, for this purpose, permanently unfit, and offering food in such a vessel to a monastic should never be accepted.

Chinese Source

不任用者，謂大小便利、洟唾所汙，膿血、肪膏此等塗染，復有所餘如是等類，若受用者，名不清淨。如世尊言：便利等器皆不清淨，不應受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

An unusable condition refers to anything soiled by excrement, urine, mucus, spittle, pus, blood, or fat; using such items is impure. As the World-Honored One said: any vessel once used for waste is impure and should never be used again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own direct citation on this point as a deliberately blunt, no-exceptions ruling, a soiled vessel remaining permanently unfit regardless of subsequent washing, precisely because the concern here is not merely hygienic but a matter of genuine discipline requiring no case-by-case judgment call. He highlights this third cause's own relative simplicity next to the two more elaborate causes preceding it, an unusable condition needing no lengthy catalogue of exceptions, as itself instructive, some matters in the vinaya admitting nuance and others deliberately admitting none at all.

69. Section: Communal Property, an Uninvited Meal, and Food Not Yet in One's Own Bowl

The fourth cause, an improperly claimed ownership, is explained next: making private use of property genuinely belonging to the entire monastic community; attending a lay household's invitation to a meal without having actually been assigned to go; accepting food not yet actually placed into one's own alms bowl, since even food set directly before oneself but not yet placed in the bowl does not yet genuinely belong to oneself and should not be used; making use of another person's property that has been neither given outright, nor relinquished to the community, nor discarded; presuming a donor's trust in a place where no such trust has genuinely been established, as when a monastic mistakenly assumes a close familiarity with a property owner that the owner does not actually share, and helps themselves accordingly; or receiving an excessive amount beyond what is properly due. Any of these renders such use impure.

Chinese Source

非攝屬者，謂或僧祇物，若不被差，不墮鉢中，亦不屬鉢；或別人物，不與、不捨、亦不捐棄，非處委信，亦復非量而受用之，名不清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

An improperly claimed ownership refers to privately using communal property, attending an invitation without assignment, using food not yet placed in one's own bowl, using another's property neither given nor relinquished, presuming unestablished trust, or receiving an excessive amount; such use is impure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the detail that even food set directly before a monastic but not yet actually placed in the bowl does not yet count as genuinely received as this catalogue's own most exacting single point, ownership here defined by a precise physical act rather than by mere physical proximity or apparent availability. He highlights presuming an unestablished trust, imagining a familiarity with a property owner the owner does not actually share, as a subtle danger worth particular attention, since it can arise from genuine warmth of feeling rather than any deliberate wrongdoing, and yet still renders the resulting use impure all the same.

70. Section: Eighty-Four Thousand Creatures in a Single Bowl of Water, Closing the Fascicle

The fifth and final cause, a failure to have been properly declared pure, is explained through five distinct kinds of such declaration. The first, receiving-purification, refers to formally receiving food, or performing the prescribed ritual act, before actually eating it. The second, damage-purification, refers to raw fruit properly treated by fire or by knife before being used, such treatment itself rendering the fruit fit for use. The third, entrustment-purification, refers to the very declaring-pure procedure already explained earlier in this fascicle, surplus robes or medicine beyond what is formally permitted first declared pure through the three-party exchange before being used. The fourth, timely-dharma-purification, refers to certain items, such as medicine formally permitted for seven days' use, a drink permitted outside ordinary mealtimes, or medicine permitted for lifelong use, each requiring a prescribed ritual act performed within its own proper time period before being used purely. The fifth, relinquishing-discrimination-purification, refers most memorably to drinking water without dwelling on whether living creatures might be present within it: since the Buddha taught that water known to contain living creatures should never be drunk, and since a single bowl of water cannot actually be inspected for the countless minute creatures it may harbor, a practitioner is instructed to recite beforehand, "the Buddha, observing a single bowl of water, saw eighty-four thousand living creatures within it; without reciting this same spell, one would be eating the flesh of sentient beings just the same," together with a brief closing mantra, thereby relinquishing the discriminating thought that living creatures might be present and drinking freely without further attachment to that thought; the same principle of relinquishing discrimination is elsewhere applied to receiving an offering from someone later discovered to be a thief, so long as the offering was made with a genuinely generous intention at the time, a monastic need not dwell on or discriminate against the giver's later-revealed character in order to receive it purely. In every case, purity of use ultimately depends on this same inward purity of mind, an untroubled mind that does not dwell on discriminating thoughts about an item's or a donor's impurity even while making proper external use of the five declarations described. The reverse of these five, failing to receive food or perform the prescribed act beforehand, failing to treat raw fruit by fire or knife, failing to declare surplus items pure, failing to observe the proper time period for certain medicines, or failing to relinquish discriminating thought when none of these five declarations has actually been made, is what renders such use impure rather than pure. **With this closing distinction, the transcript of fascicle 68 comes to rest, the entire discussion of what taints a monastic's actual use of robes, food, dwelling, and medicine, the tenth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, now complete as far as the source lecture-transcript extends, three divisions, profound depth, manner of teaching, and illustrative example, still remaining ahead for fascicle 69 to take up.**

Chinese Source

不作淨者，謂五種淨。何等為五？一、受得淨，二、損壞淨，三、委寄淨，四、時法淨，五、捨分別淨。與此相違，所有受用名為清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

A failure to have been properly declared pure refers to five kinds of declaration: receiving-purification, damage-purification, entrustment-purification, timely-dharma-purification, and relinquishing-discrimination-purification. The reverse of these five is what makes such use genuinely pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eighty-four-thousand-creatures verse as fascicle 68's own most memorable closing image, an entire ethic of non-harm compressed into a single recited couplet recited before something as ordinary as drinking a bowl of water, showing how thoroughly this treatise weaves its most exalted principles into daily monastic routine. He highlights the extension of relinquishing-discrimination to receiving an offering later discovered to have come from a thief as this fascicle's own final, quietly generous note, purity of mind measured by the sincerity of intention at the moment of receiving rather than by a donor's own hidden history, a fitting point of rest for a transcript that closes here mid-topic, its remaining divisions awaiting fascicle 69.
