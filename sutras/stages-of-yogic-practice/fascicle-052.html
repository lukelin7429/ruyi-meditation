Fascicle 52 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十二 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Continues

Completing the Four Conditions

Picking up exactly where fascicle 51 left off, this fascicle completes the treatise's account of the four conditions the Buddha taught for the arising of every conditioned phenomenon: the condition of immediate succession, the condition of the perceived object, and the six varieties of the condition of dominant influence — including the classical scholarly debate over which consciousness may serve as the "opening and guiding support" for which other.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 52 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Condition of Immediate Succession — Why the Mind Endlessly Yields Its Seat to the Mind That Follows

Fascicle 52 opens precisely where fascicle 51 left off within the Chapter of Deciding Discernment's third topic, the three times and four conditions: having fully treated the causal condition, the treatise now turns to the second of the four, the condition of immediate succession. It asks what this condition is, then answers: whenever a given mind and its accompanying mental factors have just ceased without any gap, and some other mind and its mental factors thereupon arise, the ceased mind and mental factors are called the condition of immediate succession for the mind and mental factors that follow. The lecture explains that mind admits of many distinctions — wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral; bound to the desire, form, or formless realm; with or without outflows; mundane or transmundane — all collectively called "mind," together with whatever mental factors correspond to each; whichever kind of mind currently appears before the practitioner ceases immediately, and this very ceasing serves as the necessary condition for whatever mind arises next, in any of these paired directions: wholesome followed by unwholesome or unwholesome followed by wholesome; either followed by neutral; lower-realm mind followed by middle-realm, middle by higher, or the reverse in either direction; a contaminated mind followed directly by an uncontaminated one at the very moment of first entering the path of vision, or an uncontaminated mind followed by a contaminated one upon emerging from that undivided wisdom; and a mind with mental discrimination followed by one without it, or the reverse. The treatise then identifies which consciousness itself is called by this name: if the six consciousnesses serve as the condition of immediate succession for the six consciousnesses that follow, this very preceding set of six is thereby established as the mental faculty, also called the mental sense-base, and also called the mental element — the same single reality named differently depending on whether it is being classified among the sense-faculties, the sense-bases, or the elements.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens this fascicle by noting that the seven fascicles resolving the Five Sense-Consciousness-Corresponding Ground and the Mind Ground span fascicles 51 through 57 in total, and that this fascicle continues directly into the second and third of the four conditions announced at the close of fascicle 51 — the condition of immediate succession here, followed shortly by the condition of the perceived object and the six kinds of dominant condition. He highlights the Compendium of Mahāyāna's own gloss that "mental faculty" carries two distinct meanings: the defiled manas, meaning the seventh consciousness, and the immediately-succeeding manas, meaning simply whichever consciousness has just ceased and now serves as condition for the next — a distinction worth holding clearly in mind before the treatise turns, in the next passage, to the classical scholarly dispute over exactly which consciousness may serve this "opening and guiding" role for which other.

2. Milestone: Nanda, Sthiramati, and Dharmapāla — Three Views on Which Consciousness Opens the Door for Which

The lecture supplements this passage with an extended account of a classical dispute recorded in the Cheng Weishi Lun over precisely how the condition of immediate succession — called in this context the "opening and guiding support," since the preceding moment of mind opens the way and guides forth the moment that follows — actually operates across the eight consciousnesses. Master Nanda held that the five sense consciousnesses must always be induced by the sixth, mental consciousness, which alone can both sustain its own succession and induce the five senses in turn, while the seventh and eighth consciousnesses can only succeed themselves, never inducing any other. Master Sthiramati held a more permissive view: any of the first six consciousnesses may serve as opening and guiding support for any other among the first six; the sixth consciousness may be supported by itself or by the seventh and eighth; the seventh by itself or by the sixth; and the eighth by itself or by the sixth and seventh — different kinds of mind freely inducing one another across the eight. Master Dharmapāla took the opposite position: each of the eight consciousnesses belongs to its own distinct category, and only a consciousness of the very same category can serve as the condition of immediate succession for itself — no consciousness of one category can ever induce a consciousness of a different category, since a mind-king's own arising and ceasing forms an unbroken chain only with its own kind. The lecture notes that in this present treatise's own text, the character translated "this" refers to the immediately preceding moment of a consciousness's own category, and "that" to the immediately following moment of that very same category — not to some other category entirely — suggesting the treatise's own text aligns with Dharmapāla's stricter reading, though it broadly names "the six consciousnesses" as a summary way of indicating that each of the six, considered individually within its own category, possesses this same immediate-succession relationship to its own subsequent moment.

3. Milestone: The Condition of the Perceived Object, and the First Two of Six Kinds of Dominant Condition

The treatise turns to the third of the four conditions, asking what should be called the condition of the perceived object, then answering in two parts: for the five sense-consciousness bodies, form, sound, smell, taste, and touch serve, each in its proper order, as their condition of the perceived object — form for the eye-consciousness, sound for the ear-consciousness, smell for the nose-consciousness, taste for the tongue-consciousness, and touch for the body-consciousness; while for mental consciousness, all twelve internal and external sense-bases together serve as its condition of the perceived object, since mental consciousness alone among the six can take in everything the five senses perceive and more besides. The treatise then turns to the fourth condition, the condition of dominant influence, explaining it through six distinct kinds. The first, the co-arising dominant condition, names how the six sense-bases — eye through mind — serve as the co-arising dominant condition for their corresponding six consciousnesses, since a consciousness such as eye-consciousness can only function when its own sense-faculty remains intact, together with its object currently present and attention capable of inducing it. The second, the inducing dominant condition, names how the mental factor of attention itself serves as the dominant condition inducing each consciousness toward its object, since without attention, one may look without seeing or listen without hearing — attention alone rousing the mind into activity, and arising all the more readily when an object is unusually vast, striking, or agreeable.

4. Milestone: Four More Kinds of Dominant Condition — Karma's Quiet Ledger, and Master Wùdá's Face-Sore of Pride

The treatise explains the remaining four kinds of dominant condition. The third, the mutual dominant condition, names how mind and its accompanying mental factors, arising together, serve reciprocally as each other's co-arising dominant condition, since any phenomenon requires the convergence of many causes and conditions together. The fourth, the previously-accomplished dominant condition, names how pure or impure karma performed in the past serves as the dominant condition for a later desired or undesired result and its corresponding ripened fruit — wholesome karma serving as dominant condition for a future desirable result, unwholesome karma for an undesirable one; the lecture explains that the ripened fruit corresponds to the store-consciousness itself, the total retributive embodiment across the three realms and five destinies, produced by directive karma, while the outflow-result corresponds to the particular circumstances of that embodiment — one's lifespan, fortune, wealth, status, intelligence, and beauty, along with every pleasant or painful sensation experienced through the six consciousnesses — produced by fulfilling karma; though the lecture notes that even more fundamental than either karma is the seed of conceptual proliferation attached to mere names, the truly decisive condition underlying which family, appearance, strength, lifespan, and resources one is born into. The fifth, the condition that brings a result to completion, names how a field together with manure and water serves as the dominant condition bringing crops and seedlings to fruition. And the sixth, the dominant condition of skilled craft, names how this or that craft-knowledge serves as the dominant condition for this or that worldly craft-activity, just as a computer specialist requires computer knowledge before being able to do computer work.

Having completed all six kinds of dominant condition, he notes, the treatise turns to how the four conditions as a whole should be classified — first into two broader categories of cause, then into their relationship with the ten causes taught elsewhere in the Bodhisattva Stage.

5. Milestone: Only One of Four Conditions Truly Generates — The Rest Are Merely Instrumental, and Why All Phenomena Never Arise at Once

The treatise now classifies the four conditions into two broader categories: among these four, the causal condition alone, in relation to the phenomenon it produces, is called the generative cause, since only a seed itself can truly generate a phenomenon; the remaining three conditions — immediate succession, the perceived object, and dominant influence — in relation to the phenomenon produced, should be understood as merely instrumental causes, secondary yet indispensable conditions without which the phenomenon could still not come about. The treatise explains why phenomena do not all arise simultaneously: precisely because a phenomenon can only arise once the instrumental conditions for this or that phenomenon are actually present before it, does the seed of this or that phenomenon actually give rise to it — therefore phenomena never possess the fault of arising all together at once, since even though the store-consciousness contains countless seeds, the supporting conditions for their arising are never all present simultaneously, meaning no being's past deeds, however numerous, ripen all at once — a genuinely hopeful teaching, the lecture notes, since it means present diligence in keeping the mind pure, and preventing unwholesome seeds from finding their conditions, can still meaningfully forestall karma already sown. The treatise closes this section by noting that these four conditions should also be understood as establishing the ten causes taught in the Bodhisattva Stage — the generative cause corresponding to the cause of arising itself, and the instrumental cause corresponding to the remaining nine causes together.

6. Milestone: Reconciling Scripture — How Mental Consciousness Alone Can Take Even a Non-Existent Past or Future as Its Object

Having established through reasoning that past and future are unreal while only the present truly exists, the treatise now turns to a further, extensive task: reconciling this conclusion with several scriptural passages that, read on their surface, seem to affirm the opposite. This reconciliation unfolds across four sub-topics: first, that mental consciousness can take even what is not real as its object; second, that there is meaningfully such a thing as past karma; third, that the Buddha spoke of past and future conditioned phenomena as existing; and fourth, that there are meaningfully three time-periods at all — each sub-topic resolving an apparent tension through the Buddha's own hidden intention rather than through literal, ultimate assertion. The treatise opens the first sub-topic with a scriptural objection: the Blessed One himself said that past conditioned phenomena serve as a condition giving rise to mental consciousness, and likewise that future conditioned phenomena serve as a condition giving rise to mental consciousness — yet if past and future conditioned phenomena are not truly existent, why would the Blessed One teach that they serve as a condition for mental consciousness's arising at all? The objection sharpens further: if mental consciousness can indeed arise by taking a non-existent thing as its object, how could this fail to contradict the Buddha's own subtle teaching elsewhere that consciousness of every kind arises through two conditions — for the five sense consciousnesses, their corresponding sense-faculty together with their corresponding object, and, extending this same principle, mental consciousness through mental faculty and phenomena? The treatise answers by locating exactly what distinguishes mental consciousness from the five senses: because mental consciousness alone is capable of taking hold of what the five sense-consciousness bodies themselves can never engage, the Blessed One provisionally applied the name "phenomena" to this scope — and it is precisely this expanded reach that lets one truly say mental consciousness arises by taking mind and phenomena as its condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames these four sub-topics of "reconciling with scriptural statements" as the treatise's own way of taking seriously every passage that might seem to embarrass its conclusion that past and future are unreal, rather than simply ignoring inconvenient scripture — a mark, he suggests, of genuinely rigorous argument. He highlights that this first sub-topic touches one of the sharpest disputes between the Sarvāstivādin and the Yogācāra schools: the Sarvāstivādin held that whatever serves as an object of cognition must be ultimately, substantially real — even the perceived unity of a "tortoise's hair" requires some real tortoise and real hair combined — whereas the Sautrāntika and, following them, the Yogācāra held that mental consciousness's object need not be substantially real at all, precisely because mental consciousness's own scope vastly exceeds what the five senses can ever perceive. Having identified this key distinguishing capacity of mental consciousness, he notes, the treatise turns to a second question: how do we know this expanded capacity is genuinely the Buddha's own intended meaning, rather than merely a convenient reading imposed after the fact?

7. Milestone: Why We Know This Is the Buddha's Real Intention — Empirically Available, Yet Never Substantially Real

The treatise poses a second question within this same sub-topic: by what reason or condition can one know that the Blessed One indeed held this hidden intention regarding mental consciousness's expanded scope? The treatise answers by pointing to what is directly, empirically observable: mental consciousness plainly does take past and future consciousness-related events as its object — this much is verifiable in ordinary worldly experience by anyone who introspects — yet the object taken in this way is not thereby subsumed under the category of substantially real phenomena belonging to the sense-base of mental objects. The lecture explains the underlying logic at stake: the five sense-consciousness bodies can only ever function through their own specific, self-characteristic object, and only ever in the present moment, whereas mental consciousness alone can additionally engage universal characteristics shared across many things, and can additionally range across all three time-periods — past, present, and future together — a scope of activity utterly unlike the five senses. Since this activity of taking past and future events as objects is something every person can confirm through their own ordinary experience, one cannot say that past and future events are simply incapable of giving rise to mental consciousness; yet since these very events are not substantially existent phenomena belonging to the sense-base of mental objects, one must ask how they could possibly give rise to mental consciousness at all — the resolution being that although past and future events are not themselves substantially real, mental consciousness is nonetheless capable of discriminating their appearance and thereby taking hold of them, and it is precisely this discriminated appearance that receives the provisional designation "phenomena," on the strength of which the treatise can say that "phenomena" gives rise to mental consciousness — this very account constituting the Blessed One's own hidden intention, directed against the Sarvāstivādin's insistence that every object of cognition, even a tortoise's hair or a rabbit's horn, must ultimately be composed of substantially real constituents.

8. Milestone: Why "Phenomena" Covers Both the Real and the Unreal — And the First Reasoning: Contemplating No-Self

The treatise clarifies the very name "phenomena" as applied to mental consciousness's object: whatever possesses genuine existent nature is established as having the meaning of existence, and mental consciousness is capable of holding onto that meaning of existence; whatever lacks such nature is established as having the meaning of non-existence, and mental consciousness is equally capable of holding onto that meaning of non-existence — both are therefore called "phenomena," unlike the Sarvāstivādin position that only substantially real things across all three times can ever be an object of cognition. The treatise strengthens this with two further considerations: first, that denying mental consciousness's capacity for the non-existent would directly contradict the Buddha's own teaching that mental consciousness takes hold of every kind of meaning whatsoever, both existent and non-existent — a scriptural fault; and second, that it would be simply incoherent to claim past and future are exactly as substantially real as the present — a logical fault, since a practitioner who could only ever cognize what exists could never uninterruptedly contemplate the full scope of knowable phenomena, including their impermanence and selflessness, contrary to reason itself. Having thus firmly established that mental consciousness genuinely does take the non-existent as its object, the treatise turns to five extensive reasonings that further confirm this same conclusion, the first being the reasoning that establishes selflessness: as the Blessed One's own subtle teaching states, there is no self to be found whether within, without, or in between the two — this self has no nature whatsoever, subsumed under neither the conditioned nor the unconditioned — yet the consciousness that contemplates this shared characteristic of selflessness across all phenomena does not fail to take this very absence as its object while functioning; this is called the first reasoning.

16. Milestone: The Third Topic Closes, the Fourth Opens — Arising, Aging, Abiding, and Impermanence Have No Reality Apart from the Five Aggregates

Having completed the third of five topics within this chapter's opening section — the three times and four conditions — the treatise turns to its fourth topic: mind-dissociated formations and the other provisionally established phenomena, a topic covering the arising, aging, abiding, and impermanence taught among the mind-dissociated formations, along with attainment, the life faculty, the class of sentient beings, the state of being an ordinary being, association, bodies of names, phrases, and syllables, transformation, determinate distinction, correspondence, speed, sequence, time, number, the two mindless meditative attainments, expressed and unexpressed karma, ethical restraint, and the unconditioned phenomena of space and non-analytical cessation — all provisionally established rather than substantially, separately existing entities in their own right. 17. Milestone: Not Even the Present "Arising" Is Real — Only Each Phenomenon's Own Seed Ever Truly Generates Anything

The treatise completes its refutation of arising's substantial reality by turning to the present: nor can a present "arising" generate present conditioned phenomena either. The lecture explains why through a subtle but decisive point: while present phenomena do display the mark of arising, this mark itself is never what actually generates them — each conditioned phenomenon is generated by its own distinct seed. The names "arising," for a phenomenon's actual manifestation, and "generation," for a seed's own coming-into-activity, together with "presentness" itself, are simply different labels applied to this same mark of arising, inseparable from present phenomena themselves — apart from these labels, no separate, real entity called "arising" can be found. The treatise sharpens this into a direct dilemma: if this mark of arising simply were the phenomenon's own arising, then claiming this mark "generates" that phenomenon would be incoherent, since a phenomenon cannot generate itself; and if this mark of arising were instead the phenomenon's generating cause, then each single phenomenon would absurdly require two acts of generation — one from its own seed, another from this so-called mark of arising — which is equally incoherent, since a phenomenon's own seed alone is already fully sufficient to generate it, with no need to posit any further, separately existing "arising" that generates on top of that. The treatise concludes this line of reasoning by extending it directly: just as with arising, so too should aging, abiding, and impermanence be understood by this same reasoning — none of these four possesses any separate substantial reality, being nothing more than provisional designations applied within the ongoing transformation of conditioned phenomena themselves.

18. Milestone: Four Names for One Passage — Arising, Aging, Abiding, and Impermanence Defined

Having established that arising, aging, abiding, and impermanence possess no separate substantial reality, the treatise now defines each precisely as a label applied to different phases of a single phenomenon's own passage. 19. Milestone: Why the Buddha Names Only Three Marks of the Conditioned, Not Four — Future, Past, and Present Each Get Their Own

The treatise raises a question many attentive students of the scriptures naturally ask: if conditioned phenomena fully possess four marks — arising, aging, abiding, and ceasing — why does the Blessed One usually teach only three: arising, ceasing, and abiding-with-change? The treatise answers that this is because all conditioned phenomena are revealed through the three time-periods: arising is taught with reference to the future, since a phenomenon originally absent in the future comes to arise; ceasing is taught with reference to the past, since a phenomenon, once arisen, immediately falls away into the past; and abiding-with-change is taught with reference to the present, since only in the present moment can abiding be found at all, and the very transformation from an earlier to a later phase likewise occurs only in the present — abiding and change are therefore combined into this single mark tied to the present. The treatise then raises a further, related question: since the Buddha taught noble disciples to contemplate all four marks fully, why does scripture say only that noble disciples observe arising and ceasing with respect to the five aggregates, without mentioning observation of abiding-with-change? The treatise answers that arising and abiding-with-change are both revealed through the single mark of arising, so these two are combined into one category establishing the class of "arising," while ceasing alone establishes the class of "ceasing" — meaning the instruction to observe arising and ceasing already implicitly includes abiding-with-change within it, since change is nothing but a further aspect of how arising unfolds moment to moment; the Buddha's own teaching elsewhere that one moment of genuinely seeing arising and ceasing outweighs a hundred years lived without seeing it captures precisely why this abbreviated twofold observation is already sufficient for liberating insight.

20. Milestone: Why Impermanence Is Taught At All — And Queen Mallikā's Unbroken Calm at News of Her Husband and Thirty-Two Sons

The treatise explains why the Buddha specifically emphasizes the mark of impermanence among the marks of the conditioned: because contemplating impermanence gives rise to revulsion, this text speaks only of this mark — observing impermanence within conditioned phenomena is what produces revulsion, dispassion, and liberation, and it is for this reason that impermanence's own nature and characteristics deserve special, sustained reflection. 22. Milestone: Eleven Kinds of "Arising" Begin — From Moment-to-Moment Flux to the Nine Fetters Driving Rebirth

The treatise now distinguishes eleven kinds of arising: momentary arising, continuity arising, growth arising, arising through distinct mental states, undesirable arising, desirable arising, inferior arising, middling arising, excellent arising, arising with something superior remaining, and unsurpassed arising. It explains the first six in turn. Momentary arising names how the aggregates constituting conditioned phenomena arise ever anew, moment after moment, never the same twice. Continuity arising names how a being, whether bound by the full range of fetters or only some, passes from one class of beings upon death and continues, its aggregates arising anew, within another class of beings — the lecture explaining the nine fetters at length: craving and aversion, arising when encountering agreeable or disagreeable objects; pride, arising from any comparison of self with others regardless of direction; ignorance, whether concerning grasping a self or grasping phenomena; views, mistaken conclusions formed through improper attention or bad companionship and then clung to as uniquely correct; grasping, the further emotional attachment that follows a wrong view; doubt, the absence of settled confidence in the truth of cause and effect; envy, an inability to bear another's flourishing; and stinginess, an unwillingness to give that results in poverty — any combination of these nine fetters, complete or partial, drives a being's continued rebirth throughout the three realms and five destinies. Growth arising names the successive stages of a single life, from infancy through childhood, youth, and adulthood, all the way to old age and decline. And arising through distinct mental states names how a being, encountering one object after another moment by moment throughout the day and night, gives rise to an ever-shifting parade of different minds — sometimes joined with pleasure, sometimes with pain, sometimes with neither, sometimes greedy, sometimes free of greed, and so on through every possible mental quality, this restless flux likened elsewhere to a whirling wheel of fire, ceaselessly changing shape from moment to moment.

23. Milestone: The Three Realms as Inferior, Middling, and Excellent Birth — and Four Levels of Awareness Entering the Womb

The treatise explains undesirable arising as rebirth in the hells, as an animal, or as a hungry ghost, and desirable arising as rebirth among humans or in the pleasant destinies of the heavens. It then distinguishes inferior, middling, and excellent arising in three ways. First, according to the three realms: birth in the inferior desire realm is inferior arising, since desire-realm beings crave the objects of the five senses as their lowest form of pleasure, the lecture illustrating this with the classical image of a maggot that delights in filth — pulled out of the latrine to somewhere clean, it cannot survive and must be returned to the very filth it craves, exactly as desire-realm beings cling to their own coarse pleasures; birth in the middling form realm is middling arising, its beings luminous in body, entirely male, sustained by the joy of meditative absorption rather than by coarse sensory craving; and birth in the excellent formless realm is excellent arising, the most refined of all forms of life, purely mental and free of any coarse physical obstruction. The treatise then distinguishes the same three according to how a being enters the womb: entering with no awareness at all — of entering, abiding, or leaving the womb — is inferior arising, the condition of all ordinary beings; entering the womb aware only of entering, or aware of both entering and abiding but not of leaving, is middling arising; and being aware of entering, abiding, and leaving alike is excellent arising. The lecture explains this fourfold scheme of awareness in full: a wheel-turning monarch, or a first- or second-fruit noble one, enters the womb with awareness only of whose child it will be; an independently-awakened being, or a bodhisattva in certain stages of practice, enters and abides with awareness but leaves unaware; and a bodhisattva already deep in the path, destined to attain buddhahood in this very life, remains aware throughout entering, abiding, and leaving alike, experiencing none of the ordinary suffering of birth — the lecture illustrating an ordinary being's own entry into the womb with an evocative simile of a being caught in a dark, stormy wind, desperately seeking any shelter, mistaking a narrow, uncomfortable crevice or a splendid palace for refuge depending on its accumulated merit, and thereby unknowingly entering its mother's womb.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the maggot-in-filth simile as deliberately uncomfortable precisely to jolt a listener out of complacency about desire-realm pleasures: however refined they may seem from within, the treatise insists they are, from any higher vantage, exactly this coarse and this compulsive. He notes the lecture pauses here to weigh two long-standing approaches this raises for practitioners — boldly continuing to cultivate the bodhisattva path across countless future lives without fear of forgetting, versus seeking rebirth in a pure land first to secure irreversible progress before returning to help others — presenting both as legitimate, complementary orientations rather than declaring one correct. Having completed the three-realm and womb-entry analyses of inferior, middling, and excellent arising, he notes, the treatise turns to a third and final analysis of this same threefold distinction, this time according to cause and result.

24. Milestone: Completing Eleven Kinds of Arising — Cause and Result, Realm and Peak, Continuity and Its Final Cessation

The treatise completes its third analysis of inferior, middling, and excellent arising, this time by cause and result: birth produced by defiled karma, together with its defiled resultant outflow, is inferior arising; birth produced by wholesome karma, together with its wholesome resultant outflow, is excellent arising; and birth produced by neutral karma, apart from either wholesome or unwholesome ripened results, is middling arising. The treatise then takes up its eighth kind, arising with something superior remaining and unsurpassed arising, explained first by realm and level: rebirth anywhere from the desire realm up through the sphere of nothingness still has something more excellent above it, and is therefore called arising with something superior remaining, while rebirth specifically in the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the very summit of the three realms, has nothing higher above it at all, and is therefore called unsurpassed arising. The treatise then explains this same pair a second way, by continuity: every arising throughout a being's continuum, apart from the very final moment of an arhat's aggregates at the point of complete extinction, is called arising with something superior remaining, since further arising still follows; while the arhat's own last, final moment of aggregates — after which no further arising of any kind occurs — is called unsurpassed arising, since it is the arising beyond which no further arising remains. With this, all eleven kinds of arising have now been fully distinguished; the treatise turns next to the differences among aging.

25. Milestone: Five Kinds of Aging — the Body's Long Catalog of Decline, and Why the Mind Alone Need Never Grow Old

The treatise distinguishes five kinds of aging: bodily aging, mental aging, aging of the lifespan, aging through decline, and aging through the transformation of one's own being. Bodily aging is explained through an extensive catalog drawn from the Sūtra on Dependent Origination: graying hair, slackening and wrinkling skin, diminishing vigor, accumulating ailments, a stooped and bent spine, dark blemishes marring the complexion, labored and hurried breathing, a forward-bent posture, dependence on a cane, confusion, frailty, diminishment, decline, faculties worn to full ripeness like overripe fruit ready to fall, failing function in every sense organ, decaying formations, and a body visibly decaying in form — all of this, up to but not including the body's actual dissolution when consciousness finally departs it, is called bodily aging. Mental aging is explained as any shift from a better state to a worse one: from a mind joined with pleasure to one joined with pain, from a wholesome mind to a defiled one, or from a mind full of hope regarding something desired to one whose hope has been dashed — whenever the mind moves in this declining direction, this is called mental aging, and the lecture urges that even as the body inevitably ages, the mind itself need never grow old, so long as it remains joined with wholesome states and unfailing hope, alive and vigorous regardless of the body's own condition. Aging of the lifespan is explained simply as the steady, unstoppable diminishment of one's remaining years, day by day, moment by moment, until it is entirely exhausted. Aging through decline is explained as any reversal of former flourishing — wealth turning to poverty, health turning to sickness, strength turning to weakness. And aging through the transformation of one's own being is explained as the most severe kind of all: a being's own aggregates, once belonging to a flourishing, favorable destiny, ceasing entirely upon death, only for an entirely different set of aggregates to arise within a lower, unfavorable destiny instead.

26. Milestone: Five Kinds of Abiding Begin — From a Single Fleeting Moment to an Entire Great Eon

The treatise distinguishes five kinds of abiding: momentary abiding, continuity abiding, abiding through continuity of the perceived object, undistracted abiding, and abiding by established norm. It explains the first two. Momentary abiding names how a conditioned phenomenon, once it has arisen, momentarily continues for the briefest instant before passing — this is momentary abiding. Continuity abiding names two distinct forms: sentient beings, in whatever place and whatever form of embodiment they find themselves, sustain that embodiment by relying on one of the four kinds of nourishment until their allotted lifespan is exhausted — this is a sentient being's own continuity abiding; while the external material world itself abides for the duration of an entire great eon — this is the continuity abiding of the outer physical universe; together, these two constitute continuity abiding.
