Fascicle 69 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十九 · The Śrāvaka Ground, Continued

Five Reasons the Buddha's Own Precepts Run Deep

Continuing directly from fascicle 68, Maitreya closes the vinaya's own thirteen divisions in full — the profound depth behind how the Buddha established every precept, the Three-Cart Monk permitted meat, wine, and books to enter the Dharma, ten occasions when naming a fellow monastic's broken discipline is genuinely permitted, and five kinds of illustrative teaching culminating in the Lotus Sūtra's burning house — the transcript coming to rest with the entire topic of the vinaya's own characteristics, the seventh of the śrāvaka canon's thirteen topics, brought to a full close.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 69 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Five Ways in Which Establishing the Precepts Runs Profoundly Deep

This third part of the Śrāvaka Ground's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 68, taking up the eleventh of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions: the profound depth behind how the precepts themselves came to be established. Five distinct reasons are given for why this establishing runs so deep. The first is the establishing of training itself, the deep question of when the Buddha set out only a brief, general rule of training and when he instead set out the full, detailed pratimoksha precepts one by one, together with why certain allowances and prohibitions were made as they were. The second is the establishing of what constitutes violation, the deep question of exactly what marks make an act count as an offense or not. The third is the establishing of release, the deep question of under what circumstances and by what actual process an offense may be confessed and its practitioner released from it. The fourth is the establishing of cessation, the deep question of when a monastic may be permitted to temporarily set aside observing certain minor precepts, as when the Buddha himself taught that the lesser and minor precepts could be relinquished under genuine necessity, a permission whose own proper conditions require careful discussion. The fifth is the establishing of formal procedure, referring to the Sangha's own formal assembly proceedings for deciding communal matters, in which a resolution is announced and the assembly's consent is sought, the deep principle governing how such formal procedures themselves came to be established.

Chinese Source

復次，應知毗奈耶，由五種制立為最甚深。云何名為五種制立？一、學制立，二、犯制立，三、出離制立，四、止息制立，五、羯磨制立。

Ru-Yi Translation

The vinaya's own establishing runs profoundly deep in five ways. What are the five? The establishing of training, the establishing of violation, the establishing of release, the establishing of cessation, and the establishing of formal procedure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fivefold opening as this treatise's own signal that the vinaya was never a simple, mechanically applied rulebook but a body of law whose every feature, from the choice between brief and detailed precepts through the conditions for confession, temporary relief, and communal decision-making, rests on genuinely deep underlying reasoning. He highlights the fourth reason's own citation of the Buddha's own teaching that lesser precepts could be relinquished as already anticipating a tension the coming sections will need to resolve carefully, since a permission so easily misused for convenience demands exactly the kind of deep, careful reasoning this opening promises to supply.

2. Section: Five Points Behind Why a Precept Was Established Briefly or in Detail

The first of the five, the establishing of training, is now explained through five further points. The first point, being at ease, refers to those disciples the Buddha was guiding who were not, at the time, actually swept along by the force of affliction, afflictions not rendering them un-free; for such disciples at genuine ease within themselves, the Buddha established only a brief, general rule of training, trusting their own self-controlled practice to apply it rightly according to circumstance. The second point, being not at ease, refers to the reverse: disciples who were, at the time, actually swept along by affliction's own force, rendered genuinely un-free by it; for such disciples the Buddha established the detailed pratimoksha precepts, precept-by-precept training rules matched exactly to the un-free conduct these disciples had actually shown.

Chinese Source

言自在者，若時所化不隨煩惱勢力而行，非諸煩惱令不自在，爾時即依如是所化自在學行，隨意自在。不自在者，若時所化隨諸煩惱自在而行，由諸煩惱令不自在，爾時即依如是所化不自在學行，制立不自在學處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being at ease refers to a disciple not swept along by affliction's force, for whom a brief general rule of training was established, trusted to apply it freely. Being not at ease refers to a disciple swept along by affliction's force, for whom precept-by-precept training was established, matched to that un-free conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this at-ease and not-at-ease distinction as this treatise's own quietly respectful acknowledgment that not every disciple requires the same degree of external constraint, a genuinely self-controlled practitioner trusted with only a brief general principle while another, still visibly bound by affliction, actually needs the fuller precept spelled out precisely because that same trust cannot yet safely be extended. He highlights the phrase "matched to that un-free conduct" as this section's own key insight, that the detailed pratimoksha itself is not an arbitrary catalogue but a direct, responsive record of exactly the faults disciples had actually shown, each rule tracing back to a real and specific need.

3. Section: Grave Rules Guard Against Ruin, Lesser Rules Guard Against Distraction

The third point, revealing the fault of ruined discipline, explains why the Buddha established the grave, foundational precepts as he did: observing that the intrinsic wrongs of killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech should never under any circumstance be committed, and that breaking them ruins a monastic's entire discipline, the Buddha established precepts specifically to safeguard against this ruin, so that disciples would continually guard their own foundational conduct. The fourth point, revealing the fault of delighting in base conduct, explains why the Buddha established the lesser, merely prohibitory precepts as he did: observing that unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental conduct, though not intrinsically wrong in the way killing or stealing is, nonetheless obstructs a disciple's own diligent cultivation of wholesome qualities, giving rise to restlessness and scatteredness that undermines calming and insight alike, the Buddha established these prohibitory precepts to close off that same obstruction. The fifth point, whether these same two faults were actually present or absent, explains the Buddha's own governing principle for deciding between the two approaches: observing that a disciple at genuine ease showed neither fault in actual conduct, the Buddha made no need to establish anything further beyond the brief general rule; but observing that a disciple not at ease actually displayed such faults in conduct, the Buddha established the detailed precept-by-precept training precisely in response. **This closes the discussion of the establishing of training, the first of the five ways in which establishing the precepts runs profoundly deep.**

Chinese Source

顯現尸羅壞過失者，觀諸性罪定不應行，制立隨護尸羅學處。顯現喜樂鄙業過失者，謂觀能障勤修善品，是故制立遮罪學處。彼二過失行不行者，謂佛觀彼自在所化過失不行，故無制立；觀不自在過失現行，制立學處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Revealing the fault of ruined discipline: observing that intrinsic wrongs should never be done, precepts were established to guard discipline. Revealing the fault of delighting in base conduct: observing that it obstructs cultivating wholesome qualities, prohibitory precepts were established. Whether these faults were present or absent: the Buddha established nothing further for the fault-free, and established precept-by-precept training where fault actually appeared.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of ruined discipline and base-conduct delight as this treatise's own clear map of why grave and lesser precepts differ in kind and not merely in degree, one guarding against an outright collapse of discipline itself and the other merely closing off a subtler obstruction to concentration and wisdom. He highlights the fifth point's own summary as tying the whole section together, showing that every one of the Buddha's own choices between a brief rule and a detailed one traces back to this same single governing observation, whether a fault had actually appeared in a disciple's own conduct or not.

4. Section: Five Obstructions Rising from a Single Act of Violation

The second of the five, the establishing of violation, is now taken up: what marks make an act count as an offense at all. Approached first from the angle of what an offense obstructs, an offense is defined as whatever obstructs the arising of every wholesome quality, whether the wholesome fruits of calming and insight or of discipline, concentration, and wisdom together. Such obstruction comes in five kinds. The first, the obstruction of laxity, blocks diligent effort itself. The second, the obstruction of guilt, is the fault that follows directly from having actually offended. The third, the obstruction of disrespect, arises from holding the training itself in contempt. The fourth, the obstruction of remorse, is the lingering regret that follows an unresolved offense. The fifth, the obstruction of cognitive hindrance, blocks a practitioner from genuinely understanding the four truths and the true nature of things. Each of these five, still to be explained individually, obstructs the path to genuine liberation in its own distinct way, together showing why the Buddha's own establishing of what constitutes violation runs so profoundly deep.

Chinese Source

謂能障礙所有善法，令不得生。當知此障略有五種。一、慢緩障，二、有罪障，三、輕慢障，四、惡作障，五、所知障。

Ru-Yi Translation

An offense obstructs every wholesome quality from arising. This obstruction comes in five kinds: the obstruction of laxity, the obstruction of guilt, the obstruction of disrespect, the obstruction of remorse, and the obstruction of cognitive hindrance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fivefold obstruction as this treatise's own way of showing that an offense is never merely a broken rule in isolation, but a genuine disruption reaching all the way from a practitioner's own effort through their guilt, their attitude toward training, their unresolved regret, and finally their very capacity to understand reality itself. He highlights the progression itself as worth watching closely in the sections that follow, since these five obstructions, as the coming explanation shows, tend to arise not independently but one out of another in a single unfolding chain.

5. Section: From Laxity to Cognitive Hindrance, the Five Obstructions Explained

Each of the five obstructions is now explained in turn. The obstruction of laxity refers to sheer indolence, a practitioner failing to apply diligent effort toward any wholesome quality at all. The obstruction of guilt refers to a practitioner defiled by the actual arising of craving, aversion, delusion, or any of the further secondary afflictions, such as resentment, spite, envy, or deceit, who then clings firmly to that same affliction once arisen rather than releasing it, dwelling on it again and again until it deepens further still. The obstruction of disrespect refers to a practitioner who holds the training itself in no genuine regard, showing little reverence toward the various precepts, seeing no real danger in what they violate and therefore violating it freely, and once having violated it, failing to swiftly confess and disclose the offense to a fellow practitioner, an underlying lack of genuine reverence toward the teacher and the training alike running beneath the whole pattern. The obstruction of remorse refers to a practitioner continually touched by defiled regret over a past wrongdoing, unable to skillfully and completely dispel that regret, left instead with lingering distress, dejection, and repeated unresolved remorse churning within. The obstruction of cognitive hindrance refers to a practitioner who, harboring such unresolved regret, takes no genuine joy in even genuinely pure discipline; lacking that joy, no ease of mind follows; and so the mind never actually settles into concentration, and without concentration a practitioner can neither genuinely know nor genuinely observe reality as it actually is, this final failure itself constituting the obstruction of cognitive hindrance.

Chinese Source

所知障者，謂如有一，心懷變悔，依因淨戒不生歡喜；不歡喜故，不生適悅；如是乃至心不得定；心不定故，無如實知、無如實觀。由此因緣，名所知障。

Ru-Yi Translation

The obstruction of cognitive hindrance refers to a practitioner harboring unresolved regret who, even relying on genuinely pure discipline, takes no joy in it; lacking joy, no ease follows; the mind never settles into concentration; and without concentration, no genuine knowing or observing of reality follows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the obstruction of cognitive hindrance's own chain, unresolved regret blocking joy, blocked joy blocking ease, blocked ease blocking concentration, and blocked concentration finally blocking genuine wisdom itself, as this treatise's own precise account of exactly how an unresolved moral fault quietly undermines even a sincere meditator's own formal practice. He highlights the obstruction of disrespect's own underlying cause, a simple failure to see genuine danger in violation, as this fivefold catalogue's own psychological root, since every subsequent obstruction traces back to this same failure to take the training itself seriously enough to fear breaking it.

6. Section: One Obstruction Breeds the Next, Then Five Ruined Motivations That Trap a Practitioner in Repeated Offense

These five obstructions, moreover, arise in a genuine causal sequence, one out of another. Touched first by the obstruction of laxity, a practitioner fails to apply diligent effort and so clings to whatever affliction and secondary affliction has already arisen; from this same clinging follows the obstruction of guilt; touched by guilt, a practitioner loses genuine reverence toward the various trainings and instead comes to delight in the very act of violation; from this same delight follows the obstruction of disrespect; touched by disrespect, defiled regret arises; unable to dispel that regret, the obstruction of remorse follows; touched by remorse, that same regret only deepens further, blocking joy, blocking ease, and finally blocking concentration itself; and a mind never settled in concentration falls, at last, under the obstruction of cognitive hindrance. Precisely the reverse of these five obstructions constitutes their five corresponding absences, no obstruction of laxity, no obstruction of guilt, no obstruction of disrespect, no obstruction of remorse, and no obstruction of cognitive hindrance. **This closes the discussion of violation considered from the angle of obstruction.** A further angle then opens: violation considered from the angle of a practitioner's own underlying motivation. A practitioner whose motivation toward genuine discipline has already been ruined cannot even manage to free themselves from an offense already committed, let alone avoid committing one in the first place; such ruined motivation itself comes in five kinds, no desire to arouse diligent effort, a desire that remains attached to affliction itself, an actual desire to commit the very offense in question, no desire to dispel remorse once it arises, and no desire to induce genuine concentration.

Chinese Source

與此相違，當知即是五種無障。謂無慢緩障、無有罪障、無輕慢障、無惡作障、無所知障。復次，意樂毀壞者，於其所犯尚不能出，況能無犯。謂略有五種。一、於精進無發起欲，二、於煩惱有染著欲，三、於所犯有起犯欲，四、於惡作無除遣欲，五、於等持無引發欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

The reverse of these constitutes the five non-obstructions. A practitioner with ruined motivation cannot even free themselves from an offense, let alone avoid one. Such ruined motivation comes in five kinds: no desire to arouse effort, an attached desire toward affliction, an actual desire to offend, no desire to dispel remorse, and no desire to induce concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the full causal chain, laxity breeding clinging, clinging breeding guilt, guilt breeding disrespect, disrespect breeding regret, and regret finally breeding cognitive hindrance, as this treatise's own single most instructive account of how a first small indolence can, left unchecked, escalate all the way into a genuinely blocked mind incapable of wisdom itself. He highlights the shift to the angle of motivation as deepening the same lesson one level further, since obstruction alone describes what happens once an offense occurs, while ruined motivation describes the prior inner disposition that makes repeated offense all but inevitable in the first place.

7. Section: Genuine Motivation Alone, the Buddha Taught, Truly Frees a Practitioner from an Offense

Precisely the reverse describes a practitioner whose motivation toward genuine discipline is instead fully intact: such a practitioner would not even commit an offense in the first place, let alone need to free themselves from one already committed. This fulfilled motivation likewise comes in five kinds: a genuine desire to arouse diligent effort, cultivating right understanding of the training and applying oneself to sitting meditation, reciting the Buddha's name, and the daily practices alike; a mind that holds no attachment to affliction, quick to release it the moment it arises rather than dwelling on it for days on end; no desire whatsoever to commit an offense in the first place; a genuine desire to dispel remorse the moment it arises, diligently applying the proper antidote rather than allowing regret to fester; and a genuine desire to induce concentration, understanding that meditative discipline can surpass mere formal precepts and even give rise to the path-born discipline that accompanies the noble path itself, and so diligently cultivating calming and insight toward that same end. A practitioner who fulfills all five of these motivations would never commit an offense at all, let alone require any process for being freed from one. The World-Honored One's own teaching confirms this directly: regarding an offense already committed, the Buddha declared that a practitioner is freed from it through genuine motivation, not through punishment; a practitioner who genuinely wishes to be diligent, holds no attachment to affliction, has no desire to offend, swiftly dispels any regret that does arise, and diligently seeks concentration and the noble path needs no external punishment at all in order to be genuinely freed. **This closes the entire discussion of violation considered from the angle of motivation, and with it the discussion of what constitutes violation approached from either angle.**

Chinese Source

復次，意樂具足者，尚無有犯，況出所犯。一、於精進有發起欲，二、於煩惱無染著欲，三、於所犯起無犯欲，四、於惡作有除遣欲，五、於等持有引發欲。如世尊言：於所犯罪，由意樂故我說能出，非治罰故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner with fulfilled motivation would not even offend, let alone need release. Fulfilled motivation comes in five kinds: desire to arouse effort, no attachment to affliction, no desire to offend, desire to dispel remorse, and desire to induce concentration. As the World-Honored One said: release from an offense comes through genuine motivation, not through punishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own direct citation, that genuine motivation rather than punishment is what actually frees a practitioner from an offense, as this whole discussion's own hard-won conclusion, since it locates the entire question of violation and release squarely within the practitioner's own inner disposition rather than in any external disciplinary mechanism alone. He highlights the mirrored structure of ruined and fulfilled motivation, five matching pairs standing in direct opposition, as this treatise's own way of making an otherwise abstract inner disposition into something a practitioner can genuinely examine point by point in their own actual experience.

8. Section: Ten Loci for Investigating Whether an Offense Has Actually Occurred

A further ten loci are then given by which any alleged violation should genuinely be investigated. The first locus is the individually stated pratimoksha law itself, the very code of monastic discipline a practitioner has formally received, whether the full precepts of a monk or nun, the ten precepts of a novice, the six rules of a female probationer, or the five precepts and eight precepts of a lay follower, each investigation beginning from what that practitioner actually vowed to uphold. The second locus is the offense's own particular characteristic as elaborated throughout the broader vinaya literature, determining which of the five offense-categories a given violation actually belongs to. The third locus is the six differences already explained that determine whether an offense counts as heavy, the difference of formal establishment, of the matter involved, of affliction, of habitual repetition, of the person committing it, and of time elapsed before repentance. The fourth locus concerns whatever residual fault remains once the gravest, unpardonable category of offense is set aside, since apart from that single unpardonable category every other offense retains some residual standing still capable of being confessed and purified.

Chinese Source

復次，應由十處思求所犯。謂由別解脫法故。由廣分別毗奈耶故，五犯聚中由犯自相故。由六種差別成重相故，謂制立差別、事差別、煩惱差別、穿穴差別、補特伽羅差別、時差別。由無羞恥者，除波羅闍已迦，所餘有殘相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

An alleged violation should be investigated through ten loci: through the pratimoksha law itself; through the offense's own particular characteristic within the five offense-categories; through six differences determining heaviness, formal establishment, the matter, affliction, habitual repetition, the person, and time; and through whatever residual fault remains apart from the unpardonable category.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this tenfold investigative structure as this treatise's own insistence that judging whether a genuine violation has occurred can never be a snap judgment, but instead demands working systematically through what was actually vowed, what category the act belongs to, how heavy it genuinely is, and what standing still remains afterward. He highlights the fourth locus's own quiet mercy, that every offense apart from the single gravest category still retains some residual standing capable of confession and purification, as continuing this treatise's own consistent refusal to treat any but the most extreme case as beyond genuine repair.

9. Section: How a First Offense Becomes a Formally Established Precept, Closing the Profundity of Violation

Four further loci close the full tenfold investigation. The fifth, sixth, and seventh loci together concern what does not constitute a genuine violation at all: an initial offender, one who commits an act before any corresponding precept yet exists, since the very first instance of such an act, occurring before the Buddha had formally prohibited it, does not itself count as an offense; an act compelled by genuine affliction, as when overwhelming illness or delirium forces conduct otherwise prohibited; and an act compelled by a genuine threat, as when a threat to life or to the continuation of pure conduct itself compels an otherwise prohibited act, each likewise excused rather than judged a genuine violation. The eighth, ninth, and tenth loci together trace how a precept actually comes to be established once genuine violation has occurred: a group of monastics first report the initial offense to the Buddha; the Buddha, wishing to prevent any recurrence, then assembles the full monastic community; and the assembled community, once gathered, formally establishes the offense as a violation going forward, this same threefold sequence, reporting, assembling, and establishing, together constituting how a rule first comes into being. **This closes the entire tenfold investigation into whether an offense has genuinely occurred, and with it the entire discussion of the establishing of violation, the second of the five ways in which establishing the precepts runs profoundly deep.** Once a violation has been formally established in this way, moreover, the Buddha would later, as further circumstances arose, grant further allowances suited to those same circumstances, ensuring that the precept, once established, would never itself become a source of harm to those genuinely upholding it.

Chinese Source

由初業者，非初業者現所行故，由逼惱出離故，由障難出離故。由有犯者，時諸苾芻白大師故；由彼白已，大師為欲止當所犯，集僧眾故；由僧眾集已，制立所犯故。制立所犯已，復於後時隨事開聽，令得究竟無惱害故。

Ru-Yi Translation

An initial offender, one compelled by distress, or one compelled by threat does not genuinely violate. When genuine violation occurs, monastics report it to the teacher; the teacher assembles the community; the assembled community establishes the offense. Once established, further allowances are later granted as circumstances require, so the precept never itself becomes a source of harm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing note on later allowances as this treatise's own reminder that a precept's establishment was never meant to be the end of the story, the vinaya's own historical record showing many rules genuinely revised more than once as the Buddha continued observing how they actually played out in practice. He highlights the threefold sequence of reporting, assembling, and establishing as this treatise's own transparent account of monastic law-making itself, a rule never simply handed down in the abstract but always traceable back to an actual reported incident and a full community's own considered response to it.

10. Section: Lost in the Wilderness with Nothing Left to Eat

Precisely this same threat itself constitutes release, hence its name, release through threat. A fourth kind of release, release through no alternative, describes a practitioner traveling to a distant land, passing through a desolate, resource-stripped wilderness, where some genuine hardship or threat presents itself and the food, clothing, or shelter that practitioner should properly seek and use simply cannot be obtained by any proper means; reverent toward the teaching and genuinely fearful of violating it, yet with truly no alternative left, such a practitioner may make use of whatever is actually available, committing a minor or subsidiary violation in the process without this counting as genuine violation. Several vinaya commentators illustrate this same allowance vividly: Vinaya Master Jǐng notes that where wild beasts genuinely threaten a monastic crossing open wilderness, the Buddha permitted carrying a knife or staff for self-defense despite the ordinary prohibition against a monastic bearing weapons; Vinaya Master Tài explains that in genuinely perilous places where lawful provisions simply cannot be obtained, reverence toward the teaching and fear of wrongdoing notwithstanding, a practitioner with no further alternative remaining commits no fault; and Vinaya Master Wénbèi adds that where no one is present to perform the formal rite of receiving food, a practitioner may receive and eat without it and remain blameless.

Chinese Source

無計出離者，謂若有一，遊於異方，經行曠野匱乏之處，隨有一種障難之法而現在前，隨其所有應受用事求受用法而不能得，遂生敬畏受用此事，於小隨小所犯法中隨有所犯。由此無計即是出離，是故說為無計出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Release through no alternative describes a practitioner traveling through a resource-stripped wilderness where some threat arises and proper provisions cannot be obtained, who, reverent and fearful yet with no alternative, makes use of what is available; this same absence of alternative itself constitutes release, hence its name.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three vinaya commentators' own converging testimony, each addressing a different concrete circumstance yet arriving at the very same conclusion, as this treatise's own demonstration that this allowance was never a single narrow loophole but a genuinely broad principle later teachers found themselves applying again and again to real situations the original rule-makers could not have anticipated in every particular. He highlights the phrase "reverent toward the teaching and genuinely fearful of violating it" as this section's own essential qualifier, distinguishing a practitioner genuinely troubled by an unavoidable necessity from one merely seeking convenient excuse, the very same inward disposition already established as decisive back when motivation itself was under discussion.

11. Section: A Nun Who Broke the No-Singing Precept to Ease a Dying Patient's Mind

The fifth and final kind, release through confession, describes a practitioner who, having committed some violation among the four lesser offense-categories apart from the gravest, immediately discloses it to a fellow practitioner of genuine wisdom, following the vinaya's own confidential procedure, openly declaring the offense and repenting it exactly as the Dharma prescribes; confession itself constitutes genuine purification, and the Buddha permitted release from an offense through precisely this means. Taken together, these five circumstances, absence of defilement, genuine distress, genuine threat, genuine absence of alternative, and open confession, show just how carefully and thoughtfully the Buddha considered every circumstance under which a practitioner might rightly be released from an offense rather than condemned for it. One modern teacher is remembered for a particularly moving illustration of this same spirit: attending a dying patient who loved a certain popular song about a broken heart no one understands, this teacher rewrote its lyrics into an account of the patient's own journey from illness to genuine faith, teaching the patient to sing this reworked version until the patient came, at last, to recite the Buddha's name wholeheartedly, declaring at the very end, before all present, that the Buddha had come, urging everyone to join palms together, and passing in genuine peace; though nuns are ordinarily forbidden from singing, and this teacher had never otherwise cared for singing at all, doing so here to guide a dying person toward genuine release constituted no violation whatsoever, and indeed carried genuine merit, since it helped a suffering being find freedom from affliction and from the cycle of birth and death itself. **This closes the entire discussion of the establishing of release, the third of the five ways in which establishing the precepts runs profoundly deep.**

Chinese Source

說悔出離者，謂如有一，於五犯聚有餘犯中隨有所犯，遂於有智同梵行所，以毗奈耶祕密之法，發露陳說，如法悔除。言小隨小所犯法者，謂除性罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Release through confession describes a practitioner who, having committed some lesser offense, discloses it to a wise fellow practitioner following the vinaya's confidential procedure, openly confessing and repenting as the Dharma prescribes. The minor and subsidiary offenses spoken of throughout refer to everything apart from the intrinsic wrongs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the dying-patient story as this treatise's own living proof that the fivefold framework of release just completed was never a mere abstract legal apparatus, a teacher's own reworked pop song becoming, in this telling, as genuine an instrument of liberation as any formal precept, precisely because the compassionate intent behind it left the underlying discipline entirely uncompromised. He highlights the closing clarification, that every minor and subsidiary offense discussed throughout this entire fivefold release excludes the intrinsic wrongs from the outset, as an important boundary worth remembering going forward, since none of this section's genuine flexibility was ever meant to extend to killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, or false speech themselves.

12. Section: Why Arhats and a Divided Community Are Both Released from Minor Precepts

A further topic then opens: the establishing of cessation, the fourth of the five ways in which establishing the precepts runs profoundly deep, describing five circumstances under which a monastic community may formally suspend certain minor and subsidiary precepts. The first circumstance, purity itself, concerns arhats who have already accomplished everything to be accomplished, whose minds have reached the utmost purity; since such a practitioner could never again commit a defiled offense, the community simply ceases applying every minor and subsidiary precept to that person at all. The second circumstance, preventing schism, addresses a community internally divided over how a given precept should actually be interpreted, one faction regarding a given act as a genuine violation that another faction regards as no violation at all, and vice versa, a disagreement capable of provoking genuine strife and depriving the whole community of its own peace; to quiet such a dispute, the assembled community may formally resolve, through the proper formal procedure and the full community's own consent, to suspend that disputed minor precept for the time being, thereby restoring harmony rather than allowing the dispute to fracture the community altogether.

13. Section: The Three-Cart Monk, Permitted Meat, Wine, and Books to Enter the Dharma

The third circumstance, attracting valuable people into the Dharma, describes a person of genuinely eminent lineage capable of making enormous contributions to the teaching once ordained, whom the community has no other means of actually bringing into monastic life; to attract and welcome such a person, the assembled community may formally resolve to suspend the minor and subsidiary precepts on that person's behalf, opening a genuine door of skillful means to secure a truly valuable disciple. A community encountering someone of eminent lineage, exceptional wisdom, and outstanding talent, capable of benefiting the teaching immensely, might in such a case provide fine lodging and fine food and formally permit that person to forgo certain minor precepts, such as the prohibition against eating after midday, allowing comfortable use of medicinal food in the evening until that person could gradually adjust to monastic life, after which sustained cultivation and genuine attainment of the noble path would naturally follow, benefiting countless beings in turn. Chinese Buddhist history preserves a celebrated instance of precisely this same allowance: the monk Kuījī, popularly remembered as the Three-Cart Monk, was permitted by his own teacher Xuánzàng to enter monastic life bringing three carts along with him, one laden with meat, one with wine and female companions, and one with books, Xuánzàng recognizing in him a disciple of genuinely exceptional wisdom and granting this extraordinary allowance precisely so that such a gifted person would not be turned away, trusting that once he had genuinely learned the Dharma he would in time let all three carts go of his own accord.

14. Section: Eight Allowances During Famine, Closing the Establishing of Cessation

The fourth circumstance, keeping the teaching flourishing, addresses ages of decline, an age of strife, or an age of corruption, when countless beings, their own merit diminished, take no genuine delight in cultivating the many minor and subsidiary precepts, those not yet within the teaching reluctant to enter it and those already within it inclined to drift away, the teaching itself gradually declining rather than flourishing as a result; to counter this same decline, the assembled community may formally resolve to suspend the minor and subsidiary precepts so that the teaching might continue to flourish rather than wither. The fifth and final circumstance, preventing hardship to survival, addresses these same ages of decline when the minor and subsidiary precepts themselves make it genuinely difficult for monastics to survive at all; to relieve this same hardship, the assembled community may formally resolve to suspend those precepts accordingly. The vinaya itself records eight specific allowances permitted during a genuine famine: communal eating and lodging within the monastic boundary to guard provisions from thieves, communal cooking within the boundary for the same reason, cooking for oneself for the same reason, personally gathering food for the same reason, receiving an early meal, meaning food from the community itself, without performing the formal leftover-food rite, receiving food directly from its source, meaning lay provisions, without performing that same rite, receiving walnuts and similar tree fruits after already having eaten without performing that rite, and receiving lotus root, water chestnut, and similar aquatic foods after already having eaten without performing that rite; during a genuine famine, when survival itself hangs in the balance, insisting on every minor precept regardless would only drive monastics from the teaching altogether, and it is for this reason that such allowances were made. **This closes the entire discussion of the establishing of cessation, the fourth of the five ways in which establishing the precepts runs profoundly deep, now fully complete.**

15. Section: A Once-Wealthy Convert Impoverished by His Own Generosity

16. Section: Closing All Five Ways the Vinaya's Own Establishing Runs Profoundly Deep

Each of these ten formal procedures serves the community's own genuine welfare in its own distinct way. The procedure for nourishing exists because certain disciples of pampered upbringing, such as a minister's own child or a prince entering monastic life, someone whose eventual ordination stands to benefit the teaching immensely by drawing others to follow, cannot yet manage without an evening meal as other monastics do; the community accordingly nourishes such a disciple's own gradual adjustment, formally permitting a specially prepared gruel mixed with permitted medicinal ingredients so that this same disciple might survive comfortably within the community until fully accustomed to monastic discipline. The procedure of punitive correction proceeds by genuine degrees, a formal warning issued first, assignment to a strict supervising teacher should the warning go unheeded, and only in the most serious and persistent cases formal separation, requiring a monastic to dwell and reflect alone apart from the community; and the procedure of reinstatement completes this same process, since, as no one who has not yet reached genuine sanctity remains entirely free of fault, a sincerely repentant offender who has genuinely completed this correction is properly welcomed back into full community life once more. **This closes the entire discussion of the establishing of formal procedure, and with it the entire discussion of the five ways in which establishing the precepts runs profoundly deep, the eleventh of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, now fully complete.**

17. Section: Five Kinds of Person, from Genuine Wisdom to a Flash of Lightning

A further, closing reflection completes the discussion of formal procedure: five distinct kinds of person, illustrated through five vivid similes, engage with these same ten formal procedures in five genuinely different ways. The person likened to genuine wisdom relies on the underlying meaning of a formal procedure rather than its bare wording, grasping its actual intent even where the exact prescribed phrasing was never spoken, and acting accordingly. The person likened to a parrot relies only on the bare wording, repeating the prescribed phrasing exactly as given without ever grasping its underlying meaning, sometimes producing genuine confusion, as when someone insists that a face-to-face confession must literally involve two faces turned toward one another rather than, say, toward a Buddha image, when in truth any two parties who genuinely understand one another satisfy the same purpose regardless of exact posture. The person likened to a torch, hearing one thing and understanding ten, needs only a little exposure to a given formal procedure before that same understanding kindles further insight in turn, much as one torch's flame passes readily to light many others. The person likened to a flash of lightning appears only occasionally, taking part in a formal procedure once and then vanishing again just as suddenly, touching the training only briefly and without any sustained interest returning. And the person likened to a faithful painting follows the prescribed formal procedure exactly as given, neither adding to it nor omitting from it, precisely as a realist painter renders exactly one apple where exactly one apple was actually placed before them. **This closes the entire discussion of distinguishing the genuine profundity behind establishing training, violation, release, cessation, and formal procedure.**

18. Section: Five Counterfeit Establishings, Closing the Vinaya's Own Profound Depth

A further topic then closes out this eleventh division: distinguishing five kinds of counterfeit establishing from the genuine establishing already described at length. A counterfeit establishing of training refers to a rule set forth that finds no support in the sutras, appears nowhere in the vinaya, and runs contrary to the true nature of things, specifically meaning a rule that actually increases unwholesome conduct and diminishes wholesome conduct rather than the reverse, the kind of teaching an outside tradition might present as genuine discipline while actually working against the very purposes true discipline serves. A counterfeit establishing of violation refers to declaring a genuine violation to be no violation at all, or declaring what is genuinely no violation to be a violation. A counterfeit establishing of release refers to declaring what is genuinely not yet released to have already been released, or declaring what has genuinely already been released to remain unreleased. A counterfeit establishing of cessation refers to declaring precepts that should not be suspended to be suspended, or declaring precepts that genuinely should be suspended to remain in full force. And a counterfeit establishing of formal procedure refers to declaring an improperly conducted procedure to be lawful, or declaring a genuinely lawful procedure to be improper. **This closes the entire discussion of the vinaya's own profound depth, the eleventh of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, now fully complete in every part.**

19. Section: Ten Occasions When Speaking of a Fellow Monastic's Broken Precepts Is Genuinely Permitted

A further topic then opens: the twelfth of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions, addressing when it is genuinely proper to speak, before someone outside the monastic community, of a fellow monastic's broken discipline, broken views, broken deportment, or broken livelihood. Apart from ten specific occasions still to be explained, any monastic who speaks such words before an outsider should be understood as speaking impurely, a disclosure that should not be made. The first three of these ten permitted occasions concern the three jewels themselves: if someone intends to harm or plunder the Buddha jewel, the Dharma jewel, or the Sangha jewel, and a monastic's own disclosure of a fellow monastic's broken conduct would help prevent that same harm from actually occurring, such disclosure is genuinely permitted in each of these three cases. The fourth occasion concerns witnessing the harm itself actually spreading: seeing, hearing of, or suspecting that broken discipline, views, deportment, or livelihood is gradually multiplying and growing more widespread on account of a particular offender, disclosure is likewise permitted, so that others are not drawn unknowingly toward association with a source of genuinely spreading harm.

He highlights the first three occasions' own shared logic, protecting the three jewels themselves from actual harm or plunder, as establishing the entire list's own governing principle from the outset, that permitted disclosure always serves some genuine protective purpose rather than mere candor for its own sake.

20. Section: Protecting Others from a False Assumption That an Entire Community Is Corrupt

The fifth occasion concerns directly witnessing improper conduct: seeing a monastic actually display broken discipline, views, deportment, or an improper livelihood, or hearing of or suspecting the same, disclosure is permitted so that others are not drawn toward association with that same person. The sixth occasion concerns a genuinely compassionate motive: wishing to help that same offender find their own way out of these same unwholesome states and into wholesome ones instead, disclosure made toward this same end is likewise permitted. The seventh occasion concerns protecting the wider community's own reputation: to prevent others from mistakenly concluding that every monastic without exception shares in broken discipline, broken views, broken deportment, and broken livelihood, and that the whole community merely conceals this shared corruption from one another, a monastic may speak up specifically to correct this same false, sweeping impression, guarding both the community's own good name and against the corrosive practice of mutual concealment alike.

21. Section: The Community's Last Resort When It Has No Power Left to Remove an Offender

The final three occasions close out the full list of ten. The eighth occasion concerns an explicit request from a donor: should a donor, a lay follower, or the founder of a monastery formally declare before the assembled community that no one of broken discipline, views, deportment, or livelihood may remain in residence there, and ask to be informed should any monastic witness such a person, the community may then rightly inform that same donor accordingly once such a person is actually seen. The ninth occasion concerns preventing harm from outside resentment: seeing, hearing of, or suspecting that some king, minister, or other outside party has grown resentful over a monastic's grave misconduct and intends some harmful action against the teaching as a result, a monastic may speak up to clarify that such misconduct belongs only to that particular offender rather than to monastics as a whole, thereby heading off broader harm to the teaching. And the tenth and final occasion concerns a genuine last resort: when the assembled community itself possesses no actual power to correct, punish, or expel an offender who has broken discipline, views, deportment, or livelihood and gone on to corrupt a lay household through improper conduct, the sole remaining means available is simply to make that same offender's impurity publicly known, so that the wider community may collectively decline to support such conduct any further. 22. Section: Five Kinds of Illustrative Teaching, from a Bodhisattva's Past Lives to a Parable

23. Section: Five Flavors of Milk, from Śrāvaka to Buddha

The third kind of illustrative material, parable-image material, is now explained through its own variety of forms. One celebrated instance draws on the five successive flavors that milk itself passes through as it is processed, milk itself, then curdled milk, then fresh butter, then refined butter, and finally ghee, the finest and most nourishing of all, used throughout the scriptures to illustrate a progression of spiritual capacity or teaching. One well-known mapping likens the śrāvaka to milk, the solitary-realizer to curdled milk, a bodhisattva in the first incalculable eon to fresh butter, a bodhisattva in the third incalculable eon to refined butter, and every buddha to ghee itself, the finest of all, capable of curing every illness the moment it is truly received, just as the entire body of the Buddha's own teaching, from the twelve divisions of scripture through the discourses, the extended teachings, the perfection of wisdom, and finally the great nirvana sutra itself, culminates in ghee, itself a further image for buddha-nature, which is simply the Buddha's own nature realized. Four distinct versions of this same five-flavor image appear across the tradition, mapped respectively to an ordinary being through an arhat or solitary-realizer within one system of classification, an ordinary being through a buddha within a second, an ordinary being's own impure milk refined through a bodhisattva's butter within a third, and, in a fourth and most exalted version, a mountain herb called forbearance grass which, once eaten by a cow, itself yields ghee directly, forbearance grass there standing for the eightfold noble path itself, so that a practitioner who genuinely cultivates that same eightfold path may see buddha-nature and dwell in great nirvana from the very first arousal of the aspiration for enlightenment onward, without needing to pass through the intervening stages at all.

影像事者，謂說乳、酪、生酥、熟酥、醍醐等喻，影顯最勝補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Parable-image material refers to similes such as milk, curdled milk, fresh butter, refined butter, and ghee, used to illustrate the most excellent among practitioners.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four distinct versions of this same five-flavor image as this treatise's own demonstration that a single vivid simile can serve genuinely different doctrinal purposes depending on which distinctions a given teaching context actually needs to draw, the very same progression of milk into ghee illustrating an ordinary being's path to arhatship in one telling and a bodhisattva's own path to buddhahood in another. He highlights the forbearance-grass version as this whole image's own most exalted culmination, a single eaten herb yielding ghee directly without passing through the intervening flavors at all, mirroring the sudden, complete realization this same teaching ascribes to a practitioner who genuinely embraces the eightfold path from the very first arousal of bodhi-mind onward.

24. Section: Seven Fish in the River of Birth and Death, from the Sunken to the Turtle Free in Both Worlds

A further, equally celebrated parable-image draws on seven kinds of creature found within a worldly river, used to illustrate seven kinds of practitioner within the true teaching. The first, a heavy fish permanently sunk at the river's very bottom, illustrates a being of severed wholesome roots who slanders the Mahāyāna triple gem, commits the gravest offenses, and never once sees or hears the Dharma across countless eons. The second, a fish that occasionally surfaces only to sink again, illustrates a being like Devadatta who briefly touches the Dharma's own light yet, practicing generosity, discipline, or meditation from a corrupted motive, such as seeking to outdo others, seeking gain through an outwardly pure reputation, seeking to bind others to oneself through charity, or seeking only the most refined formless absorption while still secretly attached to lesser realms, inevitably sinks back down again. The third, a fish that surfaces and remains contentedly near the light, illustrates disciples of genuinely strong wholesome roots, such as Śāriputra, who delight in the Dharma's own brightness, exemplified by Śāriputra's own insistence, even gravely ill and bedridden, on being carried to hear the Buddha teach rather than miss it. The fourth, a fish that surveys all four directions in search of food, illustrates a stream-enterer surveying the four noble truths from every angle in pursuit of genuine spiritual nourishment. The fifth, a fish that, having spotted food from a distance, swims eagerly toward it, illustrates a once-returner still diligently pursuing the remaining afflictions not yet severed. The sixth, a fish that, having reached its food, settles down to consume it, illustrates a non-returner who has left the desire realm behind and settled into the form or formless realms to continue cultivating toward arhatship. And the seventh, a turtle equally at home in water and on land, illustrates an arhat who has accomplished everything to be accomplished, moving with complete freedom through both the mundane and the transcendent alike. **Countless further parable-images beyond these two, too many to enumerate individually, exist throughout the vinaya in this same spirit and should likewise be understood.**

Chinese Source

又以世間七種河中補特伽羅喻，影顯正法中七種補特伽羅。如是所餘影像種類，皆應了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

A further simile draws on seven kinds of creature within a worldly river to illustrate seven kinds of practitioner within the true teaching. All remaining kinds of parable-image beyond these should likewise be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second fish's own catalogue of corrupted motive, seeking to outdo others, seeking gain through an outwardly pure reputation, seeking to bind others through charity, or seeking only the most refined absorption while still secretly attached to lesser realms, as this whole parable's own sharpest warning, since it shows that even genuinely virtuous-looking conduct, generosity, discipline, and meditation among them, can still sink a practitioner back down the very same river if the underlying motive itself remains corrupted. He highlights the seventh fish's own resolution, a turtle equally free in water and on land, as this entire sevenfold image's own fitting culmination, an arhat's own freedom pictured not as escape from the world but as complete, unobstructed ease moving between the mundane and the transcendent alike.

25. Section: A Great King, a Skilled Physician, and a Cat Weeping False Tears for a Mouse

The fourth kind of illustrative material, composite-analogy, draws on comparisons that hold only partially true, borrowed precisely because they share some genuine point of resemblance even where the comparison as a whole does not perfectly correspond. One such analogy likens the Buddha to a great king and beings to his ministers; another likens the Buddha to a skilled physician who prescribes the four noble truths themselves as medicine, a remedy capable of curing the sickness of birth and death in whoever genuinely takes it; countless further analogies of this same kind, some drawing on defiled circumstances and some on pure ones, appear throughout the scriptures for whoever studies them closely enough to notice. Ordinary worldly sayings offer this same kind of only-partial correspondence as well: a cat weeping over a mouse it has itself caught, feigning sympathy it plainly does not feel, illustrates a defiled circumstance, namely a person's own outwardly displayed but entirely false compassion; while a saying describing everything falling smoothly into place at hand illustrates a pure circumstance, namely an undertaking proceeding with complete, effortless ease. Every such analogy, whether drawn from the scriptures themselves or from ordinary worldly speech, is offered only because some small portion of it genuinely corresponds to the point being made, this same partial, borrowed correspondence itself being exactly what the term composite-analogy names.

Chinese Source

假合事者，謂大王喻或良醫喻，如是等類餘無量喻，隨順染汙及清淨品。復有現見世間譬喻，或依雜染品、或依清淨品，由彼少分共相應故，假合而說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Composite-analogy refers to comparisons such as a great king or a skilled physician, and countless further comparisons of this kind, some drawing on defiled circumstances and some on pure ones. Worldly comparisons likewise exist for both, offered because some small portion genuinely corresponds, hence the name composite.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the cat-and-mouse saying as this treatise's own refreshingly plainspoken acknowledgment that not every teaching tool need come from scripture at all, an ordinary worldly proverb about false sympathy serving this same illustrative purpose just as effectively as a formal parable once its own limited but genuine point of correspondence is properly understood. He highlights the physician analogy's own enduring usefulness as perhaps the single most widely recognized image in this entire fivefold catalogue, the four noble truths themselves cast as diagnosis, cause, prognosis, and prescribed remedy, a framing this treatise has returned to again and again since fascicle 67's own opening discussion of the four truths.

26. Section: The Burning House, Closing the Vinaya's Own Thirteen Divisions and Fascicle 69 Itself

The fifth and final kind of illustrative material, simile proper, refers to the many extended and elaborate similes found throughout the scriptures, a single such simile at times capable of illustrating countless circumstances at once. The Lotus Sūtra's own celebrated burning-house parable offers the clearest example of precisely this kind of long simile: the three realms of existence, it declares, offer no genuine safety at all, resembling nothing so much as a house already engulfed in flame, filled with suffering on every side and genuinely terrifying; this same burning house pictures one's own accumulated karmic result within the three realms, wherever a being might flee within it offering no true refuge from the fire, since only by actually leaving the three realms altogether, as though finally escaping the burning house itself, can genuine freedom from grief, sorrow, suffering, and affliction actually be found. Countless further similes exist throughout the scriptures in this same spirit, too numerous to enumerate individually, each one, like the burning house itself, offered so that a genuine practitioner might grasp through vivid image what mere abstract statement alone could never convey quite so forcefully. **With this, the entire discussion of the five kinds of illustrative material closes, the thirteenth and final of the vinaya's own thirteen divisions now complete, and with it the entire topic of the vinaya's own characteristics, the seventh of the śrāvaka canon's own thirteen topics, is likewise brought to a full and genuine close. The transcript of fascicle 69 comes to rest here, complete as far as the source lecture-transcript extends, six further topics of the śrāvaka canon still remaining ahead for fascicle 70 to take up.**

Chinese Source

譬喻事者，謂說廣長眾多譬喻。如長譬喻，及餘無量如是等類。

Ru-Yi Translation

Simile material refers to the many extended and elaborate similes found throughout the scriptures. Countless further similes of this same kind, beyond even this long simile just given, exist throughout the teaching as well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the burning-house parable as an unusually fitting image to close this entire thirteen-part discussion of the vinaya's own characteristics upon, a single elaborate simile capable of carrying the weight of the four noble truths themselves, suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path leading out, in one memorable image after fascicles of painstaking precept-by-precept detail. He highlights the sheer scale of what closes here, thirteen genuinely distinct divisions spanning obstacles to ordination, heavy and light offenses, dependence, receiving, use, the establishing of every precept, speaking of another's fault, and finally these five kinds of illustrative teaching, as showing just how seriously this treatise takes the vinaya, never a mere list of prohibitions but a fully reasoned discipline addressed to the whole of a monastic's inner and outer life alike.
