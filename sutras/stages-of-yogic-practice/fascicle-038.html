Fascicle 38 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十八 · The Chapter on Awakening Opens

What Buddhahood Actually Consists Of

With the chapters on power and ripening complete, the treatise opens its account of awakening itself (菩提品), the seventh and final field structuring the bodhisattva's training — beginning with the precise nature of bodhi: two obstructions eliminated down to their subtlest habitual traces, and the wisdoms this elimination makes possible.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 38 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Chapter on Awakening Opens — Two Obstructions Eliminated, Two Wisdoms Gained

With every other field of bodhisattva training now complete, the treatise turns to its seventh and final topic: awakening itself. The first of five ways of examining it defines bodhi with striking simplicity — the elimination of two obstructions, and the two wisdoms that elimination directly produces.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處菩提品第七

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Bodhisattva, the first division on yogic practice: the seventh chapter, on awakening.

Chinese Source

云何菩提？謂略說二斷、二智，是名菩提。二斷者，一、煩惱障斷，二、所知障斷。二智者，一、煩惱障斷故，畢竟離垢，一切煩惱不隨縛智；二、所知障斷故，於一切所知無礙無障智。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is awakening? In brief, it is two eliminations and two wisdoms — this is called awakening. The two eliminations are: elimination of the afflictive obstruction, and elimination of the cognitive obstruction. The two wisdoms are: because the afflictive obstruction is eliminated, one is forever free of defilement, possessing wisdom no longer bound by any affliction; and because the cognitive obstruction is eliminated, one possesses wisdom unobstructed and unimpeded with respect to everything knowable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi situates this chapter within the treatise's own larger structure: the first of the four major divisions on yogic practice contains eighteen chapters, grouped into lineage, the arising of resolve, and the factors of awakening — and within factors of awakening, five fields (self-and-other-benefit, true meaning, power, ripening, and now awakening itself) that together define everything a bodhisattva trains toward. This closing chapter asks the most basic question possible: what, precisely, does a buddha actually attain?

He explains the two obstructions as eliminated in stages rather than all at once, citing the treatise's own account of three degrees of habitual residue tied to specific grounds: the first ground permanently eliminates every affliction capable of producing a lower rebirth, and no affliction of moderate or greater intensity manifests from that point on; the eighth ground permanently eliminates every affliction that could obstruct the pure, unbroken forbearance regarding phenomena's birthless nature, so that no affliction manifests at all; and the tenth ground, culminating in buddhahood, permanently eliminates every last habitual trace and underlying tendency without exception. The cognitive obstruction follows a parallel three-stage elimination, traditionally likened to progressively deeper layers of a single wound — a surface layer cleared at the first ground, a deeper layer at the eighth, and the deepest layer, reaching what the tradition calls the very flesh beneath, cleared only at complete buddhahood.

He explains the two resulting wisdoms as simply the direct, positive consequence of each elimination: freedom from the afflictive obstruction yields liberation from craving, hatred, and delusion themselves — what the tradition calls the "liberated body," no longer capable of being pulled back into old patterns of affliction; freedom from the cognitive obstruction yields the wisdom that realizes phenomena's own emptiness without any remaining blind spot, capable of knowing anything knowable without obstruction. Elimination and wisdom, in other words, are not two separate achievements but one and the same event seen from two angles — what is removed, and what removing it makes possible. This is the first of several ways the treatise will define bodhi; a second, sharper definition, distinguishing three specific wisdoms, follows next.

2. Milestone: Omniscience and Instant Comprehension — Complete Knowledge of Every Realm, Event, Category, and Time

A second definition of bodhi names three wisdoms: pure wisdom, where every affliction and its subtlest habitual trace has been permanently eliminated; omniscience, unobstructed with respect to every realm, every event, every category, and every span of time without exception; and unobstructed wisdom, which understands completely the instant attention is turned toward something, with no need for the repeated deliberation an unenlightened mind requires.

Chinese Source

一切煩惱并諸習氣畢竟斷故，名清淨智。……於一切界、一切事、一切品、一切時智無礙轉，名一切智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because every affliction, together with its habitual traces, is permanently eliminated, this is called pure wisdom. ... Because wisdom operates without obstruction with respect to every realm, every event, every category, and every span of time, this is called omniscience.

Chinese Source

無滯智者，暫作意時，遍於一切無礙速疾無滯智轉。不由數數作意思惟，依一作意遍了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Unobstructed wisdom: the instant attention is directed toward anything at all, unobstructed and swift understanding operates throughout, without needing repeated deliberation — a single act of attention brings complete understanding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own verse locating ten specific kinds of non-defiling ignorance within the purified Dharma-realm, one eliminated at each of the ten grounds in turn — subtle ignorance clinging to phenomena rather than a personal self, harmless to a hearer's own liberation yet still a genuine obstacle to complete omniscience, fully exhausted only at buddhahood.

He unpacks omniscience across its four stated dimensions: every realm, meaning both the physical world and the world of sentient beings inhabiting it; every event, meaning both conditioned phenomena (twelve-linked dependent origination, and the path that leads beyond it) and the unconditioned reality reached at cessation; every category, meaning the countless ways any given event can be sorted — its own distinct nature, its shared nature with other things, its causes and effects, its realm of rebirth, and whether it is wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral; and every span of time, past, present, and future, established (as the treatise explains elsewhere) not as some independently real container but purely relative to a seed's own arising, functioning, and having-already-functioned.

He then draws the sharpest possible contrast between unobstructed wisdom and an unenlightened mind's own laborious process of understanding: even a highly accomplished bodhisattva or hearer typically needs to turn a question over repeatedly, examining it again and again before reaching a conclusion that may still turn out to need revision — while a buddha needs only a single, effortless act of attention to grasp anything completely and correctly the first time, with nothing further to reconsider. He notes a classical mapping of these three wisdoms onto more familiar terms: pure wisdom corresponds to fundamental, non-discriminating wisdom; omniscience corresponds to wisdom gained after that realization, applied to the entire range of knowable things; and unobstructed wisdom describes simply how effortlessly and completely both of the other two now operate together, with no remaining friction between them. This closes the second interpretation of bodhi's nature; a third interpretation, defining bodhi through one hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha, together with several specific and profound superknowledges, follows next.

3. Milestone: Why a Buddha's "Freedom from Contention" Means Something Far Deeper Than a Hearer's Own

A third definition of bodhi turns to specific, named qualities unique to buddhahood — beginning with a samādhi of freedom from contention that reveals a profound difference in compassion's own scope: a hearer avoids provoking anger in others as a matter of personal discipline, while a buddha is willing to let anger arise in a being if that very anger is what will finally lead them to the path, since the buddha's goal was never merely to avoid disturbing others but to see their afflictions genuinely uprooted.

Chinese Source

復有異門。謂百四十不共佛法，及如來無諍、願智、無礙解等，是名無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is yet another way of describing this: the one hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha, together with the Tathāgata's freedom from contention, wish-fulfilling wisdom, and unimpeded understanding — this too is called unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains a hearer's own samādhi of freedom from contention as resting on a vow, a sense of personal regret, or a wish to guard against provoking others' minds — essentially a discipline of personal restraint. A buddha's own version runs on an entirely different logic: certain beings, the treatise notes, can only reach the breakthrough of stream-entry or a higher fruit by first passing through a genuinely intense affliction of their own — and a buddha, recognizing this, does not shy away from letting that affliction arise if doing so is what will actually lead the being toward genuine liberation, precisely because the buddha's compassion aims at a being's ultimate freedom from all affliction, not merely at the buddha's own freedom from being its target. This is why the treatise insists a buddha, though fully capable of this same contention-free samādhi, does not dwell in it constantly the way a hearer characteristically does.

He explains wish-fulfilling wisdom as knowledge obtained the instant a sincere wish to know something is formed, entered through the deepest level of the fourth meditative absorption as an expedient basis — a hearer or solitary buddha can access something like this within the scope of their own vehicle's own concerns, but a buddha's own version reaches, without any restriction whatsoever, every possible object of knowledge. He then details the classical four kinds of unimpeded understanding: unimpeded understanding of the teaching itself, of its underlying meaning, of the specific words and etymology used to express it, and of the eloquence to actually deliver it persuasively — citing the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own verses praising each of these four alongside the buddha's freedom from contention and wish-fulfilling wisdom as qualities entirely surpassing anything available to a hearer, a solitary buddha, or even an unawakened bodhisattva. This closes the account of these specific, named qualities; the full list of one hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha follows next.

4. One Hundred and Forty Qualities Unique to a Buddha

The treatise names, in a single sweeping list, the full range of qualities said to belong to a buddha and no one else: the thirty-two excellent marks and eighty accompanying signs of physical perfection, four kinds of complete purity, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, the three foundations of mindfulness, the three things never needing to be guarded, great compassion, the faculty of never forgetting, the permanent destruction of every habitual trace, and wisdom perfect in every respect.

Chinese Source

百四十不共佛法者，謂三十二大丈夫相、八十隨好、四一切種清淨、十力、四無所畏、三念住、三不護、大悲、無忘失法、永害習氣、一切種妙智。是諸佛法，建立品中當廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The one hundred and forty qualities unique to a buddha are: the thirty-two excellent marks of a great being, the eighty accompanying signs, the four kinds of complete purity, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, the three foundations of mindfulness, the three things never needing to be guarded, great compassion, the faculty of never forgetting, the permanent destruction of habitual traces, and wondrous wisdom perfect in every respect. These buddha-qualities will be elaborated in detail in a later chapter on their own classification.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews each item briefly, noting the treatise itself defers full elaboration to a dedicated later chapter: the four kinds of complete purity cover the basis, object, mind, and wisdom a buddha relies on, each entirely unmixed with defilement; the ten powers name ten specific domains of unobstructed knowledge (from knowing what is and is not possible through to the extinction of contaminants); the four fearlessnesses cover confidence in one's own complete awakening, in having genuinely extinguished contaminants, in correctly teaching the path out of suffering, and in correctly identifying whatever obstructs that path; the three foundations of mindfulness describe an unshaken evenness of mind whether an audience receives the teaching eagerly, with hostility, or with indifference; and the three things never needing to be guarded refer to body, speech, and mind so thoroughly purified that no lapse could ever occur requiring active vigilance to prevent. Great compassion, the faculty of never forgetting, permanently destroyed habitual traces, and wisdom perfect in every respect close out the full one hundred and forty. This closes the third interpretation of bodhi's nature, completing the treatise's account of what awakening fundamentally consists of; the treatise now turns to why this awakening is called supreme among all forms of awakening across all three vehicles.

5. Milestone: Seven Kinds of Supremacy Open — A Physical Basis Beyond Any Other Being's

Having defined bodhi's own nature, the treatise turns to why it is supreme: seven distinct respects in which a buddha's awakening surpasses every other attainment, beginning with the physical basis itself — a body adorned with the thirty-two excellent marks and eighty accompanying signs, the very embodiment of every perfection the entire path was ever building toward.

Chinese Source

如是菩提名為最勝，七種最勝共相應故。……一者、所依最勝，二者、正行最勝，三者、圓滿最勝，四者、智最勝，五者、威力最勝，六者、斷最勝，七者、住最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Awakening of this kind is called supreme, because it is joined with seven kinds of supremacy. ... First, supremacy of physical basis; second, supremacy of conduct; third, supremacy of fulfillment; fourth, supremacy of wisdom; fifth, supremacy of power; sixth, supremacy of elimination; and seventh, supremacy of abiding.

Chinese Source

由諸如來以三十二大丈夫相等莊嚴其身，故名所依最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because Tathāgatas adorn their bodies with the thirty-two excellent marks of a great being and the accompanying signs, this is called supremacy of physical basis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews all seven kinds of supremacy the treatise is about to detail one by one: physical basis (the marks just named), conduct (benefiting both self and others out of genuine compassion for the world), fulfillment (complete moral discipline, correct view, disciplined bearing, and pure livelihood), wisdom (the four kinds of unimpeded understanding just described), power (the six superknowledges already covered at length in the previous fascicle), elimination (every affliction, its habitual traces, and the cognitive obstruction all permanently exhausted), and abiding (dwelling continuously in three supreme states — the noble abiding, the heavenly abiding, and the divine abiding). He notes the physical basis is named first because it is the most immediately visible sign to any observer: this magnificent, uniquely marked body is not incidental decoration but the direct physical expression of the Dharma body's own complete perfection, making a buddha recognizable at a glance as foremost among both humans and gods, the very king of every noble being. This closes the first of the seven kinds of supremacy; conduct, fulfillment, and the remaining five follow next.

6. Five More Kinds of Supremacy — Conduct, Fulfillment, Wisdom, Power, and Elimination

Five further kinds of supremacy round out most of the list: conduct so complete it benefits countless beings out of genuine compassion for the world; fourfold fulfillment in precepts, view, bearing, and livelihood; wisdom expressed through the four kinds of unimpeded understanding, unmatched even by advanced bodhisattvas; power expressed through the six superknowledges already detailed at length in the previous fascicle; and elimination so thorough that every affliction, its habitual traces, and the cognitive obstruction are all permanently exhausted at once.

Chinese Source

由諸如來自利利他，利益安樂無量眾生，哀愍世間，令諸天人獲得義利利益安樂而行正行，故名正行最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because Tathāgatas, benefiting both themselves and others, bring benefit and ease to countless beings, out of compassion for the world, so that gods and humans attain genuine benefit, ease, and welfare through their conduct — this is called supremacy of conduct.

Chinese Source

由諸如來無上無等四種圓滿，謂戒圓滿、見圓滿、軌則圓滿、淨命圓滿，皆悉成就，故名圓滿最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because Tathāgatas have accomplished four kinds of fulfillment, unsurpassed and unequaled — fulfillment in precepts, in view, in bearing, and in livelihood — this is called supremacy of fulfillment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that conduct's supremacy specifically singles out gods and humans among the five realms as the beings actually capable of receiving this teaching, since they alone possess the wisdom and stability to practice discipline, concentration, and wisdom in a way that leads to genuine realization — and stresses that this reach extends even to beings a buddha never personally encountered, since anyone who sincerely follows the teaching, in any era, receives the same benefit as if taught directly.

He unpacks fulfillment's four parts: precepts entirely free of the ten kinds of corruption that can weaken monastic discipline (beginning with an insincere initial ordination) and possessing instead the five marks of genuinely complete discipline (never lapsing, genuinely liberating, beyond reproach, without deficiency, and without complacency); view permanently free of five specific wrong views (mistaking the aggregates for a fixed self, clinging to extremes of eternalism or nihilism, holding one's own view as supreme, clinging to superstitious rites, and every form of denying what exists or affirming what does not); dignified bearing in every activity, whether walking, working, or engaged in wholesome practice, in harmony with both ordinary social convention and monastic discipline without violating either; and a livelihood entirely free of deceptive means of soliciting support — flattery, false claims of spiritual attainment, feigned hardship, or transactional generosity meant to provoke a return gift.

He then explains why wisdom's supremacy stands unmatched even by ninth-ground bodhisattvas, who possess a genuine but still incomplete version of the same four kinds of unimpeded understanding: only a buddha's own mastery of teaching, meaning, precise wording, and eloquent delivery is complete enough to spontaneously realize truth for oneself while also correctly guiding others without the slightest distortion, a double capacity nothing short of buddhahood ever fully achieves. Power and elimination he treats briefly, since both were already covered in exhaustive detail earlier — the six superknowledges in the previous fascicle's chapter on power, and the two obstructions' complete elimination already established at this chapter's own opening. This closes five of the seven kinds of supremacy; abiding, the seventh and richest of them, follows next.

7. Milestone: Abiding Supremacy — Why the Fourth Meditative Absorption Survives Every Cosmic Cataclysm

A buddha dwells mostly within three broad categories of meditative abiding — noble, heavenly, and divine — but within each, favors one specific state above the rest: emptiness and cessation within the noble abiding, and, within the heavenly abiding, the fourth meditative absorption specifically, called "unmoving" because it alone stands beyond the reach of the very cataclysms that periodically destroy every lower realm of the cosmos.

Chinese Source

當知此中，空、無願、無相住，及滅盡定住，是名聖住。四種靜慮、四無色定，是名天住。四種無量，是名梵住。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that abiding in emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, together with abiding in cessation, is called the noble abiding. The four meditative absorptions and the four formless attainments are called the heavenly abiding. The four immeasurables are called the divine abiding.

Chinese Source

於此三住中，如來多住四最勝住。謂於聖住中，多住空住、滅盡定住；於天住中，多住無動第四靜慮住；於梵住中，多住大悲住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these three abidings, the Tathāgata dwells mostly in four supreme states: within the noble abiding, mostly in the abiding of emptiness and the abiding of cessation; within the heavenly abiding, mostly in the unmoving abiding of the fourth meditative absorption; and within the divine abiding, mostly in the abiding of great compassion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the fourth absorption specifically earns a buddha's preference among the eight heavenly states: the first three absorptions and the four formless attainments each carry some residual imbalance — the first three still weighted toward wisdom over concentration or troubled by lingering discursive thought, and the formless attainments weighted the opposite way, deep in concentration but comparatively shallow in wisdom — while only the fourth absorption balances the two perfectly. He connects this directly to the tradition's own account of the universe's own cyclical destruction: when a world-system eventually collapses, fire consumes everything up through the first absorption's own heaven, since that absorption still carries the unsettled discursive thought that fire itself corresponds to; water then submerges everything up through the second absorption's heaven, since that absorption still carries joy's own surging quality; wind then scours everything up through the third absorption's heaven, since that absorption still carries bliss's own restless agitation — but the fourth absorption and everything above it remains entirely untouched by any of the three, precisely because it has already released the inner counterpart each outer disaster corresponds to: no unsettled discursive thought, no surging joy, no restless bliss, not even the ordinary rhythm of breathing in and out. Outer cataclysm, he stresses, is never separate from inner disturbance — a world's own destruction mirrors exactly the disturbances still present in the minds of beings dwelling there, which is why the fourth absorption's own settled evenness renders it, quite literally, indestructible. This closes the account of why the fourth absorption is called "unmoving"; the great compassion a buddha dwells in most within the divine abiding follows next.

8. Milestone: Great Compassion — The Buddha's Six-Times-Daily Survey of Every Being's Progress

Among the four immeasurables, a buddha dwells mostly in great compassion specifically, since suffering beings are what most need reaching — surveying the entire world with the buddha-eye six times daily, three times by day and three by night, constantly asking who has just begun to grow, who has begun to decline, and what precise intervention each being now needs.

Chinese Source

由是如來晝夜六時，晝三、夜三，常以佛眼觀察世間誰增、誰減，我應令誰未起善根而種善根，廣說乃至我應令誰建立最勝阿羅漢果。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, the Tathāgata, six times each day and night — three by day and three by night — constantly observes the world with the buddha-eye, asking who is growing and who is declining: whom should I lead to plant a wholesome root not yet planted? ... and, in detail, all the way to: whom should I now establish in the supreme fruit of arhatship?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why great compassion, rather than loving-kindness, sympathetic joy, or equanimity, is where a buddha dwells most often: while all four immeasurables matter, a suffering being's own need is the most urgent, and a suffering being is also, in a real sense, closest to a buddha's own heart, since a buddha will never abandon anyone in genuine distress — the connection formed at a being's lowest point runs deepest. He connects this continuous, round-the-clock watching directly to a well-known verse from the *Lotus Sūtra*: the Buddha's own constant inner reflection on how to lead every being into the unsurpassed path and swiftly bring them to buddhahood themselves — not a poetic flourish, he stresses, but a literal description of what the buddha-eye is doing at every single hour, day and night, without pause.

He briefly distinguishes the traditional five eyes this buddha-eye culminates: the physical eye, limited by distance, obstruction, and darkness exactly as an ordinary eye is; the divine eye, unrestricted by any of these limits; the eye of wisdom, through which a hearer or solitary buddha sees every phenomenon as empty of self; the Dharma eye, through which a bodhisattva sees precisely which practices lead which beings toward realization; and the buddha-eye, which contains the full function of all four others at once — what appears most distant to an ordinary eye appears as though right at hand, what appears hidden in darkness appears as though brightly lit, with nothing whatsoever unseen, unheard, or unknown. This closes the account of the four kinds of abiding a buddha dwells in most; the treatise now gathers all seven kinds of supremacy into a set of honorific titles.

9. Seven Honorific Titles, Each Rooted in a Different Supremacy

Each of the seven kinds of supremacy just detailed gives rise to its own honorific title: Great Hero from the physical basis, Greatly Compassionate One from conduct, Great Upholder of Precepts and Great Upholder of the Teaching from fulfillment, Greatly Wise One from wisdom, Greatly Miraculous One from power, Greatly Liberated One from elimination, and Widely and Vastly Abiding One from abiding.

Chinese Source

又諸如來所依最勝故，名大丈夫。正行最勝故，名大悲者。圓滿最勝故，名大戒者及大法者。智最勝故，名大慧者。威力最勝故，名大神通者。斷最勝故，名大解脫者。住最勝故，名多安住、廣大住者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because Tathāgatas possess supremacy of physical basis, they are called the Great Hero. Because of supremacy of conduct, they are called the Greatly Compassionate One. Because of supremacy of fulfillment, they are called the Great Upholder of Precepts and the Great Upholder of the Teaching. Because of supremacy of wisdom, they are called the Greatly Wise One. Because of supremacy of power, they are called the Greatly Miraculous One. Because of supremacy of elimination, they are called the Greatly Liberated One. Because of supremacy of abiding, they are called the Widely and Vastly Abiding One.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that each title simply names the same underlying quality already detailed, now expressed as a form of address — every one of them carrying the honorific "great" specifically to mark it as belonging to the foremost teacher of all, surpassing every hearer, solitary buddha, and bodhisattva alike. This closes the treatise's second sub-topic, supremacy, in its entirety; the third sub-topic, ten names for contemplating a buddha's own virtues, opens next with a striking account of why these titles were eventually reduced from ten thousand to just ten.

10. Milestone: Ten Names for Contemplation — Why the Buddha's Titles Shrank From Ten Thousand to Ten

The third way of examining awakening turns to ten specific names by which a buddha's virtues can be contemplated — a number the tradition explains was reduced over time from an original ten thousand, matching a gradual decline in beings' own capacity to remember and comprehend them, until ten names became the most that could genuinely be held in mind and put to practical use.

Chinese Source

又諸如來略有十種功德名號隨念功德。何等為十？謂薄伽梵，號為如來、應、正等覺、明行圓滿、善逝、世間解、無上丈夫調御士、天人師、佛、薄伽梵。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tathāgatas may, in brief, be contemplated through ten kinds of virtuous names. What are these ten? The Blessed One is named: Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Enlightened One, Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct, Well-Gone One, Knower of the World, Unsurpassed Guide Who Tames Others, Teacher of Gods and Humans, Buddha, and Blessed One.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recounts the tradition's own account of why the count of names changed over time: in the earliest ages, beings born from the second meditative heaven possessed such refined intelligence that ten thousand names could be held in mind and genuinely understood without difficulty; as beings' capacities gradually dulled across subsequent ages, the count shrank first to a thousand names, still too many for a duller age to retain, then to one hundred, and finally, for beings in an age as difficult as this one, to just ten — the most that could realistically be remembered and put to actual use in contemplation. He notes some more refined heavenly beings still retain a longer list of one hundred and eight names, while Śākyamuni, teaching specifically in this world among beings of comparatively limited capacity, settled on the ten names given here, matching the teaching precisely to what an audience can actually carry with them.

He explains the treatise's own division of the ten: the first five — Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Enlightened One, Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct, and Well-Gone One — describe qualities of self-benefit, what a buddha has accomplished for their own realization; the remaining five — Knower of the World, Unsurpassed Guide, Teacher of Gods and Humans, Buddha, and Blessed One — describe qualities of benefiting others, what a buddha's realization then makes possible for everyone else. This closes the introduction to the ten names; the treatise now explains each one individually, beginning with the very first.

11. The First Three Names: Tathāgata, Worthy One, and Perfectly Enlightened

The first name, Tathāgata, marks a being who speaks nothing false; the second, Worthy One, marks a being who has attained everything genuinely worth attaining and is therefore worthy of the world's highest offerings; and the third, Perfectly Enlightened, begins to describe the specific content of that very attainment.

Chinese Source

言無虛妄，故名如來。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because their words contain no falsehood whatsoever, they are called Tathāgata.

Chinese Source

已得一切所應得義，應作世間無上福田，應為一切恭敬供養，是故名應。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having attained every genuine benefit worth attaining, fit to serve as the world's unsurpassed field of merit, worthy of reverence and offerings from all beings — for this reason, they are called Worthy One.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes several classical readings of "Tathāgata" alongside the treatise's own: "arrived by way of genuine reality, thereby accomplishing correct awakening" (emphasizing the path traveled to reach buddhahood); "arrived by way of the six perfections and the eleven kinds of emptiness cultivated across countless causes" (emphasizing the specific causes behind that arrival); "arrived by way of omniscience, in order to teach and transform beings" (emphasizing the purpose of that arrival); and, from a more ultimate standpoint, "neither coming from anywhere nor going anywhere" (emphasizing the Dharma body's own nature, beyond any actual coming or going at all) — noting that the treatise's own definition here, simply that nothing spoken is ever false, points to the same underlying reality from the angle of genuine, trustworthy virtue rather than etymology.

He explains "Worthy One" as recognition that a buddha, having genuinely attained everything the entire path was ever aiming at, has become the single most fertile possible field in which any offering — material or otherwise — yields its greatest possible benefit, exactly as a seed planted in the most fertile soil produces the richest harvest. This closes the first two names; Perfectly Enlightened, describing the specific realization behind both titles, opens next.

12. Names Three Through Five: Perfectly Enlightened, Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct, and Well-Gone

Perfectly Enlightened names the specific realization behind every other title — direct awakening to the ultimate truth of every phenomenon; Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct names the three knowledges and the disciplined life that bring that realization to completion; and Well-Gone names a being who has risen to the very summit of that realization and will never fall back from it again.

Chinese Source

如其勝義覺諸法故，名正等覺。明謂三明，行如經說止觀二品。極善圓滿，是故說名明行圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because they are awakened to every phenomenon exactly as its ultimate truth actually is, they are called Perfectly Enlightened. "Wisdom" refers to the three knowledges; "conduct" refers, as the scriptures say, to calm-abiding and insight together. Both are brought to the most excellent completion — hence the name Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct.

Chinese Source

上昇最極，永不退還，故名善逝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having risen to the very summit, they never fall back again — hence the name Well-Gone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "Perfectly Enlightened" as direct realization of phenomena's own ultimate reality — the twin emptiness of person and phenomena, suchness itself, rather than any merely conceptual grasp of doctrine. He unpacks the three knowledges named in "Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct" as specifically countering three deep-seated errors: knowledge of past lives corrects the tendency to imagine a fixed, eternal self stretching backward into the past; the divine eye's knowledge of future death and rebirth corrects the opposite tendency to imagine that death simply ends everything; and knowledge of the extinction of contaminants corrects the subtler error of mistaking meditative bliss itself, in the present, for genuine nirvāṇa. These three knowledges are called more than mere superknowledges precisely because they include the causal reasoning behind what is known, not simply the bare fact itself. "Conduct" then covers both a buddha's outwardly disciplined bearing — restrained senses and impeccable conduct of body, speech, and mind — and a buddha's inward abiding in the very calm-abiding and insight that make the three knowledges possible in the first place, both brought to absolute completeness.

He explains "Well-Gone" simply as having reached the very peak of realization — the unsurpassed, non-abiding great nirvāṇa — with no possibility of ever falling back to an unenlightened state or even to a lesser vehicle's own more limited liberation, having brought both self-benefit and other-benefit to complete fulfillment across the long night of countless past lives. This closes the third through fifth names; the sixth and seventh, Knower of the World and the Unsurpassed Guide, follow next.

13. Names Six and Seven: Knower of the World, and the Unsurpassed Guide

Knower of the World names complete understanding of every pure and defiled variety found throughout both the physical world and the world of sentient beings; the Unsurpassed Guide names the single most accomplished being in that entire world, uniquely skilled at taming the mind's own afflictions and turning even life's ordinary toil into the very work of awakening.

Chinese Source

善知世界及有情界一切品類染淨相故，名世間解。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because they thoroughly understand every pure and defiled variety found throughout the physical world and the world of sentient beings, they are called Knower of the World.

Chinese Source

一切世間唯一丈夫，善知最勝調心方便，是故說名無上丈夫調御士。

Ru-Yi Translation

The one and only great being throughout the entire world, skilled in the most excellent means of taming the mind — for this reason, they are called the Unsurpassed Guide Who Tames Others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties "Knower of the World" directly to omniscience already established earlier in this chapter — every realm, event, category, and time understood without obstruction, now specifically named as knowledge of the world in both its physical and sentient dimensions, pure states and defiled states alike. He explains the Unsurpassed Guide as unmatched precisely in the specific skill of transforming worldly toil and affliction into genuine spiritual work — no other teacher reaches so deeply or so skillfully into what actually tames a mind's own craving, hatred, and confusion. This closes the sixth and seventh names; the eighth, Teacher of Gods and Humans, follows next with a fuller account of exactly what makes a teacher genuine.

14. Milestone: Teacher of Gods and Humans — Four Qualities That Make a Genuine Teacher

The eighth name unpacks exactly what makes a buddha a genuine teacher through four qualities: a direct, unmediated seeing of reality that requires no further explanation to trust; a wisdom serving as the very basis every meaning ultimately rests on; a capacity to clarify what is unclear, resolve genuine doubt, and reveal what is most profound; and a teaching that serves as the root, guide, and foundation for every other teaching whatsoever — together making it possible to correctly instruct gods and humans in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, leading them out of every form of suffering.

Chinese Source

為實眼故，為實智故，為實義故，為實法故，與顯了義為開導故，與一切義為所依故，與不了義為能了故，與所生疑為能斷故，與甚深處為能顯故……能正教誡教授天人，令其出離一切眾苦，是故說佛名天人師。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of genuine seeing, because of genuine wisdom, because of genuine meaning, and because of genuine teaching — guiding through what is already clear, serving as the basis for every meaning, clarifying what is not yet clear, resolving doubts that arise, and revealing what is most profound ... correctly instructing and teaching gods and humans, leading them out of every form of suffering — for this reason, the Buddha is called the Teacher of Gods and Humans.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws together the four qualities into a single coherent picture of genuine teaching: genuine seeing is a buddha's own direct, unmediated perception of reality through the five eyes, requiring no inference or borrowed authority; genuine wisdom is what makes that seeing expressible at all, the basis every subsequent explanation of meaning depends on; genuine meaning is the actual content delivered — clarifying whatever a listener finds unclear, settling real doubt rather than deflecting it, and opening even the most profound dimension of ultimate truth to genuine understanding; and genuine teaching is the actual body of doctrine that results, itself becoming the root and reliable foundation everything else in the tradition builds upon. Only a being possessing all four together, he notes, can rightly serve as the ultimate teacher of both gods and humans, correctly instructing them in discipline, concentration, and wisdom in a way that genuinely leads all the way out of suffering — a title earned by substance, not granted merely by title. This closes the eighth name; the ninth, simply "Buddha" itself, follows next.

15. Milestone: "Buddha" — Equal Awakening to What Helps, What Harms, and What Merely Passes the Time

The ninth name, Buddha, describes complete, equal awakening to three categories of phenomena at once: those that genuinely lead to benefit, such as the teachings of the three vehicles; those that lead to genuine harm, such as rival, misleading paths; and those that lead to neither — ordinary human and heavenly goodness, pleasant enough in its own way, yet, like a caged bird let loose only to find a string still tied to its leg, never quite the same as true freedom.

Chinese Source

於能引攝義利法聚，於能引攝非義利法聚，於能引攝非義利非非義利法聚，遍一切種現前等覺，故名為佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

With respect to phenomena that lead to genuine benefit, phenomena that lead to genuine harm, and phenomena that lead to neither benefit nor harm, they are completely and equally awakened in every respect — for this reason, they are called Buddha.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi sorts the three categories concretely: teachings genuinely leading to benefit are the three vehicles' own path toward liberation; teachings genuinely leading to harm are rival doctrines and unwholesome conduct of every kind; and phenomena leading to neither are ordinary human and heavenly goodness — generosity, moral discipline, and the pleasant but ultimately impermanent results they bring, genuinely enjoyable yet never actually liberating in themselves.

He illustrates this third, easily-overlooked category with a classical image: a bird kept in a cage, once released, at first believes itself entirely free — until it swoops toward some food and discovers a string still tied to its leg, yanking it back exactly where it started. Ordinary meditative attainment, however genuinely elevated above coarse sensory craving, works the same way: a being who cultivates concentration escapes the cage of desire-realm craving only to remain tied by a subtler string — attachment to the very bliss and stillness of concentration itself, a more refined form of the same underlying craving, not yet the true freedom final liberation actually delivers. A buddha alone sees all three categories — genuine benefit, genuine harm, and this subtler middle ground that merely feels like freedom without truly being it — with complete and equal clarity, never mistaking any one of them for what it is not. This closes the ninth name; the tenth and final name, Blessed One, follows next.

16. Milestone: Blessed One — Defeating Four Great Māras, Closing All Ten Names

The tenth and final name, Blessed One, marks victory over four classical adversaries every practitioner eventually faces — the māra of affliction, the māra of the aggregates themselves, the māra of the desire-realm's own tempter-deity, and the māra of death — together with six further qualities of genuine mastery, completing the full account of a buddha's ten names for contemplation.

Chinese Source

能破諸魔大力軍眾，具多功德，名薄伽梵。

Ru-Yi Translation

Able to defeat every great army of māras, and possessing manifold virtues — this is called Blessed One.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the four māras this title conquers: the māra of affliction, defeated through the emptiness-samādhi that dissolves every habitual trace of craving, hatred, and confusion; the māra of the five aggregates themselves, the very basis clinging to a fixed self depends on, defeated through sustained diligence and wisdom; the māra of the desire-realm's own tempter-deity, defeated through loving-kindness so complete it leaves no opening for temptation to take hold; and the māra of death itself, defeated through mastery of miraculous power, which transcends the ordinary boundary between life and death altogether.

He then explains the six further qualities the title carries, drawn from the *Buddhabhūmi-śāstra*: mastery, since every affliction and its habitual trace has been permanently severed, leaving nothing left to be bound by; blazing radiance, since wisdom itself burns like an intense flame consuming every remaining obstruction; dignified adornment, expressed through the thirty-two excellent marks and eighty accompanying signs; renown, since every excellent virtue is fully known throughout the entire universe, in every direction and every age; auspiciousness, since anyone who reveres or makes an offering to a buddha is universally regarded as having done something genuinely fortunate; and nobility, since a buddha, possessing every virtue completely, tirelessly benefits every being without exception, never once growing weary of the task. This closes the tenth and final name, completing the entire account of the ten names for contemplation — the third of the chapter's five sub-topics; the fourth sub-topic, how and when buddhas actually appear in the world, opens next with a striking account of just how many buddhas can arise from a single act of sharing the Dharma.

17. Milestone: Countless Buddhas Appear at Once Across Countless Worlds — But Never Two in the Same One

At this very moment, hundreds of bodhisattvas in this world alone are simultaneously generating the resolve for awakening and cultivating the six perfections together — let alone the countless bodhisattvas doing exactly the same across immeasurable buddha-lands throughout the ten directions. Yet however many reach full readiness at once, no single world-system ever receives two buddhas at the same time: each destined bodhisattva instead becomes a buddha in a different world-system currently without one, matched to their own long-held vow.

Chinese Source

於今現見此世界中，多百菩薩同時發願、同修惠施、同修淨戒、同修忍辱、同修精進、同修靜慮、同修智慧，況於十方無量無邊諸佛世界。

Ru-Yi Translation

It can presently be observed that, in this very world, hundreds of bodhisattvas simultaneously generate the resolve and simultaneously cultivate giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom together — how much more so throughout the ten directions' immeasurable, boundless buddha-worlds.

Chinese Source

是故當知，眾多菩薩同時修集菩提資糧俱圓滿者，於十方面無量無數隨其所淨空無如來諸佛國土，各別出世，同時成佛。

Ru-Yi Translation

For this reason, it should be understood that when many bodhisattvas simultaneously complete gathering the provisions for awakening together, they each separately appear in the world, becoming buddhas at the same time, each within whichever immeasurable, uncountable buddha-land throughout the ten directions currently has no Tathāgata and matches the purification they themselves have cultivated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the three wrong conclusions the treatise deliberately rules out before settling on the correct one. It is not the case that many bodhisattvas ready for buddhahood at once would all become buddhas simultaneously within a single world — a single buddha's capacity already extends to everything a single world-system could possibly need, making a second entirely redundant there. Nor is it the case that they simply take turns, one becoming a buddha only after waiting for another to finish and depart — nothing in the text supports this kind of polite queuing. Nor is it the case that only one bodhisattva among the many ever actually reaches buddhahood while the others remain forever unfulfilled — every bodhisattva who genuinely completes the provisions for awakening does become a buddha, without exception.

The actual resolution, he explains, rests on something each bodhisattva has cultivated across countless past lives: a specific vow, formed long ago, to purify one particular world-system and eventually become its own guide once conditions allow. When their own provisions for awakening are finally complete, each such bodhisattva simply travels to precisely that world — whichever one, among the ten directions' uncountable systems, currently has no buddha of its own and matches the purification already vowed — and becomes a buddha there. Countless bodhisattvas reach fulfillment at the very same moment, in other words, but each becomes a buddha in their own separately destined world, never crowding into the same one together. This closes the account of why many buddhas can appear across many worlds at once; the treatise now turns to the reverse question — why, within any single world, there is never more than one buddha at a time.

18. Milestone: Why Exactly One Buddha Per World — Three Reasons, and the Wisdom of Scarcity

Three reasons explain why a single world-system never has two buddhas at once: a bodhisattva's own vow, cultivated across the long night of samsara, is specifically to become the sole guide wherever no guide yet exists; a single buddha already fully accomplishes everything a world-system's beings could possibly need, rendering a second entirely redundant; and — most strikingly — beings themselves grow genuinely diligent in practice only when they know a buddha's presence in the world is a limited, precious opportunity rather than something they can simply take for granted.

Chinese Source

菩薩長夜起如是願，隨令增長：我當獨一，於無導首諸世界中，為作導首……又一如來，於一三千大千佛土，普能施作一切佛事，是故第二如來出世無所利益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Across the long night of saṃsāra, a bodhisattva generates and continually strengthens this very vow: "I alone shall become the guide within whatever world currently has no guide of its own." ... Moreover, a single Tathāgata, within a single great trichiliocosm, is universally able to accomplish every buddha-activity needed there — and so a second Tathāgata appearing in that same world would bring no further benefit at all.

Chinese Source

彼作是思：一切世間唯一如來，更無第二。若於此土化事已訖，或往餘方，或入滅度。我等何從當修梵行？我等何從當聞正法？如是思已，發起深厚欲勤精進……若一佛土多佛出世，彼於所作不能速疾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beings reflect in this way: "Throughout the entire world there is only this one Tathāgata, and no second one. If his work of teaching in this land should ever come to an end, whether by departing elsewhere or by entering final nirvāṇa, from whom shall we then learn the pure conduct of the path? From whom shall we then hear the true teaching?" Reflecting in this way, they arouse deep, earnest diligence ... whereas if a single buddha-land had many buddhas appearing in it, beings would not be swift in their own efforts at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third reason as the most psychologically penetrating of the three: a being who assumes a teacher will always be available tends to procrastinate, while a being who genuinely grasps that this particular opportunity is singular and could end at any time — through the teacher's departure to another world, or passing into final nirvāṇa — is moved to practice with real, immediate urgency instead. He connects this to a familiar saying about regret arriving too late — a tree wishing for stillness finds the wind will not stop, just as a child wishing to care for aging parents finds them no longer there to be cared for — the same ordinary human tendency to undervalue what feels abundant and grieve it only once it is gone. Śākyamuni's own eighty years in this world, he notes, were themselves a deliberately limited span for exactly this reason: had the Buddha simply remained forever, visibly available at any moment, beings would have far less reason to actually apply themselves, whereas a presence known to be finite gives practice its real edge.

He closes by connecting this to the earlier reason concerning the bodhisattva's own vow: a bodhisattva does not merely happen to become the sole buddha of a given world, but specifically vows, life after life, to be exactly that — a world's singular guide, in which capacity a single buddha's own complete sufficiency and the beneficial urgency scarcity creates in beings both reinforce the same underlying pattern. This closes the treatise's fourth sub-topic, on how and why buddhas appear in the world as they do; the fifth and final sub-topic, on the precise ways different buddhas nonetheless differ from one another despite their identical realization, opens next.

19. Milestone: Differentiation Opens — Every Buddha's Realization Is Identical; Only Their Manifested Forms Differ

The fifth and final way of examining awakening turns to a simple but important clarification: every buddha's own genuine realization — the very qualities this entire chapter has been describing — is perfectly, exactly equal, with no difference whatsoever between one buddha and another. The only differences that ever appear are four features of how each buddha's manifested, teaching form happens to look: lifespan, name, family lineage, and bodily appearance.

Chinese Source

一切如來一切功德平等平等無有差別。唯除四法。一者、壽量，二者、名號，三者、族姓，四者、身相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every Tathāgata's every virtue is perfectly, exactly equal, with no difference whatsoever — except for four things: first, lifespan; second, name; third, family lineage; and fourth, bodily appearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi confirms that "every virtue" here means precisely everything this chapter has already established — the two obstructions eliminated, the wisdoms this makes possible, the one hundred and forty unique qualities, the seven kinds of supremacy, all of it identical without exception across every buddha who has ever existed or ever will. What differs is only the outward, manifested form suited to whichever beings a given buddha appears to teach: lifespan, since Śākyamuni displayed a life of eighty years while Amitābha's own scriptures describe an immeasurable one; name, since each buddha is known by their own distinct title, Śākyamuni, Amitābha, the Medicine Buddha, and so on; family lineage, since some appear born into a ruling family and others into a family of religious scholars, whichever best suits the audience they are teaching; and bodily appearance, since Śākyamuni displayed an ordinary elder monk's form some sixteen feet in height while Amitābha's own described form radiates boundless light without any fixed measure at all — a difference some traditions further explain as reflecting the capacity of the very mind perceiving it, a vaster mind perceiving a vaster form. This closes the opening statement of the fifth and final sub-topic; the treatise now examines each of these four points of difference in turn.

20. The Four Differing Marks Explained — Lifespan, Lineage, and Appearance

Each of the four differing marks is explained in turn: lifespan varies because a buddha, having mastered complete freedom over life and death, simply remains as long as beings still need reaching and departs once that work is done; family lineage varies according to whichever household best suits the audience being taught; and bodily appearance varies to match what beings of a given era and place are actually able to perceive and recognize.

Chinese Source

一切如來於此四法有增減相，非餘功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every Tathāgata shows variation only with respect to these four things, never with respect to any other quality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that lifespan varies purely as a matter of choice: since both obstructions are fully eliminated, a buddha has complete mastery over exactly how long to remain in the world, staying as long as there is genuine benefit in doing so and departing the instant that benefit is exhausted — one buddha might display twenty thousand years, another might pass into final nirvāṇa almost immediately upon awakening, each entirely fitted to the beings being taught. Family lineage varies simply according to whichever household — a great ruling family or a family of respected religious scholars — will best serve the specific audience a given buddha appears to teach. Bodily appearance likewise varies to match what beings of a given time and place can actually perceive: a buddha appearing among beings of ordinary height displays a correspondingly ordinary-seeming stature, while a buddha appearing among unusually tall beings might appear correspondingly larger, since the entire point of physical appearance is to be recognized and trusted by the very beings encountering it, not to display some fixed, universal measurement. This closes three of the four differing marks; a fourth, more delicate teaching from the classical tradition follows next.

21. Milestone: A Classical Teaching on Gender and Buddhahood, and Its Own Careful Qualifications

The treatise here preserves a classical teaching from its own era — that a female form is never the one in which buddhahood is actually attained, since every bodhisattva, upon reaching the first ground, is said to leave a female rebirth behind for good. The text is equally careful, however, to specify exactly what this claim does and does not mean, tracing the issue to deeply ingrained habitual tendencies rather than to any fixed incapacity, and naming a single, universally available practice said to dissolve it.

Chinese Source

又非女身能證無上正等菩提。何以故？一切菩薩於過第一無數劫時，已捨女身，乃至安坐妙菩提座，曾不為女。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, a female form is not one in which unsurpassed, perfect awakening is attained. Why is this? Every bodhisattva, upon passing the first incalculable eon of the path, has already relinquished a female form — and from that point on, including at the very moment of sitting at the seat of awakening itself, is never again female.

Chinese Source

一切母邑性多煩惱、性多惡慧。非諸稟性多煩惱身、多惡慧身能證無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every woman, by deeply ingrained habitual tendency, carries more affliction and more distorted views. It is not a body with such deeply ingrained affliction and distorted views that attains unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to frame this teaching within its own historical and doctrinal context rather than leave it as a bare claim. He notes several places elsewhere in the Mahāyāna canon where a female figure attains awakening only after first transforming into a male form — a dragon-princess in one well-known sūtra, a goddess in another — a pattern the tradition itself explains not as a judgment on women's genuine spiritual capacity, but as addressing specific habitual tendencies the classical texts associate more strongly with a female rebirth in that era and cultural context, tendencies the text itself insists can be dissolved.

Indeed, he stresses the treatise's own remedy is strikingly simple and universally available: elsewhere the tradition holds that a single sincere quality — deep, genuine aspiration toward awakening itself — is sufficient on its own to leave a female rebirth behind, alongside a short list of virtues equally open to anyone regardless of gender: freedom from jealousy, from stinginess, from flattery, and from anger, together with truthful speech, gentle speech, freedom from craving, and freedom from wrong view. He is equally careful to note the treatise's own acknowledgment, elsewhere, that many women in the Buddha's own lifetime — his own aunt and foster mother prominent among them — attained arhatship directly as women, and that in later, more difficult ages, women and laypeople are repeatedly described as often practicing with even greater diligence than their male or monastic counterparts. He reads the passage's own final clarification as the real point being made: the "affliction" and "distorted views" named here are described as deeply ingrained habitual tendency (性), not a fixed, unchangeable nature — and, examined honestly, he notes, this same description of affliction and confusion applies just as easily to an undisciplined man who has not taken up genuine practice, which is precisely why the passage is best read as identifying a specific pattern of habituation to work through, not a claim about who is fundamentally capable of awakening. This closes the treatise's four differing marks between buddhas in full; the chapter now moves to its own concluding summary.

22. Milestone: The Chapter on Awakening Closes — and With It, an Entire Structure Spanning Four Fascicles

With all five ways of examining bodhi now complete — its nature, its supremacy, its ten names, its manifestation, and its differentiation — the treatise closes the chapter on awakening with a final declaration of its own inconceivable, immeasurable, unsurpassed excellence. This also closes something far larger: the entire structure of "what is to be learned," spanning five chapters and four full fascicles from fascicle 35 through this one.

Chinese Source

如是無上正等菩提，如說自性，應如實知；如說最勝，應如實知；如說十種功德名號隨念功德，應如實知；如說出現，應如實知；如說差別，應如實知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Unsurpassed, perfect awakening of this kind should be truly known exactly as described regarding its nature; truly known exactly as described regarding its supremacy; truly known exactly as described regarding its ten names for contemplation; truly known exactly as described regarding its manifestation in the world; and truly known exactly as described regarding its differentiation.

Chinese Source

又此菩提，為不思議，超過一切尋思道故；為無有量，無邊功德所集成故；為無有上，生成一切聲聞、獨覺及與如來諸功德故；是故唯佛所證菩提，最上、最尊、最妙、最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

This awakening, moreover, is inconceivable, surpassing every path of conceptual thought; immeasurable, composed of boundless virtues; and unsurpassed, giving rise to every virtue possessed by hearers, solitary buddhas, and Tathāgatas alike. For this reason, the awakening realized only by a buddha is the very highest, the most honored, the most wondrous, and the most supreme.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the four closing superlatives together precisely: "highest" seals the point that nothing surpasses this awakening; "most honored" seals the point that its virtues are truly immeasurable; "most wondrous" seals the point that it lies entirely beyond ordinary conceptual thought; and "most supreme" gathers together all seven kinds of supremacy already detailed at length. This closes the chapter on awakening (菩提品) in full — and with it, something considerably larger: the entire account of "what is to be learned" (所學處), the first of three major divisions structuring the factors of awakening, spanning five complete chapters — self-and-other-benefit, true meaning, power, ripening, and now awakening itself — across four full fascicles, from fascicle 35 all the way through this one.

He notes the treatise's own transition to what comes next: having established everything a bodhisattva is meant to learn, the treatise now turns to its second major division — how, precisely, this learning is actually to be carried out. This opens a new chapter, on the lineage that gives rise to the ten powers (力種性品), and with it a fresh seven-part structure that will carry the treatise from this fascicle all the way through fascicle 44.

23. Milestone: "How to Learn" Opens — Seven Points for the Path Ahead

Having established everything a bodhisattva learns, the treatise now turns to how that learning actually proceeds, opening a new chapter with a seven-part outline given first as a mnemonic verse: cultivating abundant resolute conviction, seeking the true teaching, teaching it to others, practicing in accordance with it, correctly instructing others, correctly admonishing them, and skillfully embracing body, speech, and mind through it all.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處力種性品第八。已說菩薩所應學處。如是應學，我今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Bodhisattva, the first division on yogic practice: the eighth chapter, on the lineage of the powers. What a bodhisattva should learn has already been explained. How this should be learned, I shall now explain.

Chinese Source

嗢柁南曰：勝解多求法，說法修法行，正教授教誡，方便攝三業。

Ru-Yi Translation

The summarizing verse says: abundant resolute conviction, seeking the teaching, teaching it and practicing it in accordance with itself, correct instruction and admonishment, and skillful means embracing the three karmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the chapter's own name: "power" refers to the ten powers already named among a buddha's one hundred and forty unique qualities, and "lineage" refers to the causes that actually give rise to them — this chapter's entire purpose is showing exactly how a bodhisattva cultivates the specific causes that eventually ripen into a buddha's own complete wisdom. He unpacks the seven-part structure the verse compresses: first, cultivating abundant resolute conviction across a specific set of foundational topics; second, actively seeking out the true teaching wherever it can genuinely be found; third, teaching it correctly to others once it has been learned; fourth, actually practicing in accordance with what has been taught rather than treating it as mere information; fifth and sixth, correctly instructing and, where needed, correctly admonishing students; and seventh, skillfully directing body, speech, and mind together throughout the entire process. Each of these seven, he notes, is itself one of the specific causes from which the ten powers' own lineage grows — the treatise now takes up the first of them, abundant resolute conviction, in detail.

24. Milestone: Abundant Resolute Conviction — Eight Foundations, Beginning With Why the Three Jewels Are Called "Treasures"

The first of the seven points, resolute conviction, rests on eight specific foundations — beginning with settled, joyful confidence in the genuine virtues of the Three Jewels, illustrated through six reasons the tradition gives for why buddha, Dharma, and saṅgha are called "treasures" at all: their rarity, their purity, their power, their capacity to adorn whoever holds them, their supremacy, and their utter unchangeability.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩於其八種勝解依處，具足成就淨信為先決定喜樂。……一者、於三寶功德勝解依處，具足成就淨信為先決定喜樂。謂於佛法僧真實功德具多勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, with respect to eight foundations of resolute conviction, accomplish settled joy resting on genuine confidence. ... First, the foundation of conviction in the Three Jewels' virtues: accomplishing settled joy resting on genuine confidence, holding abundant resolute conviction in the genuine virtues of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews all eight foundations this section will cover — conviction in the Three Jewels' virtues, in a buddha's power, in true meaning, in cause, in fruit, in the path from cause to fruit, in the means of attainment, and in the Buddha's own teachings — noting that "resolute conviction" here means something considerably deeper than casual belief: a settled, joyful certainty reached only after genuine hearing, reflection, and direct confirmation, strong enough that hearing the teaching or a sacred chant can bring tears or make the hair stand on end, a recognizable mark of genuine confidence rather than mere intellectual assent.

He draws out the *Ratnagotravibhāga*'s own six reasons the Three Jewels deserve the name "treasure": they are rare, since even an ordinary treasure eludes the poor, and encountering genuine Buddha, Dharma, or Saṅgha is exactly this rare for beings of little merit; they are stainless, free of any defiling flaw; they are powerful, possessing the inconceivable strength of the six superknowledges; they adorn whoever holds them, purifying and beautifying the conduct of anyone who genuinely takes them up; they are supreme, surpassing everything else found in the world; and they are unchanging, like true gold that fire, hammering, and grinding can never actually alter, remaining exactly what they are regardless of the world's own shifting fortunes. He pairs this with the *Vimalakīrti-nirdeśa*'s own teaching that Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha are ultimately not two separate things at all — the Buddha is the Dharma, the Dharma is the Saṅgha, and all three share the same unconditioned nature, equal to empty space itself, exactly as every other phenomenon ultimately is. This closes the first of eight foundations for resolute conviction; conviction in a buddha's power and in true meaning, both already covered at length in earlier fascicles, follow next.

25. Conviction in a Buddha's Power and in True Meaning — Callbacks to Earlier Fascicles

The second and third foundations for resolute conviction point directly back to ground already covered in exhaustive detail: settled confidence in the three kinds of power a buddha possesses, and settled confidence in the true meaning a buddha has fully realized — both already the subject of entire chapters earlier in the treatise.

Chinese Source

二者、於佛菩薩威力勝解依處，具足成就淨信為先決定喜樂。謂於如前所說威力具多勝解。三者、於真實義勝解依處，具足成就淨信為先決定喜樂。謂於如前所說真實義具多勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the foundation of conviction in the power of buddhas and bodhisattvas: accomplishing settled joy resting on genuine confidence, holding abundant resolute conviction in the power already described. Third, the foundation of conviction in true meaning: accomplishing settled joy resting on genuine confidence, holding abundant resolute conviction in the true meaning already described.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi simply points back to where each of these was already treated in full: the power described here is exactly the threefold power — the power of holiness resting on mastery of concentration, the power inherent in the Dharma itself, and innate power carried from birth to death — detailed at exhaustive length in fascicle 37's own chapter on power; and the true meaning described here is exactly the bodhisattva's purified truth, the inexpressible nature of phenomena, and everything else covered in fascicle 36's own chapter on true meaning. Genuine conviction in both, he notes, is not separate from actually understanding them — a settled confidence built on having actually worked through what power and true meaning consist of, not a vague, unexamined trust taken on faith alone. This closes the second and third foundations; conviction in cause, the fourth foundation, opens next.

26. Five More Foundations: Cause, Fruit, Attainability, the Means, and the Well-Spoken Teaching

Five more foundations round out resolute conviction: settled confidence in ten kinds of genuine cause and five kinds of genuine fruit, sorted across defiled, pure, and neutral categories; confidence that buddhahood itself is genuinely attainable, however long it takes; confidence in the bodhisattva path's own methods as capable of actually delivering that fruit; and confidence that the Buddha's teaching is authentically well-spoken, judged by three specific marks — delighting the listener, free of any corrupting compromise, and purely beneficial.

Chinese Source

四者、於因勝解依處……謂於種種如應所攝無顛倒因具多勝解。五者、於果勝解依處……謂於種種如應所攝無顛倒果具多勝解。六者、於應得義勝解依處……謂於無上正等菩提所應得義，我有堪任定當能得具多勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, the foundation of conviction in cause ... holding abundant resolute conviction in the various genuine, undistorted causes, each sorted as appropriate. Fifth, the foundation of conviction in fruit ... holding abundant resolute conviction in the various genuine, undistorted fruits, each sorted as appropriate. Sixth, the foundation of conviction in what is attainable ... holding abundant resolute conviction that "I possess the capacity, and will certainly attain, the genuine benefit of unsurpassed, perfect awakening."

Chinese Source

七者、於得方便勝解依處……謂於一切菩薩學道能得方便，由此方便得應得義具多勝解。八者、於善言、善語、善說勝解依處……謂於契經、應頌、記別等法具多勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, the foundation of conviction in the means of attainment ... holding abundant resolute conviction in the bodhisattva path's own means of attainment, by which the genuine benefit sought is actually reached. Eighth, the foundation of conviction in the well-spoken teaching ... holding abundant resolute conviction in the discourses, verses, and other forms the teaching takes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the ten kinds of genuine cause the fourth foundation covers — expressed cause, dependent cause, attracting cause, sustaining cause, generative cause, eliciting cause, determinate cause, cooperative cause, opposing cause, and non-opposing cause — each sorted as defiled, pure, or neutral depending on context, and the five kinds of genuine fruit the fifth foundation covers: karmic-ripening fruit, the neutral result any wholesome or unwholesome past action eventually yields; consistent-outflow fruit, where a cultivated habit simply reinforces more of the same, wholesome practice strengthening further wholesome practice and the reverse; liberation fruit, freedom from bondage reached through wisdom's own discernment — nirvāṇa itself; effort fruit, the direct result of one's own deliberate application, as diligent study yields good results; and dominant-condition fruit, where a supportive surrounding condition amplifies an outcome, as fertilizer helps a plant grow more vigorously than it otherwise would.

He explains the sixth foundation as settled confidence that buddhahood is not reserved for some special category of being but genuinely reachable by anyone with the lineage for it — a matter of when, not whether — and the seventh as trust that the bodhisattva path's own concrete methods, the six perfections and everything built upon them, genuinely deliver on that promise rather than remaining merely aspirational. He explains the eighth through three specific marks distinguishing genuinely well-spoken teaching from anything merely persuasive: delighting the listener, meaning it is delivered in whatever manner actually reaches a given audience's own temperament, not one rigid style forced on everyone; free of corrupting compromise, meaning it is never delivered through harsh criticism, exaggeration, or any breach of the speaker's own integrity; and purely beneficial, meaning it genuinely leads a listener out of unwholesome ground rather than merely entertaining or impressing them — closely related to the classical formula that genuine teaching is good at the beginning, good in the middle, and good at the end: pleasant on first hearing, free of both indulgence and harsh self-denial as it is put into practice, and leading all the way to complete liberation once fully realized. This closes all eight foundations of resolute conviction; the conditions that actually cultivate this conviction to its full strength follow next.

27. Milestone: Two Conditions That Cultivate Genuine Conviction, Closing the First of Seven Points

Resolute conviction across all eight foundations is cultivated through exactly two conditions: sustained practice of that very conviction through hearing, reflection, and meditative insight, and the accumulated strength of an unwavering forbearance that no longer wavers or contradicts what has genuinely been realized. With this, the treatise's first of seven points — abundant resolute conviction — closes in full.

Chinese Source

於此八種勝解依處，應知菩薩由二因緣具多勝解。一者、多修勝解故；二者、積集猛利忍故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With respect to these eight foundations of resolute conviction, it should be understood that bodhisattvas hold abundant resolute conviction through two conditions: first, through extensively cultivating that very conviction; and second, through accumulating a forceful, unwavering forbearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first condition as conviction itself becoming a sustained object of practice — carried through hearing the teaching, reflecting on it, and, crucially, cultivating the specific insight-meditation that turns intellectual acceptance into settled, lived certainty, precisely as this same cultivation was described building toward the resolve for awakening in earlier fascicles. He explains the second condition as the accumulated strength of unwavering forbearance — a genuine, settled acceptance of the teaching so firm it no longer wavers or contradicts itself even under pressure, built up specifically through consistent, harmonious agreement with the eight foundations rather than any grudging or partial acceptance. This closes abundant resolute conviction, the first of the seven points structuring how a bodhisattva actually learns; seeking the true teaching, the second point, opens next with a strikingly comprehensive scope.

28. Milestone: Seeking the True Teaching — A Bodhisattva Studies Absolutely Everything

The second point, seeking the true teaching, opens with a genuinely sweeping scope: a bodhisattva seeks out the entire bodhisattva canon and hearer canon, every non-Buddhist treatise without exception, and every worldly craft and trade — leaving no field of knowledge, sacred or secular, unexamined.

Chinese Source

彼諸菩薩求正法時，當何所求？云何而求？何義故求？

Ru-Yi Translation

When bodhisattvas seek the true teaching, what should they seek? How should they seek it? For what purpose should they seek it?

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩以要言之，當求一切菩薩藏法、聲聞藏法、一切外論、一切世間工業處論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in brief, should seek out the entire bodhisattva canon, the hearer canon, every non-Buddhist treatise, and every treatise on worldly crafts and trades.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes an interesting technical point about the canon's own twelve traditional divisions: only the "extensive" division (方廣, the vast expositions unique to the Great Vehicle) belongs exclusively to the bodhisattva canon, while every one of the remaining eleven divisions contains the hearer's own teaching, alongside a portion of bodhisattva teaching mixed in as well — meaning a bodhisattva's own scriptural study necessarily overlaps with a hearer's, rather than sitting in some entirely separate collection.

He sorts non-Buddhist treatises into three kinds: logic, tracing back to rival schools' own early systematic reasoning, which Buddhist teachers eventually adapted into their own rigorous methods for defending the Dharma; linguistics, including the striking belief held by certain schools that sound itself is eternal and constitutes the ultimate nature of reality; and medicine, the study of illness, its causes, and its cures. He draws out a deeper point buried in the treatise's own account of medicine: illness is ultimately traced not merely to physical imbalance but to having a body at all, and having a body is itself the fruit of past karma rooted, at its very root, in ignorance — meaning ordinary medicine, however genuinely helpful, only ever treats the symptom of a much deeper cycle, since a cured illness today simply returns in some other form tomorrow until ignorance itself is finally uprooted. He lists the classical twelve kinds of worldly craft the treatise names as also worth studying — farming, trade, serving a ruler, writing and arithmetic, divination, ritual formulas, construction, animal husbandry, protection from harm, reconciliation, food preparation, and music — noting that nothing genuinely useful to beings falls outside a bodhisattva's own scope of study. This closes the sweeping account of what a bodhisattva seeks; the treatise now organizes this same material into five specific fields of knowledge.

29. The Five Fields of Knowledge, and Why Inner Teaching Reveals an Enduring Yet Changeable Karma

Everything a bodhisattva studies organizes into five fields: inner teaching (Buddhism itself), logic, linguistics, medicine, and practical crafts — with inner teaching, examined closely, revealing two essential truths at once: the genuine workings of cause and effect across both defilement and purification, and the precise, careful balance that what has genuinely been done is never simply lost, while what has never been done is never simply granted.

Chinese Source

如是一切明處所攝有五明處。一、內明處，二、因明處，三、聲明處，四、醫方明處，五、工業明處。菩薩於此五種明處若正勤求，則名勤求一切明處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Everything comprised within these fields of knowledge falls into five: first, inner teaching; second, logic; third, linguistics; fourth, medicine; and fifth, practical crafts. When bodhisattvas correctly and diligently seek out these five fields, this is called diligently seeking every field of knowledge.

Chinese Source

謂內明論略二相轉。一者、顯示正因果相，二者、顯示已作不失、未作不得相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Inner teaching, in brief, operates through two aspects: first, revealing the genuine workings of cause and effect; and second, revealing that what has genuinely been done is never lost, while what has never been done is never attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first aspect as covering the entire scope of Buddhist teaching in a single stroke — the defiled cause-and-effect of ordinary rebirth, and the pure cause-and-effect of the path leading beyond it, everything the Buddha ever taught ultimately falling under one or the other. He treats the second aspect as a precise, careful clarification often missed: karma once genuinely accumulated is never simply erased on its own, yet neither is it fixed and unchangeable — sustained cultivation still strengthens it further, and sustained neglect still allows it to weaken, exactly as an untreated affliction does not disappear merely by being ignored, while genuine effort against it does gradually wear it down. Nothing not yet actually done, meanwhile, can ever simply be granted without the doing — there is no shortcut around genuine cause and effect in either direction. This closes the account of inner teaching's own two aspects; logic's own two aspects, together with a brief account of how Buddhist logic itself came to be formalized, follow next.

30. The Origins of Buddhist Logic, and Its Two Practical Functions

Logic, adapted from rival schools' own early reasoning and eventually formalized by Buddhist teachers into a rigorous three-part method — a thesis grounded in reliable authority, a reason grounded in valid inference, and an example grounded in direct, shared experience — serves two practical functions: defeating a rival's flawed argument, and defending one's own position from every possible objection.

Chinese Source

因明論亦二相轉。一者、顯示摧伏他論勝利相，二者、顯示免脫他論勝利相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Logic likewise operates through two aspects: first, revealing the mark of victoriously defeating a rival's argument; and second, revealing the mark of successfully escaping a rival's own attack.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces logic's own development: an early rival philosophical school pioneered systematic reasoning first, and Buddhist teachers, needing rigorous tools to defend the Dharma in open debate, eventually adapted and refined this into their own formal method — later systematized by the scholar Dignāga into a three-part structure of thesis, reason, and example. A thesis must rest on reliable scriptural authority, since the point being argued for is the Dharma itself, not some rival doctrine dressed up in borrowed terms; a reason must rest on valid inference, since it explains, through logically sound reasoning, why the thesis holds even though it cannot simply be pointed to directly; and an example must rest on direct, ordinary experience everyone already shares, illustrating the reasoning concretely enough that even an audience unfamiliar with formal argument can follow it — precisely the pattern any skillful teacher already uses instinctively, whether or not they have ever studied logic formally. He explains the two resulting functions plainly: skillful use of this method lets a debater expose the flaws in a rival's own position, and equally lets that same debater construct a position so carefully reasoned that no rival attack can find a genuine opening to exploit. This closes logic's own two aspects; linguistics, medicine, and practical crafts each receive their own such treatment next.

31. Linguistics' Two Aspects, and How One Million Verses Became Three Hundred

Linguistics operates through two aspects — establishing the basic elements of sound and grammar that make language possible at all, and cultivating genuine skill in eloquent expression, judged by five qualities: never vulgar, easily understood, resonant in delivery, aligned with wholesome purpose, and excellent in meaning — illustrated by a striking tradition about how a single treatise shrank from a million verses down to just three hundred.

Chinese Source

聲明論亦二相轉。一者、顯示安立界相、能成立相，二者、顯示語工勝利相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Linguistics likewise operates through two aspects: first, revealing the establishment of the basic elements of sound and what makes them combinable into meaning; and second, revealing the mark of skillful excellence in speech itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recounts a well-known tradition about the origin of Sanskrit grammar: Great Brahmā himself is said to have originally composed a treatise on linguistics running to a million verses — far too much for ordinary beings to ever learn — so Indra condensed it to a hundred thousand, and later sages condensed it further still, first to eight hundred verses and finally to three hundred, the version still studied today. The first aspect, he explains, covers the basic building blocks any language needs — its vowels and consonants, and the grammatical rules governing how they combine — while the second covers the practical skill of using those building blocks eloquently: speech free of anything coarse or vulgar, expressed clearly enough to be understood without laborious repetition, delivered with a voice resonant and confident enough to actually hold an audience, aligned throughout with a wholesome purpose that genuinely benefits the listener, and excellent in the substance of what it actually conveys. He notes that language began as pure spoken sound long before writing existed, and that both spoken and written expression fall equally under this same field — an essential skill for any bodhisattva, since language remains the basic bridge connecting one mind to another in this world. This closes linguistics' two aspects; medicine's own aspects, mirroring a far more familiar framework, follow next.

32. Milestone: Medicine's Four Aspects Mirror the Four Noble Truths

Medicine, examined closely, reveals exactly the same four-part structure as the four noble truths: skill in recognizing illness itself (suffering), skill in tracing illness back to its actual cause (origin), skill in curing an illness once it has already arisen (cessation), and skill in preventing a cured illness from ever recurring (the path) — the entire practice of medicine turning out to be a smaller reflection of the very structure the whole Buddhist path is built on.

Chinese Source

醫方明論四種相轉。一者、顯示病體善巧相，二者、顯示病因善巧相，三者、顯示斷已生病善巧相，四者、顯示已斷之病當不更生善巧相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Medicine operates through four aspects: first, revealing skill regarding the nature of illness itself; second, revealing skill regarding the cause of illness; third, revealing skill in curing an illness once it has arisen; and fourth, revealing skill in ensuring a cured illness never arises again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the parallel explicitly: recognizing the nature of illness itself corresponds to the truth of suffering, since illness in the body is simply one visible expression of the broader suffering inherent in having a body caught in birth-and-death at all; tracing illness back to its cause corresponds to the truth of origin, since a physician traces symptoms back to physical imbalance exactly as the Buddha traces suffering back through karma to its root in affliction; curing an existing illness corresponds to the truth of cessation, the genuine, felt relief of symptoms resolved exactly as cessation is suffering's own genuine resolution; and ensuring the illness never returns corresponds to the truth of the path, the specific, reliable method that keeps a cured condition from simply recurring, exactly as the noble path keeps liberation from ever being merely temporary. He notes this parallel is not a loose analogy imposed from outside but reflects something genuinely structural: any complete solution to any form of suffering, physical or otherwise, necessarily moves through exactly these four stages — recognizing the problem, tracing its cause, resolving it, and securing that resolution against relapse. This closes medicine's four aspects; the fifth field, practical crafts, receives brief treatment next before the treatise turns to inner teaching's own far more detailed exposition.

33. Practical Crafts' Single Aspect, Closing the Account of All Five Fields

Practical crafts, the fifth and final field, is characterized simply by the sheer variety of distinct results different trades and skills produce — whether smithing metal, managing workers, composing an effective speech, or writing well, each craft yielding its own specific kind of accomplished outcome.

Chinese Source

一切世間工業明論，顯示各別工巧業處所作成辦種種異相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every worldly practical craft reveals the various distinct outcomes accomplished by each specific trade and skill.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this field's own breadth as its defining feature rather than any single unifying principle — forging metal, managing a household or workforce, composing effective speech, writing clearly, each trade producing its own genuinely distinct kind of skillful result, covering the entire remaining range of practical human accomplishment not already addressed by the other four fields. This closes the treatise's brief survey of all five fields of knowledge; the treatise now returns to inner teaching specifically, unfolding what it earlier called "revealing the genuine workings of cause and effect" in far greater technical depth, beginning with a complete account of ten distinct kinds of cause.

34. Milestone: The Ten Causes Open — A Complete Account of Causality Itself

Inner teaching's account of "genuine cause and effect" now receives its own extensive, technical unpacking: every possible kind of cause behind any phenomenon whatsoever — defiled, pure, or neutral — reduces to just ten distinct categories, together forming a complete and non-overlapping map of causality itself.

Chinese Source

云何內明論顯示正因果相？謂有十種因，當知建立無顛倒因，攝一切因。或為雜染、或為清淨、或為世間彼彼稼穡等無記法轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does inner teaching reveal the genuine workings of cause and effect? It should be understood that there are ten kinds of cause, establishing a complete and undistorted account of cause that comprises every cause whatsoever — whether operating to produce defilement, purity, or neutral phenomena such as this or that worldly crop.

Chinese Source

云何十因？一、隨說因，二、觀待因，三、牽引因，四、攝受因，五、生起因，六、引發因，七、定別因，八、同事因，九、相違因，十、不相違因。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the ten causes? First, the cause of expression; second, the cause of dependence; third, the cause of distant attraction; fourth, the cause of supportive embrace; fifth, the cause of immediate arising; sixth, the cause of induced extension; seventh, the cause of determinate distinction; eighth, the cause of shared activity; ninth, the cause of opposition; and tenth, the cause of non-opposition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this ten-fold framework as one of the treatise's most foundational teachings on causation, cross-referenced elsewhere in the treatise's own earlier chapters — a complete, non-overlapping classification meant to capture literally every way one thing can be said to give rise to another, whether in the defiled cycle of ordinary rebirth, the pure cultivation of the path, or morally neutral processes like planting and harvesting a crop. He notes the treatise's own method: each cause will first be defined by its essential nature, then shown producing its corresponding kind of result across defiled, pure, and neutral phenomena in turn — a systematic, almost mathematical approach to a question every philosophical tradition eventually has to answer: what, precisely, does it mean for one thing to cause another? The treatise now defines each of the ten in turn, beginning with the first four.

35. The First Four Causes: Language, Dependence, Distant Attraction, and Supportive Conditions

The first cause is language itself, since naming makes thought possible and thought makes speech possible; the second is dependence, since something is only ever pursued relative to an actual need, as hands exist for grasping and hunger drives the search for food; the third is distant attraction, the kind a seed exercises over a fruit that will not ripen for a long time yet; and the fourth is supportive embrace, everything besides the seed itself — water, fertilizer, sunlight — that helps a seed actually come to fruition.

Chinese Source

謂一切法，名為先故想，想為先故說，是名彼諸法隨說因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding all phenomena: name comes first, enabling thought; thought comes first, enabling speech. This is called the cause of expression for those phenomena.

Chinese Source

觀待此故，此為因故，於彼彼事若求、若取，此名彼觀待因。如觀待手故，手為因故，有執持業。觀待足故，足為因故，有往來業。……觀待饑渴故，饑渴為因故，於諸飲食若求、若取。

Ru-Yi Translation

Depending on this, this becomes the cause by which this or that thing is sought or obtained — this is called the cause of dependence. Depending on the hand, the hand becomes the cause of the activity of grasping. Depending on the foot, the foot becomes the cause of the activity of walking. ... Depending on hunger and thirst, hunger and thirst become the cause for seeking and obtaining food and drink.

Chinese Source

一切種子，望後自果，名牽引因。……除種子外，所餘諸緣，名攝受因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every seed, with respect to its own fruit still to come, is called the cause of distant attraction. ... Everything other than the seed itself is called the cause of supportive embrace.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first cause as the simple but easily overlooked fact that nothing can even be thought about, let alone spoken of, without first being named — naming makes conceptual grasp possible, and conceptual grasp makes speech possible, the entire chain beginning with language itself. He illustrates the second cause with the treatise's own everyday examples: a hand is only ever put to the specific use of grasping because grasping is what a hand makes possible in the first place, exactly as feet exist relative to walking, joints relative to bending and stretching, and hunger or thirst relative to actively seeking out food or drink — nothing is pursued in a vacuum, only ever relative to some genuine, specific need.

He explains the third cause through the treatise's own image of a bean seed, which does not sprout the instant it is planted but only after a genuine span of time has passed — precisely how ignorance and the karmic formations it produces plant a seed for a rebirth that will not ripen for a long time yet, sometimes not until several lifetimes later, this "distant" quality being exactly what distinguishes it from a cause acting on something close at hand. He explains the fourth cause as everything beyond the seed itself needed to bring a result to actual fruition — a mental event needs not only its own seed but three further supportive conditions to actually arise, exactly as a planted seed needs water, fertilizer, and sunlight, none of which are the seed itself, yet all of which are indispensable to its eventual sprouting. This closes the first four causes; the fifth through seventh, describing more precisely how a seed's more immediate fruit differs from the further results that fruit itself goes on to produce, follow next.

36. Milestone: Causes Five Through Seven — Immediate Arising, the Domino Effect, and Why Melon Seeds Never Yield Beans

The fifth cause, immediate arising, describes a seed's relationship to its very nearest fruit — craving and clinging as the direct, proximate cause of a new rebirth, distinguished from karma's own more distant role; the sixth, induced extension, describes how an initial result then goes on to induce a whole chain of further results, one leading to the next like a row of falling dominoes; and the seventh, determinate distinction, is the simple guarantee that every kind of cause reliably yields its own corresponding kind of result and never gets confused with another — melon seeds always yielding melons, never beans.

Chinese Source

即諸種子，望初自果，名生起因。……即初種子所生起果，望後種子所牽引果，名引發因。

Ru-Yi Translation

These very seeds, with respect to their most immediate fruit, are called the cause of immediate arising. ... The fruit first arisen from that seed, with respect to the further fruit later drawn out by a subsequent seed, is called the cause of induced extension.

Chinese Source

種種異類，各別因緣，名定別因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Each distinct kind of phenomenon has its own distinct, separate cause and condition — this is called the cause of determinate distinction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the fifth cause from the third through a classical formula: "karma is the cause that produces rebirth in general; craving is the cause that actually generates it" — karma plants the distant seed of a future existence, while craving and clinging, present much closer to the moment of death, function as the immediate trigger that actually brings that seed to fruition as a new birth, exactly as a seed's own most proximate, nearest fruit (the sprout, not some later branch or flower) is what "immediate arising" specifically names.

He explains the sixth cause as one result setting off a whole further chain of consequences, not stopping at a single fruit: an ordinary wholesome deed within the desire realm can induce a more refined wholesome quality within that same realm, which can in turn induce a virtue belonging to the form realm, and so on upward — precisely mirrored on the unwholesome side, where one affliction like craving induces hatred, arrogance, wrong view, and eventually genuinely harmful action in a rolling chain, and even mirrored among neutral phenomena, as ordinary food sustains a being's continued existence and stability. He explains the seventh cause simply and memorably: plant a melon seed and only a melon ever grows, plant a bean seed and only a bean ever grows — every kind of cause reliably yields only its own corresponding kind of result, without any of the ten causes ever bleeding into confusion with another, a guarantee underlying the entire coherence of cause and effect across every category already described. This closes the fifth through seventh causes; the eighth through tenth — shared activity, opposition, and non-opposition — complete the full account of ten causes next.

37. Causes Eight Through Ten Complete the Ten-Fold Framework

The eighth cause, shared activity, names nothing new in itself — it is simply the previous six causes viewed together as they jointly cooperate to produce a single result; the ninth, opposition, is whatever actively blocks a result from arising; and the tenth, non-opposition, is simply the absence of that blocking factor, letting the result proceed unhindered.

Chinese Source

若觀待因、若牽引因、若攝受因、若生起因、若引發因、若定別因，如是諸因總攝為一，名同事因。

Ru-Yi Translation

The cause of dependence, the cause of distant attraction, the cause of supportive embrace, the cause of immediate arising, the cause of induced extension, and the cause of determinate distinction — all these causes gathered together as one are called the cause of shared activity.

Chinese Source

於所生法能障礙因，名相違因。此障礙因若闕、若離，名不相違因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever is capable of obstructing a phenomenon about to arise is called the cause of opposition. When this obstructing cause is lacking or absent, this is called the cause of non-opposition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains shared activity simply as a change of vantage point: the same six causes already defined — dependence, distant attraction, supportive embrace, immediate arising, induced extension, and determinate distinction — considered not one at a time but together, cooperating as a single combined force toward one shared outcome, exactly as water, fertilizer, sunlight, and the seed itself all work together, not separately, to bring a single plant to fruition. He explains opposition and non-opposition as a simple, symmetrical pair: whatever specifically prevents an otherwise-ready result from actually arising counts as an opposing cause, while the mere absence of any such obstruction — nothing more needed — counts as a non-opposing cause, letting whatever was already otherwise ready to happen simply proceed. This closes the basic definitions of all ten causes; the treatise now examines "opposition" more closely, since the word carries several distinct everyday meanings that need to be carefully separated from its precise technical sense here.

38. Milestone: Six Everyday Meanings of "Opposition" — and Which One Actually Defines the Ninth Cause

Ordinary language uses "opposition" in at least six distinct ways — a thinker's own writings contradicting themselves over time, a flawed argument failing valid logical standards, a result blocked for lack of supportive conditions, two mutually exclusive things unable to coexist in one place, natural enemies locked in conflict, and a meditative antidote directly countering the affliction it targets — yet only one of these six, the simple lack of supportive conditions, is what the ninth cause of opposition technically refers to.

Chinese Source

當知相違略有六種。一、語言相違……二、道理相違……三、生起相違，謂所生法，能生緣闕，障生緣會。四、同處相違，謂明、闇，貪、瞋，苦樂等法。五、怨敵相違……六、障治相違，謂修不淨與諸貪欲，修慈與瞋……

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that opposition has, in brief, six kinds. First, verbal opposition ... second, logical opposition ... third, opposition of arising, where a phenomenon about to arise lacks its necessary supportive conditions, obstructing their coming together. Fourth, opposition of shared location, as with light and darkness, craving and hatred, pain and pleasure. Fifth, opposition of natural enemies ... sixth, opposition of remedy and obstruction, as when cultivating contemplation on impurity opposes craving, or cultivating loving-kindness opposes hatred ...

Chinese Source

於此義中，正意唯取生起相違。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this discussion, the precise intended meaning refers only to opposition of arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all six senses with concrete illustrations: verbal opposition is simply a teacher's own views shifting over decades of maturing practice, so that an early and a late writing may genuinely disagree; logical opposition is a formal argument that fails to meet valid reasoning's own standards, already covered under logic earlier in this fascicle; opposition of arising — the sense actually meant here — is a result blocked purely for lack of what it needs, as an unwatered, unfed, sun-starved sapling simply fails to grow; opposition of shared location is two things that can never occupy the same place or moment at once, as light excludes darkness and craving excludes hatred in the very same instant of mind; opposition of natural enemies covers predator-prey relationships in nature (a snake preying on mice, a cat preying on mice) as well as harmful companionship that actively undermines one's own practice; and opposition of remedy and obstruction covers the classical meditative antidotes — contemplating impurity to loosen desire's grip, cultivating loving-kindness against hatred, cultivating compassion against cruelty, and the seven factors of awakening together with the eightfold noble path standing opposed to every affliction across all three realms at once.

He stresses the treatise's own deliberate narrowing at the end: of these six genuinely distinct uses of the same word, only the third — a result failing to arise purely because its supportive conditions are missing — is what the technical category "cause of opposition" actually names within the ten-cause framework; the other five, while real and worth understanding in their own right, use "opposition" in a broader, more ordinary sense than the precise, narrow meaning intended here. This closes the detailed account of opposition; the treatise now shows how all ten causes reduce to simpler, more familiar frameworks already used elsewhere in Buddhist philosophy.

39. Reducing Ten Causes to Two, and Mapping Them Onto the Four Classical Conditions

All ten causes reduce, in the simplest terms, to just two: a genuinely generative cause — the seed itself, in both its distant and its immediate sense — and a merely facilitating cause, covering everything else that supports a seed's fruition without actually producing it. The same ten causes map just as cleanly onto Buddhist philosophy's more familiar framework of four conditions.

Chinese Source

此一切因二因所攝。一、能生因，二、方便因。當知此中，牽引種子、生起種子名能生因，所餘諸因名方便因。

Ru-Yi Translation

All these causes are comprised within two: first, generative cause; second, facilitating cause. It should be understood that the seed of distant attraction and the seed of immediate arising are called generative cause, while every remaining cause is called facilitating cause.

Chinese Source

復有四緣。一、因緣，二、等無間緣，三、所緣緣，四、增上緣。……當知此中，若能生因，是名因緣；若方便因，是增上緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, moreover, four conditions: first, causal condition; second, immediately-preceding condition; third, object condition; and fourth, dominant condition. ... It should be understood that generative cause here corresponds to causal condition, while facilitating cause corresponds to dominant condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the two-fold reduction plainly: only the seed itself — whether considered as the distant cause of a far-future fruit or the immediate cause of its very next result — actually generates anything at all; the remaining eight causes (dependence, supportive embrace, induced extension, determinate distinction, shared activity, opposition, and non-opposition) all merely support, shape, or permit that generative process, never producing the result on their own.

He then explains how this maps onto the four conditions familiar from elsewhere in the treatise: causal condition refers to the seeds stored in the underlying consciousness, matching generative cause directly; the immediately-preceding condition, where one moment of mind must cease before the next can arise (a condition unique to mental phenomena, since only consciousness itself flows moment to moment in this way), and the object condition, whatever a mind or mental factor actually takes as its object, both fall under supportive embrace, the fourth cause, since both apply only to mental events and both support arising without themselves being the seed; and dominant condition covers everything else facilitating a result's fruition, matching facilitating cause as a whole. This closes the reduction of ten causes to simpler, more familiar frameworks; the treatise now demonstrates how all ten operate together in practice, through the concrete, everyday example of a single grain crop.

40. Milestone: How the Ten Causes Work in Practice — A Single Grain of Barley

To make the entire ten-cause framework concrete, the treatise walks through a single, ordinary example — a grain crop such as barley — showing how each of the ten causes applies to it in turn, beginning with the simplest: the very name "barley" itself, without which the grain could never be discussed, carried, ground into flour, or stored at all.

Chinese Source

謂於世間種種稼穡墮諸穀數世資生物，所有種種名想言說，謂大麥、小麥、稻穀、胡麻、大小豆等。即此望彼種種稼穡，為隨說因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the world's various crops, comprised among the grains that sustain worldly life, all the various names and designations given to them — barley, wheat, rice, sesame, and various beans — these names, with respect to the crops themselves, serve as the cause of expression.

Chinese Source

如言大麥，持去、持來，若磨、若置，如是等類種種隨說。如說大麥，餘小麥等，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

As when one says "barley" — carrying it away, bringing it back, grinding it, or storing it — all such various forms of expression apply. Just as with barley, it should be understood that the same holds for wheat and the other grains.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this extended illustration as the treatise's own way of making an otherwise highly abstract framework immediately graspable: naming a crop "barley" is what makes every subsequent action involving it possible at all — deciding to carry it, grind it into flour, or set it aside to dry all depend on first being able to think and speak of "the barley" as a distinct thing, exactly as the cause of expression was defined at the very start of the ten-cause framework. He notes that whatever applies to barley applies equally to wheat, rice, sesame, or any other grain — the same underlying causal structure, just with a different name attached. This closes the cause of expression as illustrated through grain; the treatise continues working through the remaining nine causes using this same concrete example next.

41. The Ten Causes Applied to a Grain Crop, Completing the Illustration

Working through the remaining nine causes with the same grain crop makes the entire framework concrete: hunger itself is what drives the search for food (dependence); a seed planted today is what eventually yields tomorrow's harvest (distant attraction); soil, rain, and sunlight are what actually let that seed sprout (supportive embrace); the seed's relation to its very first sprout is one thing, while the sprout's own ongoing growth into a mature, harvestable crop is another (immediate arising and induced extension); a barley seed only ever yields barley, never any other grain (determinate distinction); every contributing factor working together is needed at once for the crop to mature at all (shared activity); and frost, hail, or other calamity blocks that same maturing, while their simple absence lets it proceed (opposition and non-opposition).

Chinese Source

觀待饑渴羸劣身住……由彼各別自種子故，種種稼穡差別而生，即說彼種子為此牽引因。地、雨等緣，能生於芽，名攝受因。即彼種子望所生芽，名生起因。芽、莖、葉等展轉相續，望彼稼穡若成、若熟，為引發因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Depending on hunger and thirst, the body becomes weakened and unwell ... because each crop has its own distinct seed, various crops arise with their own distinct character — this very seed, with respect to the crop, is called the cause of distant attraction. Soil, rain, and similar conditions, able to produce a sprout, are called the cause of supportive embrace. That same seed, with respect to the sprout it produces, is called the cause of immediate arising. The sprout, stem, and leaves continuing on in succession, with respect to the crop's own eventual completion and ripening, are called the cause of induced extension.

Chinese Source

從大麥種生大麥芽、大麥苗稼，不生餘類……即彼一切從觀待因至定別因，同為稼穡而得成熟，名同事因……霜雹災等諸障礙法，望彼滋稼為相違因。彼闕無障，是諸滋稼不相違因。

Ru-Yi Translation

From a barley seed, a barley sprout and barley crop arise, never any other kind ... all of these, from the cause of dependence through the cause of determinate distinction, working together toward the crop's own maturing, are called the cause of shared activity ... frost, hail, and similar calamities, with respect to the crop's own nourishment, are called the cause of opposition. Their absence, allowing that same nourishment to proceed, is the cause of non-opposition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this extended grain illustration as the treatise's own deliberate teaching device — walking the full ten-cause framework through a single, universally familiar example so that the entire abstract structure becomes intuitively graspable at once, before the treatise applies this exact same ten-fold analysis to something far weightier: the actual causal chain behind suffering itself. This closes the grain-crop illustration in full; the treatise now turns to applying all ten causes to defiled phenomena — the twelve links of dependent origination that drive ordinary rebirth.

42. Milestone: The Ten Causes Applied to Suffering Itself — The Twelve Links of Dependent Origination

The same ten-cause framework now illuminates the twelve links of dependent origination directly: naming ignorance, formations, and the rest is what makes them speakable at all; craving for pleasant experience is what drives the entire cycle onward; ignorance and formations plant seeds now for a birth still lifetimes away; bad company, wrong teaching, and careless attention are what actually reinforce ignorance's grip; and each link, in its own turn, induces the next in an unbroken chain leading all the way to old age and death.

Chinese Source

又於一切雜染緣起所有種種名想言說，謂無明、行、識、名色，廣說乃至老死愁悲憂苦擾惱。即此望彼諸雜染法，為隨說因。……觀待境界所有愛味，於諸有支相續流轉，即彼望此諸雜染法，為觀待因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding all the defiled phenomena of dependent origination, every name and designation given to them — ignorance, formations, consciousness, name-and-form, and so on in detail up through old age, death, grief, sorrow, and affliction — these names, with respect to the defiled phenomena themselves, serve as the cause of expression. ... Craving for the pleasure found in experienced objects, driving the continued cycling through the links of existence — this, with respect to these defiled phenomena, is the cause of dependence.

Chinese Source

於現法中，無明等法所有已生已長種子，今此種子，望於餘生生老死等，為牽引因。近不善士、聞不正法、非理作意，及先串習所引勢力生無明等，名攝受因。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this very life, the seeds of ignorance and the other links, already arisen and already grown — these seeds, with respect to the old age and death of a future life, serve as the cause of distant attraction. Keeping company with the unwholesome, hearing incorrect teachings, careless attention, and the accumulated force of past habituation, all giving rise to ignorance — this is called the cause of supportive embrace.

Chinese Source

從無明支乃至有支，展轉引發後後相續，望於餘生生老死等，為引發因。餘無明支及自種子，乃至有支，能生那落迦；餘無明支及自種子，乃至有支，能生傍生、餓鬼、人、天，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the link of ignorance through the link of becoming, each inducing the next in unbroken succession — this, with respect to the old age and death of a future life, is the cause of induced extension. One version of ignorance and its own seeds, through the link of becoming, is able to produce a hell-rebirth; another version of ignorance and its own seeds, through the link of becoming, is able to produce rebirth as an animal, a hungry ghost, a human, or a god — it should be understood to work the same way in each case.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through this mapping as the treatise's own way of showing that the twelve links are not some special, separate category of causation but simply this same ten-cause framework applied to the specific case of suffering. The cause of expression is simply the fact that ignorance, craving, and the rest can be discussed and taught at all only because they have been named; the cause of dependence is craving's own pull toward pleasant experience, the engine driving the entire cycle forward turn after turn; the cause of distant attraction is ignorance and karmic formations, in this very life, quietly planting seeds for a birth that may not ripen for many lifetimes yet; and the cause of supportive embrace is exactly the surrounding conditions that let ignorance keep its grip — poor company, exposure to wrong teaching, careless attention, and the accumulated momentum of past habituation, none of which is ignorance's own seed, yet all of which keep that seed alive and thriving.

He explains the cause of induced extension as the entire forward chain from ignorance through becoming, each link inducing the next in strict succession until a future birth, old age, and death finally result — and the cause of determinate distinction as the precise reason different beings end up in different realms despite sharing the same underlying twelve-link structure: the specific character of ignorance and the specific karmic seeds cultivated determine whether that chain culminates in a hell-rebirth, an animal rebirth, or rebirth as a human or god, exactly as a melon seed yields only a melon — the general mechanism of dependent origination is universal, but its specific fruit depends entirely on what was actually planted. This closes seven of the ten causes as applied to defiled phenomena; the remaining three follow next.

43. Milestone: Completing the Application to Suffering, and the Mirror-Image Application to Liberation

The final three causes close out the account of suffering — the same ten-cause structure yielding a hell-rebirth or a heavenly one purely according to what was specifically cultivated, all seven prior causes working jointly toward that same outcome, and whatever obstructs or fails to obstruct that entire process. The treatise then applies this exact same ten-fold structure to liberation itself, revealing a striking symmetry: whatever opposes suffering's own cycle turns out to be identical to what supports the path to freedom, and whatever supports suffering's cycle is identical to what opposes that same freedom.

Chinese Source

此雜染法相違因者，謂出世間種性具足，值佛出世演說正法，親近善士，聽聞正法，如理作意，法隨法行，及與一切菩提分法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What opposes these defiled phenomena is: possessing the complete lineage for transcending the world, encountering a buddha's own teaching, keeping company with genuine teachers, hearing the true teaching, attending to it correctly, practicing in accordance with it, and cultivating every factor of awakening.

Chinese Source

又於一切清淨品法及滅涅槃所有種種名想言說，即此望彼諸清淨法，為隨說因。……觀待諸行多過患故，樂求清淨、攝受清淨、成滿清淨，彼望於此為觀待因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding every pure phenomenon and cessation itself — nirvāṇa — every name and designation given to them serves, with respect to these pure phenomena, as the cause of expression. ... Depending on the many flaws inherent in conditioned existence, one comes to seek, embrace, and bring to fulfillment genuine purity — this, with respect to purity, is the cause of dependence.

Chinese Source

若雜染品諸相違因，當知即是清淨法因；若清淨品諸相違因，當知即是雜染法因。如是現有雜染十因、清淨十因，過去、未來曾當染淨皆亦如是。一切唯有如是十因，除此無有若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever opposes defiled phenomena should be understood as precisely the cause of pure phenomena; whatever opposes pure phenomena should be understood as precisely the cause of defiled phenomena. Just as there are, in the present, these ten causes of defilement and ten causes of purity, so too in the past and future. There are only ever these ten causes — nothing beyond them, and nothing more to add.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the defiled application by noting how precisely determinate distinction operates here: the identical twelve-link structure produces a hell-rebirth, an animal rebirth, or a human or heavenly one purely depending on the specific character of ignorance and karma actually cultivated, never mixing up which cause yields which realm.

He then walks through the parallel application to genuine purity, showing the same ten causes operating in reverse: expression is simply naming the path and nirvāṇa itself, making them speakable and teachable at all; dependence is disillusionment with conditioned existence's own many flaws, the very thing that turns a mind toward seeking liberation in the first place; distant attraction is a being's own lineage — whether hearer, solitary buddha, or bodhisattva — quietly carrying the seed of a liberation still to be reached; supportive embrace is keeping good company, hearing genuine teaching, and correct attention, the conditions that let those seeds actually mature; immediate arising and induced extension trace the direct unfolding from the factors of awakening all the way to either kind of nirvāṇa, with or without residual conditioning; determinate distinction is each lineage reaching precisely its own corresponding fruit — a hearer's own nirvāṇa, a solitary buddha's own, or a bodhisattva's own unsurpassed awakening — never crossing into another's; and shared activity is simply all these working together toward that same outcome.

He draws out the treatise's own striking closing symmetry as its real point: lacking a complete lineage, never encountering a buddha's teaching, keeping poor company, hearing wrong views, and careless attention — everything that opposes liberation — is exactly, point for point, what supports the defiled cycle continuing; and everything that supports liberation is exactly what opposes that same cycle. Cause and its opposite are never two separate lists but a single relationship viewed from two directions, and this same ten-cause structure, the treatise insists, accounts for literally every defiled or pure phenomenon that has ever existed, exists now, or ever will, with nothing left over and nothing further to add. This closes the treatise's entire account of the ten causes, completing "genuine cause and effect," the first of inner teaching's two aspects; the treatise now turns to genuine effect in its own right — the five kinds of fruit these causes actually produce.

46. The Five Kinds of Fruit Named — Karmic Ripening and Consistent Outflow Defined

Five kinds of fruit complete the treatise's account of genuine cause and effect: karmic ripening, an always morally neutral result — unwholesome action ripening into a lower rebirth, wholesome action into a favorable one — and consistent outflow, where a habituated tendency simply produces more of its own kind, illustrated through the classical teaching that each of the ten unwholesome deeds ripens into its own specific, matching consequence.

Chinese Source

謂略有五。一者、異熟果，二者、等流果，三者、離繫果，四者、士用果，五者、增上果。……諸不善法，於諸惡趣受異熟果；善有漏法，於諸善趣受異熟果；是名異熟果。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, five: karmic-ripening fruit, consistent-outflow fruit, liberation fruit, effort fruit, and dominant-condition fruit. ... Unwholesome phenomena ripen into the lower realms as karmic-ripening fruit; wholesome yet still contaminated phenomena ripen into the favorable realms as karmic-ripening fruit. This is called karmic-ripening fruit.

Chinese Source

習不善故，樂住不善，不善法增。修習善故，樂住善法，善法增長。或似先業，後果隨轉，是名等流果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through habituation to the unwholesome, one comes to dwell contentedly in the unwholesome, and unwholesome tendencies increase. Through cultivating the wholesome, one comes to dwell contentedly in wholesome practice, and wholesome tendencies grow. Or a later result may simply resemble the earlier action that produced it. This is called consistent-outflow fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses karmic-ripening fruit's own defining feature: cause may be wholesome or unwholesome, but the resulting fruit itself is always morally neutral — the fruit is simply a birth circumstance, neither virtuous nor unvirtuous in itself, "ripening" precisely in the sense of arising at a different time, in a different form, from a transformed condition than its original cause.

He illustrates consistent-outflow fruit through the treatise's own classical pairing of specific unwholesome deeds with their specific matching consequence in a later human life: killing tends to ripen into a shortened lifespan; stealing tends to ripen into poverty and scarcity; sexual misconduct tends to ripen into an unfaithful spouse; lying tends to ripen into being frequently slandered oneself; divisive speech tends to ripen into estrangement from friends and family; harsh speech tends to ripen into constantly hearing unwelcome words; idle chatter tends to ripen into speech nobody takes seriously; and habituated craving, hatred, or delusion each tend to ripen into an intensified version of that same affliction later on — like clearly produces like, a habituated tendency simply reinforcing more of its own kind rather than something categorically different. He notes the ten wholesome deeds produce the exact mirror-image results. This closes the first two kinds of fruit; liberation fruit, together with an important caution about mistaking deep stillness for genuine realization, follows next.

47. Milestone: Liberation Fruit, and a Caution Against Mistaking Deep Stillness for Genuine Realization

Liberation fruit belongs only to the noble eightfold path's own genuine extinguishing of affliction — never to an ordinary being's merely temporary suppression of affliction through worldly concentration alone, however deep and stable that stillness may feel, since suppressed affliction remains fully intact as a dormant seed, ready to resurface the moment conditions allow.

Chinese Source

八支聖道滅諸煩惱，名離繫果。若諸異生，以世俗道滅諸煩惱，不究竟故，非離繫果。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eightfold noble path extinguishes affliction — this is called liberation fruit. If an ordinary, unenlightened being suppresses affliction merely through worldly concentration, this is not ultimate, and therefore is not liberation fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi compares an ordinary being's meditative suppression of affliction to pressing a stone down on grass — the grass stops growing visibly while the stone remains, yet its roots are entirely intact underneath, ready to spring back the moment the stone is lifted; only genuine insight actually uproots the affliction rather than merely holding it down. He raises a caution Elder Miàojìng himself frequently emphasized: practitioners sometimes reach a state described in a classical phrase as "stillness that constantly illuminates, illumination that remains constantly still," and mistake this experience for having attained genuine wisdom, when for a beginning meditator it is, in fact, simply a description of clear, stable concentration — the mind's own basic cognitive clarity remaining present even while deeply calm, not yet the analytical insight that actually sees into phenomena's own true nature. He is careful to note the same phrase, at a fully realized buddha or bodhisattva's own level, genuinely does describe the perfect union of calm-abiding and insight together — the point is not that the phrase itself is wrong, but that a practitioner still early in training should not assume they have already reached what only a much deeper stage of the path actually delivers. This closes liberation fruit, with its important caution intact; effort fruit and dominant-condition fruit, the remaining two kinds, follow next.

48. Milestone: Effort Fruit and Dominant-Condition Fruit — From a Farmer's Harvest to the Twenty-Two Faculties

Effort fruit is whatever a person's own skilled labor directly produces — a farmer's harvest, a merchant's profit, a scholar's calculation; dominant-condition fruit is the broadest category of all, covering everything a supportive condition makes possible, illustrated through a complete classical account of twenty-two distinct faculties, from the six senses through the five feeling-faculties, the five spiritual faculties, and the three faculties unique to the supramundane path.

Chinese Source

諸有一類，於現法中，依止隨一工巧業處，起士夫用。所謂農作、商賈、事王、書畫、算數、占卜等事。由此成辦諸稼穡等財利等果，是名士用果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Certain people, in this very life, resting on some particular craft, exercise their own skilled effort — farming, trading, serving a ruler, writing or painting, calculation, or divination. Through this, they accomplish the fruit of a harvest, profit, or similar gain. This is called effort fruit.

Chinese Source

若眼識等，是眼根增上果；乃至意識等，是意根增上果。……二十二根，各各能起自增上果。當知一切名增上果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eye-consciousness and the rest are the dominant-condition fruit of the eye faculty and the corresponding faculties, all the way through mental consciousness as the dominant-condition fruit of the mental faculty. ... Each of the twenty-two faculties gives rise to its own dominant-condition fruit. It should be understood that all of this is called dominant-condition fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains effort fruit as covering any result genuinely produced through deliberate, skilled human effort — quite distinct from karmic ripening, since it depends directly on present skill and application rather than past karma alone, though the two often work together in practice.

He then walks through the treatise's own complete classification of twenty-two faculties, each supporting its own corresponding result: the six sense faculties (eye through mind) each support their corresponding consciousness's ability to grasp an object; the two gender faculties support the continuation of family lineage; the life faculty supports a being's continued activity and livelihood for as long as life lasts; the five feeling faculties — pleasure, pain, joy, distress, and equanimity — support both the experience of past karma's ripened fruit and the generation of fresh karma going forward, which is why a genuine practitioner works to move beyond ordinary craving for pleasant feeling rather than merely chasing it, since even refined meditative bliss still belongs to conditioned, ultimately unstable experience; the five spiritual faculties — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — support progress toward freedom from worldly desire; and the three supramundane faculties — the faculty of "about to know," the faculty of "having known," and the faculty of "complete knowledge" — correspond respectively to a practitioner just before, just at, and long after the moment of direct realization, supporting the complete and final freedom from desire that only the supramundane path delivers. This closes all five kinds of fruit; the treatise now turns to what a bodhisattva actually gains from understanding this entire structure of cause and effect.

49. Milestone: The Fruit of Practice — How Understanding Cause and Effect Itself Gradually Purifies and Strengthens

A bodhisattva who genuinely understands this entire structure of cause and effect, and diligently practices in accordance with it, gradually purifies and strengthens their own capacity to discern correctly between what is genuinely possible and what is not — one of the specific powers unique to a buddha — not all at once, but through sustained, patient cultivation exactly like everything else on the path.

Chinese Source

菩薩於是內明所顯正因果相如實知已，精勤修習，令處非處智力種性漸得清淨、漸得增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, having truly understood the genuine cause-and-effect revealed by inner teaching, diligently practice in accordance with it, gradually purifying and gradually strengthening the lineage of the power to discern what is genuinely possible from what is not.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the power to discern what is possible from what is not" as one of the ten powers already named among a buddha's one hundred and forty unique qualities earlier in this fascicle — the ability to know with complete certainty that a wholesome cause never yields an unwholesome fruit and an unwholesome cause never yields a wholesome one, cause and fruit always corresponding exactly, never arbitrarily or unfairly. He notes this power's own seed is simply a bodhisattva's own genuine lineage, and that sustained practice of the ten causes and five fruits just described is precisely what ripens that seed toward its own eventual completion at buddhahood — moving, ground by ground, from initial understanding all the way to the complete, unshakeable mastery only full awakening brings.

He draws out the treatise's own refreshingly honest framing: nothing here happens instantly, only "gradually purified" and "gradually strengthened," an accurate reflection of how any genuine spiritual capacity actually develops through sustained effort rather than sudden transformation — anyone who has genuinely practiced recognizes this same gradual quality in their own experience. This closes "revealing genuine cause and effect," the first of inner teaching's two aspects, in its entirety; the second aspect — that what has genuinely been done is never simply lost, and what has never been done is never simply granted — receives its own fuller treatment next.

50. Milestone: Why Karma Never Simply Vanishes, and Never Simply Appears From Nothing — Closing Inner Teaching's Full Account

The second aspect of inner teaching receives its own precise statement: an action genuinely performed, even across hundreds of eons, never loses its capacity to eventually bear fruit; and, just as firmly, no result of any kind — ripened or otherwise — ever arises for an action that was never actually performed, nor does performing a different action produce some other action's own specific result.

Chinese Source

謂諸有情自所作業，雖復作已經多百劫，與果功能終無失壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever action beings themselves genuinely perform, even after hundreds of eons have passed since it was done, its capacity to yield its own fruit is never ultimately lost or destroyed.

Chinese Source

亦無不作、或復異作，而有異熟、或異熟果。……菩薩於是內明所顯已作不失、未作不得相如實知已，精勤修習，令其自業智力種性漸得清淨、漸得增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nor is there any case of an action never performed, or of a different action altogether, producing that specific karmic-ripening result. ... Bodhisattvas, having truly understood this teaching that inner teaching reveals, diligently practice in accordance with it, gradually purifying and strengthening the lineage of the power to know their own and others' karma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this second aspect as inner teaching's own necessary safeguard against two opposite errors: believing genuine karma can simply be outlived or forgotten with enough time, and believing a result could ever arise without its own specific, matching cause ever having been performed, or that performing one action could substitute for another action's own distinct consequence. Together, both halves guarantee that the entire causal structure already detailed at such length — the ten causes, the five fruits, defiled and pure phenomena alike — remains coherent and reliable across any span of time whatsoever, with no possibility of karma quietly expiring on its own or fruit appearing from nowhere. He notes that genuinely understanding this, and practicing in light of it, is itself what gradually strengthens a bodhisattva's own capacity to accurately know their own and others' karma — another of the ten powers named earlier — completing, alongside the power to discern what is possible, the two specific fruits inner teaching's full understanding brings.

This closes inner teaching's entire, extensive account — both of its aspects now fully unpacked — completing the treatise's detailed exposition of what a bodhisattva should specifically seek within the first of the five fields of knowledge; the treatise now turns from what to seek toward how, precisely, that seeking should actually be carried out.

51. Milestone: How to Seek Opens — Walking Burning Iron Ground for a Single Verse

The treatise's own three-part question now turns from what a bodhisattva seeks to how a bodhisattva actually seeks it, opening with an intensity of devotion to the teaching illustrated through its own vivid image: a bodhisattva willing to cross ground of blazing, red-hot iron if that were truly the only way to hear even a single four-line verse of genuine teaching.

Chinese Source

菩薩云何求聞正法？……謂諸菩薩於善說法，應當安住猛利愛重，求聞正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do bodhisattvas seek to hear the true teaching? ... Bodhisattvas, toward a well-spoken teaching, should abide in intense, deeply felt love and respect as they seek to hear the true teaching.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩為欲聽聞一善說法，假使路由猛焰熾然大熱鐵地，無餘方便可得聞是善說法者，即便發起猛利愛重，歡喜而入，何況欲聞多善言義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, wishing to hear even a single well-spoken teaching, would — if the only path to it crossed ground of blazing, fiercely burning, red-hot iron, with no other means of reaching it — at once give rise to intense love and respect, and enter joyfully; how much more so, then, wishing to hear a teaching rich in extensive meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "one well-spoken teaching" as meaning even so small a portion as a single four-line verse, and recalls the classical account of Śākyamuni Buddha, in a past life, cutting flesh from his own body to feed a rākṣasa demon in exchange for hearing just one verse of genuine dharma — a reminder that before a buddha appears in the world, hearing true teaching at all is exceedingly rare and hard-won, and a seeker in such conditions would risk everything for even a fragment of it. He notes the image is deliberately extreme: not because a practitioner is likely to ever face burning iron ground in fact, but because measuring one's own willingness against so extreme a standard exposes how lukewarm ordinary interest in the teaching usually is by comparison. This closes the opening standard for seeking the teaching; the treatise next measures that same devotion directly against a bodhisattva's love for their own body and sustenance.

52. Comparing Love of the Teaching Against Everything Else — Five Classical Measures

A bodhisattva's love for their own body and for all the food, clothing, and requisites that sustain it, however genuine, does not come within one part in a hundred — nor a thousand, nor any countable fraction at all — of a bodhisattva's love for hearing the true teaching, a comparison the treatise's tradition elsewhere unpacks through five distinct classical modes of showing one thing surpasses another.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩於自身分，及於一切資身眾具飲食等事所有愛重，於欲聽聞諸善說法所有愛重；以前愛重方後愛重，於百分中不及其一，於千分中亦不及一，於數分中亦不及一，於算分中亦不及一，乃至鄔波尼煞曇分亦不及一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas' love and regard for their own body, and for all the food, clothing, and requisites that sustain the body, compared against their love and regard for wishing to hear well-spoken teachings — measuring the former love against the latter — does not come within one part in a hundred, nor one part in a thousand, nor one part in any countable number, nor one part in any calculable fraction, not even one part in an uncountably vast fraction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the Diamond Sutra's own five classical modes for showing one thing decisively surpasses another, applying each here to why the teaching outweighs bodily comfort: surpassing by sheer quantity (the numerical comparison the passage itself gives, one part in a hundred and beyond); surpassing by power (the noble fruits of the three vehicles are compared to a wish-granting jewel vast enough to fill an entire universe, a power no worldly good can match); surpassing by category (the teaching alone leads toward suchness itself, unpacked through five treasuries — the tathāgata, the true teaching, the dharma-body, the transcendent teaching, and the intrinsically pure mind); surpassing by correspondence (only genuine dharma-practice, not worldly generosity alone, actually corresponds to and produces unsurpassed awakening); and surpassing by simile (the ultimate truth the teaching leads to is so far beyond ordinary experience that no worldly comparison can even adequately capture it). Taken together, he notes, these five measures are why a bodhisattva devoted to the Great Vehicle regards hearing genuine teaching as outweighing every worldly concern, bodily comfort included. This closes the comparison with other things, and with it the treatise's account of the proper attitude toward well-spoken teaching itself; attention now turns to the proper attitude toward the teacher who speaks it.

53. Milestone: Delighting in the Teacher — Never Tired, Never Satisfied, Never Seeking Gain

A bodhisattva's regard for the one who teaches takes two forms: an inexhaustible delight in listening that never tires and is never satisfied, however much has already been heard, and a cluster of specific inner qualities — pure and deep faith, a gentle nature, a mind and outlook that run straight toward the teaching itself, respect without any argumentative or arrogant edge, and a purpose limited strictly to establishing roots of goodness in oneself and others, never to personal gain or being admired.

Chinese Source

菩薩如是於善說法深生敬重，常樂聽聞諸善說法，無有勞倦，亦無厭足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, having in this way given rise to deep reverence for well-spoken teaching, constantly delight in hearing well-spoken teachings, without ever growing weary, and without ever being satisfied.

Chinese Source

淨信淳厚，其性柔和，心直見直。愛敬德故、愛敬法故，往法師所無難詰心，有敬重心，無高慢心。專為求善，非顯己德。為欲安立自他善根，不為利養恭敬因緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pure faith, deep and unwavering, a nature that is gentle, a mind and outlook that run straight toward the truth. Because they love and respect the teacher's virtue, and love and respect the teaching itself, they approach the teacher without any argumentative intent, holding a respectful mind, free of any arrogant mind. Their purpose is solely to seek what is good, never to display their own virtue. They act only to establish roots of goodness in themselves and others, never for the sake of gain or being honored.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts the passage's "pure faith" with a merely emotional attachment to a teacher — the kind of closeness some lay students cultivate hoping a well-liked teacher will later perform funeral rites for their family — noting genuine pure faith is instead rooted entirely in the teaching's own power to actually transform body, speech, and mind from ordinary habitual patterns toward genuine sanctity; "deep and unwavering" describes a devotion sturdy enough not to be blown over the way a young sapling bends in a storm, while an old, established tree stands firm.

He explains "a mind and outlook that run straight toward the truth" as meaning both the mind itself and the discernment guiding it point directly at ultimate truth and the teaching, with nothing else mixed in; and reads "no argumentative mind" not as forbidding honest questions born of genuine doubt, but as ruling out approaching a teacher simply to test or outmatch them. He stresses that regarding the teacher without arrogance applies regardless of whether that teacher's own realization happens to be more advanced, equal to, or even less advanced than one's own — respect is owed to the teaching being transmitted, not merely to personal seniority. This closes the treatise's account of the proper attitude toward both the teaching and the teacher; the treatise now turns from approaching the teacher to the actual manner of hearing the teaching once there.

54. Milestone: Hearing Without a Defiled Mind — Eleven Marks Across Three Defilements

Having approached the teacher with the qualities just described, a bodhisattva listens to the true teaching free of a defiled mind and free of a scattered mind; freedom from a defiled mind alone unfolds into eleven distinct marks, distributed across three specific defilements to be avoided — pride, contempt, and a cowardly sense of one's own inadequacy — each addressed through its own set of concrete listening behaviors.

Chinese Source

謂聽法時，其心遠離貢高雜染，其心遠離輕慢雜染，其心遠離怯弱雜染。由六種相，其心遠離貢高雜染；由四種相，其心遠離輕慢雜染；由一種相，其心遠離怯弱雜染。

Ru-Yi Translation

When hearing the teaching, the mind should be free of the defilement of pride, free of the defilement of contempt, and free of the defilement of cowardly inadequacy. Through six marks, the mind is freed of the defilement of pride; through four marks, the mind is freed of the defilement of contempt; through one mark, the mind is freed of the defilement of cowardly inadequacy.

Chinese Source

謂聽法時，應時而聽、殷重而聽、恭敬而聽、不為損害、不為隨順、不求過失。……又聽法時，恭敬正法、恭敬說法補特伽羅、不輕正法、不輕說法補特伽羅。……又聽法時，不自輕懱。

Ru-Yi Translation

When hearing the teaching: listening at the fitting time, listening earnestly, listening respectfully, not obstructing the teacher, not merely going along without asking, and not seeking out the teacher's faults. ... And when hearing the teaching: respecting the true teaching, respecting the person who teaches it, not belittling the true teaching, and not belittling the person who teaches it. ... And when hearing the teaching: not belittling oneself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all eleven marks concretely. Against pride: listening at the fitting time means taking a humble seat lower than the teacher's own, proper posture rather than slouching or dozing off; listening earnestly and respectfully means engaging the teaching the way one drinks water when genuinely thirsty, mind fully absorbed in its meaning; not obstructing means never distracting the teacher through fidgeting, gestures, or interruption; not merely going along means genuinely raising a question when something remains unclear rather than nodding along in silence; and not seeking faults means never mocking or blaming the teacher if an answer still leaves some doubt unresolved.

Against contempt: respecting the teaching and the teacher applies regardless of whether the teacher's own attainment happens to be greater, equal, or lesser than one's own, since respect is ultimately owed to the teaching itself; not belittling the teaching means never dismissing scripture's plain, unadorned language as crude compared to worldly literary artistry; not belittling the teacher means never weighing a teacher's family background, wealth, or social standing against one's own. Against cowardly inadequacy: never insisting one lacks the capacity to understand the teaching, since every being with a mind carries the same inherent potential for realization, and what feels incomprehensible today becomes clear with sufficient patient exposure. He notes all eleven marks reduce, in the end, to a single underlying posture — approaching the teaching, and the person who transmits it, without either inflating or deflating oneself. This closes hearing the teaching without a defiled mind in its entirety; hearing without a scattered mind, its own five marks, follows next.

55. Milestone: Gathering the Whole Mind, and Seeking to Hear the Teaching Concludes

The fifth and final mark of hearing without a scattered mind is gathering the whole mind entirely into listening, setting aside every other matter one might otherwise be occupied with, so that not one syllable, word, or phrase of the teaching passes by unnoticed — completing all five marks together, and with them the treatise's entire account of how a bodhisattva properly seeks to hear the true teaching.

Chinese Source

五者、攝一切心聽聞正法。……於一切心無不繫念者，為欲無間領解音韻文句義故，無不了知、無不通達而空過者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, hearing the true teaching with the whole mind gathered in. ... Holding the mind entirely in mindfulness at every moment is for the sake of understanding, without gap, the sound, wording, and phrasing and their meaning — so that nothing goes unknown, nothing goes unpenetrated, and the time spent passes not in vain.

Chinese Source

菩薩如是求聞正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas in this way seek to hear the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties the fifth mark back to the other four: seeking-understanding supplies the motivation, single-pointedness fixes the mind on the teaching as its object, attentive listening sharpens the ear itself to catch every nuance of tone, cleansing the mind clears out the inner obstruction that would otherwise block insight into the deeper meaning — and gathering the whole mind is simply all four working together without interruption, so that hearing the teaching is never merely half-present while some other concern quietly runs alongside it. He notes this single closing line — "bodhisattvas in this way seek to hear the true teaching" — draws together everything from the opening question of how to seek: the burning-iron-ground devotion, the comparison against bodily comfort, the proper attitude toward the teacher, and both the eleven marks free of a defiled mind and the five marks free of a scattered mind, into one coherent picture of genuine seeking. This closes "how to seek" in its entirety, the second of the treatise's three-part question; "why seek" — the specific purpose behind pursuing each of the five fields of knowledge — follows next.

56. Milestone: Why Seek the Teaching? Purposes Named for Inner Teaching and for Logic

The treatise's third question — why a bodhisattva seeks the teaching at all — receives its own field-by-field answer, opening with inner teaching, sought so as to genuinely practice in accordance with the dharma and to explain it broadly enough to benefit and awaken others, and with logic, sought to see clearly through wrong reasoning, to subdue rival doctrines, and to deepen faith wherever it is weak or already present.

Chinese Source

菩薩何故求聞正法？謂諸菩薩求內明時，為正修行法隨法行，為廣開示利悟於他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why do bodhisattvas seek to hear the true teaching? Bodhisattvas, in seeking inner teaching, do so in order to practice correctly in accordance with the dharma, and in order to broadly explain it so as to benefit and awaken others.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩求因明時，為欲如實了知外道所造因論是惡言說；為欲降伏他諸異論；為欲於此真實聖教，未淨信者令其淨信，已淨信者倍令增廣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in seeking logic, do so in order to truly recognize that the causal theories devised by other schools are flawed reasoning; in order to subdue various rival doctrines; and in order that, toward this genuine noble teaching, those without pure faith may come to have it, and those who already have pure faith may have it doubled and expanded.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies three classical readings of "practicing in accordance with the dharma" across different Abhidharma traditions — nirvana as the dharma reached through the eightfold path's practice, the scriptures themselves practiced according to what is heard, or the noble path practiced in accordance with itself — noting all three converge, at the Great Vehicle level, on genuine practice aimed at unsurpassed awakening, paired here with the equally essential outward-facing purpose of explaining the teaching so others can awaken too.

He explains logic's threefold purpose as building outward from clear self-understanding: first recognizing precisely where rival schools' causal reasoning goes wrong — such as claiming events happen without any cause at all, or from an unequal, mismatched cause — then using that clarity to actually subdue those rival positions in debate, and finally, through establishing one's own position correctly, both winning over those without faith and strengthening the faith of those who already have some, since refuting others convincingly is itself what firmly establishes one's own view. This closes inner teaching's and logic's purposes; grammar, medicine, and the practical arts follow next.

57. Why Seek Grammar, Medicine, and the Practical Arts

Grammar is sought so that beings who trust only refined, well-attested language come to feel genuine confidence in a bodhisattva's own words, and so that subtle differences of wording and usage across contexts can be rightly understood; medicine is sought simply to relieve beings of their illnesses and benefit the wider community; and the practical arts and crafts are sought so that a bodhisattva can gather wealth with little effort in order to give it away, inspire admiration that opens beings toward the teaching, and distribute resources wisely and impartially to however many beings ask.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩求聲明時，為令信樂典語眾生，於菩薩身深生敬信。為欲悟入詁訓言音文句差別，於一義中種種品類殊音隨說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in seeking grammar, do so so that beings who trust and delight in refined, well-attested language give rise to deep respect and confidence toward the bodhisattva; and so as to enter into the distinctions of interpreted wording, sound, and phrasing, and to freely express a single meaning through its many varied linguistic forms.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩求醫明時，為息眾生種種疾病，為欲饒益一切大眾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in seeking medicine, do so in order to bring an end to beings' various illnesses, and in order to benefit the whole community.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩求諸世間工業智處，為少功力多集珍財，為欲利益諸眾生故，為發眾生甚希奇想，為以巧智平等分布，饒益攝受無量眾生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, in seeking worldly practical wisdom and craft, do so in order to gather precious wealth with little effort, in order to benefit beings, in order to give rise to a sense of great wonder in beings, and in order to distribute wisely and impartially through skillful means, benefiting and gathering in countless beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes grammar's practical value in teaching — a bodhisattva able to speak with genuine literary skill, drawing on well-known references and idiom, naturally earns a hearing that plain or careless speech would not, and being able to render one and the same meaning in different registers of language lets a bodhisattva meet audiences of very different backgrounds each on their own terms, quite literally being able to speak a foreign visitor's own language or a local community's own idiom when the occasion calls for it. Medicine's purpose he treats as needing no elaboration at all — simply and directly relieving suffering bodies benefits everyone regardless of their relationship to the teaching itself.

For the practical arts, he draws on the treatise's own earlier chapter on generosity, noting that a bodhisattva without anything currently on hand to give can draw on previously mastered worldly skills — a trade, a craft, even simple ingenuity — to generate wealth quickly precisely so it can be given away, and that visible skill of this kind naturally inspires the wonder that opens people toward taking the teaching seriously in the first place; the same chapter's own image of a bodhisattva distributing to a crowd of petitioners strictly by seniority of arrival rather than by judging who deserves it more, virtuous or not, illustrates the fair, unbiased distribution this purpose calls for. This closes the purposes behind all five fields of knowledge individually; a final concluding statement, tying the whole of "why to seek" back to unsurpassed awakening itself, follows next.

58. Milestone: Seeking the Teaching Concludes — What, How, and Why All Now Answered

A single closing statement ties all five fields of knowledge together — pursued so that the vast store of wisdom needed for unsurpassed, perfect awakening is swiftly brought to completion, since without training through all five in their proper order, the unobstructed knowledge of all things simply cannot be reached — closing not only the account of purpose but the treatise's entire three-part answer to what, how, and why a bodhisattva seeks the teaching, and turning immediately toward an equally pressing task: having sought the teaching, a bodhisattva must not keep it to themselves, but actually go on to teach it.

Chinese Source

菩薩求此一切五明，為令無上正等菩提大智資糧速得圓滿。非不於此一切明處次第修學，能得無障一切智智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas seek all five of these fields of knowledge so that the great provisions of wisdom for unsurpassed, perfect awakening may swiftly reach completion. It is not the case that, without training in due order through all these fields of knowledge, one can attain the unobstructed knowledge of all things.

Chinese Source

如是已說一切菩薩正所應求，如是而求，為此義求。

Ru-Yi Translation

This concludes explaining what all bodhisattvas genuinely should seek, how they should seek it, and for what purpose they seek it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties "unobstructed knowledge of all things" back to the treatise's own earlier account of buddhahood in this same fascicle — knowledge unobstructed across every realm of existence, every kind of phenomenon, every category, and every point in time — pointing out this is precisely why cultivating all five fields, not inner teaching alone, genuinely matters: a buddha's complete knowledge has to be complete in fact, not simply complete regarding one narrow domain while remaining ordinary everywhere else. He reads the closing summary line as the treatise's own explicit signpost that "what to seek," "how to seek," and "why to seek" have now all three been fully answered in turn.

He closes with the treatise's own observation that seeking the teaching is not something to be kept sealed away inside oneself — spreading the teaching is, in his words, simply "family business" for anyone who has genuinely taken it up, and the act of actually explaining it to others is itself what exposes the gaps in one's own understanding still left to fill, forcing further clarity that private study alone never quite demands. This closes "seeking the true teaching" in its entirety — all three of its own questions, and the second of the seven points comprising "genuine learning," now fully done; the treatise turns next to the third point: how a bodhisattva should properly teach the true teaching to others.

59. Milestone: A New Question Opens — How Should a Bodhisattva Teach?

Having sought the teaching, a bodhisattva must go on to actually teach it, and the treatise opens this new topic with its own tripartite question — what should be taught, how should it be taught, and for what purpose — quickly settling the first two by pointing back to everything already covered (the same five fields of knowledge sought are what gets taught, for the same purposes already given), before opening the substantial third question: the manner of teaching, unfolding into two aspects, accommodating speech and pure speech.

Chinese Source

菩薩為他說正法時，當何所說？云何而說？何義故說？謂諸菩薩正所應求，即是所說。……為此義求，即為此義而為他說。

Ru-Yi Translation

When bodhisattvas teach the true teaching to others, what should be taught? How should it be taught? For what purpose is it taught? Whatever bodhisattvas genuinely should seek is exactly what is taught. ... Whatever purpose the seeking is for is exactly the purpose for which it is taught to others.

Chinese Source

依二種相應為他說。一者、依隨順說應為他說；二者、依清淨說應為他說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Teaching others should rest on two kinds of manner. First, teaching others resting on accommodating speech; second, teaching others resting on pure speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's economy here — rather than repeating the entire five-fields content and its purposes a second time, it simply points back to what was already established at length, since a bodhisattva teaches exactly what was worth seeking, for exactly the reasons worth seeking it. He frames the newly opened question — the actual manner of teaching — as the treatise's genuinely original contribution in this section: accommodating speech concerns skillfully fitting the teaching to whoever is listening, while pure speech concerns the teacher's own inner motivation remaining untainted by any personal agenda. This closes the setup for the whole topic; accommodating speech's own fifteen specific marks follow next.

60. Milestone: Accommodating Speech Begins — Proper Time, Weighty Regard, Right Order, Unstinting Continuity

Accommodating speech opens with four marks: teaching only at a fitting time and with proper posture, out of reverence for how rare and precious genuine teaching is; treating the teaching itself as something so precious that hearers come to prize it rather than take it lightly; presenting it in careful, unbroken order rather than piecemeal; and never holding anything back the way a miserly teacher might keep a closed fist around some part of the teaching.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩應當安住如法威儀而為他說，非不安住如法威儀。不為無病處高座者而說正法，不為坐者立說正法，不應居後為前行者而說正法，不為覆頭而說正法。……何以故？諸佛菩薩敬重法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas should abide in proper, dharma-fitting posture when teaching others, never teaching without such proper posture. One does not teach true teaching to someone seated on a high seat while healthy oneself; one does not teach standing to someone seated; one does not teach from behind someone walking ahead; one does not teach to someone whose head remains covered. ... Why is this? Because buddhas and bodhisattvas revere the teaching itself.

Chinese Source

又於正法生尊重時，令他於法起極珍貴，恭敬聽聞而不輕毀。又為一切說一切法，無間而說。又於正法不生慳吝，不作師拳。

Ru-Yi Translation

And when giving rise to reverence for the true teaching, one causes others to regard the teaching as utterly precious, listening respectfully rather than treating it lightly. And one teaches the whole of the teaching to everyone, teaching without interruption. And, toward the true teaching, one gives rise to no stinginess, never acting like a teacher who keeps a closed fist.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists concrete instances of proper timing and posture drawn from the monastic code's own seventeen listening-related rules: a healthy teacher should not sit on a raised seat while an audience sits below out of mere convenience, one should not teach while standing to those already seated, nor address someone from behind as they walk ahead, nor teach to someone whose head remains covered — all instances of a deeper principle, that how one physically arranges a teaching moment itself expresses genuine reverence for how rare and hard-won the teaching actually is, as precious, he notes, as if the Buddha stood in front of you in person.

He explains "weighty regard" as making sure the teaching's own genuinely unprecedented, rarely-heard depths come through clearly enough that listeners feel its rarity rather than assuming they already know it; "right order" as teaching systematically without gaps, the treatise's own progression from simple to profound serving as its clearest example; and "unstinting continuity" as refusing to hold back any portion of the teaching the way a stingy master might withhold an essential trick of the trade from a student, since genuine teaching, unlike a worldly craft, only grows through being given away rather than protected. This closes the first four marks; according with the text's own order, and bringing genuine benefit and delight, follow next.

61. According with the Text, Bringing Real Benefit, and Making the Teaching Welcome

Five further marks: presenting the teaching in the very order its own wording follows rather than rearranging it arbitrarily; making sure what is taught genuinely brings hearers either a favorable rebirth or final liberation so real benefit produces real joy; showing the four modes of genuine reasoning clearly enough that listeners come to love and delight in the teaching; teaching in a way that leaves those who already have understanding feeling settled and content; and encouraging those who feel discouraged by how long and difficult the bodhisattva path can seem.

Chinese Source

又於正法，如其文句次第而標，如其文句次第而釋，如其次第分別其義。又若引攝義利法義，應標、應釋、應廣分別，非不引攝義利法義。

Ru-Yi Translation

And regarding the true teaching, one presents it following the order of its own wording, explains it following the order of its own wording, and distinguishes its meaning following that same order. And whatever teaching draws in genuine benefit should be presented, explained, and broadly distinguished — never teaching in a way that fails to draw in genuine benefit.

Chinese Source

又應示現所應示現。又應教導所應教導。又應讚勵所應讚勵。

Ru-Yi Translation

And one shows whatever should be shown. And one instructs whomever should be instructed. And one praises and encourages whomever should be praised and encouraged.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains bringing real benefit as distinguishing two kinds of genuine good a teaching can point toward — a favorable rebirth among humans and gods, reached through the five precepts and ten wholesome deeds, or final liberation itself, reached through the four noble truths, dependent origination, and the six perfections — matched to whatever a given listener actually needs, so that no teaching is ever given that fails to point toward one or the other. "Showing whatever should be shown" he explains through the treatise's own four modes of genuine reasoning — reasoning from observed dependence, from function, from what can be logically established, and from the plain nature of things — deployed according to which combination of faith and understanding a given listener already has, since some have faith without understanding, some understanding without faith, some neither, and each configuration calls for a different approach.

"Instructing whomever should be instructed" he reads as the settled contentment that follows once genuine understanding has actually landed — someone taught, say, the correct view of consciousness-only or dependent origination's own lack of inherent nature, who then practices correctly under that view, naturally feels a quiet satisfaction the treatise calls contentment. "Praising and encouraging" he illustrates through a bodhisattva discouraged by the sheer length of the path — three incalculable eons can feel unbearably distant — whom a teacher should actively encourage by pointing to how surpassingly worthwhile unsurpassed awakening actually is, and notes the same principle applies equally to a disciple on the lesser vehicle losing heart about renunciation, who likewise deserves genuine encouragement rather than being left to simply give up. This closes marks five through nine; never casting out a struggling companion, and speaking only by valid means of knowledge, follow next.

62. Milestone: Never Casting Out, Speaking Only by Valid Means, Meeting Every Assembly Where It Is — Fifteen Marks Complete

The final six marks close accommodating speech: praising and comforting those who genuinely practice, so that others who have actually erred are not simply cast out of the community without a path back; speaking only by way of the three valid means of knowledge — direct perception, inference, and reliable scriptural authority; teaching only what leads toward favorable rebirth, never toward the lower realms; teaching clearly and openly, never cryptically like a corrupted incantation; staying grounded in the four noble truths; and adapting the very content of what is taught to each of five distinct kinds of audience.

Chinese Source

又應慶慰所應慶慰。又依現、比、至教道理而說正法，非不依彼三量道理。又所宣說順往善趣。又所宣說無亂、易入，而不隱密。又所宣說應四聖諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

And one praises and comforts whomever should be praised and comforted. And one teaches the true teaching resting on direct perception, inference, and reliable scriptural authority, never teaching apart from these three valid means. And what is taught leads toward the favorable realms. And what is taught is free of confusion, easy to enter, and never hidden away. And what is taught accords with the four noble truths.

Chinese Source

又處一切眾說正法時，隨眾所應而為宣說。……菩薩依此十五種相，諸隨順說普為利他，應如是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

And when teaching the true teaching amid any assembly whatsoever, one teaches according to what that assembly itself calls for. ... Bodhisattvas, resting on these fifteen marks of accommodating speech, universally benefiting others, should teach in just this way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "never casting out" as directly protecting a struggling practice-companion: rather than letting a monastic community expel someone guilty of some fault, a bodhisattva actively praises and encourages those already practicing well as visible models, giving the faltering person a path back rather than simply losing them to expulsion. He explains the three valid means of knowledge as direct perception supplying vivid, concrete illustration, scriptural authority supplying the actual doctrinal claim being taught, and inference supplying the reasoning that connects the two — so that, for instance, dependent origination's lack of inherent nature can be both logically argued and concretely illustrated in the same teaching, making it far easier for a listener to actually grasp.

He contrasts clear, open teaching directly with certain corrupted non-Buddhist incantations of the era, deliberately fragmentary and obscure so as to keep their content hidden from outsiders — the true teaching, by contrast, should always be as plain and accessible as possible, since concealment serves no one's genuine awakening. On adapting to the assembly, he lists the treatise's own five audience types — householders, renunciants, the already faithful, those with wrong views, and those in between — each calling for a different emphasis: householders hearing praise for wholesome conduct and warnings against unwholesome conduct, renunciants hearing the three trainings of discipline, concentration, and wisdom directly, those with wrong views hearing patient, reasoned cause-and-effect rather than open condemnation, and the faithful or the middling hearing the full grandeur of the teaching itself to deepen or settle their confidence. This closes accommodating speech's entire set of fifteen marks; pure speech, unfolding into its own five marks beginning with impartial loving-kindness even toward one's own enemies, follows next.

63. Milestone: Pure Speech Concludes — No Self-Praise, No Hidden Motive, and Teaching Itself Concludes

Pure speech's final two marks strike directly at a teacher's own inner motives: never praising oneself or disparaging others out of jealousy toward a rival teacher's success, and never teaching in hope of gain, honor, or praise at all — closing all twenty marks of proper teaching together, accommodating and pure speech alike, and with them the treatise's entire account of how a bodhisattva should teach the true teaching.

Chinese Source

不以嫉纏增上力故，自讚毀他。……以無染心，不希利益恭敬讚頌，為他說法。……菩薩依止此五種相，諸清淨說普為利他，應如是說。

Ru-Yi Translation

One does not, under the sway of jealousy, praise oneself while disparaging others. ... With a mind free of any taint, not hoping for gain, honor, or praise, one teaches others. ... Bodhisattvas, resting on these five marks, universally benefiting others, should teach in just this way.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩說正法相略有二十。……菩薩如是應常為他宣說正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this way, the marks of a bodhisattva teaching the true teaching are, in brief, twenty in number. ... Bodhisattvas should, in just this way, constantly teach the true teaching to others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the two closing marks of pure speech as guarding against the two most tempting corruptions of teaching itself: elevating oneself by claiming sole, superior understanding while quietly running down other teachers' credibility, and teaching in the hope of donations, reputation, or admiration rather than purely for listeners' own benefit. He notes the treatise itself then simply lists all twenty marks together as a single summary — the fifteen of accommodating speech and the five of pure speech — without adding new content, confirming nothing further needs to be said once both aspects have been genuinely absorbed.

This closes "how a bodhisattva should teach the true teaching" in its entirety — the third of the seven points comprising genuine learning, following intense conviction and seeking the teaching. The treatise turns next to the fourth point: practicing in actual accordance with the dharma one has sought and taught, rather than simply understanding or explaining it.

64. Milestone: Practicing in Accordance With the Dharma Opens — Five Aspects, and What to Avoid, What to Do

A new topic opens: actually practicing in accordance with the Great Vehicle teaching one has sought and taught, unfolding into five aspects — practicing what was genuinely sought, practicing what was genuinely received, and keeping the deeds of body, speech, and mind free of error — the first of which turns immediately to a bodhisattva's own precepts, which prohibit certain deeds outright while, for a bodhisattva alone, permitting specific exceptions when genuinely acting for others' benefit.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩法隨法行？當知此行略有五種。謂如所求、如所受法，身語意業無倒隨轉，正思、正修。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's practicing in accordance with the dharma? This practice should be understood as, in brief, five kinds: acting in accordance with what was sought, acting in accordance with the dharma received, the deeds of body, speech, and mind proceeding without error, correct thinking, and correct cultivation.

Chinese Source

若佛世尊於彼諸法，制身語意令不造作。於此諸法，開身語意令其造作。即於如是二種法中，身語意業無倒遠離、無倒修證。是名菩薩於諸法中，身語意業無倒隨轉法隨法行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever deeds the World-Honored Buddha has prohibited of body, speech, and mind, one does not perform; whatever deeds the Buddha has, for these same matters, opened as permitted, one does perform. Precisely within these two categories, keeping the deeds of body, speech, and mind free of error — rightly abstaining from the one, rightly cultivating and realizing the other — this is called a bodhisattva's deeds of body, speech, and mind proceeding without error in practicing in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts two classical readings of "practicing in accordance with the dharma" from within the Yogācāra commentarial tradition itself — one reading "the dharma" as nirvana realized and "in accordance" as the eightfold path leading there, the other reading "the dharma" simply as the scriptures themselves and "in accordance" as practicing exactly what they teach — and follows the second, more straightforward reading here.

He explains the bodhisattva precepts' own distinctive structure: certain deeds are flatly prohibited for everyone, yet a bodhisattva genuinely acting to benefit another being may, in specific circumstances the precepts themselves define, be permitted an action otherwise forbidden — the point never being personal license, but that genuine compassion sometimes calls for judgment precisely where the ordinary rule alone would fail someone in need. This closes body-speech-mind's basic without-error structure; the specific ten supporting practices this without-error conduct rests on — the classical ten perceptions cultivated to steady body, speech, and mind toward liberation — follow next.

65. Milestone: The Ten Perceptions That Steady Body, Speech, and Mind Toward Liberation

A classical list of ten specific perceptions, drawn from elsewhere in the treatise's own account of the three trainings, each cultivated as a direct antidote to a particular obstacle blocking genuine practice — from perceiving the body's own impurity to counter desire, through perceiving impermanence, suffering, and selflessness to counter attachment and complacency, to perceiving death itself to counter the anxious clinging to survival that otherwise crowds out wholehearted practice.

Chinese Source

一、不淨想；二、無常想；三、無常苦想；四、苦無我想；五、厭逆食想；六、一切世間不可樂想；七、光明想；八、離欲想；九、滅想；十、死想。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the perception of impurity; second, the perception of impermanence; third, the perception of impermanence as suffering; fourth, the perception of suffering as selflessness; fifth, the perception of disgust toward food; sixth, the perception that nothing in any world is truly enjoyable; seventh, the perception of light; eighth, the perception of freedom from desire; ninth, the perception of cessation; tenth, the perception of death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each perception's specific antidote. The perception of impurity — observing the body as composed of thirty-six unclean substances within and nine constantly leaking openings without, arising from the causes of past karma and afflicted craving and the condition of one's parents' own impure union — counters craving for youthful, attractive bodily form. Impermanence counters clinging to the five aggregates as a fixed self; impermanence-as-suffering counters laziness, since only sustained practice of discipline, concentration, and wisdom can actually end the aging, sickness, hunger, and countless other sufferings that impermanence guarantees; suffering-as-selflessness counters the deeply rooted view that a fixed self stands behind and controls one's own experience. Disgust toward food counters craving for fine flavors, since all nourishment ultimately arises from impure causes; the perception that nothing in the world is truly enjoyable counters attachment to worldly wealth and status, since even the finest worldly circumstances hold no genuine happiness for one who has actually seen clearly.

Light-perception — contemplating the light of sun and moon, the natural radiance of heavenly beings, or the light of the teaching itself — counters eleven specific faults that otherwise creep into meditative reflection; freedom-from-desire counters the subtle craving for the bliss of deep meditative absorption itself, since even the finest concentration remains conditioned and still belongs to samsara unless genuine freedom from desire — not merely from the desire realm, but eventually from the finer desires of the form and formless realms too — is also cultivated; cessation, taking nirvana's own signless quality as its object, counters an inability to stay settled and undistracted while pursuing signless concentration; and the perception of death — constantly reflecting that whoever is born must inevitably die — counters the anxious grasping at survival that otherwise crowds out wholehearted devotion to wholesome practice, since genuine readiness for death is what frees a practitioner to actually live, and practice, without holding back. He notes these ten together support the three trainings from every angle, which is why they belong here as part of a bodhisattva's genuine practice in accordance with the dharma. This closes acting without error in body, speech, and mind; correct thinking about the dharma, unfolding into its own eight marks, follows next.
