Fascicle 77 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十七 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Maitreya Asks: What Must a Bodhisattva Rely On to Practice Calm-Abiding and Insight?

This is the sixth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the four topics that make up the "object" of a bodhisattva's practice now closed — the ultimate truth, mind and consciousness, the three natures, and the three kinds of lacking a nature of one's own — the Chapter on Excellent Qualities' citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra turns to its fifth topic, on the "practice" itself: the Chapter Distinguishing Yoga, devoted entirely to calm-abiding (śamatha) and insight (vipaśyanā). It opens with the bodhisattva Maitreya asking the Buddha what a bodhisattva must rely on and abide in to cultivate calm-abiding and insight within the Mahāyāna, and the Buddha's answer: the provisional teachings of the Dharma, and an unrelinquished vow for unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 77 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Chapter on Excellent Qualities Turns to Practice — Maitreya's Question on Calm-Abiding and Insight

Further, relying on the provisional teachings of the Dharma, distinguishing and explaining in full the path of calm-abiding and insight that yoga comprises, it should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra describes: the bodhisattva Maitreya said to the Buddha: World-Honored One! What must a bodhisattva rely on, and what must he abide in, to cultivate calm-abiding and insight within the Mahāyāna? Having said this, the Buddha said to the bodhisattva Maitreya: Good sons! You should know that a bodhisattva relies on and abides in the provisional teachings of the Dharma, and in a vow for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening that is never relinquished, in order to cultivate calm-abiding and insight within the Mahāyāna.

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中菩薩地之六。復次，依法假安立分別解說瑜伽所攝奢摩他、毗鉢舍那道，當知如解深密經中，慈氏菩薩白佛言：世尊！菩薩何依、何住，於大乘中修奢摩他、毗鉢舍那？佛告慈氏菩薩曰：善男子！當知菩薩法假安立，及不捨無上正等覺願為依、為住，於大乘中修奢摩他、毗鉢舍那。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens this fascicle by placing it within the Chapter on Excellent Qualities' full sevenfold citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, which he divides into four topics of "object" and three of "practice and result." The first four — the characteristic of the ultimate truth, the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness, the characteristic of all dharmas, and the characteristic of dharmas' lacking any nature of their own — occupied the previous two fascicles in full, laying out what a bodhisattva must come to know. This fascicle opens the fifth topic, the Chapter Distinguishing Yoga, which turns from what is known to how it is practiced: through calm-abiding and insight, the two wings of all Buddhist meditation. He explains "yoga" here in its full technical range, glossing four (and, by one later commentator's count, five) senses the term carries across the treatise and its commentaries — yoga of the object, meaning any object of contemplation approached without the distorting view of permanence, self, or purity and in accordance with the Buddha's own teaching; yoga of practice, meaning any cultivation, from the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment to the paired operation of calm-abiding and insight itself, that accords with reality, follows the teaching, and moves toward its proper fruit; yoga of result, meaning every fruit of the path, from a śrāvaka's four fruits to a bodhisattva's ten grounds, that likewise accords, follows, and fulfills its cause; and yoga of the sacred teaching itself, since the teaching that describes all of this is itself in accord with reality. He then turns to Maitreya, the questioner, explaining his name through three traditional accounts — that he took his mother's clan name, Maitrī, meaning loving-kindness; that loving-kindness samādhi was the very first meditative attainment he achieved, cultivating a heart of exceptional gentleness toward all beings from his earliest stages of practice; and that he vowed, on hearing a buddha named Maitreya teach him this same samādhi in a former life, to bear that name himself when he in turn attained buddhahood — and glosses "rely" as leaning on a cause or condition the way a drum's sound depends on both the drum and the hand that strikes it, and "abide" as the place or object one continues to dwell in and draw sustenance from once a practice has arisen, so that it may keep growing rather than degrade. He sketches calm-abiding (śamatha) as guarding the mind on a single object, free of scattering and agitation, ripening through nine progressive stages of mental abiding into a settled, luminous stillness sustained by the mental factor of concentration; and insight (vipaśyanā) as the searching, discerning examination of phenomena that this same stillness makes possible, sustained by the mental factor of wisdom, without which the subtle, deeply rooted afflictions could never actually be uprooted. Finally, he unpacks the seven marks of Mahāyāna "greatness" from an earlier fascicle that distinguish its calm-abiding and insight from the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha vehicles' own: the greatness of its teachings, spanning countless vast and inconceivable scriptures; the greatness of its resolve, aspiring to unsurpassed awakening for the sake of all beings rather than to personal liberation alone; the greatness of its resolute faith in the Mahāyāna scriptures themselves; the greatness of its heightened aspiration, found only from the first ground onward, that never wavers back toward a lesser vehicle regardless of hardship; the greatness of the provisions of merit and wisdom it must perfect together; the greatness of the time it takes, spanning three incalculable eons; and the greatness of its final realization, unsurpassed awakening and great nirvāṇa themselves. It is within this vastly larger scope — object, motivation, scripture, resolve, provisions, duration, and result all alike expanded — that Maitreya now asks the question that will occupy this entire chapter: what must a bodhisattva rely on and abide in to cultivate calm-abiding and insight within so great a vehicle? The Buddha's answer names two things at once: the provisional teachings of the Dharma as what is relied upon, since no genuine practice can depart from the Buddha's own scriptural guidance, and an unrelinquished vow for unsurpassed awakening as what is abided in, since it is exactly this vow, held without wavering, that keeps a bodhisattva's cultivation of calm-abiding and insight from ever collapsing back into a lesser vehicle's aim.

2. Section: Four Objects of Meditation — Which Belong to Calm-Abiding, Which to Insight

World-Honored One! As you have taught, there are four objects of meditative cultivation: first, the object that is an image accompanied by discernment; second, the object that is an image without discernment; third, the object that is the very limit of things; and fourth, the object that is the accomplishment of what is to be done. Among these four, how many are objects of calm-abiding? How many are objects of insight? How many are objects of both together? Good sons! One is the object of calm-abiding, namely the image without discernment; one is the object of insight, namely the image accompanied by discernment; and two are objects of both together, namely the very limit of things, and the accomplishment of what is to be done.

Chinese Source

世尊！如說四種所緣境事，一、有分別影像所緣境事，二、無分別影像所緣境事，三、事邊際所緣境事，四、所作成辦所緣境事。於此四中，幾是奢摩他所緣境事？幾是毗鉢舍那所緣境事？幾是俱所緣境事？善男子！一是奢摩他所緣境事，謂無分別影像；一是毗鉢舍那所緣境事，謂有分別影像；二是俱所緣境事，謂事邊際、所作成辦。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this exchange as the doctrinal core the entire Chapter Distinguishing Yoga builds on, and situates it within an earlier fascicle's larger map of four kinds of meditative objects: pervasive objects, which subsume every form of yogic practice and are exactly the four named here; objects for purifying conduct, five specific antidotes such as contemplating a corpse's impurity or loving-kindness for a hostile mind; objects of skill, five ways of analyzing experience by aggregates, elements, sense-fields, dependent origination, and the possible and impossible; and objects for purifying affliction, the objects of both mundane meditative absorption and the supramundane path itself. This chapter, he notes, concerns only the first, pervasive kind, because every other object a practitioner might take up still passes through these same four stages on the way to realization. He then works through what each one actually names. The image accompanied by discernment is insight's own object: a meditator who has properly learned some teaching — through direct study, a teacher's instruction, reading, or reflection — brings it to mind in meditative absorption, generating a mental image that counts as belonging to the same class as the original object contemplated; discerning wisdom then works on this image through four operations of increasing depth — judicious selection, its most thorough form, general investigation, and general, thorough examination — corresponding to grasping first the exhaustive particulars of a thing and then its true, underlying nature. The image without discernment is calm-abiding's own object: the very same counterpart image, now simply rested upon single-pointedly rather than analyzed, the mind drawn inward and settled through progressive stages until absorption is attained. The limit of things belongs to both together, since it names the twofold scope any object can be known under — its exhaustive particulars, meaning nothing remains of a thing once its constituent classes are accounted for, as there is no form outside the five aggregates and no aggregate outside its elements and sense-fields; and its suchness, meaning the reality established through four modes of reasoning — from relative dependence, from function, from what can be proven, and from the plain nature of things — corresponding, respectively, to conventional and ultimate truth. And the accomplishment of what is to be done also belongs to both together, since it names calm-abiding and insight's very fruit: joined and matured through repeated cultivation, they yield, in a practitioner who has passed beyond the image altogether, non-conceptual direct gnosis of the object itself — the same fruit the treatise elsewhere calls transformation of basis, unfolding by degrees from the provisional weakening of afflictive tendencies at the earliest stages of practice all the way to its full and final consummation at buddhahood. Master Cháng Bǎi closes by citing the Tang-dynasty master Chengguan's reading of these four through the lens of principle and phenomena interfused without obstruction: the image without discernment corresponds to principle, and the image with discernment to phenomena, practiced separately before the two are joined; the latter two objects then belong to non-conceptual wisdom itself, in which calm-abiding illuminates every phenomenon at once even as insight stills every principle at once — the paradox, as he puts it, of a wisdom that is stillness within seeing and seeing within stillness, indivisibly one.
