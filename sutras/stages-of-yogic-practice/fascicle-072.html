Fascicle 72 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十二 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Ten Kinds of Aspiration for Awakening

With the Śrāvaka Ground's own determination now closed, Maitreya opens the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, a nine-fascicle analysis drawing out what the Basic Section's own eighteen chapters on the bodhisattva path left implicit. It begins where that path itself begins, with how bodhisattva lineage is established at all, and then with ten kinds of aspiration for awakening arranged in five contrasting pairs, running from a merely conventional undertaking all the way to the aspiration whose fruit is buddhahood itself.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 72 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Opening the Bodhisattva Ground's Own Determination, and How Its Lineage Is Established

The determination of the Śrāvaka Ground and of the Solitary-Realizer Ground having now been given, the determination of the Bodhisattva Ground is taken up in turn, a treatment running across the next nine fascicles and drawing out the profound and implicit meanings the Basic Section's own account of the bodhisattva path did not make explicit. That account occupied sixteen fascicles and twenty-eight chapters across four yogic divisions: the first, which sustains the practice leading to unsurpassed awakening through lineage, through the aspiration for awakening, and through the factors of awakening; the second, which cultivates calming and insight in accordance with that same awakening; the third, which sets out the fruit, describing where a bodhisattva takes rebirth from the first ground through the tenth; and the fourth, which simply explains the order in which the chapters themselves were arranged and therefore receives no separate determination here, since apart from the first three divisions it has no independent content. The determination opens with lineage: just as the existence of śrāvaka lineage is established, so too, and in exactly the same way, should the establishment of bodhisattva lineage be understood. The Basic Section had already distinguished two kinds, the lineage that abides by nature, the uncontaminated cause present beginninglessly and carried forward of itself, and the lineage produced through cultivation, developed through the perfuming influence of wholesome practice undertaken later.

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中菩薩地之一。如是已說聲聞地等決擇。菩薩地決擇，我今當說。謂如成立聲聞種性，當知成立菩薩種性，亦復如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, part one. The determination of the Śrāvaka Ground and the rest having been given, I shall now set out the determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. Just as the establishment of śrāvaka lineage is set out, so too should the establishment of bodhisattva lineage be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the earlier determination of śrāvaka lineage did not restate what lineage is, since the Ground of Lineage had already explained that fully, but instead answered the sharp objection that some beings possess no lineage at all, setting out five rounds of question and answer to show that distinguishing five lineages among beings is entirely reasonable given the actual experience of teaching them; the same reasoning is simply carried over here.

2. Section: Ten Kinds of Aspiration, Arranged in Five Contrasting Pairs

The determination then turns to the aspiration for awakening, of which ten kinds are distinguished, spanning bodhisattvas from ordinary beings through noble ones: the aspiration undertaken conventionally and the aspiration that has obtained the nature of reality; the undetermined aspiration and the determined; the impure aspiration and the pure; the feeble aspiration and the vigorous; and the aspiration whose fruit is not yet accomplished and the aspiration whose fruit is accomplished. These fall naturally into five contrasting pairs: the first pair distinguished by the conventional against the ultimate, the second by whether the aspiration is settled, the third by whether it is purified, the fourth by whether it is strong or weak, and the fifth by whether its fruit has been reached, the aspiration whose fruit is not yet accomplished covering everyone from a bodhisattva still an ordinary being through a noble bodhisattva who has not yet become a buddha, and the aspiration whose fruit is accomplished belonging to a buddha alone.

Chinese Source

復次，有十發心。謂世俗受發心、得法性發心、不決定發心、決定發心、不清淨發心、清淨發心、羸劣發心、彊盛發心、未成果發心、已成果發心。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are ten kinds of aspiration for awakening: the aspiration undertaken conventionally, the aspiration that has obtained the nature of reality, the undetermined aspiration, the determined aspiration, the impure aspiration, the pure aspiration, the feeble aspiration, the vigorous aspiration, the aspiration whose fruit is not yet accomplished, and the aspiration whose fruit is accomplished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi points out that the tenth kind belongs to a buddha alone, so that the ninth covers the entire span of the path from a bodhisattva who is still an ordinary being right through to one on the very threshold of buddhahood, a reminder that these ten are not ten different beginnings but a single aspiration described at every stage of its maturing.

3. Section: The Aspiration Merely Undertaken, and the Aspiration That Has Touched Reality Itself

The first pair is then explained. The aspiration undertaken conventionally belongs to bodhisattvas who have not yet entered the bodhisattva's own entry into the certainty of perfection, that is, to bodhisattvas still ordinary beings, the aspiration for unsurpassed awakening generated when receiving the precepts before the Three Jewels. That entry into certainty names the moment a practitioner of any of the three vehicles crosses over from ordinary being to noble one, the path of seeing being where this most characteristically occurs. The aspiration that has obtained the nature of reality covers two kinds. The first belongs to bodhisattvas who have genuinely entered that same certainty, realizing the suchness that is empty of both a self in persons and a self in phenomena, and thus covers every bodhisattva from the first ground through the tenth. The second belongs to śrāvakas and solitary realizers of indeterminate lineage, from the first fruit through arhatship, who having realized the suchness disclosed by the emptiness of a personal self then turn from the lesser vehicle toward the greater and generate the aspiration for unsurpassed awakening. Such a turning is traditionally described as available at any of five fruits, and the Sūtra Unlocking the Mysteries observes that a śrāvaka who turns toward awakening may in another sense be called a bodhisattva, since having already been freed from the obstruction of affliction, such a person may, once awakened by the buddhas, come to be freed from the obstruction to knowledge as well.

Chinese Source

世俗受發心者，謂諸菩薩未入菩薩正性離生所有發心。得法性發心者，謂諸菩薩已入菩薩正性離生，及迴向菩提諸聲聞等所有發心。

Ru-Yi Translation

The aspiration undertaken conventionally is the aspiration of bodhisattvas who have not yet entered the bodhisattva's entry into the certainty of perfection. The aspiration that has obtained the nature of reality is the aspiration of bodhisattvas who have already entered that certainty, together with the aspiration of those śrāvakas and others who turn toward awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the schools differ sharply here: the Dharma-characteristics tradition holds that only those of indeterminate lineage can make this turn, and that it must happen before entering nirvana without remainder, while the nature tradition holds that even those of fixed lineage in the two vehicles may turn, including those who have already entered that nirvana, so that in the end all beings reach buddhahood. He also cites fascicle 80's own observation that some śrāvakas relinquish their earlier aspiration while still in training and others only upon reaching the stage beyond training.

4. Section: Nine Motives That Leave an Aspiration Impure

The second pair concerns whether the aspiration is settled. The undetermined aspiration belongs to one who is not of bodhisattva lineage at all, or who, though of that lineage, does not sustain the practice, finds the bodhisattva path too demanding, and falls away from the aspiration already generated; the determined aspiration is simply the reverse, belonging to one of bodhisattva lineage who does not relinquish the vow once made. The third pair concerns purity, and nine motives are listed that leave an aspiration impure: generating it merely because someone else does, without any genuine inward conviction that this path alone completes the practice; generating it under pressure or coercion, whether a ruler's decree or a teacher's insistence, without having weighed it carefully oneself; generating it out of fear of a ruler, having fled into the monastic community to escape imprisonment or execution; out of fear of bandits; out of fear of harassment by spirits; out of fear of one's own backsliding, so that the aspiration is forced rather than freely given; for the sake of making a living; for the sake of gain and honor; and out of sheer pretense, having no bodhisattva's heart at all yet posing as one who does. The pure aspiration is the reverse of all these: generated from one's own genuine confidence in the teaching and truly for the benefit of beings, arrived at through careful inward discernment, whether moved by hearing the Dharma or, more remarkably still, moved by the actual practice of benefiting others.

Chinese Source

不決定發心者，謂非彼種性，設彼種性復退還法所有發心。與此相違，當知名為決定發心。不清淨發心者，謂如有一，或隨他轉、或被陵逼不揆不量、或怖王難、或怖賊難、或怖鬼難、或怖退轉、或為活命、或為利養恭敬因緣、或復矯誑。如是等類而發心者，當知皆名不清淨發心。與此相違而發心者，名清淨發心。

Ru-Yi Translation

The undetermined aspiration belongs to one not of that lineage, or who, though of that lineage, falls away from the aspiration; its opposite is the determined aspiration. The impure aspiration is generated merely following another, or under coercion without weighing it, or from fear of a ruler, of bandits, of spirits, or of one's own backsliding, or for a living, or for gain and honor, or out of pretense. Generating it contrary to all these is the pure aspiration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi relays Kuiji's compact summary of what makes an aspiration impure, that it arises only from outward circumstance rather than from the person themselves, and adds his own observation that an aspiration awakened through actually working for others' benefit is rarer and more valuable than one awakened through hearing alone.

5. Section: Feeble and Vigorous, Unripened and Ripened, Closing the Ten Aspirations

The fourth pair concerns strength. The feeble aspiration belongs to a bodhisattva who, having already generated the aspiration, is so overwhelmed by the manifest activity of greed, anger, and delusion that wisdom is obscured, correct practice abandoned, and wrong conduct taken up instead; the vigorous aspiration is the reverse, belonging to one whose greed, anger, and delusion are slight, who sustains correct practice, and whose conviction regarding the bodhisattva path is settled and never turns back. The fifth pair concerns the fruit. The aspiration whose fruit is not yet accomplished spans everything from the ground of resolute conduct, covering the path of accumulation and the path of preparation, all the way through the tenth ground. And the aspiration whose fruit is accomplished belongs to the ground of the Tathāgata itself, as the World-Honored One declared: I have been liberated from what is difficult to practice; regarding every difficult practice I am perfectly and completely liberated; my own right vow is fulfilled, and I can bring others too to realize awakening. Commentators are careful to note that calling this an aspiration does not mean a buddha still seeks anything, only that the mind of the fruit itself has arisen, awakening having been realized. This closes the ten kinds of aspiration; as for which among them are defiled and which undefiled, the treatise notes that no further decisive analysis is given.

Chinese Source

羸劣發心者，謂如有一已發心菩薩，貪瞋癡纏所蔽伏故，捨於正行，處於邪行。與此相違，名彊盛發心。未成果發心者，謂從勝解行地乃至第十地所有發心。已成果發心者，謂如來地所有發心。如世尊言：我已解脫難行之行，我於一切難行之行極善解脫，自正願滿，亦令於他趣證菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

The feeble aspiration belongs to a bodhisattva who, having generated it, is so overwhelmed by greed, anger, and delusion that correct practice is abandoned for wrong conduct; its opposite is the vigorous aspiration. The aspiration whose fruit is not yet accomplished runs from the ground of resolute conduct through the tenth ground. The aspiration whose fruit is accomplished belongs to the ground of the Tathāgata, as the World-Honored One said: I have been liberated from what is difficult to practice, my own right vow is fulfilled, and I bring others too to realize awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites Master Shéntài's careful gloss that a buddha's aspiration names the arising of the mind of the fruit rather than any remaining wish, and cross-references fascicle 45's own fivefold account of a bodhisattva's right vows, the vow of aspiration, of rebirth, of conduct, the right vow, and the great vow, closing the ten kinds of aspiration for awakening.

6. Section: Four Minds Guarding the Aspiration, and Nine Right Practices Benefiting Self and Other Alike

Four minds should be constantly maintained by a bodhisattva to guard the aspiration once generated. The mind formed through hearing and reflection, since only by continually hearing and reflecting on the essentials of the Mahāyāna scriptures, discipline, and treatises can the aspiration be kept from retreating. The mind of compassion, learning from the buddhas the great compassion that arises without conditions and regards all beings as one body, cultivating it constantly and regarding every being impartially. The mind of accumulating provisions, applying oneself diligently to the two accumulations, the six perfections, and the four means of gathering beings, treating every teaching and every being as an occasion for accumulating what the path requires. And the mind formed through cultivation, since a bodhisattva must complete their own virtue as well as benefit others, the Basic Section observing that one who has resources, knowledge, and vigor yet cannot rightly discern beings' capacities is not called flourishing on the path, and equally that one skilled in guiding others yet lacking any accomplishment of their own risks teaching wrongly, being unable to lead others where they have not gone themselves. The determination then turns to the chapter on benefiting self and other, listing nine right practices grounded in one's own benefit and another's alike: right practice amid birth and death; toward beings; toward one's own person; amid the objects of desire; in the actions of body, speech, and mind; in not harming beings; in cultivating wholesome qualities without interruption; in inward calming; and in the insight belonging to the higher wisdom.

Chinese Source

復次，有四種心，菩薩應當恆常隨護。一、聞思所成心，二、悲心，三、資糧心，四、修所成心。復次，菩薩有九正行，依於自義及與他義。一、於生死正行，二、於有情正行，三、於自體正行，四、於諸欲正行，五、於身語意業正行，六、於不應損惱有情正行，七、於無間修善法正行，八、於內心奢摩他正行，九、於增上慧法毗鉢舍那正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four minds a bodhisattva should constantly guard: the mind formed through hearing and reflection, the mind of compassion, the mind of accumulating provisions, and the mind formed through cultivation. A bodhisattva has nine right practices, grounded in one's own benefit and another's: right practice amid birth and death; toward beings; toward one's own person; amid the objects of desire; in the actions of body, speech, and mind; in not harming beings; in cultivating wholesome qualities without interruption; in inward calming; and in the insight belonging to the higher wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Basic Section's own tenfold analysis of benefiting self and other, sorted by whether the benefit is exclusive or shared, whether it brings understanding or ease, whether it belongs to the cause or the fruit, whether it operates in this life or a future one, and whether it carries all the way to buddhahood or not, as the fuller background against which these nine right practices should be read.

7. Section: A Patient Swallowing Bitter Medicine, a Physician, a Parent, a Merchant on the Road

Each right practice is then illustrated. Right practice amid birth and death is like a patient with an illness taking a bitter medicine: a bodhisattva regards themselves as genuinely sick, afflicted by clinging to a self in persons and in phenomena, and regards the difficult practices of the bodhisattva path as the bitter medicine that alone counters those two illnesses; the medicine is hard to swallow, but it treats the whole sickness of birth and death. Even a bodhisattva still an ordinary being, though possessed of much greed, anger, and delusion, subdues those very afflictions in the course of forgetting themselves while helping others, and a noble bodhisattva completes the provisions for unsurpassed awakening in exactly the same way, which is why a bodhisattva must understand that the path is undertaken for their own sake as much as for beings' sake, purifying oneself precisely through benefiting others. Right practice toward beings is like a good physician toward the sick: beings are what a bodhisattva exists to heal, and one gives food where the sickness is hunger and teaching where the sickness is in the mind, applying oneself fully to both bodily and mental ills. Right practice toward one's own person is like a skillful parent with a child not yet grown: a bodhisattva discerns for themselves what is right and what is not, resolves to abandon the wrong and follow the right, and supervises their own conduct exactly as a parent constantly watches over a child, correcting the thought as it arises. And right practice amid the objects of desire is like a merchant guarding goods along the road: passing through the perilous road of birth and death and meeting all manner of attractive forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and touches, a bodhisattva guards mindfulness as a caravan guards its cargo, protecting the seven noble riches, faith, precepts, conscience, shame, learning, generosity, and wisdom, rather than clinging to worldly wealth.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩於生死中行於正行？謂如病者於所有病、於辛苦藥。云何菩薩於諸有情行於正行？謂如良醫於有病者。云何菩薩於自體上行於正行？謂如善巧家長於未成就幼童。云何菩薩於諸欲中行於正行？謂如商主行於商路於諸財貨。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does a bodhisattva practice rightly amid birth and death? As a patient with an illness regards a bitter medicine. How toward beings? As a good physician toward the sick. How toward one's own person? As a skillful parent toward a child not yet grown. How amid the objects of desire? As a merchant travelling the trade road regards the goods.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the first simile's real point, that the bodhisattva path is not simply what beings need from a bodhisattva but what the bodhisattva needs for themselves, the medicine being bitter precisely because it treats an illness the practitioner genuinely has, so that self-benefit is completed in the very act of benefiting others rather than set aside for it.

8. Section: A Launderer at the Washing, and a Father Whose Infant Soils His Lap

Right practice in the actions of body, speech, and mind is like a launderer with clothing: just as such a person washes a garment clean or dyes it beautifully, a bodhisattva, while walking the path, continually cultivates precepts, concentration, and wisdom to counter the defilement in their own three kinds of action until all three are brought to completeness. And right practice in not harming beings is like a kind father whose infant soils his own lap: passing through the perilous road of birth and death and encountering beings who behave badly, a bodhisattva takes no account of it whatsoever, exactly as a father does not hold a baby's accident against the baby, treating every being as one's own small child and simply not storing up their wrongdoing in the heart.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩於身語意業行於正行？謂如浣染衣者於諸衣服。云何菩薩於不應損惱有情行於正行？謂如慈父於己膝上放失便利嬰孩小兒。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does a bodhisattva practice rightly in the actions of body, speech, and mind? As one who washes and dyes cloth regards the clothing. How in not harming beings? As a kind father regards the infant who has soiled his own lap.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the sixth simile with a modern account of the monk known as the Golden Mountain Living Buddha, who was doused with a bucket of filth by an army officer resentful of the reverence others showed him, and who neither grew angry nor complained; when a senior officer's wife, herself a disciple, saw what had happened and had the man arrested, it was the monk who prostrated himself to plead for his release, whereupon the officer wept, confessed his fault publicly, and took refuge in the Three Jewels. He puts the question to the reader directly: if someone emptied filth over your head, would you meet it as a parent meets an infant's accident, or would you hold it against them?

9. Section: Drilling for Fire, Entrusting Wealth to Someone Reliable, and a Magician Unfooled by His Own Illusions

Right practice in cultivating wholesome qualities without interruption is like someone seeking fire who works at the fire-drill: just as fire comes only from drilling the wood steadily and without stopping, a bodhisattva seeking unsurpassed awakening must cultivate hearing, reflection, and meditation, precepts, concentration, and wisdom, the six perfections and the four means of gathering beings diligently and without gaps, since only such sustained effort accomplishes awakening. Right practice in inward calming is like an owner entrusting wealth to someone genuinely reliable: just as property placed with a trustworthy custodian cannot be misappropriated, a bodhisattva walking the path needs the power of concentration, cultivating wisdom in dependence on it, so as not to be shaken by the objects of desire, by the eight worldly winds, or by any affliction; calming is what can be entrusted with one's wholesome qualities, and a bodhisattva who places them there remains unmoved and unattached whatever circumstances arise. And right practice in the insight belonging to the higher wisdom is like a skilled magician among his own illusions: just as such a person conjures elephants, horses, oxen, and sheep while never being deceived by any of them, a bodhisattva cultivates the insight of the higher wisdom, is thereby freed from clinging to a self in persons and in phenomena, and can read a listener's capacity and deploy every skillful means to teach beings while clinging to none of it, a bodhisattva accordingly cultivating the contemplations of the śrāvaka vehicle alongside those of the Mahāyāna in order to have the skill to teach beings of every kind. With this the nine right practices close.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩於無間修諸善法中行於正行？謂如求火者施功於燧。云何菩薩於內心奢摩他中行於正行？謂如其主於能致財可委付者。云何菩薩於增上慧法毗鉢舍那中行於正行？謂如善幻者於諸幻事。餘決擇文，更不復現。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does a bodhisattva practice rightly in cultivating wholesome qualities without interruption? As one seeking fire applies effort to the fire-drill. How in inward calming? As an owner regards someone reliable to whom wealth may be entrusted. How in the insight belonging to the higher wisdom? As a skilled magician regards his own illusions. No further text of decisive analysis appears.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the magician simile's double edge: the skill lies not only in producing the illusions but in never being taken in by them, so that a bodhisattva who deploys every kind of skillful means must be exactly the one least attached to any of them, closing the nine right practices.

10. Section: Five Kinds of Being Toward Whom Wrong Practice Means Compassion Has Failed

The counterpart is then set out: when a bodhisattva engages in wrong practice toward any of five kinds of being, this is described as being without pity, without compassion, and without any sorrow at their plight, and such a bodhisattva has in that moment gone against their own vow and lost compassion altogether. The five are those who ask something of one; those in danger or hardship; those who have shown one kindness; those who delight in worldly happiness; and those who delight in the Dharma. Those who ask are themselves of five kinds: asking for food and drink; for clothing; for shelter; for medicine and supplies on account of illness; and for rescue and protection. Should a bodhisattva fail to satisfy these requests and instead abuse the one asking, refusing to help, this is precisely wrong practice. Those in danger or hardship are likewise of five kinds: those living in genuine deprivation; those in confusion or disorder; those who come seeking refuge having nowhere else to turn; those who come entrusting some matter to one's care; and those who come seeking an audience in order to ask for help. Should a bodhisattva fail to relieve these and make no effort on their behalf, this too is wrong practice.

Chinese Source

復次，若於五種有情眾中起邪行時，說名無哀、無愍、無有傷歎。一、於乞求者，二、於危厄者，三、於有恩者，四、於樂樂者，五、於樂法者。言乞求者，略有五種。一、求飲食，二、求衣服，三、求房舍，四、求病緣醫藥資具，五、求救護。其危厄者，亦有五種。一、住艱乏者，二、住迷亂者，三、來歸依者，四、相投委者，五、來拜覲者。

Ru-Yi Translation

When one engages in wrong practice toward five kinds of being, this is called being without pity, without compassion, and without sorrow at their plight: those who ask something of one; those in danger or hardship; those who have shown kindness; those who delight in happiness; and those who delight in the Dharma. Those who ask are of five kinds: asking for food, clothing, shelter, medicine and supplies for illness, and rescue. Those in hardship are likewise of five: living in deprivation, in confusion, coming for refuge, entrusting a matter, and coming to seek an audience.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the force of the treatise's own wording here, that failing these beings is not merely a lapse in conduct but is named as compassion itself having failed, so that a bodhisattva who turns such a person away has, in that very moment, gone against the vow that made them a bodhisattva at all.

11. Section: Those Who Have Shown Kindness, and Those Who Simply Want to Be Happy

Those who have shown kindness are of five kinds: one's mother, whose kindness the scriptures call as deep as the ocean; one's father, whose kindness they call as high as a mountain king; one's spouse and children, who support a bodhisattva in walking the path; the servants and workers who labor on one's behalf; and one's friends, siblings, relatives, and officials. More broadly, every being whatsoever is one's benefactor, the Buddha having observed that across the long night of wandering it is hard to find any being who has not at some point been one's mother, father, brother, sister, teacher, or preceptor; and as the Sūtra on the Contemplation of the Mind-Ground puts it, since beings have circled through the five destinies over hundreds of thousands of eons and have been each other's parents across many lives, every man may be regarded as a kind father and every woman as a loving mother, that former kindness being no different from a present parent's, yet still unrepaid, and sometimes even turned into enmity through delusion, since ignorance covers the knowledge of former lives and one no longer recognizes who was once a parent. Those who delight in happiness are likewise of five kinds: delighting in the happiness of a flourishing livelihood; in that flourishing continuing without loss; in being spared the suffering that changing circumstances bring; in the happiness of relieving fatigue; and in the happiness of advancement and gain. Where such worldly flourishing would genuinely bring a being benefit and ease in this life and the next, a bodhisattva should satisfy their wish rather than frustrate them.

Chinese Source

其有恩者，亦有五種。一、母，二、父，三、妻子，四、奴婢僕使，五、朋友兄弟親屬宰官。其樂樂者，亦有五種。一、愛樂事業興盛樂，二、愛樂事業興盛不乖離樂，三、愛樂時節變異苦遠離樂，四、愛樂解疲倦樂，五、愛樂求昇進樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who have shown kindness are of five kinds: mother, father, spouse and children, servants and workers, and friends, siblings, relatives, and officials. Those who delight in happiness are likewise of five kinds: delighting in a flourishing livelihood; in that flourishing not being lost; in being spared the suffering brought by changing circumstances; in the relief of fatigue; and in advancement and gain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the widening of "those who have shown kindness" from one's immediate family to all beings as the passage's real weight, since a bodhisattva who sees present relationships as only the momentary shape of a much longer history has no principled ground for withholding help from anyone, every being having stood in a parent's place at some point along the way.

12. Section: Those Who Delight in the Dharma, and Four Shapes Wrong Practice Takes

Those who delight in the Dharma are likewise of five kinds: delighting in hearing the true teaching expounded; delighting in receiving, upholding, reading, and reciting the scriptures; delighting in discussion, analysis, and inquiry into their deeper meaning; delighting in instruction concerning concentration and wisdom and in admonition concerning the precepts; and delighting in practice in accordance with the Dharma, that is, in cultivating calming and insight and the eightfold path toward nirvana. Should a bodhisattva neither help nor rejoice in such a person, ignore them, fail to meet their wish, or worse, actively obstruct them, this is wrong practice. As to what wrong practice consists of across all five kinds of being, it may take four shapes: acting wrongly, as when someone comes asking and the bodhisattva not only refuses but abuses them; failing to act, as when someone is in hardship and the bodhisattva simply does not come to their relief; acting without benefit, as when someone has shown kindness and the bodhisattva neither acknowledges the debt nor treats them well; and acting with indifference, as when someone delights either in worldly happiness or in the Dharma and the bodhisattva remains unmoved, neither rejoicing with them nor benefiting them at all.

Chinese Source

其樂法者，亦有五種。一、樂說正法，二、樂受持讀誦，三、樂論議決擇，四、樂教授教誡，五、樂法隨法行。此中邪行者，謂於是中，或作加行故、或不作加行故、或不饒益加行故、或中庸加行故，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who delight in the Dharma are of five kinds: delighting in its being expounded; in receiving, upholding, reading, and reciting it; in discussion and analysis; in instruction and admonition; and in practice in accordance with the Dharma. Wrong practice here takes four shapes: acting wrongly; failing to act; acting without benefit; and acting with indifference. Its characteristics should be understood accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi observes that the fourth shape, mere indifference, is the easiest to overlook precisely because nothing overtly harmful is done, and directs readers to fascicle 79's treatment of the chapter on conduct, where the Ratnakūṭa's own sixteen contrasting marks of right and wrong practice are set out for fuller reflection.

13. Section: Five Ways a Being Can Be Beyond Even a Buddha's Reach

The determination then turns to who can and cannot be trained. Five kinds of incapacity are found among beings, and a being possessing them is difficult even for the buddhas to bring across, let alone bodhisattvas or anyone else; for though the buddhas wish to bring such a being genuine benefit and ease, that being cannot receive what is done for them, and further obstructs the very work undertaken on their behalf. The five are: incapacity for purity; incapacity for the preparatory practice; incapacity for accomplishing the fruit; incapacity for both practice and fruit together; and incapacity for receiving material benefit. Incapacity for purity belongs to one who by nature possesses no capacity for nirvana at all, lacking in the storehouse consciousness any pure seed for it, and therefore never manifesting purity. The Śrāvaka Ground describes such a being through six marks: an unshakeable clinging to self, so deeply perfumed across the realms and destinies that not even the buddhas can dislodge it; an inability to generate any wish for release, remaining wholly unmoved though the faults of birth and death are denounced and the virtues of nirvana praised in countless ways; a fully developed absence of conscience and shame, with no dread of wrongdoing and no belief in cause and effect, valuing only this life's advantage; an absence of wholesome roots, so that even the most beautiful and accessible teaching produces no confidence; going forth in appearance only, whether under compulsion or for a livelihood, and taking no delight in genuine training; and performing only such slight good as is done in hope of prosperity within existence, with no wish to leave birth and death at all.

Chinese Source

復次，於有情中，有五種不堪任性。若諸有情成就此者，諸佛如來尚難化度，況諸菩薩，或復餘者。何等為五？一、於清淨無堪任性，二、於加行無堪任性，三、於彼果成辦無堪任性，四、於加行及彼果成辦無堪任性，五、於攝受饒益無堪任性。於清淨無堪任性者，謂如有一，本性無有般涅槃法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among beings there are five kinds of incapacity; a being possessing them is difficult even for the buddhas to bring across, let alone bodhisattvas or anyone else. Which five? Incapacity for purity; for the preparatory practice; for accomplishing the fruit; for both together; and for receiving material benefit. Incapacity for purity belongs to one who by nature possesses no capacity for nirvana.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Bodhisattva Ground's own parallel statement that a being abiding in no lineage, lacking bodhisattva lineage, is definitively incapable of completing unsurpassed awakening even with an aspiration generated and practice undertaken as its basis, and notes that this is the tradition's hardest teaching about the limits of what compassion can accomplish where the capacity itself is absent.

14. Section: Capacity Ruined by Grave Wrongdoing, and Capacity Simply Not Yet Ripe

Incapacity for the preparatory practice belongs to one who does possess the capacity for nirvana and whose continuum has genuinely ripened, meaning that in this very life they are able to enter the certainty of perfection and realize the noble path, but who then immediately commits and accumulates karma that obstructs correct practice, such as any of the deeds of immediate retribution, and by that cause is left in this present life without the capacity to cultivate the right means. Should such obstruction be described more broadly, the Śrāvaka Ground sets out inner and outer hindrances to cultivating calming and insight: inwardly, hindrances to practice itself such as lacking requisites, overwhelming affliction, frequent illness, or disordered humors; hindrances to seclusion such as coarse food or delight in idle talk; and hindrances to inward stillness such as self-promotion or dwelling in unsuitable places; while outwardly, hindrances arise from beings, namely failing to seek out good friends and keeping company with bad ones, and from the setting itself, a place too noisy, too infested, or too dangerous to practice in. Incapacity for accomplishing the fruit belongs instead to one who possesses the capacity for nirvana and has committed no obstructing karma at all, but whose continuum has simply not yet ripened, so that although practice is undertaken, the fruit cannot yet be accomplished in this life; the Bodhisattva Ground describes the parallel case of a bodhisattva who meets a good friend, learns correctly, and applies vigorous effort, yet whose wholesome roots are not yet mature and whose provisions for awakening are not yet complete, not having accumulated the factors of awakening over a long enough span.

Chinese Source

於加行無堪任性者，謂如有一，同般涅槃法相續已熟，於此無間，造作積集能障正加行業。由此因緣，於現法中，無有堪能修正方便。於彼果成辦無堪任性者，謂如有一，同般涅槃法相續未熟，不作不集能障正加行業。由此因緣，無有功能成辦彼果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Incapacity for the preparatory practice belongs to one whose capacity for nirvana has ripened but who then immediately commits and accumulates karma obstructing correct practice, and by that cause lacks in this life the capacity to cultivate the right means. Incapacity for accomplishing the fruit belongs to one whose capacity for nirvana has not yet ripened and who commits no obstructing karma, and who by that cause lacks the power to accomplish the fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi points out how differently these two cases should be received: the first is a ripeness ruined by grave wrongdoing, while the second involves no fault at all, only a maturity not yet reached, the Śrāvaka Ground listing among its causes insufficient accumulation of the wholesome faculties, instruction that does not actually conduce to realization, and a practitioner's own still-weak concentration.

15. Section: Both Practice and Fruit Foreclosed, Poverty Fixed by Past Deeds, and the Capable by Contrast

Incapacity for both the preparatory practice and the accomplishment of its fruit belongs to one who possesses the capacity for nirvana but whose continuum has not yet ripened, and who then immediately commits and accumulates karma obstructing correct practice as well, so that by that cause both are foreclosed at once, neither the cultivation of calming and insight nor the realization of the noble path remaining possible. And incapacity for receiving material benefit belongs to one who has immediately committed and accumulated karma certain to produce the suffering of poverty and want, such as theft, so that in this present life, however hard they work, they cannot become wealthy or free from want or dwell at ease; and since such karma is fixed, not even the buddhas can alter its result. This last case applies to beings whether or not they possess lineage, since anyone who commits karma certain to produce poverty will undergo that result regardless. As for those who are capable of being trained, they should simply be understood as the reverse of these five: whoever is free of these five faults may indeed be taught and transformed, and these are the five kinds of being who possess genuine capacity. This closes the chapter on benefiting self and other.

Chinese Source

於加行及彼果成辦無堪任性者，謂如有一，同般涅槃法相續未熟，於此無間，造作積集能障正加行業。由此因緣，俱無堪能。於攝受饒益無堪任性者，謂如有一，於此無間，造作積集能感定受貧窮匱乏苦惱之業。由此因緣，於現法中，無有堪能令彼巨富無匱安樂。與此相違，當知五種有堪任性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Incapacity for both practice and fruit belongs to one whose capacity has not yet ripened and who then immediately commits karma obstructing correct practice, so that by that cause both are foreclosed. Incapacity for receiving material benefit belongs to one who has committed karma certain to produce the suffering of poverty and want, so that in this life they cannot be made wealthy, free from want, or at ease. The reverse of these should be understood as the five kinds of genuine capacity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the fifth incapacity concerns material circumstances rather than spiritual capacity, and applies equally to those with and without lineage, a reminder that fixed karma governs even the ordinary conditions under which practice must be undertaken, closing the discussion of who can and cannot be trained.

16. Section: Opening the Chapter on Ultimate Reality, and the Mahāyāna's Five Things

The determination then takes up the chapter on ultimate reality. Whoever wishes to understand what is ultimately real should first understand that everything may be gathered, in brief, into five things, this fivefold analysis serving here to explain the fourfold account of ultimate reality given in the Basic Section's own chapter. A summarizing verse gathers the whole treatment: raising them together, distinguishing them separately, whether existent, whether real, conventional or ultimate, what produces them, whether they differ, their characteristics, their modes of operation, their various names, and form and the rest. These name the eleven angles from which the five will be examined: raised together as a set; distinguished individually; sorted by existence and non-existence; by whether they are real or merely designated; by whether they are conventional or ultimate; by what produces each; by whether they differ from one another; by their own defining characteristics; by their modes of operation; by their differing names; and finally by matching them against form and the other categories, the aggregates and non-aggregates through to the foundations of mindfulness, some thirty-six headings in all. The five things themselves are: characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct knowledge.

Chinese Source

復次，若欲了知真實義者，當先了知略有五事。嗢柁南曰：總舉別分別　有實世俗事　若生若異等　相行等色等。云何五事？一、相，二、名，三、分別，四、真如，五、正智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whoever wishes to understand what is ultimately real should first understand that, in brief, there are five things. The summarizing verse: raising them together, distinguishing them separately, whether existent, whether real, conventional or ultimate, what produces them, whether they differ, their characteristics, their modes of operation, their names, and form and the rest. What are the five things? Characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct knowledge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the function of a summarizing verse in this literature, gathering a great many topics into a very few words so that a student can hold the whole structure in mind while working through the detail, and notes that these five things constitute one of the Mahāyāna's own foundational analytical frameworks.

17. Section: Characteristic, Name, Discrimination, and Suchness

Each of the five is then defined. What is characteristic? In brief, whatever serves as the ground on which speech comes to rest, that is, whatever can serve as the basis for a designation; this is the domain within which discrimination operates, which is why it is called characteristic. What is name? Whatever term is added onto a characteristic, that is, the names of things. Dependently arisen forms and mental events have of themselves no names at all; it is through the sixth consciousness's own discriminating function, the mental factor of perception taking up an image and distinguishing it, that provisional names are assigned to what is in its own nature inexpressible, and this addition is what the term names. The eye and the other faculties, and visible form and the other objects, have in themselves no names, their own characteristics being beyond expression; names are added onto them so that they may be recognized and communicated about. What is discrimination? All the minds and mental factors operating within the conditioned activity of the three realms, the eight consciousnesses and their mental factors having precisely this function of discriminating an object. And what is suchness? That which is disclosed by the absence of any self in phenomena, the domain in which a noble one's own knowledge operates, and precisely not something on which speech can come to rest, since phenomena arise from conditions, are governed by nothing, and are empty and without self; suchness is where the path of language is cut off and the workings of the mind cease.

Chinese Source

何等為相？謂若略說，所有言談安足處事。何等為名？謂即於相所有增語。何等為分別？謂三界行中所有心心所。何等為真如？謂法無我所顯，聖智所行，非一切言談安足處事。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is characteristic? In brief, whatever serves as the ground on which speech comes to rest. What is name? Whatever term is added onto a characteristic. What is discrimination? All the minds and mental factors within the conditioned activity of the three realms. What is suchness? That which is disclosed by the absence of self in phenomena, the domain in which noble knowledge operates, and precisely not a ground on which any speech comes to rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi relays five progressively narrower senses in which "characteristic" may be used: at its widest all five things are characteristics, since all have substance; excluding suchness alone, since suchness is unconditioned and beyond characteristics, the remaining four are characteristics as things speech rests upon; narrower still, characteristic, name, and discrimination are what the minds of the three realms take as objects; narrower again, only characteristic and name, when the point is what a defiled mind takes as its object-aspect; and narrowest of all, whatever the other four do not subsume, the faculties, objects, and the unassociated formations.

18. Section: The Knowledge That Is Purely Transcendent, and How the Five Sciences Purify the Obstruction to Knowledge

Correct knowledge, the fifth thing, is of two kinds: the purely transcendent, and the knowledge that is both worldly and transcendent. The purely transcendent is the fundamental non-discriminating knowledge, and it is by this that śrāvakas, solitary realizers, and bodhisattvas alike penetrate suchness, the two lesser vehicles penetrating the suchness disclosed by the emptiness of a self in persons and bodhisattvas penetrating the suchness disclosed by the emptiness of both a self in persons and a self in phenomena. It is further by this same knowledge that bodhisattvas, cultivating skillful means across the five sciences, inner knowledge, logic, medicine, the crafts, and language, and dwelling largely within the knowledge of all-pervading suchness, swiftly accomplish the complete purification of the obstruction to knowledge. Because such a bodhisattva knows that no phenomenon whatsoever is ultimately established, they can test that understanding against every kind of concrete undertaking, and the very variety of the five sciences becomes the field in which emptiness is verified in practice rather than merely held in theory, much as someone who has genuinely grasped the principles of cooking can produce any number of dishes; understanding grows more fluent, more skillful, and more complete precisely through being exercised across so many domains.

Chinese Source

何等為正智？謂略有二種。一、唯出世間正智，二、世間出世間正智。何等名為唯出世間正智？謂由此故，聲聞、獨覺、諸菩薩等通達真如。又由此故，彼諸菩薩於五明處善修方便，多住如是一切遍行真如智故，速證圓滿所知障淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is correct knowledge? In brief, of two kinds: the purely transcendent, and the worldly-and-transcendent. What is the purely transcendent? That by which śrāvakas, solitary realizers, and bodhisattvas penetrate suchness. And it is by this that bodhisattvas, cultivating skillful means across the five sciences and dwelling largely in the knowledge of all-pervading suchness, swiftly accomplish the complete purification of the obstruction to knowledge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi anticipates the natural objection that the five sciences would seem to belong to the subsequently attained knowledge rather than the fundamental one, and relays Master Jǐng's answer that a bodhisattva cultivates the sciences precisely in order to sever clinging to a self in phenomena, so that they too become objects of the transcendent knowledge of suchness. He identifies "all-pervading suchness" with the seven suchnesses said to pervade all conditioned things: the suchness of flowing, of true character, of consciousness-only, and the four corresponding to the noble truths, of establishment, of wrong conduct, of purity, and of right conduct.

19. Section: The Knowledge That Is Both Worldly and Transcendent, and Why It Bears Both Names

The knowledge that is both worldly and transcendent is what śrāvakas and solitary realizers obtain after the first knowledge has penetrated suchness; by this subsequently attained knowledge they continue to contemplate the truths as conventionally set out, so that the mind grows weary of and afraid of the faults of the three realms and comes to relish the peace beyond them. And by dwelling largely in this knowledge, entering again and again into transcendent knowledge, they swiftly accomplish the complete purification of the obstruction of affliction, severing its very seeds. The contrast with a bodhisattva's course is instructive: a bodhisattva who has attained direct realization cultivates, throughout the ten grounds, only the path that eliminates the obstruction to knowledge, applying the knowledge of phenomenal emptiness to the five sciences and the ten perfections, and though the seeds of affliction are not severed during those grounds, its coarse residue is progressively worn away, the seeds themselves being severed all at once only in the adamantine concentration at the threshold of buddhahood. As to why this knowledge bears both names: it is called transcendent because it was never obtained before, arising only once the fundamental knowledge has been realized and therefore genuinely surpassing mere designation; and it is called worldly because it takes the marks of verbal expression as its object, being with characteristics and with discrimination. For this reason it is called the knowledge that is both worldly and transcendent.

Chinese Source

何等名為世間出世間正智？謂聲聞、獨覺以初正智通達真如已，由此後所得世間出世間正智，於諸安立諦中，令心厭怖三界過患，愛味三界寂靜。又由多分安住此故，速證圓滿煩惱障淨。又即此智，未曾得義，名出世間；緣言說相為境界義，亦名世間；是故說為世間出世間。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the knowledge that is both worldly and transcendent? Śrāvakas and solitary realizers, having penetrated suchness by the first knowledge, then by this subsequently obtained knowledge contemplate the truths as conventionally set out, so that the mind grows weary and afraid of the three realms' faults and relishes their peace; and by dwelling largely in it they swiftly accomplish the complete purification of the obstruction of affliction. This knowledge is called transcendent because it was never obtained before, and worldly because it takes the marks of verbal expression as its object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Bodhisattva Ground's account of where a bodhisattva's affliction is actually worn down, the residue belonging to the lower destinies eliminated at the first ground, that obstructing the forbearance regarding non-arising eliminated at the eighth, and every remaining habitual trace eliminated only upon entering a tathāgata's own abiding, together with the parallel image of the obstruction to knowledge lying first in the skin, then the surface flesh, then the flesh itself, cleared at those same three stages.
