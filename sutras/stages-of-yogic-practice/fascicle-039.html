Fascicle 39 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十九 · The Chapter on the Six Perfections Opens

The Perfection of Giving

With the lineage of the powers now fully described, the treatise turns to the six perfections themselves — the actual practices, cultivated together with the four means of embrace, that bring a bodhisattva all the way to unsurpassed awakening. This fascicle opens with the first and most fundamental: the perfection of giving, unpacked through nine classical aspects that distinguish genuine, transcendent generosity from its ordinary, worldly counterpart.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 39 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle Thirty-Nine Opens — The Six Perfections That Complete Buddhahood

With the lineage of the powers fully traced across the previous fascicle, the treatise now turns to the practices that actually make up a bodhisattva's path in full: the six perfections, cultivated alongside the four means of embrace already described, together forming the genuine provisions that bring unsurpassed, complete awakening to its final completion.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩次第圓滿六波羅蜜多已，能證無上正等菩提。謂施波羅蜜多、戒波羅蜜多、忍波羅蜜多、精進波羅蜜多、靜慮波羅蜜多、慧波羅蜜多。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, once bodhisattvas have, in due order, brought the six perfections to completion, they are able to realize unsurpassed, perfect awakening. These are: the perfection of giving, the perfection of discipline, the perfection of patience, the perfection of diligence, the perfection of meditative concentration, and the perfection of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this fascicle's opening as marking a shift from theory to practice: everything described through the lineage of the powers explained what a bodhisattva should learn and why, while the six perfections and four means of embrace, opening here, describe what a bodhisattva actually does — cultivated simultaneously with continued learning, since a bodhisattva learns by practicing and practices by learning, the two never fully separable.

He draws a comparison to taking refuge in the three jewels: just as someone who has taken refuge in the Buddha, the teaching, and the community goes on to actually practice the four factors of stream-entry — drawing close to good teachers, hearing the true teaching, applying it correctly in reflection, and practicing in accordance with it — so too a bodhisattva who has taken unsurpassed awakening as their own genuine aim goes on to accumulate its provisions through the six perfections and four means of embrace, the cause that brings the fruit of buddhahood to completion exactly as taking refuge brings the fruit of stream-entry. This closes the six perfections' opening naming; the perfection of giving, unpacked first and most fully, follows next.

2. Milestone: The Perfection of Giving Opens — Worldly Giving, and Giving That Truly Crosses to the Far Shore

The perfection of giving opens with a fundamental distinction: ordinary, worldly giving still involves a real giver, a real recipient, and a real gift, and remains only a preliminary stage — genuine giving that actually crosses to the far shore requires seeing all three as empty, giver, recipient, and gift alike beyond ultimate grasp, so that giving itself becomes the very means by which stinginess and affliction are uprooted.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩施波羅蜜多？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's perfection of giving?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "perfection" itself carries the sense of reaching the far shore, or bringing a virtue to its full completion — giving, at its most ordinary, still involves the notion of a self who gives, another who receives, and a real object being handed over, and remains merely a preliminary exercise still caught in these fixed notions. Genuine, transcendent giving, reached through deepening insight, sees giver, recipient, and gift alike as ultimately without fixed, independent existence — the classical "threefold emptiness" — and only this kind of giving, which directly uproots the underlying stinginess and clinging at its root rather than merely performing a generous act, deserves to be called the perfection of giving in the full sense. He notes ordinary people's understanding of giving is usually quite shallow by comparison, and that the treatise's own unpacking of giving into nine distinct aspects, opening next, contains a surprising depth once properly understood.

3. Milestone: Nine Aspects of Giving, in a Verse Worth Memorizing

The treatise condenses giving's full content into nine classical aspects, set first in a short verse meant to be memorized outright, since every one of the remaining five perfections will be unpacked through this exact same ninefold structure — intrinsic nature, comprehensiveness, difficulty, universal scope, purity of motive, completeness across every circumstance, responsiveness to what is actually asked for, benefit spanning this life and the next, and freedom from any taint.

Chinese Source

嗢柁南曰：自性一切難，一切門善士，一切種遂求，二世樂清淨。如是九種相，是名略說施。

Ru-Yi Translation

The summarizing verse states: intrinsic nature, comprehensiveness, difficulty, universal scope, purity of motive, completeness across every circumstance, responsiveness to what is asked for, benefit in both this life and the next, and freedom from taint — these nine aspects are, in brief, called giving.

Chinese Source

謂九種相施，名為菩薩施波羅蜜多。一、自性施，二、一切施，三、難行施，四、一切門施，五、善士施，六、一切種施，七、遂求施，八、此世他世樂施，九、清淨施。

Ru-Yi Translation

These nine aspects of giving are called a bodhisattva's perfection of giving: first, giving in its intrinsic nature; second, comprehensive giving; third, difficult giving; fourth, giving through every gateway; fifth, giving by a genuinely good person; sixth, giving complete in every kind; seventh, giving that fulfills what is asked; eighth, giving that brings joy in this life and the next; ninth, pure giving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi urges memorizing this verse outright, since the treatise will return to this identical ninefold framework for discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom in turn — once genuinely understood here, the same structure illuminates all six perfections at once. He previews each aspect briefly: intrinsic nature names giving's basic defining character; comprehensiveness distinguishes giving's many concrete forms, benefiting others and benefiting oneself alike; difficult giving means genuinely letting go of what is hardest to part with; giving through every gateway means every possible occasion for giving is gathered under this one heading; giving by a good person means the act springs from a wholesome motive through and through; complete giving means the practice matures across every circumstance life presents; giving that fulfills what is asked means matching the gift to what the recipient actually needs or requests, so they are genuinely satisfied; giving that brings joy in both lives means benefiting the recipient now and hereafter at once; and pure giving means the mind, the manner, the method, and the object given are all free of fault. This closes the ninefold overview; giving's own intrinsic nature, unpacked first and in the greatest detail, follows next.

4. Milestone: The Intrinsic Nature of Giving — Not Every Gift Should Be Given

Giving's own intrinsic nature rests on a bodhisattva's complete freedom from attachment to their own body and possessions, joined with real discernment about what should actually be given at all — since a gift that would harm the recipient, however freely offered, is no genuine act of giving — carried out through a volition free of greed, expressed through actual deeds of body and speech, and grounded in both a correct view of the teaching and unshakeable confidence that giving genuinely bears fruit.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩自性施？謂諸菩薩於自身財無所顧惜，能施一切所應施物。無貪俱行思，及因此所發能施一切無罪施物身語二業，安住律儀阿笈摩見、定有果見，隨所希求，即以此物而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's giving in its intrinsic nature? Bodhisattvas, having no attachment to their own body or wealth, are able to give away whatever should genuinely be given. This consists of the volition accompanied by non-greed, and the deeds of body and speech this volition gives rise to, able to give whatever blameless object is called for; resting in moral discipline, correct view of the teaching, and certainty that giving bears fruit; and, according to whatever is genuinely sought, giving that very thing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes inner wealth, one's own body and life, from outer wealth, one's material possessions, noting a bodhisattva free of attachment to either is able to give both away. He stresses "whatever should genuinely be given" is doing real work here: not everything a recipient wants ought actually to be given — offering a set of mahjong tiles to someone who would only sink deeper into addiction is no genuine gift, however freely handed over, while offering a book that genuinely builds wisdom is exactly the kind of giving meant here. Genuine giving, in other words, requires real discernment about consequences, not simply generosity of impulse.

He explains the volition of non-greed as the underlying mental factor that makes an act of giving what it is, always accompanied in practice by non-hatred, non-delusion, and diligence, even though non-greed is singled out here since it directly counters the stinginess giving is meant to overcome; the deeds of body and speech as actually carrying out the physical act of giving while also praising giving's own genuine benefit and encouraging others toward it; and correct view of the teaching as requiring that a bodhisattva remain free of both broken discipline and broken view — noting the classical tradition still counts as praiseworthy someone whose conduct is imperfect but whose view of the teaching remains genuinely sound, since correct understanding of cause and effect is what actually distinguishes a wise act of giving from mere generosity untethered from any real understanding of its consequences. This closes giving's basic character and its grounding in correct view; the treatise's own account of certainty that giving bears fruit, with its four classical patterns of giving and reward, follows next.

5. Milestone: Certainty That Giving Bears Fruit — Four Classical Patterns of Giving and Reward

Genuine giving rests on real confidence that giving actually bears fruit, illustrated through four classical patterns matching how much is given to how much merit actually results — since giving abundantly with no wisdom behind it, or giving out of stingy, ill intent, produces far less merit than giving even a small amount to someone whose own practice multiplies its effect, or giving generously with genuine understanding of what the gift is actually for.

Chinese Source

定有果見：謂於因果決定信解，知布施必得果報，不由他緣而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Certainty that giving bears fruit means holding a settled, confident understanding of cause and effect, knowing that giving must yield its result, and giving not because led by some outside influence, but from one's own genuine conviction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the classical Scriptural Similes text's four patterns of giving and its resulting merit to illustrate why this certainty matters so much in practice. Giving much for little merit describes someone who spends lavishly on things like ritual sacrifice, drinking, and entertainment — even funding an entire concert purely for pleasure, or killing animals for a sacrificial feast — genuinely generous in scale, yet entirely without wisdom behind it. Giving little for much merit describes offering even a modest gift to a genuinely disciplined practitioner who then applies it toward diligent study and practice, since the practitioner's own effort multiplies the small gift's effect many times over. Giving little for little merit describes a stingy, ill-intentioned gift from someone of wrong view, offered grudgingly and without wisdom, where both the giving and its result remain slight. And giving much for much merit describes a person of genuine ability who, moved by a clear sense of the world's impermanence, funds temples and monastic support on a grand scale, enabling countless others to plant roots of goodness and study the teaching — generous in scale and wise in application at once.

He draws the practical lesson directly: anyone who wishes to give well should cultivate genuine understanding of cause and effect first, since the same act of generosity, done with wisdom or without it, produces dramatically different results — this settled confidence in cause and effect is what actually makes giving faultless and effective, rather than a well-meaning act performed blindly. This closes giving's intrinsic nature in its entirety — freedom from attachment, right discernment, the volition of non-greed, the deeds of body and speech, correct view, and certainty of fruit, all now covered; comprehensive giving, the second of the nine aspects, follows next.

6. Milestone: Comprehensive Giving Opens — What Counts as "Everything" Given

Comprehensive giving covers two basic categories, one's own body and one's outer possessions, unfolding in practice into three concrete forms: giving only the body itself, as far as a practitioner is genuinely able; a mixed form combining both, illustrated through the tender image of chewing food to feed a being unable to chew for itself; and giving purely external things — every object, whether belonging to another being or not, that can rightly be given at all.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切施？謂一切者略有二種。一、內所施物，二、外所施物。若諸菩薩但捨己身，是名唯施內所施物。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive giving? "Everything," in brief, is of two kinds: first, what is given from within; second, what is given from without. If bodhisattvas give only their own body, this is called giving only what is given from within.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩為愍食吐活命眾生，數數食已，吐所飲食而施與之，是名雜施內外施物。若諸菩薩除上所說，施餘一切所應施物，是名唯施外所施物。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas, out of compassion for a being that lives on food it cannot chew, repeatedly eat and then bring up that food to feed it, this is called mixed giving of both what is given from within and from without. If bodhisattvas, apart from what has been described above, give every other object that should genuinely be given, this is called giving only what is given from without.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that giving purely from within need not mean giving up one's entire body outright — an ordinary practitioner, unlike a fully realized sage, might genuinely give blood, bone marrow, or even a small portion of a liver, since the liver alone can regenerate and life is not thereby endangered, while anything that would actually threaten life remains, for most practitioners, still a matter for sincere aspiration rather than immediate action, to be fulfilled once genuine capacity for it has grown. He reads the mixed form's classical image tenderly, comparing it to how a mother in earlier generations would chew fruit before feeding it to an infant not yet able to chew for itself — an act of literal, bodily nourishment carried out purely from compassion. This closes what counts as "everything" given; the specific manner of giving one's own body, whether entirely or piece by piece, follows next.

7. Milestone: Giving One's Own Body — Total Surrender, and Piece by Piece

Giving the body itself takes two forms: surrendering the entire body to another's will, exactly as someone might submit to servitude purely for food and clothing, except that a bodhisattva does so entirely free of any such craving, motivated only by swiftly reaching supreme awakening, benefiting and bringing happiness to beings, and swiftly perfecting the very practice of giving itself; and giving specific parts of the body — hands, feet, head, eyes, limbs, blood, flesh, sinew, bone, even marrow — exactly as requested, a far more advanced practice reserved for those whose realization can genuinely sustain it.

Chinese Source

一、總求身者，以身施彼，隨所欲為，繫屬於彼、隨順於彼。譬如有人為衣食故，彊自為他而作僕使。如是菩薩無愛染心，但為速證最勝菩提，但為眾生利益安樂，但為布施波羅蜜多速圓滿故，以身施彼，隨所欲為，繫屬於彼、隨順於彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, for one who requests the entire body: giving the body to that person, to do with as they wish, belonging entirely to them, following their will entirely. Just as someone, for the sake of food and clothing, forces themselves to serve another as a servant — so too a bodhisattva, with no craving mind at all, but only in order to swiftly realize supreme awakening, only for beings' benefit and happiness, only to swiftly perfect the perfection of giving, gives the body to that person, to do with as they wish, belonging entirely to them, following their will entirely.

Chinese Source

二、別求手、足、頭、目、支節、血、肉、筋、骨乃至髓者，隨其所欲，一切施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, for one who specifically requests hands, feet, head, eyes, limbs, blood, flesh, sinew, bone, or even marrow: giving all of it, according to what they wish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the striking contrast in the treatise's own illustration: an ordinary servant submits out of need, craving food and clothing they lack, while a bodhisattva submits from an entirely opposite motivation, free of any craving at all, purely to hasten awakening and benefit others — the outward act may look similar, but the inner motivation could not be more different. He cites Elder Miàojìng's own caution regarding piece-by-piece bodily giving specifically: this level of practice genuinely belongs only to a practitioner who has reached at least the acceptance stage of practice, settled in a correct worldly view stable enough that rebirth in the lower realms is no longer a risk, someone who has habitually contemplated emptiness deeply enough that the aggregates are seen through and through as without a fixed self or anything belonging to a self — without that foundation, giving away even a small part of the body remains, for most practitioners, simply beyond present capacity, however admirable the aspiration. This closes giving the body itself, whole or in part; external giving, in its own two forms, follows next.

8. Milestone: Giving External Things — What's Requested for Use, and Full Transfer of Ownership

External giving likewise takes two forms: giving whatever a being requests simply to use, freely and according to their own preference, whether food, drink, transport, clothing, ornaments, everyday goods, garlands and perfume, a place to rest, or light itself; and giving full ownership outright, releasing all attachment entirely, reserved for cases where doing so will not feed the recipient's own arrogance or unwholesome habits, but will instead draw them toward genuine trust and openness to the teaching.

Chinese Source

一、求受用者，恣彼所須，如其所樂隨意受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, for one who seeks something to use: freely providing whatever they lack, letting them use it as they please, entirely according to their own preference.

Chinese Source

二、求自在者，一切斷心并皆施與。……縱任自在是此自在義故。若彼有情，不因施彼多行憍逸惡行，亦不增長諸不善根，是名一切斷心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, for one who seeks genuine ownership: giving it entirely, with a mind that lets go completely. ... Being able to do with it entirely as one wishes is what "ownership" means here. If that being, through receiving this gift, does not thereby fall into arrogance or unwholesome conduct, and does not thereby strengthen any unwholesome root, this is called a mind that lets go completely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates giving for mere use through the classical image of goods left openly at a place of shared giving, free for anyone drawn to take according to their own need, alongside the more ordinary case of directly supplying whatever a specific person asks for, whether food, water, a vehicle, clothing, or simply somewhere to rest. Giving full ownership he distinguishes as a deeper release entirely — not merely lending a jar of medicine but handing over the whole container, not merely lending a car but transferring its title outright — precisely because a bodhisattva's own attachment to the object itself must be uprooted, not merely loosened.

He stresses the condition attached to this second form is not incidental: full ownership is given only where doing so will not feed a recipient's own arrogance or reinforce unwholesome tendencies, but will instead open them toward genuine trust and eventual receptivity to the teaching — the same underlying wisdom that governs whether to give at all runs through this deeper form of giving as well, since a gift that only deepens someone's affliction is no gift in the deepest sense, however freely surrendered. This closes external giving's two forms; the treatise's own account of genuinely wise giving — knowing precisely when to give and when to withhold — follows next.

9. Milestone: Wise Giving — Sometimes Planting a Seed First, Sometimes Withholding Entirely

Genuine giving is never simply reflexive generosity: a bodhisattva gives only after weighing whether a gift will actually benefit the recipient, sometimes first asking for something small in return — even a single recited line of the teaching — to plant a genuine seed of goodness, and sometimes withholding a gift entirely when it would bring only fleeting pleasure, or neither pleasure nor benefit at all, while giving freely whenever real benefit is assured, whether or not pleasure accompanies it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩非無差別以一切種一切內外所有施物施諸眾生；是諸菩薩以其種種內外施物，於諸眾生或有施與、或不施與。云何施與？云何不施？

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not, without any discernment, give every kind of internal and external object to every being; rather, with their various internal and external gifts, bodhisattvas sometimes give and sometimes do not give. What determines giving? What determines not giving?

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，若知種種內外施物，於彼眾生唯令安樂、不作利益，或復於彼不作安樂、不作利益，便不施與。若知種種內外施物，於彼眾生定作利益、不定安樂，或復於彼定作利益、定作安樂，即便施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, if they know that some internal or external gift would bring a being only pleasure without benefit, or would bring neither pleasure nor benefit at all, do not give it. If they know that some internal or external gift will bring a being certain benefit, whether or not pleasure is certain, or will bring both certain benefit and certain pleasure, they give it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates discernment in giving through the Upāsaka Precepts Sūtra's own classical practice: encountering someone destitute, a wise giver first asks whether they would take refuge in the three jewels, or simply recite a single line such as "all conditioned things are impermanent" or even just the Buddha's name, giving the alms only once they agree — not withholding out of stinginess, but because that small act plants a genuine seed of goodness that outlasts the material gift itself, however modest an exchange it may seem. He balances this with the opposite case: a wealthy but foolish person clinging tightly to some possession — absorbed for hours in idle entertainment at the expense of their own spiritual practice, say — is someone a bodhisattva actively encourages to give the very thing away, precisely because releasing it serves them, whatever short-term reluctance that release might cause.

He explains the core criterion directly: pleasure alone, without any accompanying benefit — handing someone a mahjong table simply because they would enjoy playing on it, say, feeding only craving with nothing gained — is exactly the kind of gift a bodhisattva withholds, since it deepens affliction rather than easing it. Certain benefit is what actually matters, whether or not pleasure comes with it: teaching someone discipline may bring no immediate pleasure at all, since restraint rarely feels comfortable in the moment, yet the benefit it secures is certain, and its pleasure simply arrives later, once its fruit ripens — benefit functioning as the cause, pleasure as its eventual result. This closes wise giving's underlying criterion; the treatise's detailed working-through of specific cases where giving should be withheld, beginning with never becoming an accomplice to another's wrongdoing, follows next.

10. Milestone: Detailed Refusals Begin — Never Aiding Coercion or Harm, Whatever the Cost

Among the detailed cases where giving must be withheld, the first and most absolute concerns anyone seeking a bodhisattva's own body or service in order to coerce, harm, or deceive another being — a request a bodhisattva refuses without exception, since giving up one's own body and life countless times over remains preferable to ever becoming complicit in wronging someone else.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，若有來求共為伴侶，欲作非理逼迫、損害、誑惑於他，便不以身而施於彼，隨所欲為，繫屬於彼、隨順於彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, if someone comes seeking companionship in order to unreasonably coerce, harm, or deceive another being, do not give their body to that person to do with as they wish, belonging to them, following their will.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩寧於百反、千反、或百千反，捨自身命施諸眾生，終不隨他教命、稱悅彼情，於諸眾生非理逼迫、損害、誑惑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas would rather give up their own body and life a hundred times, a thousand times, or a hundred thousand times over to benefit beings, than ever follow another's command or gratify their wishes by unreasonably coercing, harming, or deceiving other beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this stands as an absolute limit within giving's otherwise expansive generosity: however far a bodhisattva's willingness to give of themselves extends, it never extends to helping wrong another being, since a bodhisattva exists to benefit beings, and knowingly assisting harm against them would directly contradict that purpose at its root. He points to the treatise's own earlier catalogue, elsewhere in the work, of twenty-two specific forms such coercion or provocation can take — false measures, false claims, forged documents, wrongful livelihood, open confrontation, mockery, betrayal, litigation, cursing, anger, reproach, intimidation, beating, killing, binding, imprisonment, mutilation, expulsion, flattery, deceit, entrapment, and false speech — noting a bodhisattva's refusal applies without exception across every one of them, regardless of who is asking or how the request is framed. This closes the absolute refusal to aid coercion or harm; a subtler case — declining even a legitimate request for a body part when a far greater task of benefiting beings is already at hand — follows next.

11. Milestone: When Not to Give a Body Part, Even to a Genuine Request — Priority, Malice, and a Deranged Mind

Three specific cases call for withholding a requested body part even when the request itself is sincere: when a bodhisattva's own giving-intention is already fully pure and a vast task benefiting countless beings is currently at hand, giving would abandon that greater good rather than serve any genuine purpose; when the one asking is a malicious demonic being seeking only to provoke destruction, giving would spare them nothing and only deepen the grave harm they would bring upon themselves; and when the one asking is genuinely deranged, their request reflects no coherent intent at all, only confused, groundless speech.

Chinese Source

彼菩薩為令意樂得清淨故，須捨現前利眾生事，而施身分。由彼意樂已清淨故，不應棄捨正現在前利眾生事，而施身分。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a bodhisattva's giving-intention is not yet pure, in order to purify it, they should set aside the task of benefiting beings currently at hand and give the body part instead. But if their giving-intention is already pure, they should not abandon the vast task of benefiting beings currently at hand in order to give the body part.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若魔眾天懷惱亂心，現前來乞身分支節，不應分碎支節施與。……勿彼當獲上品過罪及損害故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If malicious heavenly demons, harboring intent to provoke destruction, come seeking a body part, bodhisattvas should not sever it and give it to them. ... This is so that they do not incur the gravest fault and harm upon themselves.

Chinese Source

或有眾生癡狂心亂，來求菩薩身分支節，亦不應碎支節施與。由彼不住自性心故，不為義利而求乞故，其心狂亂不自在故，空有種種浮妄言說，是故不應施彼身分。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a being, confused and deranged in mind, comes seeking a body part, bodhisattvas likewise should not sever it and give it to them. Because that being does not abide in their own genuine mind, does not ask out of any genuine purpose, and their mind, deranged and out of control, produces only empty, groundless speech, a body part should not be given to them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi resolves what looks at first like a contradiction — sometimes giving a body part purifies the mind, sometimes withholding it protects a greater good — by noting the deciding factor is simply whether the giving-intention is already pure. A bodhisattva still struggling with attachment to their own body genuinely benefits from the discipline of giving it away precisely because it is hard, difficult-to-give-yet-given being exactly what purifies clinging at its root; but a bodhisattva whose intention is already settled and pure, actively engaged in benefiting vast numbers of beings, would only cause real harm to those countless others by abandoning that work for one person's request — the giving here would serve no one, since the point was never the act itself but the purification it enables.

He explains the demon case as an act of genuine protection rather than refusal born of stinginess: harming or destroying a bodhisattva whose own giving-intention is pure carries an especially grave karmic consequence, and declining the request spares the demon that consequence rather than protecting the bodhisattva's own body for its own sake. He reads the deranged case similarly straightforwardly — a request made from a mind not genuinely one's own, incapable of coherent purpose, is not a real request at all, and giving in response to it would serve no one. This closes the three cases for withholding; when giving a body part is genuinely appropriate follows next.

12. When Giving a Body Part Is Right, and the Principle for Internal Giving Closes

Apart from the specific cases already named, whenever none of those obstacles apply, a bodhisattva gives a requested body part exactly as asked, whether the whole self or a specific part — closing the treatise's full account of what should and should not be given from within the body itself.

Chinese Source

除上所說，與上相違，來求菩薩身支節者，隨其所欲應施彼身，隨所欲為，繫屬於彼、隨順於彼，或分支節而施與之。當知是名菩薩於內所可施物，或應施與、或不應與。

Ru-Yi Translation

Apart from what has been described, in cases contrary to those, when someone comes seeking a bodhisattva's body or a part of it, the body should be given exactly as they wish, to do with as they will, belonging to them, following their will, or a specific part severed and given to them. This should be understood as a bodhisattva's principle regarding what may or may not be given from what is given from within.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this closing statement simply confirms the default: absent the specific obstacles just named — an already-pure intention with a greater task at hand, malicious demonic intent, or a genuinely deranged request — a bodhisattva gives exactly as asked, since giving remains the rule and withholding the carefully bounded exception. This closes internal giving's entire account, whole and in part; external giving's own detailed cases, opening with objects capable of causing harm, follow next.

13. Milestone: Nine Kinds of External Objects — Poison and Weapons Given Only to Heal, Never to Harm

External objects bring their own detailed cases, opening with anything genuinely capable of harm — poison, fire, blades, and alcohol — withheld whenever sought to injure oneself or another, yet given freely the moment the very same object is sought for genuine benefit, since a poison used to heal and a poison used to harm are, in the end, entirely different acts of giving.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩於外施物，若有眾生來求毒、火、刀、酒等物，或為自害、或為害他，即不應施。若有眾生來求毒、火、刀、酒等物，或自饒益、或饒益他，是即應施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, regarding external objects to give: if a being seeks poison, fire, a blade, or alcohol in order to harm themselves or another, this should not be given. If a being seeks poison, fire, a blade, or alcohol in order to benefit themselves or another, this should be given.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own criterion here is never the object itself, but the purpose it will actually serve — a lesson that translates directly into contemporary terms: morphine given to ease a terminal cancer patient's pain, snake venom refined into an antivenom that saves a life, or a compound drawn from the yew tree's own toxin repurposed as a genuine cancer treatment are all, in substance, the same "poison" the treatise names, given here in service of healing rather than harm. A knife given to prepare a meal, or fire used to cook food for a gathering, are equally acts of benefit rather than danger, and equally appropriate to give. The determining question in every case remains identical: does this specific instance of giving serve life, or threaten it? This closes objects capable of harm; property that genuinely belongs to someone else, and objects that would draw a recipient into wrongdoing, follow next.

14. Never Giving What Isn't Genuinely One's Own to Give

Three further limits protect against a subtler kind of wrongful giving: never giving away property that actually belongs to someone else without their genuine consent, since doing so causes that owner real distress regardless of any merit the gift might otherwise carry; never acting as a go-between that facilitates access to someone else's spouse; and never giving food or drink known to contain living creatures, since consuming it would take their lives.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不以屬他非同意物而行惠施。又諸菩薩，不行媒媾，以他妻妾而行布施。又諸菩薩，不以有蟲飲食等物而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give away an object belonging to someone else without their consent. Bodhisattvas do not act as a go-between, giving access to another's wife or consort. Bodhisattvas do not give food or drink known to contain living creatures.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the classical teaching that giving away property without its rightful owner's consent, however generous the underlying impulse, still causes the true owner real distress, and though a measure of merit may still result, it tends to be accompanied by recurring illness or hardship — genuine giving requires the object actually be one's own to give in the first place. Facilitating access to someone else's spouse he treats as a form of enabling sexual misconduct regardless of any surrounding generosity, and giving food or drink containing living creatures as an act that would cause the recipient to unknowingly kill upon eating, implicating both giver and receiver in that harm. This closes what should never be given regardless of the asking; frivolous or purely entertainment-oriented gifts, weighed by whether they build genuine trust or merely deepen unwholesome habit, follow next.

15. Milestone: Frivolous Gifts — Never Feeding Corruption, But Given Freely When They Build Genuine Trust

Objects meant purely for amusement, carrying no genuine benefit in themselves, are withheld whenever they would deepen a recipient's arrogance and heedlessness toward a future spent in misery — echoing the classical preference for real poverty over a corrupting wealth that only breeds suffering later — yet given freely the moment the very same kind of object would instead ease genuine distress, build real trust, and open a recipient toward the teaching rather than away from it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若有眾生來求種種能引戲樂、能引無義所施之物，不應施與。何以故？若施彼時，雖暫令彼於菩薩所心生歡喜，而復令彼廣作種種不饒益事。謂因施故，令彼多行憍逸惡行，身壞已後墮諸惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, if a being comes seeking various objects meant purely for amusement, carrying no genuine benefit, do not give them. Why is this? Because giving them, though it might briefly bring that being joy toward the bodhisattva, would go on to cause them to do a great many harmful things — since, because of the gift, that being comes to indulge heavily in arrogant, heedless misconduct, and after the body perishes, falls into the miserable realms.

Chinese Source

若有種種戲樂等具雖復施與，不令眾生墮諸惡趣，亦不增長諸不善根，菩薩為欲令彼眾生，因此所施戲樂等具，攝受饒益，心生淨信，易可化導、易可成熟，隨彼所求悉皆施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

If various objects meant for amusement, though given, would not cause a being to fall into the miserable realms nor strengthen any unwholesome root, and giving them would draw that being toward genuine benefit, give rise to pure faith, and make them easier to guide and ripen, bodhisattvas give exactly what is asked.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a classical verse directly bearing on this principle: better that someone remain genuinely poor in wealth and status, thereby staying free of the misery-bound misconduct that riches often invite, than be thrown into confusion by a prosperity that later ripens into suffering — some people, given real means, turn quickly toward wrongdoing precisely because they now can, whereas the same person, lacking that means, might never have been tempted at all. He stresses this is never a blanket prohibition on anything enjoyable, only a caution against feeding a recipient's own arrogance or heedlessness; the same category of gift, given where it genuinely eases distress and opens someone toward trust and eventual receptivity to the teaching rather than toward further indulgence, is entirely appropriate and should be given without hesitation. This closes objects meant for amusement; a parallel set of cases concerning entertainment-related activities themselves, rather than objects, opens next.

16. Milestone: Never Teaching the Skills of Harm, Never Aiding Blood Sacrifice

Entertainment-related activities themselves, not merely objects, carry their own clear limits: a bodhisattva never teaches anyone the techniques of hunting or fishing, however skilled they might personally be at it, since doing so only spreads the capacity to take life; and never takes part in or teaches the killing involved in impure sacrificial rites, even those conducted at a temple, however widely people believe such killing brings good fortune.

Chinese Source

是諸菩薩若有來求諸戲樂事，何者應施？何者不施？謂諸菩薩終不施人捕獵等法。

Ru-Yi Translation

If beings come seeking entertainment-related activities, which should be given and which not? Bodhisattvas never teach anyone the techniques of hunting or trapping.

Chinese Source

又於雜穢諸祠祀中，作大方便多集眾生，損害其命，獲無量罪。於彼祠祀終不自作，亦不教他，亦不於彼諸天寺中殺羊祠祀。

Ru-Yi Translation

And regarding impure sacrificial rites that gather great numbers of beings through elaborate means only to take their lives, incurring immeasurable fault, bodhisattvas never perform such rites themselves, never teach them to others, and never kill sheep in sacrificial rites even within temples to the gods.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes a wry irony in the treatise's own observation: someone genuinely clever at ordinary tasks is often just as clever at devising more effective ways to harm other beings, so a bodhisattva's own cleverness, wherever it lies, is never placed in service of hunting or killing, whatever practical skill they might personally have with it. He treats the sacrificial-rite prohibition as addressing a widespread but mistaken belief — that killing animals to offer at a shrine or temple somehow secures merit or good fortune — noting a bodhisattva who genuinely understands cause and effect knows exactly the opposite is true, and refuses to participate in or endorse the practice under any circumstance. This closes the first two of entertainment's harmful activities; providing the grounds or tools for harming life, and assisting an act of revenge, follow next.

17. Milestone: Never Providing the Grounds or Tools for Harming Life, Never Assisting Revenge

Two further limits close this cluster: a bodhisattva never lends land, water, or any other resource, once its purpose to kill beings is known, and never gives nets, snares, or traps to anyone intending to harm living creatures with them, even if the request is only to learn how such tools work; and never hands over someone under their own protection to an enemy seeking to abuse, imprison, kill, rob, or punish them.

Chinese Source

若有來求或水、或陸無量眾生所依止處，為欲殺害彼生命故，菩薩知已，終不施與。若有來求罩羅、罝、弶，為害眾生及為習學，皆不施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

If someone comes seeking a place on water or land that countless beings depend on, intending to kill them there, bodhisattvas, once they know this, never give it. If someone comes seeking nets or snares or traps, whether to harm beings or merely to learn how, none of it is given.

Chinese Source

若有怨家來求讎隙，為欲呵罵、縛戮、殺害、奪財、治罰，終不施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

If an enemy comes seeking out an old grievance, wishing to curse, bind, kill, rob, or punish someone under a bodhisattva's protection, that person is never handed over.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the first case through a simple, practical example: if a pond or plot of land is requested so that fish or animals can be released and given a place to live, lending it is entirely appropriate, but the same request made for the purpose of killing whatever creatures live there is refused outright, since the underlying purpose, not the object itself, always governs whether giving is appropriate. He explains the tools-of-harm case as covering even someone who claims only academic curiosity about how a net or trap works, since providing the means makes eventual harm possible regardless of stated intent. And he reads protecting someone from a vengeful enemy as a direct extension of a bodhisattva's basic responsibility toward anyone under their care — having taken someone in, a bodhisattva does not then surrender them to whoever comes seeking to settle an old score. This closes the harmful side of entertainment-related activities; the treatise's own summary of the underlying rule, together with when luxury and entertainment genuinely should be given, follows next.

18. Milestone: The Rule in Brief, and When Luxury or Entertainment Should Be Given Freely

Summed up in a single principle: whatever would oppress or harm another being is never given, regardless of who asks; yet the very same category of fine possessions — carriages, clothing, exquisite food, music and dance, perfume, ornaments, gardens and mansions, attendants, or training in a genuine craft — is given freely and without hesitation whenever it would instead draw the recipient toward genuine, pure-hearted trust in the bodhisattva and, through that trust, toward the teaching itself.

Chinese Source

以要言之，所有一切逼迫、損害他諸有情戲樂等具，有來求者，是諸菩薩皆不施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

Stated concisely: whatever entertainment-related object or activity would oppress or harm other beings, whoever requests it, bodhisattvas never give.

Chinese Source

若復種種象馬車輿、衣服莊嚴、珍妙飲食、習歌舞等，及諸樂器、塗飾香鬘、珍玩眾具、園林樓觀、舍宅侍女、習學種種工巧業處，如是一切戲樂等具，為欲令其於菩薩所因此發起清淨信心，有來求者悉皆施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

But various fine carriages, clothing and adornments, exquisite food, song and dance, musical instruments, perfumes and garlands, precious objects, gardens and mansions, homes and attendants, or training in various genuine crafts — all such things, when giving them will draw the recipient toward pure faith in the bodhisattva, are given freely to whoever requests them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing pair as the natural summary of everything just covered: the underlying test was never whether an object is inherently frivolous or luxurious, but strictly whether giving it causes harm — an identical carriage, meal, or piece of music can be entirely appropriate to give in one circumstance and entirely inappropriate in another, depending purely on the effect it will actually have on the recipient. This closes the entertainment-related category; food withheld from the sick and the merely gluttonous, and the refusal to ever assist an act of self-harm, follow next.

19. Food Withheld From the Sick and the Gluttonous, and Never Aiding Self-Harm

Two further categories protect a recipient from themselves: food given to someone ill must genuinely suit their condition, in both amount and kind, never simply whatever they happen to crave, and is likewise withheld from someone already full who persistently craves more out of sheer gluttony; and a request born of despair — to be beaten, poisoned, thrown from a cliff, drowned, or burned — is never granted, however sincerely and desperately it is asked.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若有病者來求非量、非宜飲食，亦不施與。若有眾生食飽滿已，性多饞嗜，數復來求珍妙飲食，亦不施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

If someone ill comes seeking food in an improper amount or of an unsuitable kind, this is not given. If a being, already full, is by nature given to gluttony and repeatedly seeks exquisite food, this too is not given.

Chinese Source

若諸眾生愁憂所逼，求欲殺害毆擊自身，食毒、墜巖、投淵、赴火，皆悉不應施其所欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a being, overwhelmed by despair, seeks to kill or beat themselves, ingest poison, leap from a cliff, drown, or set themselves ablaze, none of this desire should ever be granted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads both cases as expressions of the same underlying wisdom already established: giving is never mere compliance with what is asked, but always weighed against genuine benefit — feeding an illness with the wrong food, or feeding gluttony with more indulgence, harms the very person a gift is meant to help, and a request made from despair reflects a mind in crisis rather than a genuine, considered wish, deserving compassionate refusal rather than compliance. This closes both categories; the treatise's own account of why parents and teachers can never be given away, whatever the circumstance, follows next.

20. Milestone: Parents and Teachers Are Never Given Away — A Debt That Outlasts Even Their Own Cruelty

A bodhisattva absolutely never gives away their own parents or teachers to anyone requesting them, since the debt owed to those who nursed, raised, and taught them is one to be carried gratefully for as long as life lasts, without the slightest weariness — so complete a devotion that a bodhisattva would not even privately entertain giving them away were they themselves to demand cruelty, beating, or sale in return, let alone actually hand them over to someone else's request.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若有來求父母師長，定不應施。何以故？以諸菩薩於其父母尊重師長乳哺養育微有恩者，於長夜中常思頂戴不生厭倦，恆持自身繫屬隨順，任所屠害、捶縛、貨賣。尚自不敢竊懷施心，何況顯然施來求者。

Ru-Yi Translation

If someone comes seeking a bodhisattva's own parents or teachers, this is absolutely never given. Why is this? Because bodhisattvas, toward parents and honored teachers who nursed and raised them with even the smallest kindness, constantly reflect through the long night of life on carrying that debt with reverence, never growing weary, keeping themselves devoted and compliant to them — even should those parents or teachers wish to slaughter, beat, bind, or sell the bodhisattva outright, the bodhisattva would not dare even privately consider giving them away in return, let alone openly give them to someone else who asks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies parents and teachers together as those to whom a genuine debt of kindness is owed — parents for the plain, irreplaceable fact of having raised a child to adulthood at all, and teachers, whether birth-parents in another sense or spiritual guides, for having nurtured a person's own capacity for genuine goodness and understanding, sometimes described as a second, spiritual kind of nourishment. He draws out the treatise's own striking standard: even if a parent or teacher were to turn against the bodhisattva with real cruelty, the devotion owed them would not waver in the slightest, since the point of gratitude is not conditional on the other person's present conduct but rooted in what they have already, irrevocably given. This closes the case of parents and teachers; even a king's own limits on what may be given away — never another's spouse or children, only what genuinely belongs to him — follow next.

21. Milestone: Even a King May Only Give Away What Is Genuinely His — And His Own Family Only With Their Consent

A bodhisattva who rules as king, however great their authority over the realm, never seizes a subject's own spouse or children to give away to someone else, giving only what genuinely belongs to the crown itself — land, towns, or territory; and even members of a bodhisattva's own household are given away only with their own genuine, willing consent, never to an enemy or violently disposed person, and never a person of noble birth reduced to servitude.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若作國王，灌頂自在，統領方域。於自國界所有僚庶，終不抑奪取餘妻子而轉施餘。唯持村邑、聚落、川土，或全或分以用布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas rule as king, anointed and sovereign over their realm, they never seize the spouse or children of any subject in their domain to give away to someone else. They give only towns, villages, or territory, whole or in part.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於自妻子、奴婢、僕使、親戚、眷屬，若不先以正言曉喻，令其歡喜，終不彊逼令其憂惱施來求者。……而不施與怨家、惡友、藥叉、羅剎凶暴業者。不以妻子形容軟弱族姓男女，施來求者令作奴婢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, regarding their own spouse, children, servants, or relatives, never coerce them into distress and give them to someone who asks unless they have first genuinely explained the matter and won their willing consent. ... Nor are they ever given to an enemy, a wicked companion, or a violently disposed being. Nor is a spouse or child of noble, gently-raised birth ever given away to become someone's servant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a bodhisattva-king's own restraint even at the height of political power: authority over a realm never extends to treating its people's own families as property to dispose of, and only what actually belongs to the crown — territory, not persons — is genuinely his to give. He reads the three further conditions on giving away one's own household as a coherent whole: genuine consent comes first, since even one's own family member is never coerced against their will merely because a bodhisattva has the standing authority to do so; never to an enemy or a violent being, since handing a loved one over to someone likely to harm them serves no one's genuine good; and never reducing someone of gentle, noble upbringing to servitude, since some transitions cause a suffering out of proportion to whatever the gift might accomplish. This closes what a bodhisattva may and may not give away regarding family and dependents; the closing case in this category — a kingship never given to a tyrant — follows next.

22. Milestone: A Kingship Withheld From a Tyrant — Benefit to Others Concludes, Benefit to Oneself Opens

The final case closing this whole account of giving weighed by benefit to others: a bodhisattva never gives a kingship to someone who would use it to oppress and brutalize the people, and would, if already capable, actively depose a tyrant already occupying the throne rather than let the oppression continue — closing this entire framework and opening the treatise's next angle on giving: what a bodhisattva must weigh for the sake of their own genuine integrity, unfolding into thirty-one further points.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若有上品逼惱眾生樂行種種暴惡業者來求王位，終不施與。若彼暴惡補特伽羅先居王位，菩薩有力尚應廢黜，況當施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

If someone who delights in oppressing beings through every kind of violent, unwholesome deed comes seeking kingship, bodhisattvas never grant it. If such a violent person already occupies the throne, a bodhisattva with the power to do so should even depose them — how much more so, then, never grant it to begin with.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this closes the treatise's entire, remarkably detailed account of giving weighed by benefit to others — every case from poison and weapons through parents, spouses, and kingship itself governed by the single underlying question of whether a gift genuinely serves the recipient and the wider world, or merely satisfies a request at real cost to others. He reads the willingness to actively depose an already-installed tyrant as a striking extension of that same principle: a bodhisattva's responsibility to protect beings from harm is not limited to declining to enable it, but extends, where genuine capacity allows, to actively ending harm already underway. This closes the entire category of benefit to others; the treatise turns next to giving considered from the angle of a bodhisattva's own integrity, opening with the first of its thirty-one points — never plundering one's own family's property in order to give, illustrated through a story of a mother reborn as a beast of burden to repay an old debt.

23. Milestone: A New Angle Opens — Never Plundering One's Own Family, and a Mother Reborn as a Donkey

The treatise turns to giving weighed from the angle of a bodhisattva's own integrity, unfolding into thirty-one points, the first of which forbids ever seizing property belonging to one's own parents, spouse, or household staff in order to give it away, or coercing them into distress so that a gift can be made — illustrated through a striking classical account of a mother reborn as a family's own donkey to repay a debt, her cries of pain under the whip revealed, in a son's dream, to be exactly what they were.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，終不侵奪父母、妻子、奴婢、僕使、親戚、眷屬所有財物，持用布施。亦不逼惱父母、妻子、奴婢、僕使、親戚、眷屬，以所施物施來求者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never seize property belonging to their parents, spouse, servants, or relatives in order to give it away. Nor do they coerce their parents, spouse, servants, or relatives into distress in order to give what belongs to them to someone who asks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a classical devotional text's own well-known account: a family near Nanjing kept a donkey that had labored for them faithfully for sixteen years; the family's son, in a moment of impatience on the road, struck the exhausted animal hard enough that it cried out in pain. That night, he dreamed his own late mother appeared to him, explaining that she had, in life, secretly taken four ounces of silver belonging to his father, and had been reborn as this very donkey to repay that debt through sixteen years of labor — her time now finally complete, she asked only that he strike her no more. Waking, the son never raised a hand against the donkey again, caring for it gently until it died of old age.

He draws the lesson directly: even property belonging to one's own family carries a debt of its own, invisible though it usually remains, and taking it without genuine consent — even from someone as close as a parent, even for the ostensibly generous purpose of giving it to someone else — still causes real harm and still bears its own consequence, exactly as the classical teaching elsewhere notes that a large resulting merit from such giving tends to come paired with a body that suffers recurring illness. The lesson, he stresses, holds for family exactly as it holds for strangers: nothing is truly free simply because it happens to sit within reach. This closes the first of the thirty-one points on giving weighed by a bodhisattva's own integrity; wealth honestly gained, and giving that never requires breaking a precept to accomplish, follow next.

24. Milestone: Wealth Honestly Gained, Precepts Never Broken, Equal Regard for Every Recipient

Three further points on integrity in giving: wealth used for giving must itself be gained through fair and lawful means, never through fraud or force; a bodhisattva bound by monastic precepts never violates them for the sake of a gift, however generous the impulse; and giving itself must rest on genuinely equal regard for every recipient, without silently ranking friend above stranger, the virtuous above the flawed, or the fortunate above the suffering.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，以其正法、以無卒暴積集財物而行惠施，不以非法、不以卒暴，亦不逼迫損惱於他而行惠施。又諸菩薩，若在諸佛聖教出家，終不違越所有學處而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas give using wealth gathered lawfully and without violence, never through unlawful means, violence, or by oppressing and harming others. Bodhisattvas who have gone forth in the Buddha's teaching never violate their precepts for the sake of giving.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩行布施時，普於一切有情之類起平等心，住福田想，而行惠施。終不分別怨親中庸、有德有失、劣等勝品、有苦有樂品類差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, when giving, give with a mind of equal regard toward every kind of being, regarding each as a field for planting merit, never distinguishing enemy from friend from stranger, the virtuous from the flawed, the lowly from the exalted, or the suffering from the joyful.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes wealth gained through fraud or violence taints any giving built on it, however generous the gesture, since the treatise elsewhere describes such tainted giving as ripening into an animal rebirth even amid outward comfort. On equal regard, he resolves an apparent tension in scripture: some passages rank giving to more advanced practitioners above giving to lesser ones, emphasizing the field's own worth, while others praise giving to a suffering animal above giving to a buddha, emphasizing the giver's own depth of compassion in that moment — both true, depending on which side of the exchange is being highlighted, but the deepest teaching is that a mind of genuinely equal regard, held toward whoever happens to be in front of one, produces exactly the same vast merit regardless of who receives it, since ultimate reality itself draws no such distinctions. This closes wealth's honest origin, unbroken precepts, and equal regard; keeping faith with what was promised, in both quantity and quality, follows next.

25. Giving Exactly as Promised — Never Shrunk, Never Downgraded

Two further points on keeping faith with a promise: whatever amount was promised is given in full, never quietly reduced, and may only ever be matched or increased; and whatever quality of object was promised is likewise honored in full, never secretly substituted for something inferior, though a bodhisattva who promised something modest may certainly upgrade it later if better becomes available.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，如先所說、如先所許，終無減少施來求者，唯有施彼或等、或增。又諸菩薩，終無先許勝妙財物，後施下劣；唯有先許下劣財物，若有勝妙後施勝妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never give less than what was previously stated or promised to someone who asks, only ever giving the same amount or more. Bodhisattvas never promise a fine object and then give something inferior; only, having promised something modest, may they later give something finer if it becomes available.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a classical scripture's own list of three ways giving fails to bear its proper fruit: pledging generously at first, when moved by enthusiasm, and then quietly scaling the gift back once practical circumstances change; choosing deliberately inferior goods to give away rather than what is genuinely fitting; and giving something, only to regret it afterward, wishing it had been kept instead — all three undermine what would otherwise have been a genuinely fruitful act of generosity. This closes keeping faith with a promise, in both amount and quality; giving entirely free of resentment, and never boasting of one's own generosity afterward, follow next.

26. Milestone: Giving Free of Resentment, and Eight Faults That Silently Undo a Gift's Merit

Genuine giving requires an inner state entirely free of ill will toward the recipient, holding steady goodwill and patient compassion even toward someone who has personally wronged the giver; and, just as important, giving that is never followed by self-congratulation — a classical list of eight subtle faults, from resenting a recipient afterward to expecting a favor in return, each capable of quietly erasing merit that would otherwise have been genuinely earned.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不以異意、不以憤怒撓濁之心而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give with a changed heart toward the recipient, nor with a mind disturbed and clouded by anger.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，終不施已而自稱讚，數數告言：我於汝所曾行如是如是惠施，攝受長養濟拔於汝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never, after giving, praise themselves for it, repeatedly telling the recipient: I have done such-and-such generosity for you, gathering, nurturing, and rescuing you.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a scripture's warning that even a great practitioner of giving, if quick to anger and careless of genuine mindfulness, may still be reborn as a powerful yet lower-realm being — vast generosity does not by itself guarantee an unblemished result if resentment accompanies it. He then lists a classical catalogue of eight faults that, even where actual giving has occurred, prevent the giver from ever encountering a genuine teacher or noble being as a result: regretting the gift upon later noticing some flaw in the recipient; giving without equal regard from the start; expecting the recipient to perform some service in return; boasting of one's own generosity afterward; first claiming to have nothing to give and only later relenting; verbally abusing the recipient after giving; demanding the gift be repaid twofold; and giving rise to lingering doubt about whether the gift was even appropriate. Any one of these, he notes, quietly undoes what would otherwise have been a genuinely fruitful act. This closes giving free of resentment and self-praise; giving with genuine respect, and giving that never tires of a difficult recipient, follow next.

27. Milestone: Genuine Respect in Giving, and Never Growing Weary of a Difficult Recipient

Two further points: even toward the lowliest recipient, a gift is never simply flung over carelessly without basic respect, let alone toward someone of genuine virtue; and even a recipient behaving badly — undisciplined, agitated, hurling abuse at the very moment of receiving help — never earns weariness or withdrawal, since a bodhisattva sees through such behavior to an underlying nature merely clouded by affliction, and responds with deepened compassion rather than retreat.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於卑賤者行布施時，尚無不敬撩擲而與，況於有德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, giving even to the lowliest person, never carelessly toss the gift over without respect, let alone when giving to someone of genuine virtue.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若來求者安住種種毀犯邪行、掉舉、躁擾，不自防護，專行罵詈、瞋忿、呵責，終不於彼暫起邪行帶厭倦心而行惠施。唯即於彼了知為諸煩惱所媚，令改本性，深更安住憐愍之心而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, if someone asking for a gift is caught up in undisciplined, agitated conduct, unguarded in speech, hurling abuse or anger or reproach, never respond to that person's momentary misconduct with a weary mind and give reluctantly. Rather, recognizing them as simply led astray by affliction rather than their true nature, they abide all the more deeply in compassion and give.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads genuine respect as extending the equal-regard already established into the actual physical manner of giving — a careless toss can carry real contempt even when the object given is generous, so the manner must match the substance. He treats patience with a difficult recipient as one of giving's more demanding tests: ordinary people instinctively withdraw from someone abusive or undisciplined, yet a bodhisattva reads such behavior as a passing affliction obscuring a fundamentally clean nature, not a fixed identity, and gives with even deeper compassion precisely because that person's need is evidently greater. He raises, and firmly rejects, a folk-heroic model of generosity that seems superficially similar — robbing the wealthy to give to the poor, celebrated in popular legend as righteous — noting that even a wealthy person's own property was rightfully earned through their own past causes, and seizing it without consent, however sympathetic the intended beneficiary, remains a genuinely unclean act of giving whatever admiration it draws in the world. This closes respectful and patient giving; giving free of superstition, and giving correctly understood as only one provision among six, follow next.

28. Milestone: Giving Free of Superstition, and Not Mistaken for Liberation Itself

Two further points guard against distorted views entangling giving itself: never treating killing as somehow lawful simply because it happens within a ritual offering, and never restricting genuine giving to supposedly auspicious days as though ordinary days were unfit for it; and never mistaking the practice of giving, however perfected, for complete freedom from desire in either its worldly or ultimate sense, since giving is only one provision supporting that freedom, not freedom itself.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不由惡見妄有執取而行惠施，謂如廣大暴惡祠祀。不計殺生布施為法，亦不妄取吉祥瑞應相應相狀而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give while clinging to some distorted view — for instance, treating the killing done in some grand, violent sacrificial rite as though it were lawful conduct, nor wrongly insisting on auspicious signs or occasions before giving.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，終不妄計唯一切種極善清淨而行惠施，即是世間及出世間離欲清淨。唯審了知所行布施，但是離欲清淨資糧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never wrongly assume that giving, however excellently and purely practiced, is itself worldly or ultimate freedom from desire. They understand clearly that the giving they practice is only a provision supporting that freedom, not the freedom itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the superstition point as directly countering the widespread belief, still common in folk religion, that offering a sacrificial killing to a deity secures good fortune, or that generosity practiced only on particular calendar days somehow counts for more than the same generosity offered any other day — in genuine practice, he stresses, every day is equally fit for giving. On mistaking giving for liberation, he notes the six perfections are cultivated together precisely because no single one, giving included, accomplishes the whole path alone; giving purifies attachment to possessions specifically, while genuine freedom from desire, worldly or ultimate, requires the discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom that giving alone cannot supply. This closes freedom from superstition and from mistaking giving for the whole path; giving without expectation of a personal reward, and unwavering confidence in exactly what kind of fruit each gift produces, follow next.

29. Milestone: Not Giving for a Reward, and the Four Ways a Gift's Fruit and Its Use Can Combine

Genuine giving is never oriented toward securing a personal reward at all, dedicated instead entirely toward swiftly reaching awakening; and a bodhisattva holds unwavering confidence in giving's own specific fruits — food yielding strength, clothing yielding fine appearance, a vehicle yielding ease, a lamp yielding clear sight — while recognizing, through a classical fourfold distinction, that a gift's resulting merit and a person's ability to actually enjoy that merit do not always travel together.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不觀其果而行布施，一切布施皆為迴向速證無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give while eyeing some resulting reward; all giving is dedicated toward swiftly realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，如實了知一切品類所行布施，一切品類施果異熟，深生信解，不由他緣、非他所引而行布施。謂施飲食能感大力，施諸衣服能感妙色，施諸車乘能感快樂，施諸燈明能感淨眼，如是等類廣說應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, truly understanding every kind of giving and the specific ripened fruit each kind produces, hold deep confidence in this, giving neither because led nor induced by anyone else. Giving food yields great strength; giving clothing yields fine appearance; giving a vehicle yields ease; giving light yields clear eyesight — and so on in detail, as should be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a scripture's own pointed contrast: a bodhisattva's giving seeks neither fine appearance nor wealth, nor even rebirth among gods or humans, but only unsurpassed awakening — and precisely because the aim is so vast, even a modest gift given with this motivation yields immeasurable merit, since the goal itself is immeasurable. He then unpacks a classical fourfold distinction worth holding onto: some giving yields merit the giver can never actually enjoy — giving without real sincerity, not with one's own hand, or with a half-hearted attitude, produces a result that remains curiously unusable, like vast wealth a careless giver somehow never gets to spend; some enjoyment comes without any merit accruing to the one enjoying it — using a resource someone else provided, such as a citizen enjoying goods a king supplies, or, more fruitfully, someone who never had anything to give themselves but rejoiced sincerely in another's giving, and so shares a portion of that giver's own eventual result; some giving yields both — sincere, respectful giving of one's own accord, like the historical almsgiver Anāthapiṇḍika's own careful generosity, produces merit the giver both owns and can genuinely enjoy, the most complete outcome; and some giving yields neither, which the classical tradition applies specifically to a fully realized sage's own giving, since a mind entirely free of clinging generates no further karmic result to either possess or enjoy at all. This closes giving free of any eye toward personal reward and giving's confidence in its specific fruits; giving free of the fear of poverty, and giving that always suits the occasion, follow next.

30. Giving Free of the Fear of Poverty, and Never a Concealed or Improper Gift

Giving should never be driven by anxious fear of one's own future poverty, but by genuine compassion alone; and whatever is given to a monastic recipient must never conceal something the recipient would object to — food secretly mixed with onion, meat, or alcohol against someone's known abstinence — nor place a monastic in a setting genuinely unsuited to their calling, such as a wedding celebration, since any of these would fail to actually serve the recipient's own path.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不為怖畏自身貧窮而行布施，唯由悲愍眾生意樂而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give out of fear of their own future poverty, but purely from a heart of compassion for beings.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩於來求者，終不施與不合儀物。……又不告白不令覺知，如棄捨法施糜飯等。謂不食葱者，施以葱雜葱染飲食；不食肉者，施以肉雜肉染飲食；不飲酒者，施以酒雜酒染飲食。或復處置不合儀式所有事業而行布施。如是等類不合儀施，菩薩不為。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never give an improper object to someone who asks. ... Nor do they give without disclosure, concealing what is actually mixed into rice porridge or the like — giving onion-laced food to one who abstains from onion, meat-laced food to one who abstains from meat, or alcohol-laced food to one who abstains from alcohol. Nor do they place a recipient in circumstances genuinely unsuited to their calling while giving. None of this does a bodhisattva do.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts genuine giving with a fear-driven motive: scripture does encourage giving partly because even an unfortunate future rebirth becomes more bearable with accumulated merit, but a bodhisattva's own motive is never self-protective in this way, resting solely on compassion for others. He illustrates concealed, improper giving concretely — an unscrupulous vendor adding animal gelatin as a binding agent to what is sold as vegetarian food is exactly the kind of deception meant here — and improper occasions through the plain example of inviting a monastic to attend a wedding banquet, a setting that serves no genuine spiritual purpose for them at all. This closes freedom from fear and from concealed or improper gifts; never making a petitioner wait, and giving entirely free of any ulterior motive, follow next.

31. Milestone: Never Making a Petitioner Wait, and Four Kinds of Giving Entirely Free of Ulterior Motive

A bodhisattva never keeps a petitioner returning again and again, weary and dependent, before finally giving — the gift comes the moment it is asked for; and giving itself rests on none of four common ulterior motives: never for worldly fame or praise, never expecting the recipient's gratitude in return, never angling for a favorable rebirth as a reward, and never as a manipulative tactic to win someone's trust for some other purpose.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不令求者數數來求，往還親附，隨順繫屬，稽留疲倦，然後方施，唯暫來求即便施與。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never make a petitioner come again and again, growing dependent and weary, before finally giving; the moment someone comes to ask, the gift is given.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不依世間名聲讚頌而行布施，不依於他反報恩德而行布施，不依帝釋、魔王、輪王、自在等果而行布施，亦復不為誑誘他故而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give for the sake of worldly fame or praise, nor expecting the recipient's gratitude in return, nor for the sake of a future rebirth as a great ruler among gods or humans, nor for the sake of deceiving or manipulating another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats making a petitioner wait as a subtle form of cruelty dressed up as generosity, since deliberately prolonging someone's need in order to enjoy their continued dependence or attention undercuts genuine giving at its root. He unpacks the four motives to avoid directly: seeking recognition from kings, ministers, or wealthy patrons who will then honor the giver for their generosity; expecting the recipient to eventually repay the favor; giving in hope of a glorious future rebirth as a ruler among gods or an emperor among humans; and, most troublingly, giving a small kindness now purely to win someone's affection and dependence before turning against them later — a manipulative tactic the treatise names outright as something a bodhisattva never resorts to, whatever else the wider world may practice. This closes prompt giving and giving free of ulterior motive; a generous heart regardless of one's actual means, and never using kindness as a tool of manipulation or division, follow next.

32. A Generous Heart Regardless of Means, and Never Sowing Division Through a Gift

Three further points: a bodhisattva's heart remains expansive in giving even with very little to give, all the more so with abundance; a bodhisattva never offers small kindness now purely to win someone's trust and dependence, only to turn against them once that trust is secured; and a bodhisattva never gives in order to sow division — buying a village's, or a nation's, loyalty away from its rightful ruler toward oneself instead.

Chinese Source

又不狹劣而行惠施。謂財雖少，尚廣心施，何況財多。又不誑他而行惠施。謂先於彼少行惠施，令起愛著、令親附已，然後傾滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nor is giving ever narrow or meager: even with little wealth, one still gives with an expansive heart, how much more so with abundant wealth. Nor does one give deceptively: first offering small kindness to someone so as to draw out their attachment and dependence, only to then destroy them.

Chinese Source

又復不為乖離於他而行惠施。謂我以施乖離村邑、村邑一分，乖離國土、國土一分，令背其主而來屬我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nor does one give in order to sow division: giving so as to draw a village, or part of one, or a territory, or part of one, away from its rightful ruler and toward oneself instead.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the first point as a matter of inner disposition rather than outward scale — a genuinely generous heart gives expansively in spirit whatever the actual amount, while a stingy heart gives grudgingly however much it has to spare. He names the second point plainly for what it is: a calculated cruelty dressed as kindness, luring someone into dependence purely to exploit or discard them later, a tactic common enough in worldly dealings that the treatise addresses it directly precisely so a bodhisattva learns to recognize and avoid it. He reads the third point as ruling out any use of generosity for political manipulation — using gifts to peel away loyalty that rightfully belongs elsewhere is a corruption of giving's entire purpose, whatever practical advantage it might secure. This closes generosity regardless of means and freedom from manipulation or division; leading by example, and distributing fairly among a crowd, follow next.

33. Leading by Example, and Distributing Fairly Among a Crowd

A bodhisattva never urges others toward generosity while remaining lax about it themselves, practicing diligently and giving first before ever encouraging anyone else to follow; and when a great crowd gathers at once seeking alms, distributes fairly by simple seniority rather than by silently judging who among them is more or less deserving, so that everyone present receives their share.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩翹勤無惰，起策具足，勇銳自嚴。先自行施，後勸他施。非自懈怠，策他勤施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, diligent and never lax, fully rousing themselves, courageous and self-disciplined, first practice giving themselves, and only then encourage others to give — never lax themselves while urging others to be diligent.

Chinese Source

又無量眾同集來乞，如實了知持戒、犯戒，隨其長幼以次而坐，從上至下周旋往返，窮諸施物分布與之。

Ru-Yi Translation

When countless beings gather together seeking alms, knowing truly who among them keeps or has broken precepts, one nonetheless seats them simply by seniority, moving from the front to the back and around again, exhausting every gift by distributing it among them all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the plain wisdom behind leading by example: teaching through action rather than mere instruction carries a persuasive weight that words alone rarely match, and a bodhisattva who preaches generosity while remaining stingy themselves undercuts their own teaching entirely. He reads fair distribution among a crowd as deliberately setting aside private moral judgment about who deserves more — even knowing some in the crowd keep precepts well and others poorly, the bodhisattva distributes by simple seniority, ensuring the gift itself never becomes an occasion for anyone to feel excluded or judged. This closes leading by example and fair distribution; giving without limit, and never harming anyone in the course of it, follow next.

34. Giving Without Artificial Limit, and Never Harming Anyone in the Course of It

A bodhisattva possessing genuinely abundant wealth never rations giving down to some arbitrary, limited amount, but gives to the fullest extent that wealth allows; and, whatever the scale of giving, never causes harm to anyone in the process — never cursing, striking, threatening, humiliating, binding, imprisoning, wounding, or expelling one being in order to give to another.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩現有無量廣多財物，終不行於有量之施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who genuinely possess vast and abundant wealth never give only some artificially limited amount.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩不損惱他而行惠施。謂不訶罵、捶打、恐怖、毀辱、縛害、拘禁、斫刺、驅擯於此，而施於彼。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not harm one being in order to give to another — not through cursing, striking, threatening, humiliating, binding, imprisoning, wounding, or expelling one being in order to give the resulting gain to someone else.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads giving without artificial limit as simply matching effort to genuine capacity — a bodhisattva of real means gives to the full extent of that means rather than holding back out of habit or caution, since the whole point of accumulating wealth as a bodhisattva is precisely to give it away. He reads never harming one being to benefit another as ruling out any giving whose resources come at another's direct expense, an echo of the earlier prohibition against seizing family property to give away, now extended to any being whatsoever. This closes giving without limit and free of harm; joy before, purity during, and no regret after giving, together with the refusal to ever give a counterfeit gift, follow next.

35. Joy Before, Purity During, No Regret After — And Never a Counterfeit Gift

Genuine giving carries a settled emotional arc across its entire course: real joy in the anticipation of giving, a mind free of any clinging to self, recipient, or gift while actually giving, and complete absence of regret afterward; and, whatever is given, it is never a counterfeit substituted for what a recipient genuinely seeks — no imitation gem handed to someone hoping for the real thing.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，施前意悅，施時心淨，施後無悔。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas feel genuine joy before giving, hold a mind free of clinging while giving, and feel no regret whatsoever after giving.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩不以諂詭而行惠施。謂終不以非實末尼、真珠、琉璃、珂貝、璧玉、珊瑚等寶，而施希望真實寶者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give deceptively: never giving a counterfeit gem, pearl, lapis, coral, or similar treasure to someone hoping for a genuine one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the three-stage emotional arc as directly reflecting the threefold emptiness already established at the very opening of this chapter — anticipating a gift with genuine joy, since it advances the provisions for awakening; giving with a mind that clings to neither giver, recipient, nor gift as ultimately real; and feeling no regret afterward, since regret would imply the gift itself was somehow a loss rather than a gain. Refusing a counterfeit gift he treats as simple honesty extended into giving itself — a fake treasure handed to someone hoping for the genuine article is no gift at all, merely deception wearing generosity's appearance. This closes the emotional arc of giving and freedom from deception; the profound teaching that giving is, at bottom, simply returning what was never one's own to begin with, follows next.

36. Milestone: Giving as Simply Returning What Was Never One's Own

A striking reframing closes this long account: a bodhisattva mentally regards all personal wealth, however much or little, as already given over to every being from the very start, so that when someone actually comes to ask for something, they are, in truth, merely taking back what was already theirs, and the bodhisattva, in giving, is doing nothing more than returning it — dissolving, at a stroke, any lingering sense of loss, sacrifice, or personal ownership in the act of giving at all.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩所有財物若少、若多，無不運心先施一切。後來求者，如取自財，菩薩與時，如還彼物。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, regarding all their wealth, whether little or much, have already, in mind, given it entirely to everyone from the start. When someone later comes seeking it, it is as though they simply take what is already their own; and when the bodhisattva gives, it is as though returning that object to them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as one of giving's deepest teachings on genuine selflessness: a bodhisattva who has fully internalized the absence of a fixed, separate self has nothing left to call exclusively their own in the first place, holding all possessions instead as something already belonging to every being, entrusted to the bodhisattva only for safekeeping until actually needed. Framed this way, giving is never really "giving" at all in the ordinary sense of a sacrifice made — it is simply returning something to its rightful, universal owner, exactly the way a monastic's own robes and bowl are traditionally understood to belong not to that individual monastic but to the entire monastic community across all directions, held only in trust. He notes this reframing also quietly resolves any occasion for hesitation: if a bodhisattva genuinely judges, in a given moment, that giving something away would not actually serve the asker, they may simply explain that the object in question is not, in fact, theirs alone to release — since it was never truly private property to begin with, only something entrusted for a time. This closes giving reframed as return; giving free of every remaining fault — mistimed, careless, improperly mannered, or distracted — follows next.

37. Free of Every Remaining Fault — Timed Well, Properly Mannered, With an Undistracted Mind

A final cluster of faults to avoid: giving at a genuinely fitting time rather than an ill-considered one; giving with a mind clean whether the act is carried out personally or entrusted to someone else acting on the bodhisattva's behalf; giving in a manner that suits both worldly propriety and the bodhisattva's own precepts; and giving with a mind fully present and gathered, never distracted or careless.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩應時而施，不以非時；自他淨施，非不清淨；合儀而施，不以非儀；無亂心施，不以散亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas give at a fitting time, not an unfitting one; give with a mind clean whether acting themselves or through another, never uncleanly; give in a manner that is proper, never improper; and give with an undistracted mind, never a scattered one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads clean giving whether personal or delegated as covering the case where a bodhisattva asks someone else to carry out an act of giving on their behalf — the same volition of non-greed and the same deeds of body and speech that define giving in its own intrinsic nature apply equally whether the bodhisattva's own hands perform the act or another's do, so long as the underlying intention remains genuinely clean. He reads giving with an undistracted mind as simply the natural expression of how seriously a bodhisattva regards this practice — approached with the same earnest, undivided attention given to any genuinely important undertaking, never carried out absentmindedly. This closes this cluster of remaining faults; never mocking or embarrassing a petitioner, and giving freely to whoever simply helps themselves, closing all thirty-one points together, follow next.

38. Milestone: Never Mocking a Petitioner, Giving Freely to Whoever Helps Themselves — Thirty-One Points Conclude

The final two points close this entire account: a bodhisattva, seeing someone come to ask for something, never mocks or belittles them, never causes them shame or embarrassment, never meets them with a frown, but with an open, welcoming expression and a warm greeting, giving swiftly rather than making them wait; and provides freely, without even being asked, for whoever simply comes and takes what they need from what has been openly set out — closing, with these two, the full thirty-one points on giving weighed by a bodhisattva's own genuine integrity.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩見來求者，終不嗤笑，亦不輕弄，亦不令其面生赧愧；亦不顰蹙，舒顏平視，前笑先言；終不稽留，疾疾而施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, seeing someone come to ask, never mock them, never belittle them, never cause them shame; nor do they frown, but greet them with an open expression and a warm word first; and never make them wait, giving swiftly.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，他雖不求，自恣求者必有所求，稱須而與，常開求者歡情自取。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, even when no one has specifically asked, knowing whoever comes freely seeking something genuinely needs it, provide exactly what is needed, keeping it always openly available for whoever wishes to take it for themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a classical scripture's own vivid warning behind the first point: frowning at someone who has come to ask for help is, in its own words, like opening the very door to the hungry ghost realm within oneself — the frown betrays an underlying stinginess, and stinginess is precisely the affliction that ripens into that realm's own particular suffering. A bodhisattva instead greets every petitioner the same warm way, dignity intact on both sides, and gives without delay. The final point closes the entire account with a picture of giving made genuinely effortless and unconditional — resources simply kept available for anyone drawn to take them, no formal request even required.

This closes all thirty-one points on giving weighed by a bodhisattva's own integrity, and with it, the treatise's entire, remarkably thorough account of comprehensive giving from every angle — benefit to others and benefit to oneself alike. The treatise turns next to giving guided by genuine skillful wisdom, the third of the nine aspects into which giving as a whole is organized.

39. Milestone: Giving With Genuine Skillful Wisdom Opens

The treatise turns to giving guided by genuine skillful wisdom, distinguished from giving guided by a distorted or unwise view, and unfolding across several distinct circumstances — from giving when one has nothing material to give, through giving material wealth, teaching the true dharma, resolving another's doubts, and giving shaped by an especially deep or elevated motivation.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，不以惡慧而行布施，常以巧慧而行布施。云何菩薩巧慧布施？

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not give guided by a distorted or unwise view, but constantly give guided by genuine skillful wisdom. What is a bodhisattva's giving guided by skillful wisdom?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the range of circumstances this next section will cover, since giving's outward circumstances vary so widely: giving when there is genuinely no material wealth on hand to give, giving material wealth itself, giving the true teaching, giving through clarity that resolves another's confusion, giving shaped by an unusually deep or elevated intention, and giving that specifically addresses some inner obstacle a recipient is struggling against — each circumstance calling for its own particular expression of the same underlying wisdom. This closes the setup; the treatise's detailed account of skillful giving in each of these specific circumstances follows next.

40. Milestone: Skillful Giving With Wealth — Prioritizing the Desperate, Gently Declining the Comfortable

Before any petitioner even arrives, a bodhisattva first resolves in advance how to act should two very different petitioners come at once — one comfortable and secure, another destitute and without any support at all — giving fully to both if wealth allows it, and if not, tactfully declining the comfortable petitioner while giving everything to the one in genuine crisis, using words gracious enough that even the one turned away leaves without resentment.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩現有種種可施財物，求者未至，先發是心：設二求者俱來我所，一是安樂、非貧非賤、有依有怙；二是危苦、是貧是賤、無依無怙。我於爾時，應自揆量所有財物，若堪於二充足滿願，即應俱施滿願充足。如其財物不堪於二充足滿願，即應方便發遣安樂、非貧非賤、有依有怙，盡己所有施彼危苦、是貧是賤、無依無怙。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, possessing various objects that could be given, resolve in advance, before any petitioner arrives: should two petitioners come to me at once, one comfortable, neither poor nor lowly, with support and protection, and the other in genuine crisis, poor and lowly, without any support or protection, I should weigh my own wealth. If it can fully satisfy both, I give fully to satisfy both. If it cannot satisfy both, I should skillfully send away the one who is comfortable, neither poor nor lowly, with support and protection, and give everything I have to the one in genuine crisis, poor and lowly, without support or protection.

Chinese Source

於安樂等諸來求者，既無力能足滿其願，先當方便發意思惟，辭謝發遣：我此施物，於危苦等先捨先許，故今與之；非我於汝無樂施心，但更無力。唯願賢首，勿於我所嫌恨棄背。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward a comfortable petitioner one lacks the means to fully satisfy, one first considers carefully how to graciously decline: this gift was already promised in advance to someone in genuine crisis, which is why it goes to them now; it is not that I have no wish to give to you, only that I have no further means. I only hope, good friend, that you will not hold this against me or turn away in resentment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the deliberate advance planning here as itself a mark of genuine wisdom — deciding the principle calmly beforehand, rather than scrambling to choose under the pressure of two petitioners actually standing in front of one at once, ensures the decision is made clearly and fairly rather than by whichever request happens to feel more urgent in the moment. He notes the graciousness of the declining script matters as much as the decision itself: framing the refusal as an earlier commitment already made, rather than as a judgment against the person being turned away, allows even the one who receives nothing to leave with dignity intact, addressed respectfully as a worthy friend rather than dismissed. This closes prioritizing genuine crisis over comfort; a strikingly resourceful technique for gently converting a genuinely stingy household follows next.

41. Milestone: Skillfully Converting a Miser — "Borrow From My House, You Will Lose Nothing"

Encountering a household so miserly it has never once given anything to anyone, a bodhisattva approaches with real tact, inviting the miser to send any future petitioners straight to the bodhisattva's own storehouse to take whatever they need, freely and at no cost to the miser at all — letting the stingy household experience the genuine joy of having given, without actually parting with anything, and gradually, through this taste of what generosity feels like, drawing them step by step toward truly giving of their own accord.

Chinese Source

菩薩即便往詣其舍，慰問安不？恭順方便，告言：汝來，我不令汝庫藏減盡，而於現前作大饒益。我家現有廣多財寶、廣多施物，為滿我施波羅蜜多，若有求者來到汝所，勿令空返，可至我家取諸財寶，隨意施與。……或有求者來至我所，我行施時，汝於此施當生隨喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

The bodhisattva goes directly to that household, offers a warm greeting, and, with respectful, skillful tact, says: come, I will not let your own storehouse diminish in the least, while still accomplishing something of great benefit right now. My own household has abundant wealth and abundant gifts ready to give — to help complete my own perfection of giving, should anyone come seeking help at your door, do not let them leave empty-handed; simply send them to my house to take whatever wealth they need, freely. ... Or should someone come to me instead while I am giving, you are welcome to rejoice together in that very act of giving.

Chinese Source

彼聞是已，便生欣悅：於我庫藏既無所減，復得稱彼善男子心，故應隨順成辦所作。菩薩如是令彼漸種當來調伏慳吝種子，由慧為先善巧方便，令漸修習自捨少財，依下無貪進得中品，依中無貪進得上品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hearing this, the miser feels genuine delight: since their own storehouse suffers no loss at all, and this also fulfills the wish of that good bodhisattva, they willingly go along and carry it out. In this way, the bodhisattva plants, little by little, the seed that will eventually tame the miser's own stinginess — through wisdom-guided skillful means, gradually leading them to give away a small amount of their own wealth, progressing from a lesser degree of non-greed to a middling degree, and from a middling degree to a genuinely great one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the psychological subtlety here: a household gripped by real stinginess cannot simply be persuaded by argument to start giving, since the fear of loss itself is the obstacle, so the bodhisattva sidesteps that fear entirely by removing loss from the equation altogether — the miser experiences the social credit and quiet satisfaction of having "provided" for a petitioner's need, while nothing actually leaves their own storehouse. He traces the gradual arc this sets in motion: over time, as the miser witnesses the gratitude and joy on a petitioner's face again and again, occasionally their own household happens to have exactly what is needed on hand, and they begin offering a small item of their own without even being asked — a first crack in a lifetime of stinginess — which, repeated and encouraged, deepens naturally from occasional small offerings into eventually shouldering an entire act of giving unprompted. This closes the technique for converting genuine stinginess; helping fellow renunciants make their own offerings, and never exposing a petitioner's dishonesty, follow next.

42. Milestone: Helping Fellow Renunciants Give, and Never Exposing a Petitioner's Deception

Toward fellow renunciants who are either genuinely stingy or simply lack the means to give, a bodhisattva supplies the actual resources needed for an offering to the three jewels, but lets them personally carry out the act of giving rather than doing it for them, so the merit and the growth genuinely belong to them; and toward any petitioner at all, including one plainly acting in bad faith — a swindling merchant, say, or someone secretly taking more than was ever offered — a bodhisattva neither exposes nor rebukes the deception, sometimes quietly rejoicing in the theft itself as a way of sparing the offender its karmic consequence.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若親教師及軌範師、共住弟子同梵行者，性是慳貪，是慳貪類；或性雖非慳貪種類，而闕資財，所欲匱乏。菩薩欲寄佛法僧田樹修布施褔業事時，捨所施物與彼令作，己自不為。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, if their own preceptor, instructing teacher, fellow resident, or fellow renunciant is by nature stingy, or is not stingy by nature but genuinely lacks the means, when wishing to cultivate the merit of giving toward the field of the three jewels, give them the object to be offered and let them personally carry out the act, rather than doing it themselves.

Chinese Source

復有商人為性矯詐，欲行欺誑，菩薩知已，尚掩其過不令他知，況觸於彼。稱滿其願，令無羞慚，踊躍無畏，歡喜而去。復有矯詐欺誑，菩薩初不覺知，後時乃覺；雖復覺知，不以此事舉發彼人，亦不訶責為作憶念。但生悲愍：彼於我所悞行如是不與取事，我今隨喜，令彼無罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Should a merchant, deceitful by nature, attempt to swindle the bodhisattva, the bodhisattva, aware of this, still conceals the person's fault rather than exposing it, let alone confronting them — fulfilling their wish so completely that they feel no shame, leaving joyfully and without fear. And should someone deceive the bodhisattva in a way not noticed until later, even once recognized, the bodhisattva does not expose or reprimand them for it, but simply feels compassion: this person has mistakenly taken what was not given from me; I now rejoice in it, so that they incur no fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads giving fellow renunciants the means rather than performing the act oneself as respecting their own genuine share in the merit — the whole point of supplying the resources is so that they, too, cultivate their own roots of generosity, which handing over an already-completed act of giving would quietly deny them. He treats the deception cases as a particularly striking expression of compassion extended even toward someone actively wronging the bodhisattva: a con artist exposed publicly suffers real humiliation on top of whatever wrong they have done, so the bodhisattva simply lets the deception pass unremarked, sparing them that additional suffering; and where theft has already occurred unnoticed, retroactively "rejoicing" in it — treating the taken object as though freely given after the fact — genuinely absolves the thief of what would otherwise have been an act of taking what was not given, since consent, even offered belatedly, changes the nature of the act entirely. This closes both cases; giving skillfully when a bodhisattva has no material wealth at all to give follows next.

43. Milestone: Giving With No Material Wealth at All — Four Methods

A bodhisattva with nothing material on hand to give is never thereby prevented from genuinely giving, through four distinct methods: earning wealth quickly through some previously learned skill or craft in order to give it away, teaching the true dharma so skillfully that even the poor and the stingy are moved to give, directing a petitioner toward a wealthy and genuinely generous patron, and personally assisting another's own act of giving so that no fault of neglect, poor quality, favoritism, or carelessness spoils it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若現無有可施財物，先所串習彼彼世間工巧業處作意現前，少用功力，多集財寶，施諸眾生。是諸菩薩，或復為他種種美妙、善巧言詞宣說正法，令貧苦者尚樂行施，況富樂者；令慳貪者猶能惠施，況習施者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, if presently without any material object to give, bring to mind some previously mastered worldly craft, and with little effort gather wealth to give to beings. Bodhisattvas also teach the true dharma to others in language both doctrinally sound and skillfully expressed, moving even the poor and suffering to delight in giving, how much more so the wealthy and comfortable; moving even the stingy to give, how much more so those already accustomed to it.

Chinese Source

或有淨信多饒財寶常樂施家，數教乞者往彼求索，令其布施。或彼惠捨修福業時，躬詣其所，翹勤無惰，起策具足，深心歡喜，隨力隨能身助、語助，令施求者得善滿足。以彼施時，事力闕尠，或惡供贍，或墮朋黨，或不恭敬，或念忘失，由善助故，斯過皆無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, knowing a household of genuine faith, abundant wealth, and constant delight in giving, one repeatedly directs petitioners there to ask, so that household can give. Or, when another is carrying out an act of giving as their own merit, one personally attends, tirelessly diligent, wholeheartedly joyful, helping with body and speech according to one's own ability, so that both giver and petitioner are genuinely satisfied — since, without such help, giving often suffers from insufficient hands, poor-quality offerings, favoritism toward some over others, careless or disrespectful handling, or a distracted, forgetful mind; through this skillful assistance, none of these faults arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two registers of skillful dharma-teaching named here: language that is doctrinally sound speaks directly and accurately to genuine teaching, while language that is skillfully expressed reaches an audience through story, illustration, and relatable example — some listeners respond best to direct reasoning, others to narrative, and combining both tends to work best of all, since a stingy heart is rarely moved by argument alone but often softens at a well-told story.

He reads assisting another's act of giving as addressing exactly the practical faults that quietly undermine even a well-intentioned donor's own generosity: too few hands to manage a crowd properly, offerings that end up shabby or unclean for lack of proper preparation, unconscious favoritism toward familiar faces over strangers, careless handling that comes across as disrespectful, or a donor's own attention lapsing into distraction partway through — a bodhisattva's genuine, attentive help prevents every one of these, letting the giver's own generosity actually land the way it was meant to. This closes all four methods of giving without material wealth, completing the treatise's account of skillful giving guided by wisdom for bodhisattvas still on the path before the first ground; how giving differs once a bodhisattva has reached the first ground and beyond follows next.

44. Milestone: Ground-Level Bodhisattvas Never Lack Wealth, and Why Teaching the Dharma Surpasses Giving Material Things

Bodhisattvas who have reached the first ground and beyond, their intention already purified beyond the reach of the miserable realms, are reborn again and again with inexhaustible wealth as a matter of course, closing the account of skillful giving for those still on the path before that point — and opens a new topic entirely: giving the true teaching itself, distinguished from material giving across many dimensions, and shown, through a classical balancing image, to be genuinely indispensable yet still incomplete without material practice alongside it.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩已證增上清淨意樂，如已獲得超諸惡趣，如是生生必定獲得無盡財寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who have already realized an elevated, purified intention, having already gone beyond the miserable realms, are in this way reborn life after life with inexhaustible wealth as a matter of course.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the account of material giving by noting a first-ground bodhisattva, returning to help beings, is never troubled by scarcity — abundant wealth simply attends their rebirth, freeing them to undertake giving on any scale their compassion calls for. He then turns to giving the true teaching itself, drawing on a rich classical comparison across many dimensions: material giving is limited in quantity while teaching is boundless in the benefit it can produce; material giving belongs to the desire realm's own concerns while teaching points beyond all three realms entirely; material giving eases circumstance without touching the underlying affliction, while teaching, by contrast, genuinely purifies the mind and moves a being toward liberation itself; anyone, wise or foolish, can give material things, while only genuine wisdom can teach the dharma well; material giving benefits only the giver's own merit, while teaching benefits both teacher and listener at once, since the listener's own reflection cultivates real wisdom; even an unthinking animal can receive material generosity, while only a genuinely receptive mind can take in the teaching; material giving benefits the body alone, while teaching benefits the mind and spirit directly; and material giving, given carelessly, can even deepen craving, while genuine teaching directly counters craving, aversion, and delusion at their root.

Yet he immediately balances this against a striking classical image from a commentary on the Flower Ornament Sūtra: wisdom is like a pair of eyes, while the first five perfections — giving, discipline, patience, diligence, and concentration — are like a blind man's own legs. Legs without eyes can certainly move, but easily stumble into danger; eyes without legs can see the way clearly but never actually walk it. Wisdom without material practice remains merely theoretical, prone to the same errors it claims to see through, while practice without wisdom risks falling into real harm however sincere the effort. Genuine giving therefore always requires both — teaching alone, however superior in principle, cannot substitute for material generosity actually carried out, and material generosity alone, however well-intentioned, remains incomplete without the wisdom that keeps it on course. This closes the general comparison between material and dharma giving; specific guidance on precisely when scripture itself should and should not be given follows next.

45. Milestone: When Scripture Should Never Be Given — Guarding the Teaching From Misuse

Four specific limits protect the teaching from genuine misuse: never orally teaching the true dharma to an outsider seeking only to find fault with it, never handing over a written scripture to someone likely to treat it disrespectfully, never giving to someone whose only intent is to resell it or show it off, and never giving at all to someone not genuinely seeking wisdom — reserved instead for whoever truly and sincerely seeks it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，終不口授求過外道所有正法，亦不施彼所寫經典；知性多貪，求欲衒賣經卷等者，亦不施與；知欲秘藏，亦不施與；不求勝智，亦不施與，必求勝智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas never orally teach the true dharma to an outsider seeking to find fault with it, nor give them a written copy of scripture. Knowing someone of greedy character seeks a scripture only to sell or show it off, this too is not given. Knowing someone wishes only to hoard it away secretly, this too is not given. To one not genuinely seeking excellent wisdom, nothing is given — it is given only to one who genuinely does.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads each restriction as protecting something specific: teaching left unguarded against a hostile outsider risks being twisted into ammunition against the very tradition it comes from; a written text handed to someone careless with it risks real disrespect toward what took a buddha's own sacrifice to produce; and a scripture given to someone seeking only profit or display never actually serves its genuine purpose at all. The single governing question throughout, he notes, is simply whether the recipient is genuinely seeking wisdom — where that sincerity is present, scripture and teaching alike should be given freely and without hesitation. This closes when scripture is properly withheld; the more delicate question of a bodhisattva's own currently needed scripture — when to lend or give it away regardless — follows next.

46. Milestone: A Scripture Already Mastered Is Given Freely — One Still Being Studied Is Read Like a Treasure First

If a bodhisattva has already fully grasped a scripture's meaning, it is given away freely the moment someone asks; but if its meaning is not yet mastered and the bodhisattva still genuinely needs it, the first response is not refusal but resourcefulness — seeking another copy from a fellow practitioner, or personally producing a new copy to give instead — illustrated through a classical, almost devotional account of what genuine study actually looks like, treasuring a text through patient, repeated immersion rather than skimming past it.

Chinese Source

若自了知於經卷等其義已辯，即隨所樂如應施與。若自了知於經卷等其義未辯，為辯義故恆自披轉。如是菩薩若見其餘有經卷等，即應方便轉求施與，或更書寫而施與之。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas know that a scripture's meaning has already been mastered, they give it away as requested, freely. If they know its meaning is not yet mastered, and they still constantly study it in order to grasp that meaning, then, seeing that a fellow practitioner has another copy, they should skillfully arrange for that copy to be given instead, or personally produce a new copy to give.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi pauses on what "constantly studying" a text genuinely means, drawing on a classical scholarly ideal: steeping oneself in a passage again and again rather than rushing past it, approaching the text the way one savors a genuine treasure, turning it over slowly from every angle, and — most importantly — carefully noting rather than skipping over anything still unclear or in doubt, returning to resolve it rather than letting it quietly slide from memory. He cites the nineteenth-century statesman Zeng Guofan's own well-known advice to his students: never move to the next sentence until the one before it is genuinely understood, and never rush through many books rather than truly making even one's own — a scholar with a hundred books skimmed but none mastered has, in the end, no real learning to show for it, while genuine depth on even a single text yields insight that keeps renewing itself.

He recalls, in the same spirit, a modern teacher who spent long hours sheltering from wartime air raids with nothing to do but read, working through the same short classical text a full thousand times over — emerging afterward able to teach it with a depth and authority far exceeding what any formal credential could have supplied, purely through that sustained, patient repetition. He draws the parallel directly to devotional recitation and meditation practice as well: no single repetition of a name or a breath brings realization on its own — it is the same patient return, again and again, that eventually brings the mind to genuine stability. This closes what to do when a copy or a replacement is genuinely available; the more difficult case, where no other copy exists and none can be made in time, follows next.

47. Milestone: Honest Self-Examination Before Declining — Is It Stinginess, or Genuine Need?

When no other copy exists and none can be produced in time, a bodhisattva does not simply decline — first turning inward to honestly examine the actual motive: is this reluctance to give really nothing more than stinginess toward the teaching itself, or quiet resentment toward this particular person, or is it, in fact, a genuine and legitimate ongoing need? If honest examination reveals mere stinginess or personal dislike, the bodhisattva overrides it and gives anyway, since enduring one's own affliction matters less than giving the teaching, and giving would even help dissolve that very affliction.

Chinese Source

若不見餘有經卷等，亦無力能更為書寫，即應審諦觀察自心：勿我於法慳垢纏心，不能施耶？勿我於法別意所礙，不欲施耶？為我於法有勝所須，不應施耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

If no other copy can be found, and there is no ability to produce a new one, one should carefully examine one's own mind: is it that I am gripped by stinginess toward the teaching, and so cannot give it? Is it that some other affliction toward this person obstructs me, and so I do not wish to give it? Or is it that I have a genuine, excellent need for it, and so should not give it?

Chinese Source

如是審諦觀察心已，若自了知，我於此法少有慳纏、別意所礙而不施者，即作是念：我今決定應行法施。設我由此行法施故，於現法中即成癡瘂，不忍煩惱尚應法施，況令闕乏妙智資糧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having carefully examined the mind in this way, if one recognizes that stinginess or some other affliction is genuinely the obstacle preventing giving, one thinks: I should now decisively give the teaching. Even should giving the teaching leave me, in this present life, without the means to further my own understanding, giving it is still called for simply to overcome my own affliction — how much more so, then, when withholding it would leave my own provisions of excellent wisdom lacking in the first place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this honest self-examination as itself a genuine act of practice — the point is never to rationalize a comfortable refusal, but to actually locate whether the reluctance comes from something worth overriding. Stinginess he identifies as the affliction directly opposed to giving itself; a second, related obstacle he names more broadly, citing the treatise's own discipline chapter directly: refusing someone who asks for the teaching out of resentment, irritation, jealousy, or a change of heart mid-conversation, all count equally as this second obstacle, regardless of how they are dressed up as reasonable hesitation.

He reads the conclusion as remarkably bracing: even at the cost of leaving oneself temporarily without a resource one still needed, overcoming stinginess or resentment by giving anyway is worth doing simply because the affliction itself is worth uprooting — and if that were not reason enough, failing to give would also directly stunt the giver's own eventual wisdom, since clinging to a text out of stinginess is itself an obstacle to the very growth that text is meant to support. This closes the case where the honest answer is stinginess or resentment; the more legitimate case — genuine, ongoing need — receives its own careful further examination next.

48. Milestone: When Keeping the Scripture Genuinely Serves More Beings Than Giving It Away

If honest self-examination instead reveals no trace of stinginess or resentment at all, only a genuine ongoing need for the text, a bodhisattva reflects further still: would giving it away actually help dissolve some remaining affliction, complete the provisions of wisdom, or express genuine love for beings more than keeping it would? Concluding, if keeping the text and deepening one's own wisdom through it will eventually serve every being, while giving it away now would help only this one person, declining is entirely faultless — free of regret and fully consistent with a bodhisattva's own precepts.

Chinese Source

又觀察已，若自了知，我於此法無少慳纏，亦無別意，但為成辦勝所須義不應施者，菩薩爾時應更思忖：我持此法施於彼者，為為損害自煩惱耶？為為圓滿智資糧耶？為為愛念諸眾生耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Having examined further, if one recognizes no trace of stinginess or any other obstructing affliction at all, but genuinely should not give it because of an excellent, legitimate need, one should then further consider: in giving this teaching to that person, would I be overcoming my own affliction? Would I be completing my provisions of wisdom? Would I be expressing genuine love for beings?

Chinese Source

若不施彼，便能修集利益安樂一切眾生巧方便智，即為愛念此一眾生及餘一切；若施於彼，唯成愛念此一眾生，非餘一切。菩薩如是如實知已，不施彼者，無罪、無悔，亦不違越菩薩淨戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, by not giving it away, one is able to cultivate the skillful wisdom that benefits and brings peace to all beings, this is expressing genuine love for this one being and for every other being at once; if one gives it away, this expresses love only for this one being, not for all the rest. Bodhisattvas, having genuinely understood this, incur no fault and no regret in declining, and do not violate their own pure precepts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this second round of self-examination as the treatise's own careful safeguard against a subtler danger — using "genuine need" as a convenient excuse once stinginess has already been ruled out. The deciding question is never simply "do I still need this," but whether keeping it genuinely serves a wider purpose: overcoming one's own remaining affliction, completing the wisdom a bodhisattva needs precisely in order to help others more broadly, or expressing a form of love that reaches beyond just the one person currently asking.

He draws out the striking arithmetic underlying the conclusion: a single scripture given away satisfies exactly one petitioner's immediate request, while that same scripture, studied further and genuinely mastered, becomes wisdom the bodhisattva can then offer to countless beings for the rest of their path — the deeper love, paradoxically, sometimes looks like withholding rather than giving. He stresses this reasoning only holds when it is genuinely true and honestly arrived at, not a cover story invented after the fact, which is precisely why the treatise insists on such careful self-examination beforehand rather than simply permitting refusal outright. This closes the case where keeping the text is genuinely justified; the graceful, tactful manner in which such a decline should actually be communicated follows next.

49. How to Gracefully Decline — Never a Blunt Refusal

Even where declining to give a scripture away is genuinely justified, a bodhisattva never simply states the refusal outright — always finding some skillful, considerate way of explaining the situation so the petitioner leaves reassured rather than wounded, exactly as with any other gracious decline already described.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩方便不施？謂諸菩薩不忍直言遣來求者，謂我不能惠施於汝。要當施設方便善巧，曉喻發遣。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do bodhisattvas skillfully decline? Bodhisattvas cannot bear to bluntly tell a petitioner, "I cannot give this to you." They must instead find some skillful, considerate approach, gently explaining the matter so the petitioner departs at ease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this brief but important point as consistent with everything already established about declining any request gracefully — the substance of the decision may be firm, but the delivery must always leave the petitioner's own dignity intact, exactly as with the earlier example of turning away a comfortable petitioner in favor of one in genuine crisis. This closes the manner of graceful decline; the treatise's own remarkable practice for "purifying" ownership of everything a bodhisattva stockpiles for giving — treating it as already belonging to the buddhas of the ten directions — follows next.

50. Milestone: Purifying Ownership — Everything Already Given to the Buddhas of the Ten Directions

A remarkable practice closes this account: a bodhisattva mentally "purifies" ownership of everything they stockpile for future giving by treating it as already given over, in intention, to the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions, retaining only the right to use it rather than genuine ownership — exactly as a monastic ritually transfers ownership of personal robes to a preceptor while continuing to wear them — so that even a large stockpile held in reserve for giving never actually contradicts the deeper spirit of non-attachment, and the merit from this purified intention only grows the more it is reflected upon.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩先於所畜一切資具、一切施物為作淨故，以淨意樂，捨與十方諸佛菩薩。譬如苾芻於己衣物為作淨故，捨與親教、軌範師等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas first purify all the requisites and objects they stockpile for giving, with a genuinely pure intention giving them over to the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions. This is just as a monastic, in order to purify ownership of their own robes, gives them over to a preceptor or instructing teacher.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩淨施因緣，雖復貯畜種種上妙一切資具、一切施物，猶得名為安住聖種，生無量福。常於此福多思惟故，於一切時隨逐增長。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through this cause of purified giving, bodhisattvas, though they still stockpile every kind of fine requisite and object for giving, are nonetheless said to abide in the noble lineage, generating boundless merit. Because they constantly reflect on this merit, it continues to grow at every moment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the parallel directly: a monastic's own robes are, by longstanding convention, formally "given" to a preceptor to purify any lingering attachment to personal ownership, while the monastic continues wearing them purely by that preceptor's permission — the object never actually moves, but the underlying claim to private ownership dissolves entirely. A bodhisattva applies the identical logic to an entire stockpile of goods held in reserve for future giving: since everything is already, in intention, given over to the buddhas and bodhisattvas of the ten directions, the bodhisattva who continues to hold and use these goods does so only as a custodian, never a true owner, which is precisely why amassing considerable resources for giving never actually contradicts non-attachment, however large the stockpile grows.

He explains "abiding in the noble lineage" through its classical fourfold description — contentment with whatever robe, food, or bedding one happens to receive, and delight in both eliminating affliction and cultivating the path — noting all four rest on the same underlying freedom from craving that this purifying intention itself expresses, which is why a bodhisattva practicing this way is said to abide in that same noble lineage regardless of how much is actually stockpiled. He closes by noting the merit from this single purifying intention is not a one-time transaction completed and then forgotten — every subsequent moment spent reflecting on it renews and increases that same merit further, so that a bodhisattva's own inner disposition toward complete non-ownership becomes, quite literally, an ongoing and ever-growing source of merit in its own right. This closes the entire, extensive treatment of comprehensive giving guided by genuine skillful wisdom, the second of the nine aspects of giving in its full and remarkable depth; difficult giving, the third aspect, follows next.

51. Milestone: Observant Giving Closes Dharma-Giving — Freely Given When Fitting, Gracefully Declined When Not

Regarding everything already purified through this mental transfer to the buddhas of the ten directions, a bodhisattva still observes each request carefully: when giving it away genuinely fits what is right, it is given freely, on the reflection that no buddha or bodhisattva ever withholds even the smallest thing from a being in need; but when giving would not actually fit what is right, the bodhisattva gently explains that the object in question already belongs to another and is not theirs to release, while making sure the petitioner never leaves empty-handed, offering instead some other gift of double or triple value with genuine respect.

Chinese Source

恆於一切作淨施物，如佛菩薩所寄護持，見來求者即應觀察。若隨所欲作淨施物惠施彼時，稱當正理，應作是念：諸佛菩薩無有少物於諸眾生而不施者。如是知已，取淨施物施來求者，令所願滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding everything already purified in this way, held only as though entrusted by the buddhas and bodhisattvas for safekeeping, seeing a petitioner arrive, one carefully observes. If giving the purified object exactly as requested genuinely fits what is right, one reflects: no buddha or bodhisattva ever withholds even the smallest thing from beings. Knowing this, one takes the purified object and gives it, fully satisfying the request.

Chinese Source

若觀施時，不稱正理，即應念先作淨施法。告言：賢首！如是等物是他所有，不許施汝。軟言曉喻，方便發遣。或持餘物二倍三倍，恭敬施與，然後發遣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If giving does not fit what is right, one recalls the earlier act of purification and gently explains: good friend, this object belongs to another, and I am not permitted to give it to you — offering this in soft, considerate words. Or one gives some other object worth double or triple, respectfully, before sending the petitioner off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that even after mentally purifying ownership of every possession, a bodhisattva does not thereby give away everything indiscriminately — the earlier careful discernment about what should and should not be given still fully applies, now framed through this same lens of custodianship rather than ownership. He reads the graceful decline as protecting the petitioner from ever feeling personally refused: the object is framed as belonging to someone else entirely, not withheld out of the bodhisattva's own reluctance, and generously compensating with something else of greater value confirms this is genuinely so, since a truly stingy person would never offer more in place of what was asked for. This closes giving the true teaching in its entirety, together with its own careful account of purified ownership; giving through four kinds of unobstructed wisdom follows next.

52. Milestone: Giving Understood Through Four Kinds of Unobstructed Wisdom

A bodhisattva's giving — whether material, teaching, or freedom from fear — is further distinguished through four unobstructed forms of understanding: knowing the many different terms by which giving is described, since the six perfections mutually support one another so thoroughly that giving is sometimes even referred to by discipline's or patience's own name; grasping giving's own intrinsic nature directly; being able to explain its terminology clearly in different words; and being able to reason through its full causes and effects — together marking a level of understanding well beyond ordinary, uninformed generosity.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩於一切施，謂法施、財施、無畏施。若異門、若體相、若釋名、若因果差別，如實了知而行惠施，當知亦名菩薩巧慧而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, regarding every kind of giving — giving the teaching, giving material things, giving freedom from fear — truly understand its different terms, its intrinsic nature, the explanation of its name, and its distinct causes and effects, and give accordingly. This too should be understood as a bodhisattva's giving guided by skillful wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first form of understanding through a striking classical observation: because the six perfections mutually reinforce one another so completely, giving is sometimes described using the very language of discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, or wisdom instead — restraining body and speech while giving draws on discipline, enduring an ungrateful recipient draws on patience, persevering through giving's difficulty draws on diligence, remaining settled while giving draws on concentration, and understanding giving's own genuine causes and effects draws on wisdom itself, so that giving, properly understood, is never really isolated from the other five perfections at all — precisely the nine-aspect framework already set out at this chapter's very opening. He reads the second form, giving's own intrinsic nature, as pointing directly back to the freedom from attachment and clean motivation already described in giving's opening definition. The third, explaining giving's terminology, he ties to a classical commentary's own account of why giving is even called "giving" at all — because it breaks apart stinginess and poverty and draws forth abundant wealth and merit in their place. And the fourth, giving's causes and effects, he ties to another classical text's teaching that great compassion is the cause underlying every perfection, and beneficial results for all beings is their shared fruit. This closes the four kinds of unobstructed understanding; giving shaped by the four immeasurable states of mind follows next.

53. Milestone: Giving Shaped by the Four Immeasurable States of Mind

A bodhisattva's giving is further deepened by matching it to the four immeasurable states of mind depending on who stands before them: giving toward someone who has personally wronged them is carried out with loving-kindness; giving toward someone genuinely suffering is carried out with compassion; giving toward someone of genuine virtue is carried out with shared, rejoicing gladness; and giving toward those already close — benefactors, dear friends, the like-minded — is carried out with equanimity, releasing any lingering favoritism even toward one's own circle.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，於諸有怨，以慈意樂而行惠施；於諸有苦，以悲意樂而行惠施；於諸有德，以喜意樂而行惠施；於諸有恩、親善、同意，以捨意樂而行惠施；當知亦名菩薩巧慧而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward those who bear them ill will, give with loving-kindness; toward those who suffer, give with compassion; toward those of genuine virtue, give with shared gladness; and toward those to whom they owe kindness, or who are dear friends or of like mind, give with equanimity. This too should be understood as a bodhisattva's giving guided by skillful wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this fourfold matching as ensuring giving is never emotionally flat or indifferent to who actually stands in front of the giver: an enemy is met with loving-kindness precisely because resentment is what needs dissolving there; a suffering being is met with compassion aimed at actually relieving that suffering; virtue is met with genuine shared joy rather than quiet envy; and even one's own benefactors and dear friends — where favoritism comes most naturally — are met with equanimity, so that closeness itself never distorts the underlying fairness giving is meant to express. This closes giving matched to the four immeasurables; the treatise's own careful account of giving's characteristic obstacles, and how each is specifically overcome, follows next.

54. Milestone: Four Classic Obstacles to Giving, Named

A bodhisattva genuinely understands giving's own characteristic obstacles and how each is overcome: a lack of habituation from never having practiced generosity in the past, a genuine scarcity of anything to give, attachment to some particularly fine and cherished possession, and — more subtly — an excessive, absorbing anticipation of the wealth a future rebirth will bring as reward, quietly distorting what giving is even for.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩於其施障及彼對治如實了知。此中施障略有四種。一、先未串習，二、施物尠闕，三、耽著上妙悅意財物，四、觀見當來具足財果而深欣樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas truly understand giving's own obstacles and how each is overcome. These obstacles are, in brief, four: first, not having habituated giving in the past; second, having genuinely little to give; third, attachment to some particularly fine and pleasing possession; fourth, deeply anticipating and delighting in the abundant wealth a future result will bring.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews each obstacle's own particular character: the first is simply the friction of an unpracticed habit, generosity that has never become second nature and so meets real inner resistance even when the will is present; the second is the very real practical difficulty of having genuinely little to spare; the third is the specific pull of a possession one happens to love especially dearly; and the fourth is subtler still — not reluctance to give at all, but a corrupted motive quietly creeping in, where the anticipated future reward, rather than awakening itself, becomes the real reason for giving. This closes naming all four obstacles; overcoming the first — the sheer friction of an unpracticed habit — follows next.

55. Milestone: Overcoming the Habit of Never Having Given Before

When a bodhisattva notices real reluctance to give despite having ample means and a petitioner genuinely present, swift self-examination reveals the cause: simply never having habituated generosity in past lives — and, recognizing that failing to correct this now will only entrench the same reluctance more deeply into future lives as well, the bodhisattva deliberately forces the choice to give anyway, breaking the pattern through sheer determined practice rather than waiting for the habit to somehow correct itself.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩現有種種可施財物，雖見求者正現在前，而於惠施心不趣入；菩薩爾時即以正慧速疾通達，是我於施先未串習所作過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas, though possessing various objects that could be given, and though a petitioner genuinely stands before them, find their mind reluctant to give — they swiftly recognize, through genuine wisdom, that this reluctance is the fault of never having habituated giving in the past.

Chinese Source

我於先世決定於施曾未串習，致令今世……若於今世不彊思擇而行施者，復於來世定當憎背所應行施。菩薩如是正通達已，用此施障對治為依，力勵思擇而行惠施，能不隨逐先未串習所作過失，自在而行。

Ru-Yi Translation

I certainly failed to habituate giving in past lives, which is why this present reluctance arises now. ... If, in this present life, I do not force myself to deliberately choose to give, I will certainly come to resent and turn away from giving in future lives as well. Having genuinely recognized this, bodhisattvas rely on this very understanding as the antidote, forcefully choosing to give, and thereby no longer follow the fault of unhabituated giving, acting instead with genuine freedom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own quiet acknowledgment that even a bodhisattva with genuinely abundant wealth may still feel real inner resistance to giving, simply because the habit itself has not yet been built — this is not hypocrisy, only an honest description of how habituated tendencies work, requiring deliberate, repeated practice to establish rather than good intentions alone. He stresses the urgency the treatise gives this: today's forced practice, however uncomfortable, is precisely what prevents tomorrow's resistance from calcifying into settled aversion, since a habit left uncorrected across one lifetime tends only to deepen across the next. This closes overcoming the first obstacle; overcoming genuine scarcity — even to the point of real hardship — follows next.

56. Milestone: Giving Even Amid Genuine Scarcity — Better to Go Hungry Than Turn Someone Away

When a petitioner stands before a bodhisattva who genuinely has very little to give, the bodhisattva recognizes scarcity itself as an obstacle to be overcome rather than an excuse to withhold — resolving to bear whatever hardship results, even hunger, even death itself, rather than ever send a petitioner away empty-handed, so long as anything at all, down to the simplest wild greens, remains to sustain life.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩若見求者正現在前，由諸財物有尠闕故，於其惠施心不趣入。菩薩爾時即以正慧速疾通達如是匱乏障施因緣，忍受匱乏所作眾苦，力勵思擇，起悲愍心而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, seeing a petitioner genuinely present but finding their own means genuinely scarce, may find their mind reluctant to give. They swiftly recognize this scarcity itself as an obstacle to giving, resolve to bear whatever hardship results from that scarcity, and, forcing the choice through compassion, give anyway.

Chinese Source

設我今者由行惠施，因饒益他，於現法中受種種苦，乃至殞歿，如是行施猶為最勝，非空發遣諸來求者；況當更有諸菜葉等可以活命。菩薩如是忍受匱乏所作眾苦而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Even should I, by giving in order to benefit another, suffer all manner of hardship in this very life, even unto death, giving in this way is still supreme — never sending a petitioner away empty-handed — how much more so, then, when I still have even wild greens enough to sustain life. Bodhisattvas bear the hardship of genuine scarcity in just this way, and give.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as one of giving's most demanding standards, deliberately set high: a bodhisattva facing real scarcity first honestly names the cause — perhaps some flaw in past karma, perhaps present circumstances genuinely beyond their control — and rather than using that honest diagnosis as a reason to stop, uses it as the very occasion for compassion to override self-preservation. He stresses the treatise's own extreme framing is not incidental: even facing death from the hardship that giving away one's last resources might bring, giving remains the better choice than sending a petitioner away with nothing, and, in nearly every real circumstance, some minimal sustenance — as little as foraged greens — remains available regardless, so the standard, while severe in principle, rarely demands quite so much in practice. This closes overcoming genuine scarcity through compassionate resolve; cutting attachment to a particularly cherished possession follows next.

57. Milestone: Cutting Attachment at Its Root — Giving Away Precisely the Most Cherished Thing

When a petitioner asks for exactly the object a bodhisattva happens to treasure most, and reluctance arises precisely because of how fine and pleasing that object is, the bodhisattva recognizes this reluctance itself as a distorted, upside-down attachment to something ultimately impermanent and unreal — and, understanding this clearly, deliberately gives away exactly that treasured object, cutting the attachment at its very root rather than merely managing around it.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，若見求者正現在前，由可施物極悅意故、最上妙故，於行惠施心不趣入。菩薩爾時即以正慧速疾通達，是我耽著所作過失，我今於苦發起虛妄樂想顛倒，由此能生當來眾苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, seeing a petitioner genuinely present but finding the object requested especially pleasing and exquisite, may find their mind reluctant to give. They swiftly recognize, through genuine wisdom, that this reluctance is the fault of their own attachment, giving rise to a distorted, false sense of pleasure in what is genuinely suffering, and that this very attachment produces further suffering to come.

Chinese Source

於此顛倒遍了知故，為欲斷除，力勵思擇，用此財物而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fully recognizing this distortion, and wishing to root it out entirely, the bodhisattva forces the choice and gives away precisely this cherished object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this obstacle as attachment in its most concentrated, personal form — not scarcity, not an unpracticed habit, but a specific object that has come to feel like an extension of the self, prized precisely because it is fine and pleasing. He notes the treatise's own diagnosis cuts to the heart of the matter: what feels like genuine pleasure in possessing something is, from a clearer view, itself a subtly painful bondage, since everything of this kind is fundamentally impermanent and beyond any lasting control, so clinging to it more tightly only stores up greater suffering for later. The remedy the treatise offers is deliberately direct rather than gradual — giving away precisely the object attachment clings to hardest is what actually breaks its grip, in a way that giving away something one barely cared for never could. This closes overcoming attachment to a cherished possession; correcting the fourth and subtlest obstacle — an overly eager eye on giving's future reward — follows next.

58. Milestone: Seeing Through Impermanence Dissolves the Wrong View of Future Reward

The fourth obstacle — becoming quietly motivated by an eager eye on a rich future reward rather than by awakening itself — dissolves through genuinely seeing that every conditioned thing is fundamentally unstable, arising and perishing moment by moment, so that whatever pleasure a resulting reward might bring vanishes just as quickly as it comes; seeing this clearly, a bodhisattva no longer delights in that anticipated reward, and dedicates all giving instead toward unsurpassed awakening.

Chinese Source

如實觀察一切諸行皆不堅牢，一切諸行皆念念滅，所受用果速疾滅盡、速疾離散。如是觀時，即能斷滅能生欣樂邪果之見，諸所行施一切迴向無上菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Truly observing that every conditioned thing is fundamentally unstable, arising and perishing moment by moment, and that whatever result is enjoyed swiftly perishes and swiftly scatters away — observing in this way, one is able to dissolve the distorted view that delights in a future reward, and dedicates all giving instead toward unsurpassed awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this fourth antidote works differently from the first three, which each directly override a specific reluctance to give at all — here, the obstacle is not reluctance but a subtly misdirected motive underneath giving that does happen. Seeing clearly how fleeting any resulting pleasure genuinely is removes the temptation to give primarily for that pleasure's sake, redirecting the same generous action toward its only truly worthwhile aim. He cites the same classical verse already invoked earlier in this chapter — that giving in pursuit of unsurpassed awakening rather than worldly reward yields immeasurable merit even from the smallest gift — as worth committing to memory as giving's own guiding principle. This closes the fourth obstacle's antidote; how all four antidotes work together, and which secures giving's supreme fruit, follow next.

59. Milestone: Four Antidotes Working Together, and Giving's Supreme Fruit Secured

The four obstacles just described are each met by their own specific antidote — awareness of an unpracticed habit, willingness to bear real hardship, full recognition of a distorting attachment, and seeing through the instability of all conditioned things — the first three of which make a bodhisattva genuinely capable of giving at all, while the fourth alone is what secures giving's own supreme fruit, unsurpassed awakening itself, rather than some lesser, worldly reward.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩四種施障，當知四種能對治智對治於彼。一者、覺悟，二者、忍受眾苦，三者、遍知顛倒，四者、見一切行性不堅牢。

Ru-Yi Translation

These four obstacles to a bodhisattva's giving are each met by their own antidote of understanding: first, awareness; second, willingness to bear hardship; third, full recognition of distortion; fourth, seeing that all conditioned things are fundamentally unstable.

Chinese Source

是諸菩薩由前三種能對治智，決定堪能正行惠施；由後一種能對治智，能正攝受施福勝果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, through the first three antidotes, become genuinely capable of correctly practicing giving; through the fourth antidote alone, they rightly secure giving's own supreme, resulting fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this closing distinction as important precisely because it is easy to miss: simply managing to give at all, however difficult the circumstances, is not yet the same as giving toward the right end. A bodhisattva could conceivably overcome habit, hardship, and attachment, and still give primarily hoping for wealth or a favorable rebirth — only the fourth antidote, seeing through impermanence clearly enough to release that hope entirely, redirects the whole of giving toward unsurpassed awakening, which is the only fruit genuinely worth securing. This closes obstacle-overcoming giving and, with it, five of the six categories comprising giving guided by skillful wisdom; the sixth and final category — giving through the meditative mastery available to advanced bodhisattvas — follows next.

60. Milestone: Giving Through Meditative Mastery — Manifesting Inexhaustible Treasures

A bodhisattva who has genuinely realized deep meditative mastery gives in a manner entirely unavailable to an ordinary practitioner: resting in quiet solitude, sustained by pure faith and settled conviction, repeatedly calling to mind abundant, exquisite wealth of every kind, and, through the sheer power of that conviction, manifesting real food, clothing, jewels, and vehicles for beings to genuinely use — producing boundless merit through remarkably little effort.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩內居閑靜，由淨意樂淳厚淨信分別勝解，數數緣念種種上妙無量財寶，以勝解力，於諸眾生樂行惠施。由此因緣，是諸菩薩以少功用生無量福，當知亦名菩薩巧慧而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, dwelling inwardly in quiet solitude, through pure intention, deep and genuine faith, and discerning conviction, repeatedly call to mind abundant, exquisite wealth of every kind, and, through the power of that conviction, delight in giving to beings. Through this, bodhisattvas generate boundless merit through remarkably little effort. This too should be understood as a bodhisattva's giving guided by skillful wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as belonging specifically to a bodhisattva who has attained genuine mastery over meditative absorption, able to manifest food, clothing, jewels, and vehicles through the sheer force of visualization, indistinguishable in their actual usefulness from ordinarily obtained goods — a power already described earlier in the treatise's own account of a bodhisattva's extraordinary abilities. He notes this level of giving rests on the same underlying qualities already named throughout this chapter — pure faith, genuine conviction, and compassionate intention to benefit beings — simply expressed at a vastly more powerful level once meditative mastery itself has matured. This closes the sixth and final category of giving guided by skillful wisdom, completing the entire, remarkable account of skillful giving in every one of its dimensions; a summary naming all six categories together, closing this whole extensive section, follows next.

61. Milestone: Skillful Giving Concludes — Six Categories Named Together, a New Angle Opens

A summary closes the entire account of giving guided by skillful wisdom, naming all six categories together — giving from wealth or without it, giving the teaching, giving through unobstructed understanding, giving shaped by the four immeasurables, giving that overcomes its own obstacles, and giving through meditative mastery — closing, with this, the treatise's complete account of both giving's own intrinsic nature and its comprehensive variety, the first two of the nine aspects; the treatise now turns to organize everything already covered through an entirely different lens: seven further angles on giving, beginning with difficult giving.

Chinese Source

如是妙慧大慧菩薩巧慧行施總略義者，由有財、無財，財施所攝故；如是由法施故，由無礙解施故，由勝意樂施故，由施障對治智慧施故，由增上意樂勝解施故，當知是名唯諸菩薩不共行施。

Ru-Yi Translation

The summary of a bodhisattva's giving guided by fine, expansive, skillful wisdom is this: giving from wealth or without it; giving the teaching; giving through unobstructed understanding; giving shaped by the immeasurables; giving that overcomes its own obstacles; and giving through meditative conviction. This should be understood as giving unique to bodhisattvas, shared with no one else.

Chinese Source

如是廣說於內外事菩薩所行一切施差別相已，自斯已後，即於一切施差別相中，分出所餘難行施等一切差別，應當了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having broadly explained the distinctions of every kind of giving a bodhisattva practices, both internal and external, from this point on, the remaining distinctions within comprehensive giving — difficult giving and the rest — should further be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this closes intrinsic-nature giving and comprehensive giving together, the first two of the nine aspects the chapter's own opening verse named — comprehensive giving alone having grown, across the treatise's own account, into by far the most extensive treatment of any single aspect, covering benefit to others, benefit to a bodhisattva's own integrity, and giving guided by skillful wisdom in every one of its six forms. He explains the treatise's own reason for now revisiting much of the same ground through seven further named angles — difficult giving, giving through every gateway, giving as a genuinely good person, giving complete in every circumstance, giving that fulfills what is asked, giving that brings joy in this life and the next, and pure giving — not because the earlier account was incomplete, but because organizing the same material by these seven distinct angles brings certain practical emphases into sharper focus that the earlier organization, thorough as it was, did not equally highlight. This closes the transition; difficult giving, unfolding into three specific kinds, opens the new sequence next.

62. Milestone: Difficult Giving — Three Kinds

Difficult giving names three specific circumstances where genuine generosity becomes especially hard-won: giving generously despite having very little oneself, bearing the resulting poverty with patience; giving away something deeply cherished, long depended upon, owed to for great past benefit, or simply the finest thing one owns, letting go before the chance to give it is lost rather than waiting until it is no longer wanted; and giving away wealth that was itself obtained only through extreme personal hardship and effort.

Chinese Source

謂若諸菩薩財物尠少，自忍貧苦惠施於他，是名菩薩第一難行施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, having very little wealth, personally bear poverty and hardship while still giving to others — this is called a bodhisattva's first kind of difficult giving.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩所可愛物，或性深愛著，或長時串習，或有上品恩，或最上妙物，極生耽著，能自開解，惠施於他，是名菩薩第二難行施。若諸菩薩極大艱辛所獲財物惠施於他，是名菩薩第三難行施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, toward something genuinely cherished — deeply attached to by nature, long relied upon, owed to for great past kindness, or simply the very finest thing owned, generating real, deep attachment — are nonetheless able to let go and give it to another: this is called a bodhisattva's second kind of difficult giving. Bodhisattvas who give away wealth obtained only through extreme hardship: this is called a bodhisattva's third kind of difficult giving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the first kind as characteristic of a bodhisattva still early on the path, before wealth has ripened as an accompanying fruit of practice — precisely the circumstance where generosity is hardest and therefore most genuinely meaningful. He treats the second kind as encompassing anything from a cherished keepsake to a beloved companion one has long depended on, and stresses the treatise's own practical urgency here: a bodhisattva releases such attachment while a petitioner still genuinely wants what is offered, rather than waiting until the opportunity has quietly passed and the gift no longer matters to anyone. The third kind he reads as giving away wealth that itself represents real personal sacrifice to obtain — money earned through unusually difficult labor, say — noting that giving away easily acquired wealth costs comparatively little, while giving away hard-won wealth costs a great deal more, and is correspondingly more genuinely difficult and more genuinely meritorious. This closes difficult giving's three kinds; giving through every gateway, unfolding into its own four aspects, follows next.

63. Milestone: Giving Through Every Gateway — Four Aspects

Giving through every gateway gathers every possible source and target of giving into four aspects: giving from one's own wealth, giving from wealth persuaded or gathered from others, giving away one's own dependents — spouse, servants, friends, ministers, and relatives, though never one's own parents or teachers — with their genuine consent, and giving freely to whoever comes asking.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，或自財物，或勸化他所得財物，或施親屬、父母、妻子、奴婢、作使、善友、大臣、親戚、眷屬，或復施與他來求者。如是四相，是名菩薩一切門施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas give from their own wealth, or from wealth gathered by persuading others; give away relatives, spouse, servants, staff, close friends, ministers, or kin; or give to whoever comes seeking help. These four aspects are called a bodhisattva's giving through every gateway.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi clarifies that giving away a spouse, servant, or dependent here never means anything like selling them into servitude, a possibility already firmly ruled out earlier in this chapter — it means, for instance, a parent willingly entrusting a child into another household's care with genuine consent, or a household staff member reassigned with their own agreement to serve someone else's genuine need. He notes parents and teachers remain the sole permanent exception even within this fourth gateway, precisely as established earlier, since the debt owed them is never subject to any circumstance whatsoever. This closes giving through every gateway; giving as a genuinely virtuous person, unfolding into five distinct marks, follows next.

64. Milestone: Giving as a Genuinely Good Person Opens — Pure Faith, Respect, One's Own Hand, Right Timing

Giving as a genuinely good person — someone whose conduct is free of greed, hatred, and delusion — unfolds into five marks, the first four of which are: giving from pure faith rooted in genuine understanding of cause and effect rather than blind superstition; giving with real respect in manner and speech; giving personally, with one's own hand, whenever actually possible; and giving at precisely the right moment, illustrated through water offered to a parched traveler, a prompt visit to someone ill, and freshly harvested produce offered first to a virtuous practitioner before the giver's own household even eats it.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩善士所行名善士施？當知此施略有五相。謂諸菩薩，淨信而施，恭敬而施，自手而施，應時而施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called giving as a genuinely good person? This giving should be understood as, in brief, five marks. Bodhisattvas give from pure faith, give with respect, give with their own hand, and give at the right time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains pure faith here as genuine understanding rather than superstition — the same deep, reasoned confidence in giving's own causes and effects already established throughout this chapter, arising naturally and spontaneously rather than requiring anyone else's persuasion or encouragement. He treats giving with one's own hand as more meritorious than delegating the act whenever it is genuinely possible to do so personally, though delegation remains appropriate when circumstances require it.

He illustrates right timing through the classical example of offering water to a traveler suffering from thirst and exhaustion exactly when needed, visiting someone gravely ill promptly rather than waiting until they have already recovered or, worse, already died, and a further teaching from the Ekottarika Āgama on offering freshly harvested grain or produce first to a diligent, virtuous practitioner before one's own household even tastes it — small, timely acts of generosity said to ripen into a life where whatever one lacks appears again precisely when needed. This closes the first four marks of a good person's giving; the fifth and final mark follows next.

65. Never Causing Distress to Give — A Good Person's Giving Concludes

The fifth and final mark of a genuinely good person's giving is never causing distress to another being in the course of it — even where a bodhisattva holds formal authority over a dependent, that authority is never used to coerce their consent, closing all five marks together as the complete picture of giving practiced by someone truly free of greed, hatred, and delusion.

Chinese Source

不損惱他而行惠施。……如是五相，是名菩薩善士所行名善士施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Giving without causing distress to another. ... These five marks are called a bodhisattva's giving as a genuinely good person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this fifth mark directly back to the earlier teaching on one's own dependents — even holding legitimate authority over a spouse, servant, or child, a bodhisattva never overrides their genuine willingness, since coercing distress for the sake of completing a gift produces exactly the tainted result already described, wealth paired with recurring suffering rather than clean, unblemished merit. This closes giving as a genuinely good person in its entirety; giving complete across every kind, unfolding into thirteen marks, follows next.

66. Milestone: Giving Complete in Every Kind — Thirteen Marks

Giving complete in every kind gathers everything already covered into thirteen marks: giving with no ulterior motive, giving on a genuinely broad scale toward every being regardless of worthiness, giving with real joy, giving repeatedly rather than only occasionally, giving to a worthy field and to one that is not, giving every kind of object, giving in every place, giving at every time, giving only what is lawfully obtained, giving sentient and non-sentient objects alike, giving local specialties suited to a particular place, and giving wealth and grain.

Chinese Source

當知此施有十三相。一、無依施，二、廣大施，三、歡喜施，四、數數施，五、田器施，六、非田器施，七、一切物施，八、一切處施，九、一切時施，十、無罪施，十一、有情物施，十二、方土物施，十三、財穀物施。

Ru-Yi Translation

This giving should be understood as having thirteen marks: giving without dependence on any ulterior motive; broad, expansive giving; joyful giving; repeated giving; giving to a worthy field; giving to one that is not; giving every kind of object; giving in every place; giving at every time; giving free of any fault of origin; giving sentient objects; giving local specialties; and giving wealth and grain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes most of these thirteen marks simply restate principles already established at length earlier in this chapter — freedom from ulterior motive, joy across the whole arc of giving, and so on — gathered here under a single, memorable heading. He highlights two worth dwelling on further: broad, expansive giving means never selecting recipients by their worthiness at all, treating ordinary beings and noble ones as equally valid recipients of generosity, since the point is never to curate a field of only impressive recipients; and repeated giving reflects the simple truth that stinginess is rarely uprooted by a single act, however generous, but only through sustained, habitual practice — which is precisely why giving, alone among the six perfections, is traditionally regarded as the most accessible starting point for a beginning bodhisattva, its own perfection marking the transition into the first ground itself. This closes giving complete in every kind; giving that precisely fulfills whatever is actually requested follows next.

67. Milestone: Giving That Fulfills Exactly What Is Asked — Eight Kinds

Giving that fulfills what is asked means matching the gift precisely to the actual lack behind each request: food for hunger, a vehicle for lacking transport, clothing for lacking garments, ornaments for lacking adornment, everyday goods for lacking daily necessities, perfume and garlands for lacking these, housing for lacking shelter, and light for lacking illumination — eight kinds together covering the full range of a petitioner's genuine practical need.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，匱乏飲食而求乞者，施以飲食；匱乏車乘而求乞者，施以車乘；匱乏衣服而求乞者，施以衣服；匱乏嚴具而求乞者，施以嚴具；匱乏種種資生什物而求乞者，施以種種資生什物；匱乏種種塗飾香鬘而求乞者，施以種種塗飾香鬘；匱乏舍宅而求乞者，施以舍宅；匱乏光明而求乞者，施以光明。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas, toward one lacking food who asks, give food; toward one lacking a vehicle, give a vehicle; toward one lacking clothing, give clothing; toward one lacking ornaments, give ornaments; toward one lacking everyday goods, give everyday goods; toward one lacking perfume and garlands, give perfume and garlands; toward one lacking housing, give housing; toward one lacking light, give light.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this list as simply making explicit what genuine attentiveness to a petitioner's actual need already implies — the gift given is never generic, but precisely what that specific lack calls for, right down to a community's own street lighting when that is what is genuinely missing. This closes giving that fulfills exactly what is asked; giving that brings joy across both this life and the next, unfolding into nine marks across three kinds, follows next.

68. Milestone: Giving That Brings Joy in Both This Life and the Next — Nine Marks Across Three Kinds

Giving that brings joy in both this life and the next unfolds into nine marks across three underlying kinds — material giving, giving freedom from fear, and giving the teaching — material giving and fearlessness together securing genuine happiness in the present, while giving the teaching, through transforming the mind itself, secures happiness especially in the life to come.

Chinese Source

當知此施略有九相。謂財施、無畏施、法施，總說名為能令眾生此世他世樂施。

Ru-Yi Translation

This giving should be understood as, in brief, nine marks: material giving, giving freedom from fear, and giving the teaching, together called giving that brings beings joy in both this life and the next.

Chinese Source

財施者，謂以上妙清淨如法財物而行惠施，調伏慳吝垢而行惠施，調伏積藏垢而行惠施。無畏施者，謂濟拔師子、虎狼、鬼魅等畏，拔濟王賊等畏，拔濟水火等畏。法施者，謂無倒說法、稱理說法，勸修學處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Material giving means giving fine, clean, lawfully obtained wealth; giving that overcomes the taint of stinginess; and giving that overcomes the taint of hoarding. Giving freedom from fear means rescuing beings from lions, wolves, and malevolent spirits; from oppressive rulers and bandits; and from flood and fire. Giving the teaching means teaching without distortion, teaching in accordance with genuine principle, and encouraging others to train in its practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the three marks of material giving as targeting three distinct failure points at once — fine, clean, lawfully obtained wealth ensures the object itself carries no taint; overcoming stinginess directly counters the attachment that would otherwise prevent giving at all; and overcoming hoarding counters the subtler urge to accumulate resources purely for one's own future enjoyment rather than genuine generosity. He treats freedom from fear as covering danger from wild predators or malevolent spirits, from oppressive human power whether official or criminal, and from natural disaster alike — a bodhisattva's protection extending across every register of genuine threat a being might face. And he reads teaching the dharma as requiring both accuracy, since a distorted teaching helps no one, and genuine correspondence to principle, since even accurate words poorly matched to a listener's actual situation fail to land, alongside active encouragement toward genuine practice rather than mere information.

He notes the treatise's own distinction between which kind secures happiness now and which secures it later reflects each one's own natural strength rather than a rigid boundary — material giving and rescue from danger address an immediate circumstance and so bring relief right away, while teaching works by transforming the mind itself, a change whose fullest benefit tends to ripen more gradually, into future lives as much as this one — though in genuine practice all three tend to support both, this life and the next together. This closes giving across this life and the next; pure giving, unfolding into ten marks and closing the entire chapter on the perfection of giving, opens next.

69. Milestone: Pure Giving Opens — Ten Marks Named

The ninth and final aspect of giving, pure giving, gathers ten specific marks of a giving genuinely free of every subtle taint: never making a petitioner wait, never clinging to a distorted view about giving, never hoarding resources before giving them all at once, never giving with an air of superiority, never giving for the sake of praise, never shrinking from the highest standard of giving out of fear, never settling for an inferior gift, never favoring some recipients over others, never expecting gratitude in return, and never giving merely to secure a favorable future rebirth.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩清淨施？當知此施有十種相。一、不留滯施，二、不執取施，三、不積聚施，四、不高舉施，五、無所依施，六、不退弱施，七、不下劣施，八、不向背施，九、不望報恩施，十、不希異熟施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's pure giving? This giving should be understood as having ten marks: giving without delay; giving free of distorted views; giving without first hoarding resources; giving without an air of superiority; giving without dependence on praise; giving without shrinking back; giving nothing inferior; giving without favoritism; giving without expecting gratitude; and giving without hoping for a resulting reward.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes pure giving closes the entire nine-aspect structure this whole chapter has followed, gathering many threads already developed throughout — prompt giving, right view, humility, indifference to praise, courage, quality, impartiality, and freedom from any personal expectation — into a single, final, comprehensive standard. This closes the overview naming all ten marks; the treatise's own detailed unpacking of each, beginning with never making a petitioner wait, follows next.

70. Milestone: Never Making a Petitioner Wait, Never Distorting Giving Through Wrong Views

The first two marks of pure giving: responding to a petitioner's presence with real speed, faster even than the petitioner themselves might dare hope for; and never clinging to one of three specific distorted views about giving itself — that giving produces no genuine result at all, that ritual killing performed as sacrifice counts as legitimate practice, or that giving alone, perfected, already constitutes the complete and ultimate purity of the entire path.

Chinese Source

云何不留滯施？謂諸菩薩見來求者正現在前，速疾惠施，不作留滯；非來求者疾望得財，如諸菩薩速希惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is giving without delay? Bodhisattvas, seeing a petitioner genuinely present, give swiftly, without delay — the petitioner does not even hope to receive it as quickly as the bodhisattva actually gives it.

Chinese Source

云何不執取施？謂諸菩薩不以妄見執取於施。或執此施空無有果，或執殺害而行惠施以為正法，或執唯施極淨圓滿是世出世究竟清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is giving free of distorted views? Bodhisattvas do not cling to any distorted view regarding giving — neither the view that giving produces no result at all, nor the view that killing performed as ritual sacrifice counts as legitimate practice, nor the view that giving alone, perfected, already constitutes ultimate purity in both worldly and transcendent terms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the first wrong view through a classical account of nihilistic reasoners who, observing a lifelong, diligent giver reborn into poverty regardless, concluded that giving produces no genuine result at all — a conclusion that mistakes a single visible case, viewed with no insight into the full sweep of causes across many lives, for the whole picture. He illustrates the second through the same classical text's account of sacrificial traditions claiming that reciting incantations before ritually killing an animal ensures a heavenly rebirth for killer and victim alike — a view a bodhisattva, understanding genuine cause and effect, never entertains. And he ties the third directly back to an identical caution already given earlier in this chapter: giving, however perfected, remains only one of six perfections, never a substitute for the discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom a complete path still requires. This closes the first two marks; never hoarding resources before giving them all at once follows next.

71. Milestone: Never Hoarding Resources to Give in One Grand Gesture

A bodhisattva never spends a long stretch of time quietly accumulating wealth in order to give it all away at once in some grand, impressive gesture — since a petitioner genuinely present now should never be turned away simply so resources can grow larger later, incremental giving produces no less merit than a single large gift, and hoarding before giving actually causes real harm: earlier petitioners turned away in resentment, and sometimes a large, unwanted gift later forced on someone who no longer needs it at all.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩，不於長時漸漸積集聚多財物，然後頓施。何以故？非諸菩薩現有施物，見來求者正現在前堪能不施；不見不施是稱正理。云何積財而不速施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas do not spend a long time gradually accumulating abundant wealth in order to give it all away at once. Why is this? Bodhisattvas, possessing objects that could be given, seeing a petitioner genuinely present, are never capable of not giving; they never regard withholding as fitting what is right. How, then, could they accumulate wealth while failing to give it swiftly?

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，見積聚施，其施有罪；見隨得施，其施無罪。何以故？若積聚已然後頓施，是則先時有來求者，其數或百而不施與，令生嫌恨，不忍、不信；後有一類或不希求，畜積珍財，彊而頓施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas see that giving after hoarding carries fault, while giving as resources are obtained carries none. Why is this? If one hoards and then gives all at once, then earlier petitioners — perhaps a hundred of them — go unaided, giving rise to resentment and mistrust; and later, someone who no longer even wants it may have accumulated wealth forced upon them all at once regardless.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this mark as a direct, practical corrective to a tempting but mistaken intuition — that one grand, dramatic act of giving must surely outweigh many small ones. He notes the treatise's own reasoning dismantles this directly: giving is measured by the genuine intention behind it, not by its total visible scale, so ten small gifts given with full sincerity carry no less merit than one large gift of equivalent total value, while the hoarding itself actively harms real people in the meantime, denying earlier petitioners in real need for the sake of a single impressive gesture that, by the time it finally arrives, may not even suit anyone's actual circumstances anymore. This closes hoarding-free giving; never giving with an air of superiority, and never depending on praise, follow next.

72. Milestone: Never Boastful, Never Swayed by Praise — Worldly Fame Is Like Empty Grain Husks

Genuine giving carries no trace of superiority over the one receiving it — a bodhisattva regards every petitioner as a genuine benefactor, since it is only through their asking that the perfection of giving can be completed at all, never competing with other givers or congratulating themselves as uniquely generous; and giving never depends on winning praise, a bodhisattva seeing clearly that worldly acclaim is nothing more than empty sound, as insubstantial as a valley's own echo or a pile of hollow grain husks.

Chinese Source

云何不高舉施？謂諸菩薩，於來求者謙下心施，亦不與他競勝而施，亦不施已而生憍慢。謂我能施，我是施主，餘則不爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is giving without an air of superiority? Bodhisattvas give to petitioners with a humble mind, never competing with others over who gives more, and never growing arrogant after giving, thinking: I alone am capable of giving, I alone am a true benefactor, unlike everyone else.

Chinese Source

云何無所依施？謂諸菩薩不依稱譽聲頌而施。體達世間稱譽聲頌，虛妄分別文字所起，唯是虛音，繫屬妄響，譬如世間䅌䴰葉聚。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is giving without dependence on praise? Bodhisattvas do not give depending on acclaim or praise. They fully understand that worldly acclaim and praise arise only from distorted conceptual thought and mere words, being nothing but empty sound, bound up with hollow echoes — like a worldly pile of husked, empty grain stalks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a classical scripture's own striking reframing here: whoever comes asking for the teaching or for material help is, properly understood, a genuine spiritual benefactor in their own right, since it is precisely their asking that gives the bodhisattva the very occasion to complete material giving, teaching, and freedom from fear at all — approached this way, a bodhisattva bows to the petitioner as much as the petitioner might be tempted to thank the giver. He renders the image of worldly praise as empty grain husks vividly: the outer stalk looks whole and full, but contains nothing of substance inside, exactly like praise itself, which flatters the ear but leaves nothing genuinely nourishing behind — a bodhisattva who has seen through this clearly no longer needs acclaim to sustain their own motivation to give. This closes freedom from superiority and from dependence on praise; never shrinking from the highest standard, never settling for less than the finest gift, and never favoring some recipients over others follow next.

73. Milestone: Never Discouraged by the Highest Standard, Never Second-Rate, Never Playing Favorites

Three further marks close in on giving's inner resilience and fairness: hearing of the most supreme, ultimate giving a bodhisattva can eventually reach — offering even one's own body and life for the sake of the teaching — a bodhisattva never responds with self-doubt or fear, trusting that such capacity grows through patient practice rather than demanding it all at once; whatever is actually given is always sought out carefully and given at its very finest, never something merely adequate; and giving proceeds with a genuinely equal heart toward friend, stranger, and enemy alike, never secretly favoring one circle of people over another.

Chinese Source

云何不退弱施？謂諸菩薩，施先意悅，施時心淨，施已無悔；聞諸菩薩廣大第一最勝施時，不自輕懱、恐怖、退弱。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is giving without shrinking back? Bodhisattvas feel joy before giving, a clean mind while giving, and no regret after giving; and hearing of bodhisattvas' own broad, supreme, ultimate giving, they do not belittle themselves, nor grow fearful, nor shrink back.

Chinese Source

云何不下劣施？謂諸菩薩，於諸施物勤數簡擇，最勝最妙飲食、車乘、衣服等物，持用布施。云何不向背施？謂諸菩薩其心平等，不墮朋黨，於怨親中悲心等施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is giving nothing inferior? Bodhisattvas diligently and carefully select the very finest food, vehicles, clothing, and similar objects to give. What is giving without favoritism? Bodhisattvas hold an equal mind, never falling into partiality, giving with equal compassion toward enemy and friend alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads never shrinking from the ultimate standard as an important safeguard against a discouragement that could otherwise derail a beginning bodhisattva entirely — hearing that supreme giving eventually means offering one's very body and life, a practitioner still far from that capacity might reasonably despair of ever measuring up, when the treatise's own point is simply that such capacity is cultivated gradually across the whole path, beginning right where one actually stands. He reads always choosing the finest object as an expression of genuine respect for the recipient rather than mere extravagance, and equal regard toward enemy and friend as the completion of a fairness already established many times over throughout this chapter, now named as one of pure giving's own defining marks. This closes these three; giving free of any expectation of gratitude, and giving free of any hope for a resulting worldly reward, close this entire account next.
