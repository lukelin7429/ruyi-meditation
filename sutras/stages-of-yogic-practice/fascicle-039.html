Fascicle 39 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十九 · The Chapter on the Six Perfections Opens

The Perfection of Giving

With the lineage of the powers now fully described, the treatise turns to the six perfections themselves — the actual practices, cultivated together with the four means of embrace, that bring a bodhisattva all the way to unsurpassed awakening. This fascicle opens with the first and most fundamental: the perfection of giving, unpacked through nine classical aspects that distinguish genuine, transcendent generosity from its ordinary, worldly counterpart.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 39 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle Thirty-Nine Opens — The Six Perfections That Complete Buddhahood

With the lineage of the powers fully traced across the previous fascicle, the treatise now turns to the practices that actually make up a bodhisattva's path in full: the six perfections, cultivated alongside the four means of embrace already described, together forming the genuine provisions that bring unsurpassed, complete awakening to its final completion.

Chinese Source

復次，菩薩次第圓滿六波羅蜜多已，能證無上正等菩提。謂施波羅蜜多、戒波羅蜜多、忍波羅蜜多、精進波羅蜜多、靜慮波羅蜜多、慧波羅蜜多。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, once bodhisattvas have, in due order, brought the six perfections to completion, they are able to realize unsurpassed, perfect awakening. These are: the perfection of giving, the perfection of discipline, the perfection of patience, the perfection of diligence, the perfection of meditative concentration, and the perfection of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this fascicle's opening as marking a shift from theory to practice: everything described through the lineage of the powers explained what a bodhisattva should learn and why, while the six perfections and four means of embrace, opening here, describe what a bodhisattva actually does — cultivated simultaneously with continued learning, since a bodhisattva learns by practicing and practices by learning, the two never fully separable.

He draws a comparison to taking refuge in the three jewels: just as someone who has taken refuge in the Buddha, the teaching, and the community goes on to actually practice the four factors of stream-entry — drawing close to good teachers, hearing the true teaching, applying it correctly in reflection, and practicing in accordance with it — so too a bodhisattva who has taken unsurpassed awakening as their own genuine aim goes on to accumulate its provisions through the six perfections and four means of embrace, the cause that brings the fruit of buddhahood to completion exactly as taking refuge brings the fruit of stream-entry. This closes the six perfections' opening naming; the perfection of giving, unpacked first and most fully, follows next.

2. Milestone: The Perfection of Giving Opens — Worldly Giving, and Giving That Truly Crosses to the Far Shore

The perfection of giving opens with a fundamental distinction: ordinary, worldly giving still involves a real giver, a real recipient, and a real gift, and remains only a preliminary stage — genuine giving that actually crosses to the far shore requires seeing all three as empty, giver, recipient, and gift alike beyond ultimate grasp, so that giving itself becomes the very means by which stinginess and affliction are uprooted.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩施波羅蜜多？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's perfection of giving?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "perfection" itself carries the sense of reaching the far shore, or bringing a virtue to its full completion — giving, at its most ordinary, still involves the notion of a self who gives, another who receives, and a real object being handed over, and remains merely a preliminary exercise still caught in these fixed notions. Genuine, transcendent giving, reached through deepening insight, sees giver, recipient, and gift alike as ultimately without fixed, independent existence — the classical "threefold emptiness" — and only this kind of giving, which directly uproots the underlying stinginess and clinging at its root rather than merely performing a generous act, deserves to be called the perfection of giving in the full sense. He notes ordinary people's understanding of giving is usually quite shallow by comparison, and that the treatise's own unpacking of giving into nine distinct aspects, opening next, contains a surprising depth once properly understood.

3. Milestone: Nine Aspects of Giving, in a Verse Worth Memorizing

The treatise condenses giving's full content into nine classical aspects, set first in a short verse meant to be memorized outright, since every one of the remaining five perfections will be unpacked through this exact same ninefold structure — intrinsic nature, comprehensiveness, difficulty, universal scope, purity of motive, completeness across every circumstance, responsiveness to what is actually asked for, benefit spanning this life and the next, and freedom from any taint.

Chinese Source

嗢柁南曰：自性一切難，一切門善士，一切種遂求，二世樂清淨。如是九種相，是名略說施。

Ru-Yi Translation

The summarizing verse states: intrinsic nature, comprehensiveness, difficulty, universal scope, purity of motive, completeness across every circumstance, responsiveness to what is asked for, benefit in both this life and the next, and freedom from taint — these nine aspects are, in brief, called giving.

Chinese Source

謂九種相施，名為菩薩施波羅蜜多。一、自性施，二、一切施，三、難行施，四、一切門施，五、善士施，六、一切種施，七、遂求施，八、此世他世樂施，九、清淨施。

Ru-Yi Translation

These nine aspects of giving are called a bodhisattva's perfection of giving: first, giving in its intrinsic nature; second, comprehensive giving; third, difficult giving; fourth, giving through every gateway; fifth, giving by a genuinely good person; sixth, giving complete in every kind; seventh, giving that fulfills what is asked; eighth, giving that brings joy in this life and the next; ninth, pure giving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi urges memorizing this verse outright, since the treatise will return to this identical ninefold framework for discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom in turn — once genuinely understood here, the same structure illuminates all six perfections at once. He previews each aspect briefly: intrinsic nature names giving's basic defining character; comprehensiveness distinguishes giving's many concrete forms, benefiting others and benefiting oneself alike; difficult giving means genuinely letting go of what is hardest to part with; giving through every gateway means every possible occasion for giving is gathered under this one heading; giving by a good person means the act springs from a wholesome motive through and through; complete giving means the practice matures across every circumstance life presents; giving that fulfills what is asked means matching the gift to what the recipient actually needs or requests, so they are genuinely satisfied; giving that brings joy in both lives means benefiting the recipient now and hereafter at once; and pure giving means the mind, the manner, the method, and the object given are all free of fault. This closes the ninefold overview; giving's own intrinsic nature, unpacked first and in the greatest detail, follows next.

4. Milestone: The Intrinsic Nature of Giving — Not Every Gift Should Be Given

Giving's own intrinsic nature rests on a bodhisattva's complete freedom from attachment to their own body and possessions, joined with real discernment about what should actually be given at all — since a gift that would harm the recipient, however freely offered, is no genuine act of giving — carried out through a volition free of greed, expressed through actual deeds of body and speech, and grounded in both a correct view of the teaching and unshakeable confidence that giving genuinely bears fruit.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩自性施？謂諸菩薩於自身財無所顧惜，能施一切所應施物。無貪俱行思，及因此所發能施一切無罪施物身語二業，安住律儀阿笈摩見、定有果見，隨所希求，即以此物而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's giving in its intrinsic nature? Bodhisattvas, having no attachment to their own body or wealth, are able to give away whatever should genuinely be given. This consists of the volition accompanied by non-greed, and the deeds of body and speech this volition gives rise to, able to give whatever blameless object is called for; resting in moral discipline, correct view of the teaching, and certainty that giving bears fruit; and, according to whatever is genuinely sought, giving that very thing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes inner wealth, one's own body and life, from outer wealth, one's material possessions, noting a bodhisattva free of attachment to either is able to give both away. He stresses "whatever should genuinely be given" is doing real work here: not everything a recipient wants ought actually to be given — offering a set of mahjong tiles to someone who would only sink deeper into addiction is no genuine gift, however freely handed over, while offering a book that genuinely builds wisdom is exactly the kind of giving meant here. Genuine giving, in other words, requires real discernment about consequences, not simply generosity of impulse.

He explains the volition of non-greed as the underlying mental factor that makes an act of giving what it is, always accompanied in practice by non-hatred, non-delusion, and diligence, even though non-greed is singled out here since it directly counters the stinginess giving is meant to overcome; the deeds of body and speech as actually carrying out the physical act of giving while also praising giving's own genuine benefit and encouraging others toward it; and correct view of the teaching as requiring that a bodhisattva remain free of both broken discipline and broken view — noting the classical tradition still counts as praiseworthy someone whose conduct is imperfect but whose view of the teaching remains genuinely sound, since correct understanding of cause and effect is what actually distinguishes a wise act of giving from mere generosity untethered from any real understanding of its consequences. This closes giving's basic character and its grounding in correct view; the treatise's own account of certainty that giving bears fruit, with its four classical patterns of giving and reward, follows next.

5. Milestone: Certainty That Giving Bears Fruit — Four Classical Patterns of Giving and Reward

Genuine giving rests on real confidence that giving actually bears fruit, illustrated through four classical patterns matching how much is given to how much merit actually results — since giving abundantly with no wisdom behind it, or giving out of stingy, ill intent, produces far less merit than giving even a small amount to someone whose own practice multiplies its effect, or giving generously with genuine understanding of what the gift is actually for.

Chinese Source

定有果見：謂於因果決定信解，知布施必得果報，不由他緣而行布施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Certainty that giving bears fruit means holding a settled, confident understanding of cause and effect, knowing that giving must yield its result, and giving not because led by some outside influence, but from one's own genuine conviction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the classical Scriptural Similes text's four patterns of giving and its resulting merit to illustrate why this certainty matters so much in practice. Giving much for little merit describes someone who spends lavishly on things like ritual sacrifice, drinking, and entertainment — even funding an entire concert purely for pleasure, or killing animals for a sacrificial feast — genuinely generous in scale, yet entirely without wisdom behind it. Giving little for much merit describes offering even a modest gift to a genuinely disciplined practitioner who then applies it toward diligent study and practice, since the practitioner's own effort multiplies the small gift's effect many times over. Giving little for little merit describes a stingy, ill-intentioned gift from someone of wrong view, offered grudgingly and without wisdom, where both the giving and its result remain slight. And giving much for much merit describes a person of genuine ability who, moved by a clear sense of the world's impermanence, funds temples and monastic support on a grand scale, enabling countless others to plant roots of goodness and study the teaching — generous in scale and wise in application at once.

He draws the practical lesson directly: anyone who wishes to give well should cultivate genuine understanding of cause and effect first, since the same act of generosity, done with wisdom or without it, produces dramatically different results — this settled confidence in cause and effect is what actually makes giving faultless and effective, rather than a well-meaning act performed blindly. This closes giving's intrinsic nature in its entirety — freedom from attachment, right discernment, the volition of non-greed, the deeds of body and speech, correct view, and certainty of fruit, all now covered; comprehensive giving, the second of the nine aspects, follows next.
