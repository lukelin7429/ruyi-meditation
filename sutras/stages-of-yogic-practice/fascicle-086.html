Fascicle 86 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十六 · The Aggregates Section Continues

Perception and Formation, and Swift Penetration

Fascicle 86 closes the aggregates section's third overall topic, perception and formation (想行攝), entirely — its full ten parts, from the five marks of impermanence and the fool's threefold conceit through the eye, ten benefits, nine kinds of wisdom, freedom from delusion, advancement, the view of self distinguished, threefold mark and formation, and the dharma summarized via the three gates of liberation, corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's sūtras 36 through 45. It then opens the fourth topic, swift penetration (速通攝), and carries it through all ten of its own parts: the three powers of wisdom, non-laxity, and habituation; one's own being's impermanence and the Buddha's own past-life account; wisdom's object and the five aggregates likened to a mass of foam, a floating bubble, a mirage, a plantain trunk, and a magical illusion; flowing, the causes and bonds of birth-and-death; the four kinds of contentment of foolish ordinary beings; practice according with the current, and going against it; knowing the mark of cutting off; perception; establishing what runs counter to the provisions; and the teacher's own five tasks, from rightly subduing to rightly teaching purity — closing swift penetration entirely. Fascicle 87 continues the aggregates section with its next overall topic, cause.

3. Section: Perception and Formation Begins — Five Marks of the Perception of Impermanence Named

Among the formations, cultivating the perception of impermanence has five kinds — namely, through the nature of impermanence, the nature of inconstancy, the nature of being unable to endure, the nature of being unreliable, and the nature of being subject to change and ruin.

Chinese Source

於諸行中，修無常想行有五種。謂由無常性、無恆性、非久住性、不可保性、變壞法性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the formations, cultivating the perception of impermanence has five kinds — namely, through the nature of impermanence, the nature of inconstancy, the nature of being unable to endure, the nature of being unreliable, and the nature of being subject to change and ruin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Perception and formation's own first section names five approaches to cultivating the perception of impermanence toward the aggregates, whether one's own body or another's, past, present, future, inner or outer, coarse or fine, far or near — each to be unpacked individually in what follows.

4. Section: The Five Marks Explained

Here, ruin moment by moment is impermanence. One's own being, bound to a limited span of life, is inconstancy. The outer world, certain to have no abode once the kalpa ends, is the inability to endure. The lifespan not yet fulfilled, still liable to be destroyed by conditions, dying before its proper time, is unreliability. And, for however long one abides, never settled in ease throughout that span, is the very nature of change and ruin.

Chinese Source

此中剎那剎那壞，故無常。自體繫屬有限住壽，故無恆。外事劫後決定無住，故非久住。壽量未滿，容被緣壞，非時而死，故不可保。乃至爾所時住，於其中間不定安樂，故變壞法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, ruin moment by moment is impermanence. One's own being, bound to a limited span of life, is inconstancy. The outer world, certain to have no abode once the kalpa ends, is the inability to endure. The lifespan not yet fulfilled, still liable to be destroyed by conditions, dying before its proper time, is unreliability. And, for however long one abides, never settled in ease throughout that span, is the very nature of change and ruin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whether one's own body or another's, past, present, or future, inner or outer, coarse or fine, far or near, every formation alike decays moment by moment — were this not so, aging and death could never occur; a body healthy today will nonetheless sicken in time, its vigor and complexion fading in increments too subtle to notice directly, hence impermanence. Even setting momentary change aside, a single lifetime itself has its own fixed limit — seventy, eighty, a hundred years, or more — never truly enduring forever, hence inconstancy, distinguished from impermanence by looking to time's larger span rather than the instant. The physical world fares no better: mountains and rivers pass through formation, continuance, decay, and emptiness in turn, gone entirely once the kalpa of decay arrives — a sudden flood or landslide can just as easily sweep away field and home within a single day, proof enough that even the physical world cannot endure. Even before one's own natural lifespan is exhausted, unexpected conditions can bring an untimely death that ought not yet have come — hence unreliability. And, finally, even granting a full and healthy life, no single day passes in unbroken ease — some ache here, some sorrow there — this too is the very nature of change and ruin. Once these five marks are genuinely seen, a practitioner gradually releases attachment to the aggregates, hastens toward wholesome practice, and moves toward escaping the three realms entirely, even unsurpassed awakening itself; for a śrāvaka specifically, this means cutting off craving and views so that no further such body need ever be taken up again.

5. Section: Delusion's Mark Begins — Three Marks of the Fool Named

Further, an ordinary being, briefly speaking, carries three marks of foolishness.

Chinese Source

復次，愚夫略有三種愚夫之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, an ordinary being, briefly speaking, carries three marks of foolishness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Perception and formation's second section, delusion's mark, names three ways an ordinary being's own foolishness manifests, each concerning the comparison of self to others, to be examined individually in what follows.

6. Section: The First Mark — Believing Oneself Superior

This means that ordinary beings, unable to reflect on the five natures of impermanence just explained regarding every formation, instead discriminate superiority and inferiority within what is not genuinely real, weighing self against other, and conclude that they themselves are superior. This is called the ordinary being's first mark.

Chinese Source

謂諸愚夫，於一切行如上所說五無常性不能思惟；於非真實勝劣性中，分別勝劣，稱量自他，謂己為勝。是名第一愚夫之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that ordinary beings, unable to reflect on the five natures of impermanence just explained regarding every formation, instead discriminate superiority and inferiority within what is not genuinely real, weighing self against other, and conclude that they themselves are superior. This is called the ordinary being's first mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

An ordinary being, still bound by self-clinging, cannot reflect on the aggregates' own fivefold impermanence, and instead endlessly measures self against other within a superiority and inferiority that was never genuinely real to begin with, concluding — however momentarily sharp their own faculties may seem — that they themselves come out ahead, never recognizing that with no genuine self at all, no one is truly superior, equal, or inferior to anyone else; the aggregates themselves are illusory, no real self to be found in them, and comparing at all is simply a dream born of ignorance. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm: every formation's own superiority or inferiority is merely a provisional distinction established through mutual comparison, never genuinely real — one may seem clever measured against a fool, yet foolish measured against someone still cleverer; each person is sharper in some respects and duller in others, and any comparison drawn depends entirely on which respect is chosen, never a fixed, objective fact. Taking such comparison as genuinely real, and taking pride in coming out ahead, is exactly the ordinary being's own first mark — and, without encountering the true dharma, this same restless, beginningless comparing continues without any hope of ever waking from it.

7. Section: The Second and Third Marks — Equal or Inferior, and the Wise One's Contrast

As with believing oneself superior, so too with believing oneself equal or inferior — extensively explained the same way. And, contrary to this, the wise one likewise carries three marks of wisdom.

Chinese Source

如謂己勝，謂等、謂劣，廣說亦爾。與此相違，當知智者亦有三種智者之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

As with believing oneself superior, so too with believing oneself equal or inferior — extensively explained the same way. And, contrary to this, the wise one likewise carries three marks of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Believing oneself merely equal to another, or genuinely inferior, is the same delusion as believing oneself superior — all three alike arise only because the self one is comparing was never real to begin with, and all three alike breed further affliction through the very act of dividing self from other. The wise one, contrariwise, understands that superiority, equality, and inferiority are all merely provisional distinctions set up through mutual comparison, never genuinely real; where some deficiency does show up, one need only gather the right conditions and apply oneself further to make it up, so no one is ever truly, fixedly superior, equal, or inferior to anyone else. Understanding no-self and no-what-belongs-to-self, the wise one never engages in such pointless comparison at all.

8. Section: The Eye Begins — Two Marks of the Purified Wisdom-Eye Named

Further, through two marks one should know a noble one's wisdom-eye to be purified — namely, through being far from dust and free of defilement.

Chinese Source

復次，由二種相，當知聖者慧眼清淨。謂由遠塵及離垢故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through two marks one should know a noble one's wisdom-eye to be purified — namely, through being far from dust and free of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eye's own section explains the "eye" in the first line of perception and formation's particular verse. Among the five eyes — flesh, heaven, wisdom, dharma, and Buddha — this concerns the wisdom-eye, purified through being far from dust and free of defilement, echoing the Saṃyukta Āgama's own line, "far from dust, free of defilement, the dharma-eye purified." A practitioner who cultivates calm and insight upon the four truths' sixteen aspects, penetrating the five aggregates' impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, enters direct realization of the noble truths and turns from ordinary being into a noble one, gaining the wisdom-eye — this is called being far from dust and free of defilement. What follows unpacks the two through two further methods.

9. Section: Far From Dust and Free of Defilement, First Method — Current Affliction and Its Latent Seed

Through being freed from the bondage of afflictions cut off by the path of seeing that manifest as actual affliction, this is called far from dust. Through being freed from the bondage of their latent tendencies, this is called free of defilement.

Chinese Source

由見所斷諸煩惱纏得離繫故，名為遠塵。由彼隨眠得離繫故，說名離垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through being freed from the bondage of afflictions cut off by the path of seeing that manifest as actual affliction, this is called far from dust. Through being freed from the bondage of their latent tendencies, this is called free of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One entering the path of seeing cuts off the discriminated afflictions proper to that path — those arising chiefly from one's own views and reasoning, clinging to the five aggregates as self or as belonging to self, and the further afflictions this spawns across the three realms. Freed from their actual manifestation, this is far from dust — dust here being the coarser, more visible kind. Freed even from their seed, the subtler defilement remaining after the coarse dust is gone, this is free of defilement.

10. Section: Far From Dust and Free of Defilement, Second Method — Conceit and the Remaining Afflictions

Further, at the time of direct realization, there is a coarse self-conceit that arises interruptedly or uninterruptedly, mixed into one's attention. If one thoroughly knows the grasped, the grasper, and their object to be equal, that conceit is then extinguished; through its extinction, this is called far from dust. Through the cutting off of the latent tendencies of all afflictions cut off by the path of seeing, this is called free of defilement.

Chinese Source

又現觀時，有麤我慢隨入作意間無間轉，若遍了知所取、能取、所緣平等，彼即斷滅；彼斷滅故，說名遠塵。一切見道所斷煩惱隨眠斷故，說名離垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, at the time of direct realization, there is a coarse self-conceit that arises interruptedly or uninterruptedly, mixed into one's attention. If one thoroughly knows the grasped, the grasper, and their object to be equal, that conceit is then extinguished; through its extinction, this is called far from dust. Through the cutting off of the latent tendencies of all afflictions cut off by the path of seeing, this is called free of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Practicing insight within calm abiding, observing the five aggregates as without self and without what belongs to self, a coarse conceit can still slip in — a fleeting sense that "only I can practice this observation," a subtle pride arising with or without one's noticing. Once one thoroughly knows the observed aggregates, the observing mind itself, and their shared object all alike to be without self, grasper and grasped become equal and conceit no longer arises — its actual manifestation cut off, this is far from dust. Continuing into the union of calm and insight until all one hundred and twelve latent tendencies cut off by the path of seeing across the three realms are cut off at the root, this alone is properly called free of defilement.

11. Section: Benefit Begins — Ten Benefits Named and Asked

Further, being far from dust and free of defilement, when one gains the dharma-eye within all dharmas, one should know that one thereby gains ten kinds of benefit. What are the ten?

Chinese Source

復次，遠塵離垢，於諸法中得法眼時，當知即得十種勝利。何等為十？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, being far from dust and free of defilement, when one gains the dharma-eye within all dharmas, one should know that one thereby gains ten kinds of benefit. What are the ten?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Through cultivating calm and insight, a practitioner accomplishes uncontaminated wisdom, is freed from both the discriminated self-clinging's actual manifestation and its seed, and thereby gains the dharma-eye — seeing directly, through one's own experience, the five aggregates' impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self. Ten distinct benefits follow, unpacked one by one below.

12. Section: Benefits One Through Four — Seeing the Dharma, Gaining the Dharma, Knowing the Dharma, and Thorough Firmness in the Dharma

First, having thoroughly seen the four noble truths, this is called seeing the dharma. Second, attaining one of the fruits of the ascetic path accordingly, this is called gaining the dharma. Third, being able to know for oneself what one has realized — that one has now exhausted all rebirth as a hell-being, an animal, or a hungry ghost, that one has attained stream-entry, and so on at length — through this, it is called knowing the dharma. Fourth, attaining the four kinds of confirmed purity, knowing the Buddha, the dharma, and the saṅgha as they truly are, this is called being thoroughly firm in the dharma.

Chinese Source

一者、於四聖諦已善見故，說名見法。二者、隨獲一種沙門果故，說名得法。三者、於己所證能自了知：我今已盡所有那落迦、傍生、餓鬼，我證預流，乃至廣說。由如是故，說名知法。四者、得四證淨，於佛法僧如實知故，名遍堅法。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, having thoroughly seen the four noble truths, this is called seeing the dharma. Second, attaining one of the fruits of the ascetic path accordingly, this is called gaining the dharma. Third, being able to know for oneself what one has realized — that one has now exhausted all rebirth as a hell-being, an animal, or a hungry ghost, that one has attained stream-entry, and so on at length — through this, it is called knowing the dharma. Fourth, attaining the four kinds of confirmed purity, knowing the Buddha, the dharma, and the saṅgha as they truly are, this is called being thoroughly firm in the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Seeing the dharma is the first fruit, penetrating the four truths by direct uncontaminated wisdom. Gaining the dharma follows once the discriminated afflictions of the path of seeing are cut off and the dharma-eye purified. Knowing the dharma means the stream-enterer knows for themselves that this life's causes for the three lower realms are exhausted — even though seeds for those realms remain, the conditions to ripen them no longer arise — and that stream-entry, never again to fall back into ordinary being, has been attained. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain "attained stream-entry, and so on at length" as including the full exhaustion of the seven, two, or single further rebirth still owed by a once-returner or a family-to-family stream-enterer, detailed already in fascicle 85. Thoroughly firm in the dharma is the fourth benefit: uncontaminated faith in the Three Jewels arising together with uncontaminated wisdom at the moment the truths are realized, and this faith, unshakeable by any outsider, is called thorough firmness.

13. Section: Benefits Five and Six — No Confusion and No Doubt

Fifth, having no confusion regarding what one has realized oneself. Sixth, having no doubt regarding what others have realized.

Chinese Source

五者、於自所證無惑。六者、於他所證無疑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, having no confusion regarding what one has realized oneself. Sixth, having no doubt regarding what others have realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having directly realized suffering as suffering, its cause as its cause, cessation as cessation, and the path as the path, the stream-enterer has awoken from ignorance's great dream and holds no confusion at all about their own realization. And having realized this for themselves, they likewise recognize the same quality in another who has realized it, holding no doubt about it either.

14. Section: Benefits Seven Through Nine — Needing No Outside Aid, Bending to No Other's Word, and Fearlessly Declaring All One Has Realized

Seventh, when expounding the teaching corresponding to the noble truths, not relying on any outside condition. Eighth, not watching another's face, not looking to another's mouth — within this true dharma and discipline, unmoved by any outsider's doctrine whatsoever. Ninth, declaring all the liberation one has realized, with no fear at all.

Chinese Source

七者、宣說聖諦相應教時，不藉他緣。八者、不觀他面，不看他口，於此正法毗奈耶中，一切他論所不能轉。九者、記別一切所證解脫，都無所畏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, when expounding the teaching corresponding to the noble truths, not relying on any outside condition. Eighth, not watching another's face, not looking to another's mouth — within this true dharma and discipline, unmoved by any outsider's doctrine whatsoever. Ninth, declaring all the liberation one has realized, with no fear at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having gained fundamental wisdom, the stream-enterer needs no text, no teacher's further instruction, to expound the four truths in subsequently attained wisdom — having walked the ancient sages' own path in person, they simply relate what they themselves have verified. Their wisdom already accords with right view, so no outside doctrine, however presented, can shake their confidence in the Buddha's dharma and discipline. And, having personally realized the true character of all dharmas, they can declare exactly which afflictions among the three realms' one hundred and twelve are resolved and which remain, without any trace of dread when others come to challenge them.

15. Section: Benefit Ten — Entering the Noble Teaching, Closing "Perception and Formation, Delusion's Mark, the Eye, and Benefit"

Tenth, through two conditions, one enters into the noble teaching — namely, through correct convention and through the ultimate meaning.

Chinese Source

十者、由二因緣，隨入聖教，謂正世俗及第一義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tenth, through two conditions, one enters into the noble teaching — namely, through correct convention and through the ultimate meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Correct convention is the teaching as conveyed through language, called the wisdom that abides in the dharma; the ultimate meaning refers to nirvāṇa-wisdom. Relying on the wisdom that abides in the dharma to penetrate suffering, its cause, cessation, and the path, and cultivating calm and insight upon it, one cuts off the corresponding afflictions and accomplishes nirvāṇa-wisdom — the dharma-abiding wisdom always coming first, nirvāṇa-wisdom following after. With this tenth benefit, the four sections perception and formation, delusion's mark, the eye, and benefit — all corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-sixth sūtra — are complete.

16. Section: Background — Sūtra 36, Śroṇa's Dialogue with Śāriputra

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-sixth sūtra

The Buddha was dwelling at Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove; the venerable Śāriputra was staying on Vulture Peak. A householder's son, Śroṇa, often came there and asked Śāriputra why ascetics and brahmins, comparing their own impermanent, ever-changing, unsettled body to another's, conclude "I am superior," "I am equal," or "I am inferior" — precisely the three foolish marks already explained above — and what clings beneath such thinking that keeps them from seeing things as they truly are.

Rather than answering directly, Śāriputra questioned Śroṇa in turn: is form permanent or impermanent? Impermanent, Śroṇa answered. Then is it suffering? It is suffering. Then would the Buddha's noble disciples, knowing form to be impermanent and therefore suffering and subject to change, cling to it as self, as other than self, or as self-in-form and form-in-self? No, Śroṇa answered — a noble disciple sees no self in form, no self apart from form, no self in form or form in self. The same holds for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Śāriputra then explained that a practitioner observes each aggregate's exhaustive nature across past, future, present, inner, outer, coarse, fine, superior, inferior, far, and near, and its shared nature — not self, not other than self, not mutually contained — and this, sustained through calm and insight, is called knowing as it truly is; from it come weariness — the fruit of the path of seeing — freedom from desire — the fruit of the path of cultivation — liberation, and the knowledge of liberation, until one knows: birth for me is exhausted, the pure life established, what had to be done is done, no further existence is to be known. Hearing this, Śroṇa himself cut off the discriminated afflictions, both their manifestation and their seed, and attained the first fruit — then rose, knelt, and took refuge in the Three Jewels as a lay follower for the rest of his life, departing in joy.

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are nine kinds of wisdom able to thoroughly know and transcend the formations — namely, the wisdom of the formations' flowing, the wisdom of the formations' reverse cessation, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of defilement, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of purification, the wisdom of purity, and the wisdom of suffering, the wisdom of its cause, the wisdom of cessation, and the wisdom of the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The nine kinds of wisdom, in "九智無癡與勝進," explain the aggregates section's third particular verse. Ordinary beings are all troubled by the aggregates' own blazing suffering; accomplishing these nine allows one to thoroughly know and transcend the formations rather than remain controlled by them.

18. Section: The First Two Kinds of Wisdom — Flowing and Reverse Cessation

Here, the wisdom of the formations' flowing is, in brief, the right wisdom regarding the gathering of every formation through three causes and conditions — namely, through the gathering of delight, the gathering of contact, and the gathering of name-and-form — and, according to what corresponds, the gathering of form, or of feeling and so on, or of consciousness. Through the cessation of these same three causes and conditions, the three kinds of formation cease — this is called the wisdom of the formations' reverse cessation.

Chinese Source

此中諸行流轉智者，略由三種因緣集故，一切行集所有正智。謂喜集故、觸集故、名色集故，隨其所應，若色集、若受等集、若識集。即此三種因緣滅故，三種行滅，是名諸行還滅智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the wisdom of the formations' flowing is, in brief, the right wisdom regarding the gathering of every formation through three causes and conditions — namely, through the gathering of delight, the gathering of contact, and the gathering of name-and-form — and, according to what corresponds, the gathering of form, or of feeling and so on, or of consciousness. Through the cessation of these same three causes and conditions, the three kinds of formation cease — this is called the wisdom of the formations' reverse cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 87)

Delight here is a kind of craving, distinct from outright greed: delight is the joy felt toward an object not yet met, as an intermediate-state being feels joy glimpsing its future parents; greed is the clinging that follows once the object is actually before one, like a cup filled till it overflows. Delight leads to craving, craving leads to grasping, grasping leads to becoming, becoming leads to birth — so delight's gathering gives rise to form's gathering, entering a mother's womb. Contact between the six sense-faculties and their six objects gives rise to the six kinds of contact, and from contact arise the corresponding feeling, perception, and formation — so contact's gathering gives rise to feeling's, perception's, and formation's gathering. And consciousness continues only because of name-and-form — the mind-dharmas and physical dharma present from conception onward — so name-and-form's gathering gives rise to consciousness's gathering. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 87) explain further: every formation's cause divides into a shared cause, delight, common to both mind and matter, whose ripening does not wait on further conditions since it was seeded by a prior joy already directed at that place of rebirth; and unshared causes proper to matter or mind separately — contact, the immediately preceding cessation of mind, co-arisen mental factors, and the ten kinds of matter — each of which still needs its own further conditions, moment by moment, before it can actually arise, even once the prior seed is present.

19. Section: The Next Three Kinds of Wisdom — Defiled Condition, Purifying Condition, and Purity

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The wisdom of the causes and conditions of defilement, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of purification, and the wisdom of purity are, respectively, relish, danger, and escape, to be known as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 85)

As fascicle 85 already explained: observe relish, danger, and escape through three matters and two marks to see whether one's own mind is defiled or pure. First, observe whether relish for the formations is present — relish for suffering is itself suffering, and relish keeps one from being freed from suffering, so relish is the very condition of defilement. Second, observe the aggregates' great faults and calamities — their impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self — for only by truly seeing the danger does one come to weariness, the condition of purity; ordinary beings prize the five aggregates as a treasure and cling to them all the more tightly for it, which is exactly why purity is so hard to reach, while a noble one like Maudgalyāyana, at death, let go of the aggregates as one lets go of a poisoned vessel, without the least attachment. Third, observe purity itself directly — whether desire and craving toward one's own or another's body, or toward form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, has actually been left behind, whether some degree of awakening and accordant realization has arisen. This threefold observation is relish, danger, and escape together: relish is the condition of defilement, danger's observation is the condition of purity, and escape is purity's own accomplishment, from cutting off affliction's actual manifestation through to cutting off its seed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 34, Śrāvaka Section)

Fascicle 34's Śrāvaka Section teaches ten aspects for observing the truth of suffering, entering its four aspects — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self. The first five aspects (changing, perishing, being separated from, having the nature of a conditioned dharma, and being bound up together) belong to the wisdom of impermanence; the next three (being fettered through pleasant feeling, being unlovable through painful feeling, being unsettled through neutral feeling — since even neutral feeling keeps moving on toward further pleasure or pain) belong to the wisdom of suffering; the ninth (finding nothing at all to grasp as self within the aggregates) belongs to the wisdom of emptiness; and the tenth (being unable to master oneself, since the aggregates simply arise from conditions) belongs to the wisdom of no-self. Fascicle 34 explains this in full; here it need only be introduced. 21. Section: Thorough Knowledge and Transcendence Explained, and the Nine Wisdoms Summarized

If the wisdom that thoroughly knows the formations through the principle of the truths is in any way lacking, one certainly cannot advance further in cultivation, employ the power of the antidote to cut off the afflictions, and transcend all formations; contrary to this, one is then able to transcend them. 22. Section: Background — Sūtras 37-38, Thorough Knowledge and Transcendence Retold to Śroṇa

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth sūtras

Śroṇa returned to Śāriputra again. In the thirty-seventh sūtra, on thorough knowledge, Śāriputra explained that any ascetic or brahmin who does not truly know form's own and shared marks, does not know its arising, does not know its cessation, and does not know the path to its cessation, lacks the capacity to actually cut off this illusory form — and the same holds for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Questioned again on whether form and the other aggregates are permanent, Śroṇa answered as before that they are impermanent, hence suffering, hence not self, not other than self, not mutually contained. Śāriputra then taught the same elevenfold observation of each aggregate's exhaustive and shared nature, leading to weariness, freedom from desire, liberation, and freedom from birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, and affliction; Śroṇa departed in joy as before.

In the thirty-eighth sūtra, on transcendence, Śāriputra explained the same five wisdoms — knowing form, knowing its gathering (from delight and craving), knowing its cessation, knowing relish toward it, knowing its danger, and knowing escape from it — as together the wisdom of the formations' flowing and reverse cessation, defiled condition, purifying condition, and purity; lacking these five, one cannot transcend form, nor, by the same reasoning, feeling, perception, formation, or consciousness. Śāriputra again led Śroṇa through the same elevenfold exhaustive-and-shared-nature observation of the five aggregates as without self and without what belongs to self, from which follows freedom from craving for anything in the world at all, non-clinging, and arhatship — the first fruit's birth exhausted, the third fruit's pure life established, the fourth fruit's task completed, and the knowledge that no further existence remains. Śroṇa again departed in joy. These passages together show that, before the noble path is realized, one must return again and again to the same truths in thought.

29. Section: Delight and Greed Distinguished, Six Ways

Delight named for gladness arising conditioned upon a support-feeling; attachment arising conditioned upon a feeling toward an object is called greed. Further, arising toward an object about to be obtained is called delight; arising toward an object already obtained is called greed. Further, at the point of having obtained an object and being about to enjoy it is called delight; at the very point of actually enjoying this matter is called greed. Further, toward the means of obtaining an object is called delight; toward the object itself is called greed. Further, toward future existence is called delight; toward a present object is called greed. Further, toward the honor and benefit of another loved being is called delight; toward honor and benefit obtained for oneself is called greed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Six angles distinguish delight from greed. First, by feeling's own basis: joy toward one's own body-based feeling — as the ease felt after a hot spring — is delight, a self-love; craving toward the feeling an outer object provokes — coming to crave the hot spring itself — is greed, an object-love. Second, by timing relative to obtaining: joy that a winning ticket has been drawn, before it is even collected, is delight; attachment to the money once collected, and to multiplying it further, is greed. Third, by timing relative to enjoying: the eager joy of a trip already booked, not yet begun, is delight; the attachment that extends the return flight once the trip is actually underway is greed. Fourth, by means versus object: enjoying gambling as a quick means to money is delight; attachment to the money itself is greed. Fifth, by future versus present: craving for a future existence is delight; craving for the present object is greed. Sixth, by self versus other: rejoicing in a loved one's own honor and gain is delight; joy in one's own honor and gain is greed.

30. Section: Background — Sūtras 39-41, Mahākauṣṭhila and Śāriputra on Ignorance and Clarity, Closing "Freedom From Delusion"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-ninth through forty-first sūtras

At Vulture Peak, the venerable Mahākauṣṭhila — himself already accomplished — asked Śāriputra, for the benefit of later generations: what is ignorance, and who has it? Śāriputra answered: ignorance is simply not knowing. Pressed further on what exactly is not known, Śāriputra explained across the thirty-ninth sūtra: not knowing form's past nature as impermanent, its present nature as a ceasing dharma, and its future nature as an arising-and-ceasing dharma — and the same for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness — is ignorance; knowing all this as it truly is, is clarity, called direct knowledge, direct seeing, brightness, awakening, wisdom, and unbroken realization.

In the fortieth sūtra, Śāriputra answered the same question through the four noble truths instead: not knowing form, form's arising, form's cessation, and the path to form's cessation — and the same for the remaining four aggregates — is ignorance; knowing all this as it truly is, is clarity. In the forty-first sūtra, Śāriputra answered through the nine kinds of wisdom already explained above: not knowing the first five (the wisdom of the formations' flowing, reverse cessation, defiled condition, purifying condition, and purity) is ignorance; and clarity requires further realizing the four truths' wisdoms of suffering, its cause, cessation, and the path. With this, freedom from delusion — corresponding to sūtras 39 through 41 — is complete: knowing the three times' impermanence brings clarity; failing to know it brings ignorance, and ignorance in turn gives rise to greed, hatred, and delusion, whose further synonyms and the distinction between delight and greed have already been explained above.

厭背想者，復有四行。謂於諸行思惟如病、如癰、如箭、惱害。如病者，謂如有一，因界錯亂所生病苦，修厭背想。如癰者，謂如有一，因於先業所生癰苦，修厭背想。如箭者，謂如有一，因他怨箭所中之苦，修厭背想。

The perception of weariness and turning away has, further, four aspects — namely, contemplating the formations as a disease, as a boil, as an arrow, and as an affliction. As a disease is, for instance, like one whose suffering arises from the disorder of the elements, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away. As a boil is like one whose suffering arises from prior karma, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away. As an arrow is like one whose suffering arises from being struck by an enemy's arrow, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As a disease: the elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, consciousness — falling into disorder brings bodily sickness and suffering; recognizing this danger, one cultivates weariness and turning away. As a boil: the aggregates, produced by affliction and karma, are like a poisoned growth, their very nature suffering — wherever conditions meet, affliction flows out from them, dooming one to further suffering ahead; recognizing the aggregates as carrying the seeds of every affliction, hidden trouble festering beneath the surface, one cultivates weariness and turning away. As an arrow: the aggregates' danger is like an enemy's arrow lodged deep in the body, agonizing and hard to draw out; recognizing this, one cultivates weariness and turning away.

35. Section: The Fourth Aspect — Affliction, and the Summary of Weariness and Turning Away

As an affliction is, for instance, like one whose various sufferings arise from one's own wrong reckoning amid the loss of loved ones, wealth, and the like, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away. Thus is it named: a practitioner cultivating insight cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away regarding the formations.

Chinese Source

惱害者，謂於親財等匱乏中，因自邪計所生諸苦，修厭背想。如是名為修觀行者，於諸行中修厭背想。

Ru-Yi Translation

As an affliction is, for instance, like one whose various sufferings arise from one's own wrong reckoning amid the loss of loved ones, wealth, and the like, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away. Thus is it named: a practitioner cultivating insight cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away regarding the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

The Notes gloss "loss of loved ones, wealth, and the like" as the death or estrangement of loved relatives, the loss of one's wealth, or the loss of one's own health, and "one's own wrong reckoning" as the unwise discrimination that turns such loss into suffering. Because beings wrongly conceive of self and what-belongs-to-self within the aggregates, the ordinary losses of relatives, wealth, and health become occasions of anguish — recognizing this, one cultivates weariness and turning away, completing the fourfold contemplation.

36. Section: Danger — Impermanence and Suffering

The perception of danger has, further, two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations as impermanent and contemplating them as suffering.

Chinese Source

過患想者，復有二行。謂於諸行思惟無常及思惟苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The perception of danger has, further, two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations as impermanent and contemplating them as suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Impermanence is every conditioned dharma's shared mark: the body ages, sickens, and dies; thoughts never cease shifting; feeling swings ceaselessly between pain, pleasure, and neither; consciousness discriminates without a moment's rest. Being impermanent, none of it can hold lasting ease, hence suffering — recognizing the aggregates' great danger through these two, a practitioner advances further.

37. Section: True Meaning — Emptiness and No-Self, and the Concluding Statement on Cause and Fruit

The perception of true meaning likewise has two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations' emptiness-nature and their no-self-nature. Here, one first properly cultivates the perception of danger and the perception of true meaning, and only afterward is able to abide in the perception of weariness and turning away. One should know that here the fruit is spoken of first, and the cause afterward.

Chinese Source

實義想者，亦有二行。謂於諸行思惟空性及無我性。此中先於過患想及實義想正修習已，然後方能住厭背想。當知此中，先說其果，後說其因。

Ru-Yi Translation

The perception of true meaning likewise has two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations' emptiness-nature and their no-self-nature. Here, one first properly cultivates the perception of danger and the perception of true meaning, and only afterward is able to abide in the perception of weariness and turning away. One should know that here the fruit is spoken of first, and the cause afterward.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

No-self means there is no self within the aggregates; emptiness means there is no self apart from the aggregates either, inside or outside — hence empty. Through repeated hearing, reflection, and cultivation of calm and insight, one first cuts off the discriminated self-clinging and, further, the innate clinging to self and to what belongs to self. The treatise explains that only after cultivating danger and true meaning does weariness and turning away truly settle — the four-aspect contemplation (disease, boil, arrow, affliction) is a stream-enterer's own advancement, its fruit, described first; the impermanence/suffering/emptiness/no-self contemplation that precedes stream-entry is the cause, described after. In practice, all eight are cultivated both before and after the first fruit, only with a shifting emphasis: before stream-entry, weariness through disease-boil-arrow-affliction leads into impermanence-suffering-emptiness-no-self and the uninterrupted concentration that enters the path of seeing; after stream-entry, the same weariness-contemplation purifies what has already been seen, still resting finally on the same four marks, all the way through the fourth fruit and the present dwelling in ease.

38. Section: Background — Sūtra 42, Mahākauṣṭhila and Śāriputra on Advancement, Closing "Advancement"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-second sūtra

At Vulture Peak, Mahākauṣṭhila asked Śāriputra how one not yet directly realizing the truths should think in order to advance toward uninterrupted realization. Śāriputra answered: to attain the first fruit, diligently contemplate the five aggregates through three perceptions — weariness and turning away (as disease, boil, thorn, and slayer — corresponding to this treatise's arrow and affliction), danger (impermanence and suffering), and true meaning (emptiness and not-self). Asked the same question for the second, third, and fourth fruits, and finally for the present dwelling in ease of an arhat already accomplished, Śāriputra gave the very same answer each time — the same eightfold contemplation carrying a practitioner, ever more finely observed, from ordinary being all the way through arhatship and the present dwelling in ease. With this, advancement — corresponding to sūtra 42 — is complete.

39. Section: The View of Self Distinguished Begins — Four Kinds of Self-View Named

Further, there are four kinds of view of self serving as a basis, able to give rise to conceit.

Chinese Source

復次，有四種我見為所依止，能生我慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are four kinds of view of self serving as a basis, able to give rise to conceit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The view of self distinguished explains the third line of perception and formation's verse, "the view of self distinguished, threefold mark and formation," corresponding to sūtra 44.

40. Section: The Four Kinds of Self-View Explained

First, the discriminated view of self, which arises among outsiders. Second, the innate view of self, which arises even down among birds and beasts. Third, the view of self conditioned upon one's own basis, which arises with regard to each one's own inner body. Fourth, the view of self conditioned upon another's basis, which arises with regard to another's body.

Chinese Source

一、有分別我見，謂諸外道所起。二、俱生我見，謂下至禽獸等亦能生起。三、緣自依止我見，謂於各別內身所起。四、緣他依止我見，謂於他身所起。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the discriminated view of self, which arises among outsiders. Second, the innate view of self, which arises even down among birds and beasts. Third, the view of self conditioned upon one's own basis, which arises with regard to each one's own inner body. Fourth, the view of self conditioned upon another's basis, which arises with regard to another's body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The discriminated view of self is what outsiders' own texts and doctrines argue for explicitly, clinging to the aggregates as self or as belonging to self — though in truth any ordinary being not yet awakened to reality carries both this and the innate view, not outsiders alone; the treatise singles out outsiders here only to mark Buddhism's basic difference from them, since outsiders alone refuse to teach no-self. The innate view needs no argument at all — even an animal facing a tiger instinctively flees, a pig or sheep led to slaughter instinctively panics, proof enough that every being clings instinctively to its own life. The view conditioned on one's own basis, clinging to a real self within this present body, is called the view of a perishable aggregate; the view conditioned on another's basis takes another's aggregated body as a genuinely real other being, and from this comparison the discrimination of self, other, and others-among-others arises.

41. Section: How the Discriminated Self-View Generates Conceit — the Mirror Simile

Regarding the discriminated view of self serving as a basis to generate conceit: through this view, one observes one's own and another's body and reckons a real self to exist; through these two kinds of self-view as a basis, conceit arises. It is like the surface of a clean, round mirror, upon which, conditioned by the reflected object, an image arises; conditioned by this image, in dependence on itself it gives rise to the thought of inferior, middling, or superior. Thus, through wrong discrimination, the view of self conditioned on one's own basis serves as a condition, giving rise to the view of self conditioned on another's basis — just as an image arises in dependence on a reflected object. And this in turn serves as a condition, giving rise to conceit, which faces others and reckons oneself superior, equal, or inferior.

Chinese Source

分別我見為所依止生我慢者，謂由此見觀自、他身，計有實我；由此二種我見為依，發生我慢。譬如清淨圓鏡面上，質像為依，發生影像；影像為依，於自依止發生劣中勝想。如是由邪分別故，緣自依止我見為緣，發生緣他依止我見。如依質像，發生影像。又此為緣，發生我慢，方他謂己或勝、或等、或劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the discriminated view of self serving as a basis to generate conceit: through this view, one observes one's own and another's body and reckons a real self to exist; through these two kinds of self-view as a basis, conceit arises. It is like the surface of a clean, round mirror, upon which, conditioned by the reflected object, an image arises; conditioned by this image, in dependence on itself it gives rise to the thought of inferior, middling, or superior. Thus, through wrong discrimination, the view of self conditioned on one's own basis serves as a condition, giving rise to the view of self conditioned on another's basis — just as an image arises in dependence on a reflected object. And this in turn serves as a condition, giving rise to conceit, which faces others and reckons oneself superior, equal, or inferior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and Master Yin Shun's Modern Treatise on the Middle Way

Standing before a clean mirror, one's reflection appears; wrongly taking that reflection to be "me" is the view of self conditioned on one's own basis. Seeing another's body then calls up a corresponding image in one's own mind, wrongly taken as another real self — the view of self conditioned on another's basis. Comparing these two, before and after, or against another, gives rise to the thought of inferior, equal, or superior — precisely conceit. Master Yin Shun distinguishes the view conditioned on one's own basis (the view of a perishable aggregate, a direct felt sense of one's own reality) from the view conditioned on another's basis (clinging to a person as a real being, called the view of a person) — the two are quite different, since the perishable-aggregate view concerns only one's own body and mind directly, while the view of a person can regard even animals as real beings without generating any sense of "self" in them.

42. Section: How the Innate Self-View Generates Conceit — the Clear-Water Simile

Regarding the innate view of self serving as a condition to generate conceit, one should know the simile differs from before — it is like a person of clear sight, standing before a vessel of clean water, observing their own eyes and ears. The rest is as before, and its mark should be known accordingly.

Chinese Source

俱生我見為緣生我慢者，當知譬喻與前差別。如明眼人，臨淨水器自觀眼、耳。所餘如前，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the innate view of self serving as a condition to generate conceit, one should know the simile differs from before — it is like a person of clear sight, standing before a vessel of clean water, observing their own eyes and ears. The rest is as before, and its mark should be known accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary Notes, fascicle 22 (Master Tài)

Where the mirror was man-made, standing for the discriminated view comparing self against other, the water is entirely natural, standing for the innate view, which needs only one's own reflection to arise — seeing one's own eyes and ears in the water and thinking, unprompted, "how fine my eyes are," "what fortunate ears I have." The mirror-conceit arises by placing oneself above another, elevating self over other; the water-conceit arises simply by clinging fondly to one's own excellence, closer to what is elsewhere called pride — arising from one's own basis alone, without any comparison to another at all.

43. Section: How Self-View Is Cut Off — Only Through the Well-Taught Dharma, and True Gratitude

This entire perishable-aggregate view, of every kind, can be permanently cut off only by relying on the well-taught dharma and discipline, and by no other, wrong teaching. Thus does the Tathāgata, and fellow practitioners of the pure life known to the community, or a teacher's own disciples who are fellow practitioners, hold great kindness. Solely through this one condition is it named true repayment of kindness to the great teacher, or, after his passing, to one's fellow practitioners of the pure life.

Chinese Source

此一切種薩迦耶見，唯依善說法毗奈耶方能永斷，非餘邪教。如是如來，及眾共知同梵行者、或諸弟子同梵行者，有大恩德。唯由如是一因緣故，名於大師、或滅度後同梵行者真實報恩。

Ru-Yi Translation

This entire perishable-aggregate view, of every kind, can be permanently cut off only by relying on the well-taught dharma and discipline, and by no other, wrong teaching. Thus does the Tathāgata, and fellow practitioners of the pure life known to the community, or a teacher's own disciples who are fellow practitioners, hold great kindness. Solely through this one condition is it named true repayment of kindness to the great teacher, or, after his passing, to one's fellow practitioners of the pure life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Only the Buddha's own dharma and discipline — nowhere found among outsider teachings — can permanently cut off all four kinds of perishable-aggregate view; the Buddha and the noble disciples who transmit this liberation from generation to generation hold immeasurable kindness toward beings. The Buddha himself, across countless kalpas of difficult practice, sought out and realized this very dharma for the sake of beings; relying on it to attain genuine liberation is therefore called true repayment of that kindness.

44. Section: A Second Kind — Partial Repayment of Kindness, and Background on Sūtra 44

Further, through a second condition: if there is one able, in reliance on the meaning of these very distinctions, to diligently cultivate right practice for the sake of benefit, this too is named partial repayment of kindness, since what one hopes for is not yet fulfilled.

Chinese Source

又由第二，謂若有能即依如是差別句義為利益故，勤修正行，如是亦名隨分報恩，彼所希望未滿足故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through a second condition: if there is one able, in reliance on the meaning of these very distinctions, to diligently cultivate right practice for the sake of benefit, this too is named partial repayment of kindness, since what one hopes for is not yet fulfilled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 20) and on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-fourth sūtra

A practitioner still diligently cultivating toward liberation, whether for their own sake or for the sake of all beings, but not yet established in the pure life, has not yet fulfilled the hope of cutting off self-view — yet this sincere effort is itself already, by its very nature, an offering to the great teacher, and so is called partial repayment. The Notes cite the Cultivation-Perfected Ground: a teacher who rightly practices the dharma in accordance with the dharma, manifesting complete awakening only to establish the true teaching, thereby shows others how to practice rightly in turn.

The venerable Pūrṇa Maitrāyaṇīputra, foremost in teaching the dharma, once told Ānanda at Kauśāmbī's Ghoṣila Park: beings cling to arisen dharmas as self, not to the unarisen — no one clings to unconditioned nirvāṇa as self, but the moment-to-moment continuity of the arisen body is clung to as self, and likewise for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Using the same mirror and clear-water similes just explained, Pūrṇa showed Ānanda how the discriminated and innate self-views arise. Questioned in turn, Ānanda confirmed the aggregates as impermanent, hence suffering — the truest, most ultimate suffering, known only to the noble — hence not self, not other than self, not mutually contained; observing each aggregate's exhaustive nature across past, future, present, inner, outer, coarse, fine, superior, inferior, far, and near, and its shared nature, leads to weariness, freedom from desire, and liberation, up through all four fruits. Ānanda declared that Pūrṇa's teaching this very method was what had first brought him to the dharma-eye and the first fruit, and that he himself had transmitted this same method ever since — his own true repayment of kindness, a method found nowhere outside the Buddha's own teaching. With this, the view of self distinguished, corresponding to sūtra 44, is complete.

45. Section: Threefold Mark and Formation Begins — Three Marks of the Cessation of Formations Named

Further, through three marks the formations cease, and this is named the realm of nirvāṇa without residue. First, through the cessation of formations previously arisen. Second, through the cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature. Third, through the permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions.

Chinese Source

復次，由三種相諸行滅故，說名無餘依涅槃界。一者、先所生起諸行滅故；二者、自性滅壞諸行滅故；三者、一切煩惱永離繫故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through three marks the formations cease, and this is named the realm of nirvāṇa without residue. First, through the cessation of formations previously arisen. Second, through the cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature. Third, through the permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Threefold mark and formation explains the verse's fourth line, distinguishing nirvāṇa with residue — where the aggregated body remains but affliction is already gone — from nirvāṇa without residue, where the aggregates themselves are entirely extinguished, through these three marks.

46. Section: The First Mark — Formations Previously Arisen Cease

The cessation of formations previously arisen means that the aggregates giving rise to future existence, produced by karma and affliction from a former life, and sought through a former vow, permanently cease.

Chinese Source

先所生起諸行滅者，謂於先世能感後有諸業煩惱之所造作，及由先願之所思求，令所生起諸行永滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

The cessation of formations previously arisen means that the aggregates giving rise to future existence, produced by karma and affliction from a former life, and sought through a former vow, permanently cease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This present aggregated body was produced by past karma and affliction and by a past craving for further existence; its permanent cessation, at the exhaustion of this life's span, is the first mark.

47. Section: The Second and Third Marks — Self-Ruin and the Permanent Release From Affliction

The cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature means that, having arisen, they perish of their own accord, never abiding to their ultimate extent, and the formations permanently cease. The permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions means that the afflictions are cut off entirely, without residue, and, through their present cessation, do not arise again hereafter.

Chinese Source

自性滅壞諸行滅者，謂彼生已任性滅壞，非究竟住諸行永滅。一切煩惱永離繫者，謂諸煩惱無餘斷滅，由今滅故，後不更生。

Ru-Yi Translation

The cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature means that, having arisen, they perish of their own accord, never abiding to their ultimate extent, and the formations permanently cease. The permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions means that the afflictions are cut off entirely, without residue, and, through their present cessation, do not arise again hereafter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Not one moment of the aggregates truly abides — arisen, they ruin themselves of their own accord; an ordinary being's afflictions, uncut, water the seeds of language and karma into a further life, so the aggregates never cease for good, only an arhat truly permanently ceasing them. And since every affliction is cut off root and branch, no further cause remains to draw a future life's aggregates into being — with these three marks together, the formations cease entirely, and this is nirvāṇa without residue.

48. Section: Concluding the Name — Nirvāṇa Is Not Utter Non-Existence, and the Refutation of Four Wrong Positions

Therefore, through these three marks of the cessation of formations, it is named quiescence — not that it is utterly without mark, but that its mark is different. If it were utterly without mark, it could not even be posited as being named quiescence.

Chinese Source

是故由此三相諸行滅故，說名寂滅。非永無相，其相異故。若永無相，不可施設說名寂滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore, through these three marks of the cessation of formations, it is named quiescence — not that it is utterly without mark, but that its mark is different. If it were utterly without mark, it could not even be posited as being named quiescence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi's own Investigation (fascicle 65)

Nirvāṇa without residue must not be clung to as utter nothingness — quiescence is inconceivable, an absolute beyond the relative, neither rightly called existent nor non-existent. The Investigation into the Ground of Wisdom Born of Reflection rejects all four possible positions as unreasonable to ask, answer, or think: that nirvāṇa is different from conditioned dharmas — impossible, since it is only what conditioned dharmas' own cessation reveals, with no nirvāṇa apart from them; that it is not different — equally impossible, since defilement and purity, the impermanent and the permanent, are simply not the same; that it is both different and not different — mere idle speculation, since nirvāṇa is realized, not spoken of; and that it is neither different nor not different — itself a form of annihilationism, equally unreasonable. Nirvāṇa's true mark is only what every wholesome dharma — precept, concentration, wisdom — reveals: quiescent, beyond all speculation, realized only within, a matter where words and mental grasping both give out, known only by direct realization, never to be settled by declaring it existent or non-existent in advance.

The Investigation further explains conditioned dharmas by five marks (the future not yet arisen, the past already ceased, the present abiding in its own mark, causes continuing in succession, and effects continuing in succession) and unconditioned dharmas by the reverse of these five — since affliction, karma, and suffering are neither identical nor separate, nirvāṇa likewise can be called neither the same as nor different from conditioned dharmas; its mark is known only through direct realization.

49. Section: Background — Sūtra 43, Śāriputra and Ānanda on Cessation, Closing Threefold Mark and Formation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-third sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, Śāriputra — an arhat — humbly asked the first-fruit Ānanda: what is cessation, and who has it? Ānanda answered that the five aggregates are themselves the fruit of past karma and affliction, born of a past craving for further existence — one should blame no parent for one's birth, only one's own past delight, since "delight gathers, and so form gathers." Formations already arisen are impermanent, ceasing moment by moment; present formations likewise inevitably perish of their own nature; and once the future arising-and-ceasing dharma itself ceases — because affliction is permanently cut off — this alone is truly named cessation, nirvāṇa without residue: the sūtra's "impermanent" corresponds to the treatise's first mark, its "ceasing dharma" to the second, and "that dharma having ceased, this is named cessation" to the third. Śāriputra then repeated Ānanda's own answer back to him, adding: were the five aggregates not produced by past karma and affliction, nor drawn forth by craving for further existence, how could they ever cease at all? — precisely, "with cause and condition the world arises, with cause and condition the world ceases." The two rejoiced together, each confirming the other's understanding. With this, threefold mark and formation, corresponding to sūtra 43, is complete.

51. Section: The Wishlessness Gate — Impermanence and Suffering; the Emptiness Gate — No-Self

All formations are impermanent, and all formations are suffering — these, relying on the gate of wishlessness, establish the first and second dharma-summary verses. All dharmas are without self — this, relying on the gate of emptiness, establishes the third dharma-summary verse.

Chinese Source

一切行無常、一切行苦者，依無願解脫門，建立第一、第二法嗢柁南。一切法無我者，依空解脫門，建立第三法嗢柁南。

Ru-Yi Translation

All formations are impermanent, and all formations are suffering — these, relying on the gate of wishlessness, establish the first and second dharma-summary verses. All dharmas are without self — this, relying on the gate of emptiness, establishes the third dharma-summary verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Seeing the aggregates' exhaustive and shared nature as impermanent and hence suffering brings weariness and, at last, freedom from any wish for further existence in the three realms — this is wishlessness, an arhat's own case. Seeing the aggregates, past, present, future, inner, outer, coarse, fine, superior, inferior, far, and near, as neither self nor belonging to self, no fixed ruling self ever to be found in or apart from them, is the gate of emptiness.

52. Section: The Signlessness Gate — Nirvāṇa's Quiescence

Nirvāṇa's quiescence — this, relying on the gate of signlessness, establishes the fourth dharma-summary verse.

Chinese Source

涅槃寂靜者，依無相解脫門，建立第四法嗢柁南。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nirvāṇa's quiescence — this, relying on the gate of signlessness, establishes the fourth dharma-summary verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 50's fourfold quiescence in both kinds of nirvāṇa

Having seen the aggregates as empty and without self or what-belongs-to-self, one comes to see that what the self depended on — the aggregates themselves — is equally ungraspable, and thus enters signlessness; realizing all signs as ungraspable is itself liberation. Nirvāṇa is called quiescent precisely because it carries no mark of the aggregates or the six sense-faculties at all. Fascicle 50 further distinguishes four kinds of quiescence within nirvāṇa with residue (of suffering, of affliction, of not harming beings, and of equanimity) and four within nirvāṇa without residue. Altogether: the gate of wishlessness establishes impermanence and suffering; the gate of emptiness establishes no-self; the gate of signlessness establishes nirvāṇa's quiescence — the four dharma seals in full.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 54) and the Compendium for Elucidation

Self-view is never a single, indivisible thing but always this threefold operation together. The grasped object is simply the five aggregates. The grasper is the four kinds of grasping: grasping through desire (chiefly a householder's clinging to bodies, one's own and another's); grasping through view (wrong views such as eternalism, annihilationism, and the sixty-two views, chiefly among outsider renunciants); grasping through rules and vows (an outsider's mistaken belief that certain observances alone bring liberation); and grasping through the assertion of self (self-clinging built into the very word "I," common to renunciant and householder alike). As for how grasping occurs: consciousness abides in and clings to form, feeling, perception, and formation — the four abodes of consciousness — taking each as its object; form thus becomes the basis of the grasped mind through being clung to and taken as an object, while the coarse residue this clinging leaves behind becomes the basis of the grasper mind, and the same holds for feeling, perception, and formation. Fascicle 89 offers a related but distinct account, worth reading alongside this one.

64. Section: Background — Sūtra 45, the Elder Chanda and Ānanda on Fear of No-Self, Closing "The Dharma Summarized" and Sūtras 36-45

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-fifth sūtra

Shortly after the Buddha's passing, at the Deer Park in Vārāṇasī, the elder Chanda went from elder to elder seeking teaching on the dharma's true reality. Each told him the same three seals — form and the other aggregates are impermanent, all formations impermanent, all dharmas without self, nirvāṇa quiescent, corresponding respectively to the gates of wishlessness, emptiness, and signlessness. Chanda found he already knew this intellectually, yet whenever he actually contemplated all formations' impermanence, all dharmas' no-self, and the utter cessation of craving in nirvāṇa, his mind grew confused and afraid, unable to settle or accept it, wondering: if this is so, where am I, and what supports my continued existence? Unsatisfied, he thought of Ānanda, foremost in learning, and traveled to Kauśāmbī to find him — the "empowerment of the person."

Ānanda praised Chanda's honesty in admitting his fear rather than pretending understanding, and, seeing him now ready through wholesome desire's understanding-mind and pliant-mind, taught him what he himself had heard from the Buddha regarding Mahākātyāyana: ordinary people fall to one of two extremes, clinging to real existence or real non-existence; but one who neither clings to nor grasps at the aggregates as self, who simply knows suffering as it arises and ceases without doubt, sees rightly. Seeing the world's arising as it truly is, dependently originated through affliction, karma, and suffering, one does not fall into the extreme of non-existence; seeing its cessation likewise as dependently arisen, one does not fall into the extreme of existence. The Tathāgata, apart from both extremes, teaches the middle: this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises — thus with ignorance formations arise, on through birth, aging, sickness, death, and sorrow; this not existing, that does not exist; this ceasing, that ceases — thus with ignorance's cessation, formations cease, and so on to the cessation of the whole mass of suffering. Understanding dependent origination this way, one falls into neither extreme and walks the middle path — the "empowerment of the dharma," resolving exactly Chanda's own oscillation between eternalism and annihilationism, and his own "empowerment of self" in genuinely grasping it.

Chanda thereby gained the pure dharma-eye and the stream-enterer's benefits — seeing the dharma, gaining the dharma, knowing the dharma, arousing the dharma (the fourth benefit, elsewhere called thorough firmness), transcending doubt about himself and about others, needing no other's aid, and fearlessness before the teacher's own doctrine — eight of the ten benefits already explained above. Chanda declared he would never again retreat from this understanding, holding no further self or what-belongs-to-self, seeing only the true dharma; Ānanda confirmed his attainment, and the two parted in mutual joy. With this, the dharma summarized, corresponding to sūtra 45, is complete — and with it, perception and formation's own particular verse, spanning sūtras 36 through 45, is entirely closed. A closing verse gathers all ten: "Śroṇa's three" (36-38), "ignorance also three" (39-41), "uninterrupted realization" (42), "cessation" (43), "Pūrṇa" (44), and "Chanda" (45).

The power of habituation is patient, results-blind practice — like a seed sown in the ground: checking daily for a sprout and despairing when none appears only breeds the very weariness that causes one to quit, whereas a digger who keeps digging the same well, without giving up, eventually strikes water; digging halfheartedly in many places never does.

74. Section: Background — Sūtra 46, the Hen and Her Eggs, Closing "Swift Penetration"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-sixth sūtra

At a cowherds' settlement in the Kuru country, the Buddha told the monks: it is only through wisdom and vision — never without them — that he continued cultivating the higher path and cut off every affliction; knowing form, its arising, and its cessation, and the same for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness (the wisdom of flowing and reverse cessation already explained), is exactly this wisdom and vision. Relying on this wisdom and vision as the power of wisdom, one must then diligently apply the powers of non-laxity and habituation — the four skillful matters, the thirty-seven factors of awakening in full — or liberation remains out of reach.

The Buddha then gave four similes. A hen who lays many eggs but cannot sit steadily to warm them will find her chicks unable to peck safely through their shells, however much she might wish it — she has simply not done the necessary work; a monk who is lax and does not repeatedly cultivate the thirty-seven factors cannot reach liberation either. But a hen who broods steadily, keeping the eggs at the right warmth, need not even wish for her chicks to hatch — when conditions ripen, they break through safely on their own; likewise a diligent monk, cultivating calm and insight without dwelling on when liberation will come, naturally reaches arhatship once conditions ripen — precisely the earlier warning against wondering "will I finish today, tomorrow, or the day after," since that very anxious wondering is itself what breeds the weariness that stalls practice. Next, an axe handle gripped daily by a craftsman or apprentice wears smooth without their ever noticing the wear day to day — so too a practitioner's affliction wears thin through daily cultivation, unnoticed yet real, the mark of habituation showing itself in time even without being felt. Finally, a great ship left tied at the shore through six months of sun and wind has its mooring rope slowly rot through — so too a diligent monk's every fetter, bond, driving urge, and entanglement gradually comes loose through sustained cultivation of the thirty-seven factors. Hearing this teaching, sixty monks in the assembly cut off every affliction on the spot and became arhats; all present rejoiced and put the teaching into practice. With this, swift penetration, corresponding to sūtra 46, is complete.

81. Section: Background — Sūtra 47, the Clay Ball and the Buddha's Own Past as Wheel-Turning King, Closing "One's Own Being"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-seventh sūtra

At Śrāvastī, a monk meditating wondered whether any aggregate could remain forever unchanging, and brought the question to the Buddha. The Buddha held up a small lump of clay and asked whether the monk saw it — yes, he said — explaining that this tiny lump stands for "self": ungraspable, exactly as no truly permanent, unchanging self can ever be found, since nothing in the world is permanent, constant, and unchanging.

The Buddha then recalled his own countless past lives: even the most vast and flourishing existence eventually wears away. Through repeatedly practicing giving, precepts, and cultivation across saṃsāra's long night, he had once gained the three distinguished fulfillments described above, and through seven years of boundless loving-kindness, passed seven full cycles of the universe's formation and dissolution without returning to this world — reborn as a radiant god at each dissolution, and as the great Brahmā, unsurpassed ruler of a thousand worlds, at each formation; thereafter reborn thirty-six times as Śakra, lord of the Thirty-Three, and over ten thousand times as a wheel-turning king ruling the four continents with the ten wholesome ways, possessed of the seven treasures (a flying wheel, war-elephant and war-horse, a wish-jewel, a consort, a treasurer, and a general), a thousand valiant sons, lands free of thorns, and neighboring kingdoms won over by virtue rather than force — with eighty-four thousand of everything: elephants, horses, chariots, palaces, jeweled beds, robes, consorts, and dishes of food, though, the Buddha pointed out, a king can only ever ride one elephant, wear one robe, sleep in one bed, live in one palace at a time. And yet, the Buddha said, all of it has passed, changed, and become utterly unobtainable now — so, monks, do not cling to these aggregates; grow weary of them, become free of desire for them, and accomplish liberation.

Questioned again, the monk confirmed the aggregates as impermanent and hence suffering, moving from the perception of danger into the perception of true meaning — no self within the aggregates, no self apart from them, no mutual containment — and, guided further through their exhaustive and shared nature, came at last to weariness, freedom from desire, and liberation. He departed in joy, and, continually recalling this very teaching — the clay-ball simile above all — dwelling alone in diligent, unremitting reflection, in time also attained arhatship. With this, one's own being, corresponding to sūtra 47, is complete.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Wisdom's object explains swift penetration's third topic, corresponding to sūtra 48. Wisdom already attained is subsequently attained wisdom; wisdom not yet attained is uncontaminated wisdom still in preparatory application; unerring wisdom is fundamental non-discriminating wisdom. The fourth knows what is truly absent as absent — for a śrāvaka, that no self is ever found within the aggregates. The fifth knows what still remains as not empty — the aggregates themselves, illusion-like yet functioning, arising and ceasing though selfless, never to be denied outright. The sixth sees the aggregates' suffering and impurity; the seventh, their moment-to-moment perishing. A practitioner seeking to cut off craving and views observes the aggregates through all seven angles together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The body is roughly seventy percent water — blood and the rest — proof enough it is no true self, only resembling one by its constant presence before the mind. Nor does it obey the will: one wishes for good eyesight, yet the eyes water at the mere effort of reading; unlike a lump of clay shaped at will, the body cannot be commanded not to age or change. Like foam thrown up by rushing water, form crumbles bubble by bubble, always dissolving even as it seems to hold together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary Notes

Sense-faculty, object, and consciousness come together as contact, and from contact comes feeling — appearing real, but only ever a dharma resembling reality, arisen from a threefold meeting, as clouds (the object), earth (the sense-faculties), and rain (consciousness) come together to produce sudden rainfall. Like a bubble that rises where a raindrop strikes the ground and vanishes again almost at once, feeling appears and dissolves in the same fleeting instant.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Being a dharma resembling something real, able to display, able to burn, and able to cause confusion regarding a known object — through these, one awakens to every perception as like a mirage. Through the fundamental cutting off of the view of a perishable self, through the difference among the causes of one's own being's many categories, and through there being, moment by moment, no dharma resembling even a brief pause afterward — through these, one awakens to every formation as like a plantain trunk.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary Notes

Perception displays an object the way a mirage shimmers on the horizon; it then burns the mind the way a thirsty deer, chasing a mirage's illusory water, only inflames its own craving; and, being merely a confused resemblance of the real, mistaking it for truth breeds the very confusion that hardens, over time, into fixed wrong view. Formation — willed action of body, speech, and mind — is what ordinary beings most readily mistake for a controlling self; but peeling it apart like a plantain trunk, leaf by leaf, reveals no solid core at all, cutting self-view off at the root. Three reflections dissolve it: first, that it is hollow at the center, like the plantain; second, that its many categories all trace to differing causes and conditions, never one fixed "I" deciding; third, that no single decision even persists a moment before shifting to the next, so the very sense of a strong, ruling will is itself an illusion — will, too, is without self.

Contaminated consciousness carries grasping and karma within it; abiding in and clinging to form, feeling, perception, and formation in turn, it throws up ever-changing appearances the instant it stirs — exactly as a magician conjures elephants, horses, and soldiers from nothing at a crossroads, real to the eye yet without any substance behind them. Fascicle 84 already gives the same five similes together: form like a mass of foam, feeling like a floating bubble, perception like a mirage, formation like a plantain trunk, consciousness like a magical illusion.

87. Section: Background — Sūtra 48, the Five Similes on the Ganges' Bank, Closing "Wisdom's Object"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-eighth sūtra

By the Ganges, the Buddha taught the monks: when the river suddenly floods, the mass of foam it carries is, to the wise, seen at once as having nothing substantial in it, rising and vanishing with no solidity at all — and form, in every one of its eleven aspects across time and place, is exactly the same: without substance, like a disease, a boil, a thorn, a slayer, impermanent, suffering, empty, not self. Contemplating this leads through weariness, danger, and true meaning to the same conclusion: form is not solid, not real. So too, a bubble thrown up where heavy rain strikes the ground — earth standing for the six sense-faculties, dark clouds for the six objects, falling rain for the six consciousnesses, and the bubble itself for feeling, born the instant they meet and gone again just as fast — is no more solid. Perception is like the shimmering "wild horses" of heat above the ground in the dry season before the rains: approach for water and there is none. Formation, the Buddha said, is like a great, straight plantain trunk that a keen-eyed woodsman, seeking sound timber, cuts down and peels leaf by leaf, only to find nothing solid at the core — precisely the mark of every formation examined closely. And consciousness is like the illusions a magician conjures at a crossroads — phantom troops of elephants, horses, and infantry, vivid yet empty of any real substance.

The Buddha then gathered all five in verse: "See form like a mass of foam, feeling like a bubble on water, perception like a mirage in spring, formations like the plantain, and consciousness's own dharma like an illusion." He added that once vitality, warmth, and consciousness depart the body, it becomes an insensate log, cast out among the graves; hence the wise say to watch the body as one watches grass or wood. Diligent practitioners, contemplating the aggregates' utter lack of solidity day and night, with right mindfulness ever present, bring the conditioned formations at last to long cessation and enduring coolness. Hearing this, the monks rejoiced and put it into practice. With this, wisdom's object, corresponding to sūtra 48, is complete.

88. Section: Flowing Begins — Two Kinds of World Named

Further, there are two kinds of world that encompass every formation — first, the world of sentient beings; second, the container world. The world of sentient beings is named the birth-and-death of categories of being; the container world is named the birth-and-death of the container itself.

Chinese Source

復次，有二世間，攝一切行。一、有情世間，二、器世間。有情世間名種類生死，器世間名器生死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are two kinds of world that encompass every formation — first, the world of sentient beings; second, the container world. The world of sentient beings is named the birth-and-death of categories of being; the container world is named the birth-and-death of the container itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Flowing explains swift penetration's fourth topic, corresponding to sūtra 49. Every conditioned formation falls into one of two worlds: sentient beings, whose turning is birth-and-death by category, or the container world — mountains, rivers, cities, dwellings — whose turning is the birth-and-death of formation, continuance, decay, and emptiness itself.

89. Section: Five Points Distinguishing the Two Kinds of Birth-and-Death

The birth-and-death of categories of being differs from other kinds of birth-and-death; compared with the container's birth-and-death, one should know there are, in brief, five points of difference. First, the container's birth-and-death is produced by a shared cause; the birth-and-death of categories is produced only through an unshared cause — this is the first point, differing by cause. Further, the container's birth-and-death, without beginning or end, is interrupted between former and later periods; the birth-and-death of categories, without beginning or end, continues in unbroken succession, never interrupted — this is the second point, differing by time. Further, the container's birth-and-death may be destroyed by fire, water, or wind; the birth-and-death of categories is not so — this is the third point, differing by remedy. Further, the container's birth-and-death has a cause never permanently cut off; the birth-and-death of categories is not so — this is the fourth point, differing by cutting-off. Further, the container's birth-and-death, once interrupted, resumes again; the birth-and-death of categories, once cut off, does not resume — this is the fifth point, differing by continuation.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The birth-and-death of categories of being differs from other kinds of birth-and-death; compared with the container's birth-and-death, one should know there are, in brief, five points of difference. First, the container's birth-and-death is produced by a shared cause; the birth-and-death of categories is produced only through an unshared cause — this is the first point, differing by cause. Further, the container's birth-and-death, without beginning or end, is interrupted between former and later periods; the birth-and-death of categories, without beginning or end, continues in unbroken succession, never interrupted — this is the second point, differing by time. Further, the container's birth-and-death may be destroyed by fire, water, or wind; the birth-and-death of categories is not so — this is the third point, differing by remedy. Further, the container's birth-and-death has a cause never permanently cut off; the birth-and-death of categories is not so — this is the fourth point, differing by cutting-off. Further, the container's birth-and-death, once interrupted, resumes again; the birth-and-death of categories, once cut off, does not resume — this is the fifth point, differing by continuation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary Notes

By cause: the container world is shaped by shared karma, held in common by all who dwell in it, while each being's own birth is shaped by unshared, individual karma — the karma for a male body yields a male body, and so on, unshared with any other. By time: the container passes through formation, continuance, decay, and the empty kalpa — during which it vanishes entirely, dark as an inkwell, no day or night — before forming again after twenty empty kalpas, an interruption sentient beings' own birth-and-death, whirling like a firebrand without beginning, never shows. By remedy: the container is destroyed in stages by fire, water, and wind at the great kalpa's end, while a being's birth-and-death is ended only by cutting off affliction. By permanence of cause: as long as any being exists, the container's cause can never be fully exhausted, while an individual's own cause for rebirth can be cut off through the noble path. By continuation: the container, once emptied, forms again after the interval, while an arhat's own birth-and-death, once cut off, never resumes.

What ultimately drives craving is simply not understanding the emptiness of self and of dharmas — ignorance itself is craving's root cause, from which every turn of saṃsāra unfolds.

Ignorance-driven craving and grasping drive body, speech, and mind to create the karma that leads to rebirth among gods and humans or among the three lower realms — willingly embraced in the first case, dragged into against one's wishes in the second, but a fetter either way.

94. Section: The Fourth and Fifth Marks — Causes Succeeding One After Another, Without End

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, across a long span of time, through causes succeeding one after another, the formations continue in succession — their former limit hard to know, and their latter limit without end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Since beginningless ignorance has driven karma after karma in unbroken succession, the aggregates have never once paused — asking when they first began is itself a mistaken assumption of some fixed starting nature, since the formations, having no nature of their own, simply appear wherever conditions gather; hence the former limit is hard to know. And unless ignorance is actually cut off through practice, the latter limit remains equally without end — these are the five marks of flowing that explain birth-and-death's own cause and fruit: craving's cause (ignorance), craving's fruit (karma leading to good or bad destiny), craving's own nature (the three or two kinds), causes succeeding one another (hence the hard-to-know beginning), and the endless latter limit if ignorance goes uncut.

Never recognizing this present body and its surroundings as anything to be wearied of, one continues to act, generating meritorious, non-meritorious, and unmoving karma alike, and so is bound again to a next aggregated body, repeating the same bondage without end — and with that body comes the full weight of birth, aging, sickness, death, parting from the loved, meeting the unloved, not getting what is sought, and the blazing suffering of the five aggregates themselves. With this, flowing, corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-ninth sūtra, is complete.

99. Section: Background — Sūtra 49, the Dog Tied to a Post, Closing "Flowing"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-ninth sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, the Buddha told the monks: from beginningless birth and death, beings covered by ignorance and bound by the fetter of craving have wandered on through the long night, not knowing where suffering begins; so long as the fetter of craving is not cut, they never reach the end of suffering. A time may come when, after a long night without rain, the great ocean dries up entirely; a time may come when this great earth itself is utterly destroyed — and still beings covered by ignorance and bound by the fetter of craving go on wandering through birth and death, never reaching the end of suffering.

The Buddha then gave a simile: a dog tied to a post, so long as its tether is not broken, keeps circling that post through the long night. So too the foolish ordinary being, not knowing as it truly is form, form's arising, its cessation, its relish, its danger, and the escape from it — and likewise feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness — wanders on through the long night, turning along with form and with each of the other aggregates. Turning along with them, one is not freed from them; not freed from them, one is not freed from birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, distress, and suffering. The learned noble disciple, knowing each aggregate, its arising, its cessation, its relish, its danger, and the escape from it as they truly are, does not turn along with them, and so — the Buddha declared — is freed from form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, and freed from birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, distress, and suffering. Hearing this, the monks rejoiced and put it into practice. With this, flowing, corresponding to sūtra 49, is complete.

Contentment (喜足) explains swift penetration's fifth topic. What follows matches these four to going, standing, sitting, and lying down.

101. Section: The Four Matched to Going, Standing, Sitting, and Lying Down

Regarding these, in their proper order, ignorant beings either go toward them, or stand, or sit, or lie down.

102. Section: The Range of Craving Elaborated — Five Paths Along Which Craving Travels

Chinese Source

復有五種一切愚夫愛所行路。一者、後有，二者、未來所求境界，三者、將得現前境界，四者、已得所有境界，五者、現前受用境界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are five paths along which the craving of all ignorant beings travels. First, further existence; second, objects sought in the future; third, objects about to be obtained and now before one; fourth, every object already obtained; fifth, objects presently being enjoyed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Further existence: hoping, at death, to obtain yet another body as karmic fruit — wishing, for instance, that one's next life be as wealthy and honored as this one. Objects sought in the future: greed for future pleasant objects. Objects about to be obtained and now before one: joy as a desirable object about to be gained appears. Objects already obtained: joy at what one wanted, now at hand though not yet enjoyed. Objects presently being enjoyed: craving toward what one is enjoying right now.

The first is craving for further existence; the second, craving for this or that source of joy; the third through fifth, craving accompanied by delight and greed.

104. Section: How the Four Postures Encompass the Five Paths

One should know that here, going toward is of two kinds: first, toward further existence; second, toward objects sought in the future.

One should know that here, going toward is of two kinds: first, toward further existence; second, toward objects sought in the future.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Going toward (趨) means heading for, that is, pursuing, and it covers two: craving for further existence, pursuing the fruit of a coming life, and craving for this or that source of joy, pursuing desirable objects in the future. Standing (住) is waiting expectantly for a desirable object about to be obtained and now before one; sitting (坐) is being ready to enjoy what has been obtained but not yet enjoyed; lying down (臥) is the whole craving mind sinking into the object presently being enjoyed.

109. Section: Two Distinctions in the Fruit of Craving's Paths

Further, there are two distinctions in the marks of the fruit of the paths along which craving roams: first, the distinction of mind; second, the distinction of body.

The distinction of mind: as craving appears, the six consciousnesses each seize upon form and their other objects, with going toward, standing, sitting, and lying down regarding the beings and environments of the three realms. The distinction of body: as the mind differs, the body changes with it, so that, following what the mind craves, bodily form differs across the three realms and five destinies.

110. Section: The Distinction of Mind — Two Kinds Named

The distinction of mind is, further, of two kinds: first, the distinction of category; second, the distinction of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

111. Section: The Distinction of Category

The distinction of category arises through own nature, through support, through object, and through accompanying factors.

Chinese Source

品類差別者，謂由自性故、所依故、所緣故、助伴故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The distinction of category arises through own nature, through support, through object, and through accompanying factors.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Own nature is what a dharma does not share with any other: relying on the eye and discerning form is eye consciousness, and so on up to mind consciousness, relying on the mind faculty and discerning mental objects. Support is what a consciousness depends upon in order to arise: the co-existent support (for eye consciousness, the eye faculty; for mind consciousness, the mind faculty); the immediately preceding support, which is both the cessation of the previous moment of consciousness and the defiled mind, the seventh consciousness; and the seed support, each consciousness's own seeds held in the storehouse consciousness.

Object: a consciousness arises only when a faculty meets its object. Accompanying factors are the mental factors that arise together with it — the five omnipresent ones beginning with attention, the object-specific ones, and wholesome or unwholesome ones. The fifth factor, activity, is the function of the consciousnesses — for mind consciousness, seventeen kinds in all, such as discriminating, examining objects, drunkenness, madness, dreaming, waking, fainting, reviving, rousing karma, becoming free of desire, dying, and being born. When these come together, differing minds arise.

120. Section: Background — Sūtra 50, the Dog at the Post and the Many-Colored Bird, Closing "Contentment"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's fiftieth sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, the Buddha again told the monks that beings covered by ignorance and bound by the fetter of craving wander through the long night, not knowing where suffering ends — like a dog tied by a cord to a post, the knot unbroken, turning around the post and never leaving it, whether standing or lying down. So the foolish ordinary being, never free of desire, craving, thought, and thirst for form, wanders in form and turns along with it, never leaving it whether standing or lying down; and likewise for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Therefore, the Buddha said, reflect well and observe the mind: through the long night the mind has been stained by greed, hatred, and delusion; when the mind is afflicted, beings are afflicted; when the mind is pure, beings are pure. He knew of no creature as variegated as the dappled bird — yet the mind surpasses even that, since it is because the creature's mind is varied that its colors are varied. Asked whether they had seen the many-colored jiēlánnà bird, the monks said they had; just so, the Buddha said, the mind is varied, as a painter or a painter's apprentice, having well prepared a plain ground and gathered every color, paints whatever images he pleases. The foolish, not knowing form, its arising, its cessation, its relish, its danger, and the escape from it as they truly are, delight in and cling to form, and so give rise to future forms — and likewise for the other aggregates — and are not freed from birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, distress, and suffering; the learned noble disciple, knowing them as they truly are, does not cling, gives rise to no future aggregates, and is freed.

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the sūtra through the treatise's contentment and the paths craving travels: bound by craving and contented with contaminated things, beings go, stand, sit, and lie down along craving's path, turning around the post of human and divine rewards or the stake of the three lower destinies, never leaving them. The remedy the Buddha gives is to observe this very mind, stained by greed, hatred, and delusion since beginningless time: when it is afflicted, one dislikes oneself and is displeased by every being one sees; when it is pure, one is at ease and all beings appear lovable — differences of mind that in turn make bodies differ, just as the bird's many colors, its body's own reward, were painted by its many afflictions, the way a painter paints whatever he wishes. Not understanding the aggregates' defiled cause, how they cease, and their relish, danger, and escape, beings keep craving their own body and its surroundings, and that craving produces karma and so future aggregates; understanding them, one stops craving, escapes, and no future aggregates arise. With this, contentment, corresponding to sūtra 50, is complete.

First, through objects, it inclines toward the lower portion; second, it follows along subtly and is hard to detect; third, it is hard to turn back from objects; fourth, up to the peak of existence, it goes along with all the vast and various formations; fifth, with its unquiet mark, it disturbs body and mind.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Following the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, the first mark speaks of craving for objects: inclining toward the desire realm, called the lower portion — and since most beings love desire, the desire realm holds the most beings. The second speaks of craving for one's own being: born together with the body, its operation is hard to discern, hence called subtle — unawakened beings constantly protect and love this body, held by consciousness as their own being, without ever noticing it.

The rest, the Notes say, is easy to understand. The third is craving for objects that, meeting fine sights, sounds, and the like, or the quiet joy, pleasure, and equanimity of meditative absorption, cannot turn back. The fourth is craving for existence, that is, for form and the formless — holding fast to the radiant bodies of the form heavens and to the absorptions of the formless heavens up to the peak of existence; the Saṅgītiparyāya (fascicle 1) defines craving for existence as the greed of the form and formless realms, storing, guarding, and clinging. The fifth is craving's own mark: as an affliction, it leaves the mind unquiet and disturbs body and mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Fascicle 85 already set out observing defilement and purification through three matters: observing relish as relish, danger as danger, and escape as escape.

A wise householder guards property and goods properly so they are not lost, managing them well so they can serve offerings to the Three Jewels and charity over the long term. Illness, too, has causes and conditions; relying solely on repentance and recitation while refusing treatment is foolish. The Dharmapada Parable Sūtra tells how the Buddha warned a sick man who refused a doctor that there are three kinds of wrongful death — not treating illness, being careless in treatment, and not believing in cause and effect — and urged him both to treat the imbalance of the four elements and to practice the true dharma. Medicine is one of the five sciences, and the Buddha also treated the mind's three great illnesses, greed, hatred, and delusion, with the contemplation of impurity, loving-kindness, and wisdom; the wise observe and select medicine suited to the illness, and take it.

Untimely death is dying before one's lifespan is exhausted. The wise protect themselves skillfully against its causes, avoiding needless danger and harmful food, so that the body does not perish before the path is complete.

134. Section: Background — Sūtra 51, the Mountain Torrent and the Grasses on Its Banks, Closing "Practice According With the Current"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-first sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, the Buddha told the monks: a river pours out of a mountain gorge, deep and swift, its current rushing and sweeping much away; grasses and trees grow along both banks, bent down toward the water by the flood. Many who try to cross are swept off and drown; when a wave carries one of them near the bank, he grabs at the grasses and trees, but they too break off, and he is swept away again. So too the foolish ordinary being, not knowing form, its arising, its cessation, its relish, its danger, and the escape from it as they truly are, delights in and clings to form, calling form the self — and that form breaks off in turn; and likewise for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. The learned noble disciple, knowing them as they truly are, does not cling to them, and not clinging, knows for himself that he has attained parinirvāṇa: birth is exhausted, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done is done, and there will be no further existence. Hearing this, the monks rejoiced and put it into practice.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the simile: the grasses and trees on the two banks stand for beings' resultant bodies, one bank the reward of a good destiny, the other of a bad destiny; grabbing at them to climb out is clinging to the resultant body as self; the grass breaking off is that body perishing, death returning one to the great river of birth and death to drift on. Not knowing that the aggregates are gathered by craving, not knowing their flowing and reverse cessation, and not knowing that their relish carries danger, one cannot escape, and so holds the aggregates as self or what belongs to self, wishing the body to live long even as it perishes — this is going with the current. Only by not craving the aggregates does one go against the current, and the marks of such non-craving are exactly the treatise's ten marks of the wise; practicing on in this way, one knows for oneself that one will enter nirvāṇa and reach arhatship — or, for a bodhisattva of the Great Vehicle, unsurpassed awakening. With this, practice according with the current, corresponding to sūtra 51, is complete.

149. Section: Background — Sūtra 52, What Is Not Yours and the Trees of the Jeta Grove, Closing "Knowing the Mark of Cutting Off"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-second sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, the Buddha told the monks: whatever is not yours, abandon it completely; having abandoned it, you will have happiness through the long night. And what is not yours? Form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are not yours — abandon them quickly. If someone came into the Jeta Grove, cut branches from its trees, and carried them off, you would not grieve, because those trees are not self and do not belong to self; just so, abandon what is not yours. He then asked: is form permanent or impermanent? Impermanent, they answered. Is what is impermanent suffering? It is suffering. Could a learned noble disciple see in what is impermanent, suffering, and subject to change a self, something other than self, or the two containing each other? No. The same held for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Therefore all form — past, future, or present, inner or outer, coarse or fine, fine-looking or ugly, far or near — is not self, not other than self, and not mutually contained, and so with the other four. The noble disciple observes these five aggregates subject to grasping as neither self nor belonging to self; so observing, he grasps at nothing in the world, and grasping at nothing, attains nirvāṇa for himself: birth is exhausted, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done is done, and there will be no further existence. Hearing this, the monks rejoiced and put it into practice.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that what is not yours means the formations of the five aggregates, not dharmas a practitioner should seek to possess — the four great elements, like poisonous snakes, to be abandoned quickly. One should observe the aggregates as one would grass and trees, then contemplate their impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, and go on to examine form's exhaustive and shared nature, and likewise for the other aggregates in turn. Observing them as without self and without what belongs to self, right mindfulness is constantly present: when objects appear, the mind does not leak outward, does not lose right mindfulness, and does not stray from clear comprehension — the treatise's own marks of one whose view of self is cut off. Seeing the body as grass and trees, knowing the aggregates are not self and not one's own, one does not grasp at them; not grasping, one attains nirvāṇa, knows for oneself the fruit of arhatship, and no longer wanders through birth and death. With this, knowing the mark of cutting off, corresponding to sūtra 52, is complete.

Constantly cultivating insight that observes the impermanence and suffering of the five aggregates removes all craving for desire, for form, and for the formless, together with the restlessness, conceit, and ignorance that meditative concentration can bring. Of these, form-greed, formless-greed, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance are the five higher fetters, the afflictions of the form and formless realms.

Craving for desire easily brings restlessness; craving for the form-realm absorptions easily brings conceit, which usually follows relish for concentration; and relish for the formless realm comes with ignorance, for when concentration deepens into the state of neither perception nor non-perception, one easily feels there is an abiding true mind and comes to cling to a spiritual self.

155. Section: The Distinction by Exhaustion of the Fetters

There is a further distinction: namely, here the exhaustion of the lower and higher fetters is shown.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An arhat has cut off both the five lower fetters that bind beings to the desire realm — the view of a perishable self, attachment to rules and vows, doubt, and the desire realm's greed and hatred — and the five higher fetters of the form and formless realms. The distinction of fruit thus shows that the afflictions cut off at each fruit differ in depth, until at arhatship every affliction of craving and view in the three realms is cut off.

Connected only with the wisdom of hearing — understanding the words without deep reflection — it is merely drawing near. With the wisdom of reflection one enters cultivation, with strong power to suppress afflictions — cultivating. With the wisdom of cultivation, able to cut off afflictions, which requires at least the preliminary concentration (未到地定) and insight repeated again and again, it is extensively cultivating.

This is called the second distinction, in three kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

In the attention that discerns characteristics, one is still reflecting on the marks of impermanence and suffering — as in the Śrāvaka Ground's long account (fascicle 34) of fifteen perceptions of the inner body's impermanence, sixteen of outer things, three aspects of suffering, and five of impermanence — still cultivating the wisdom of hearing: this is drawing near.

The attentions of firm conviction, seclusion, gathering delight, and examination — seclusion cutting off the desire realm's higher-grade afflictions, gathering delight its middling ones — are cultivating, reaching into reflection and cultivation. The attention of culmination of application, reaching the uninterrupted path that cuts off the subtlest affliction of the desire realm, is extensively cultivating; next comes the path of liberation, the attention that is the fruit of culmination of application.

158. Section: Vehicle, Task, and Following — Support, Object, and Attention

Further, through support, object, and attention, in their proper order, one should know these are established as the vehicle, as the task, and as following accordingly.

Chinese Source

又由所依、所緣、作意，隨其次第，當知是名為乘、為事、為隨建立。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through support, object, and attention, in their proper order, one should know these are established as the vehicle, as the task, and as following accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The three trace, in order, how cultivating these perceptions builds the noble path: the sense faculties are the support, the perception of impermanence and suffering is the object, and attention in accordance with principle, unceasing, is the means — support like a vehicle, object like the task to be done, and attention, the mind and its factors that do it, following along to build the noble path.

Following the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, the Ground of the Five Consciousness-Bodies (fascicle 1) says one should regard the five consciousnesses' support as the vehicle ridden by one going elsewhere, and their object as the task for which one goes; the present passage should be read in the same way.

159. Section: Thoroughly Ripened, Well Received, and Well Aroused

Further, through long, habitual cultivation, it is called thoroughly ripened; through repeatedly and unerringly cultivating the means, it is called well received and well aroused.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Thoroughly ripened is the fruit of habitual practice built up day by day. Well received means understanding the meaning of impermanence and suffering rightly and without error; well aroused means rousing oneself to cultivate these perceptions, which serve as a basis for wholesome dharmas both worldly and world-transcending.

160. Section: The Distinction of Category — Uprooting the Latent Tendencies and Rising Higher

The distinction of category means that, when cultivating the perception of impermanence in this way, one is swiftly able to permanently uproot every latent tendency, relinquish every wholesome dharma of the lower ground, and take up every wholesome dharma of the higher ground.

Chinese Source

品類差別者，謂修如是無常想時，速能永拔一切隨眠，棄捨下地一切善法，攝受上地一切善法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The distinction of category means that, when cultivating the perception of impermanence in this way, one is swiftly able to permanently uproot every latent tendency, relinquish every wholesome dharma of the lower ground, and take up every wholesome dharma of the higher ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Cultivating the perception of impermanence without error swiftly cuts off the seeds of affliction, letting one relinquish even the desire realm's wholesome dharmas and take up and accomplish those of the form and formless realms. The corresponding sūtra 53 compares this to a farmer at summer's end and autumn's start who plows deep and pulls the weeds out by the roots before sowing: cutting weeds at the root is cutting the seeds of affliction.

So cultivating impermanence and suffering keeps desire-realm craving from growing while the higher realms' wholesome dharmas mature and keep advancing — and, as a fierce wind shaking the branches brings down every mango from the tree, these perceptions shake loose craving for desire, form, and the formless, together with restlessness, conceit, and ignorance.

161. Section: Similes Distinguishing This Perception — First, the Elephant's Footprint

Compared with all the other perceptions, such as the perception of impurity, it is highest and broadest in nature; well able to sustain, it pervades everything.

Chinese Source

於餘一切不淨想等最高廣性能善任持，遍行一切。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared with all the other perceptions, such as the perception of impurity, it is highest and broadest in nature; well able to sustain, it pervades everything.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Compared with the perception of impurity, loving-kindness, and the rest, the perceptions of impermanence and suffering are highest and broadest, for every conditioned formation of the three realms is impermanent and suffering. Form-realm beings have radiant bodies, hard to fit to the contemplation of impurity, and formless beings have no body at all; but in every realm the aggregates are impermanent and suffering — if not the suffering of suffering, then the suffering of change, and if not that, the suffering of formation.

The sūtra's simile is the elephant's footprint, the largest of all creatures' footprints. Following the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, the Compendium of Synonyms (fascicle 83) says the elephant's footprint stands for being first among the perceptions of impurity and the rest, because its object is vast — which is why this passage says it sustains well and pervades everything.

162. Section: The Pavilion Simile — The Grasped and the Grasper Alike

Just as one observes the matter that is grasped, so one observes the matter that grasps; liberated from their mark, one is able to attain the uncontaminated perception of impermanence.

Chinese Source

猶如觀察所取之事，即如是觀能取之事，彼相解脫能得無漏無常之想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as one observes the matter that is grasped, so one observes the matter that grasps; liberated from their mark, one is able to attain the uncontaminated perception of impermanence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Seeing the known object as impermanent and suffering, one turns the same observation on the very mind that grasps it, which shares that nature; once free of clinging to the object's mark, the grasping mind too can accomplish the uncontaminated perception of impermanence, and the wisdom and vision of impermanence arises.

In the sūtra, the simile is a pavilion's central beam, the firm frame on which all the other timbers rest: the perceptions of impermanence and suffering are the frame of every contemplation, and cultivating them extensively cuts off the afflictions of the three realms.

163. Section: The Flowing Simile — Every Perception Tending Toward Nirvāṇa

Whether contaminated perception or uncontaminated perception, all of these are well able to accord with, incline toward, and verge on entering nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

若有漏想、若無漏想，如是一切，皆於涅槃善能隨順、趣向、臨入。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether contaminated perception or uncontaminated perception, all of these are well able to accord with, incline toward, and verge on entering nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The sūtra's simile: every river flows into the great ocean, foremost because it receives them all; so these perceptions encompass every other. At first, the perceptions of impermanence and suffering are only mixed into wholesome and unwholesome thoughts, and through the stages conducive to liberation and conducive to penetration they are still contaminated; only from the path of seeing onward are they uncontaminated. Yet, contaminated or not, they accord with nirvāṇa (the wholesome roots conducive to liberation), incline toward it (those conducive to penetration), and verge on entering it (the path of seeing and beyond).

164. Section: The Sunrise Simile — Dispelling the Great Darkness of Ignorance

All of them are able to counteract the great darkness of ignorance, permanently cutting it off entirely; through permanently cutting it off, they are pure and bright white.

Chinese Source

皆能對治無明大闇，一切永斷；永斷彼故，清淨鮮白。

Ru-Yi Translation

All of them are able to counteract the great darkness of ignorance, permanently cutting it off entirely; through permanently cutting it off, they are pure and bright white.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The wisdom gained by constantly cultivating these perceptions is like the rising sun dispelling the darkness of the earth, counteracting the great darkness of ignorance in beings' minds. Without deeply recognizing the aggregates as suffering, without holding firmly to the right view of impermanence with the urgency of one whose head is on fire, ignorance lingers.

With that right mindfulness, one wastes not a single moment, and can permanently cut off the afflictions, reaching what is pure and bright white — pure meaning free of defilement, bright white meaning that purity continuing unbroken.

165. Section: The Wheel-Turning King Simile — Perceptions Beyond Training

Chinese Source

諸無學想，皆由一切無漏學想增上故得。

Ru-Yi Translation

All the perceptions of one beyond training are attained through the dominant power of all the uncontaminated perceptions of one in training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An arhat's fully perfected perception of impermanence and suffering is called the perception beyond training; it is brought to completion as the uncontaminated right view and uncontaminated perceptions of stream-enterers, once-returners, and non-returners, still in training, gradually exert their dominant power. Hence the arhat is likened to a wheel-turning king, highest of all kings across the four continents.

166. Section: The Distinction of Means — From Impermanence to No-Self to Nirvāṇa, Closing "Perception"

The distinction of means is this: dwelling alone in solitude, without error, repeatedly applying attention, one observes the impermanent nature of the formations. Through the perception of impermanence, one abides in the perception of no-self. Within the path of seeing, having abided in the uncontaminated perception of no-self, then on the higher path of cultivation, through the perception of one in training, one permanently destroys self-conceit, and accordingly attains nirvāṇa, both kinds complete.

Chinese Source

方便差別者，謂獨處空閑，以無顛倒數數作意，觀察諸行無常之性。由無常想，住無我想。於見道中，既住無漏無我想已，於上修道，由有學想永害我慢，隨得涅槃二種皆具。

Ru-Yi Translation

The distinction of means is this: dwelling alone in solitude, without error, repeatedly applying attention, one observes the impermanent nature of the formations. Through the perception of impermanence, one abides in the perception of no-self. Within the path of seeing, having abided in the uncontaminated perception of no-self, then on the higher path of cultivation, through the perception of one in training, one permanently destroys self-conceit, and accordingly attains nirvāṇa, both kinds complete.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Dwelling alone in solitude and applying attention repeatedly without inversion, one observes the impermanence of the inner body, outer things, and both. From impermanence one finds that form and the other formations are without self, being neither in control nor permanent, and so abides in the perception of no-self — impermanence, no-self, and nirvāṇa being of a piece. After the path of seeing, the noble one in training still has innate self-clinging, and to destroy self-conceit permanently keeps cultivating impermanence and suffering in the same way.

On "accordingly attains nirvāṇa, both kinds complete" (隨得涅槃二種皆具), the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary Notes take the two as the path of means, where one in training accords with nirvāṇa through the seven attentions, and the unobstructed (uninterrupted) path, where afflictions are actually being cut off. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes instead take them as nirvāṇa with remainder and without remainder, citing the Śrāvaka Ground (fascicle 34): an arhat dwelling in the realm of nirvāṇa with remainder has crossed the sea of birth-and-death and reached the far shore, holding only a final body; when the aggregates drawn forth by past karma and afflictions cease of themselves, with nothing further grasped and no continuation, the arhat attains parinirvāṇa in the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder.

Master Cháng Bǎi finds both readings tenable, since one who attains arhatship in any case accomplishes both kinds of nirvāṇa. With this, perception, corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-third sūtra, is complete.

167. Section: Background — Sūtra 53, Eight Similes for the Perception of Impermanence, Closing "Perception"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-third sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, the Buddha told the monks that the perception of impermanence, cultivated and much cultivated, can cut off all desire-realm craving, form-realm craving, formless-realm craving, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — and gave eight similes for it: a farmer at the end of summer and beginning of autumn plowing deep and pulling up weeds by the root; a man cutting grass who grasps it by the tips and shakes it so the withered blades fall away, keeping the long ones; mangoes on a tree that all drop when a strong wind shakes the branches; a pavilion whose firm central pillar supports and holds together all its timbers; the elephant's footprint, largest of all creatures' tracks, containing them all; the ocean, foremost of all waters, into which every river of Jambudvīpa flows; the rising sun, which dispels all the world's darkness; and the wheel-turning king, highest and most excellent among all lesser kings. How is it cultivated? A monk in a hut, on open ground, or among the trees rightly reflects that form is impermanent, and feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are impermanent; so reflecting, he cuts off all these afflictions — because the perception of impermanence establishes the perception of no-self, and the noble disciple abiding in the perception of no-self has a mind free of self-conceit and thereby attains nirvāṇa. Hearing this, the monks rejoiced and put it into practice.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the treatise's discussion of the perceptions of impermanence and suffering is itself a commentary on this sūtra. Its distinction of nature explains the sūtra's terms: wisdom born of hearing is cultivation, wisdom born of reflection is practice, and wisdom born of meditation is much practice; or, by the seven attentions, the attention of discerning the mark is cultivation, the attentions of resolve, separation, gathering delight, and examination are practice, and only from the attention of final application on is it much practice, after which one realizes the path of liberation in the fruit of final application. The sūtra's list of what is cut off is the treatise's distinction of result: desire-realm craving, the craving of the two upper realms, and restlessness, conceit, and ignorance alongside them.

In the eight similes, the master reads the weeds as afflictions pulled up by the root; the long blades as the crucial point, the perception of impermanence itself; the wind as shaking loose the suffering fruits of the three realms; the pillar as the firm core around which the pavilion of arhatship is built; the footprint as these perceptions encompassing every other contemplation, since impermanence and suffering are shared marks of everything conditioned; the ocean as their being the highest and broadest of all contemplations, pervading all formations; the sun as cutting off the three realms' afflictions of craving and view; and the wheel-turning king as their being most excellent, leading to arhatship. As for method, wherever one sits, one examines whether one's own body, feelings, thoughts, activities, and consciousness are impermanent; deep impermanence reveals no-self, for what changes with its conditions is not self-governing and cannot remain unchanged, so no permanent, single, controlling self can be found. The key, the master concludes, is the perception of no-self, reached through the perception of impermanence: cutting off the view of self and leaving conceit behind, one enters nirvāṇa and in due course attains the four fruits up to arhatship. With this, perception, corresponding to sūtra 53, is complete.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

First: though wishing to cultivate the noble path, one keeps recalling past talk, laughter, play, merriment, and being served and flattered; longing for those pleasures, one finds practice dry and bitter and grieves and sighs. The mind, matching desire rather than wholesome dharma, runs counter to the provisions of renunciation — most easily in one whose resolve to renounce is still weak. Second: because of such recollection, one finds no joy in the dharma being learned and no fit with impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, and often loses mindfulness, so that nirvāṇa and the ultimate truth cannot appear clearly in the mind.

Fourth: loving sleep and never training to cut it off, going to bed early and rising late, one is wrapped in the heaviest torpor and cannot practice the yoga of wakefulness. Fifth: keeping close to noisy company, whether laypeople or renunciants given to idle chatter and laughter, one cannot get away from crowds to reflect single-mindedly on the true dharma, nor apply oneself to calm and insight.

171. Section: The First Supporting Dharma — Free of Desire by Neither of the Two Kinds

First, regarding the two kinds of freedom from desire, one has not yet become free of desire through either one — namely, freedom from desire that keeps the manifest afflictions at a distance, through diligently cultivating wholesome qualities, and freedom from desire that permanently destroys the latent tendencies, through obtaining the right antidote.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

First, regarding the two kinds of freedom from desire, one has not yet become free of desire through either one — namely, freedom from desire that keeps the manifest afflictions at a distance, through diligently cultivating wholesome qualities, and freedom from desire that permanently destroys the latent tendencies, through obtaining the right antidote.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Following the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, freedom from desire comes by two different paths, the worldly and the world-transcending, hence two kinds; diligently cultivating wholesome qualities and obtaining the right antidote correspond, in order, to worldly and world-transcending freedom from desire.

Worldly freedom from desire is meditative concentration, which keeps afflictions from manifesting, accomplished through diligently gathering the provisions for freedom from desire and cultivating calm; world-transcending freedom from desire cuts off affliction entirely, cultivating insight within calm to eradicate its seeds. One who has achieved neither — and without the worldly kind, the world-transcending is out of reach — will often grow restless, recalling past pleasures, obstructing both calm and insight.

172. Section: Unguarded Senses, Immoderate Eating, Neglected Wakefulness, and Not Observing the Culmination of Wholesome Dharmas

Not guarding the six sense faculties, letting them chase their objects without vigilance even while enjoying them, one cannot raise mindfulness toward the ultimate dharma received, often loses mindfulness, and cannot guard the mind with mindfulness — this goes along with the second opposing dharma. Not knowing moderation in eating goes along with the third: the body must be healthy to be fit for practice, and too much or too little food makes it heavy or weak.

Failing, in the first and last watches of the night — roughly six to nine in the evening and three to six in the morning — to walk and sit in meditation and practice the yoga of wakefulness, as the Śrāvaka Ground teaches, goes along with the fourth, for the highest grade of torpor obstructs diligence. Being unable to observe the culmination of wholesome dharmas — not knowing what is wholesome or unwholesome, nor what an arhat or a buddha is — one spends precious time in idle chatter, which drifts into talk of others' faults and makes right mindfulness ever harder to raise; failing to cultivate calm and insight constantly through to completion, one goes along with the fifth.

173. Section: According With the Provisions Begins — The Contrary of What Precedes

與上相違，當知是名順資糧法，及能隨順彼隨順法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contrary to the above, one should know, is named the dharmas according with the provisions, together with the according dharmas able to accord with them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Reversing the five dharmas contrary to the provisions (違資糧法) gives the five dharmas according with the provisions (順資糧法): not restlessly recalling the pleasures of the past; keeping right mindfulness constantly present; moderation in food; diligently practicing the yoga of wakefulness; and being able to examine the ultimate of wholesome dharmas.

Reversing their five supporting causes likewise gives the five according dharmas: attaining at least one of the two kinds of freedom from desire, worldly or world-transcending; cultivating restraint of the senses; moderation in food; practicing the yoga of wakefulness; and examining the ultimate of wholesome dharmas.

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, for the śrāvakas who practice such dharmas according with the provisions, together with their causes and conditions, while in the interval they are seeking nirvāṇa, the great teacher establishes five kinds of teaching on the right path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The teacher's own establishment (明師制立) shows what the Buddha set down for his śrāvaka disciples who are practicing the provisions and the causes that support them: in the course before the noble path is accomplished, while still seeking nirvāṇa, they are given five teachings that accord with the noble path. Three belong to times of dwelling and two to times of activity, explained next.

175. Section: The Teachings for Dwelling Begin — Free of Perplexity and Doubt Through Observing What Was Heard

First, through relying on observing the dharma as heard — that all formations are impermanent, all dharmas are without self, and nirvāṇa is quiescent, pervading everything — one attains, for the time being through worldly attention, freedom from perplexity and freedom from doubt.

Chinese Source

一者、由依觀察如所聞法，遍於一切諸行無常、諸法無我、涅槃寂靜，且以世間作意而得無惑無疑。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, through relying on observing the dharma as heard — that all formations are impermanent, all dharmas are without self, and nirvāṇa is quiescent, pervading everything — one attains, for the time being through worldly attention, freedom from perplexity and freedom from doubt.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner moves from wisdom born of hearing to wisdom born of reflection, and only then comes to accord with wisdom born of cultivation. At the outset one examines whether the dharma one has heard accords throughout with the three seals: all formations impermanent, all dharmas without self, nirvāṇa quiescent. Reflecting with worldly attention, still working through names and words, is contaminated attention; yet to be free of perplexity and doubt, seeing the matter clearly and no longer half believing, one must at least accomplish worldly right view.

This belongs to the early, shallower stage: when one sits to cultivate calm and insight, one first observes the dharma one has heard, and the content of that reflection is chiefly the three seals.

176. Section: Not Clinging to Wrong Discursive Thought About Three Matters

Second, precisely while dwelling, one does not cling to wrong discursive thought about three matters. What are the three matters? First, the many requisites for sustaining life; second, the marks of harming others; third, either another's reviling and abuse, or any one disagreeable occurrence that manifests, a dharma out of accord with fellow practitioners of the holy life.

Chinese Source

二者、即於住時，不著三事不正尋思。何等三事？一者、資命眾具；二者、他損害相；三者、或他毀罵，或隨有一非愛現行同梵行者不同分法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, precisely while dwelling, one does not cling to wrong discursive thought about three matters. What are the three matters? First, the many requisites for sustaining life; second, the marks of harming others; third, either another's reviling and abuse, or any one disagreeable occurrence that manifests, a dharma out of accord with fellow practitioners of the holy life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

While dwelling, for instance sitting quietly in one place, one does not cling to wrong discursive thought arising from three matters. The first is the requisites that sustain life, which one does not chase after. The second is thoughts of desire, ill will, and harm that would injure others. The third is being reproached by others: if the fault is one's own, one corrects it; if not, one practices patience and does not keep recalling the harm, since the past cannot be grasped.

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) explain the disagreeable occurrence as any one transgression against the six dharmas that are delightful, lovable, worthy of respect, and free of dispute, that is, the six harmonies (六和敬): harmony in precepts, views, intention, dwelling, speech, and shared gain. Holding a different view, wanting more than others of a donor's offerings, staying away from the community's gatherings, or wishing to leave the community are all examples. Where the community keeps all six, it lives together in harmony; the six are lovable because they help one cultivate the noble path, worthy of respect because such a community follows the teaching, and free of dispute. Going against any one of them is what the treatise calls disagreeable.

Afflictions lie in one's own mind, not outside; without right view, one's own false discrimination produces this wrong discursive thought wherever one goes, and only by applying wise attention again and again can it be cut off.

177. Section: Right View Born From Hearing and Reflection

Third, with instruction leading the way, through relying on another's voice and wise attention, one is able to give rise to right view and able to cut off wrong view.

Chinese Source

三者、教授為先，由依他音、如理作意，能生正見，能斷邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, with instruction leading the way, through relying on another's voice and wise attention, one is able to give rise to right view and able to cut off wrong view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Relying first on a good friend's instruction and hearing the wholesome dharma explained, one applies wise attention, gives rise to right view, and through right view cuts off wrong view. While seeking nirvāṇa one draws near good friends and gives time to reading the sūtras and treatises; after hearing, one keeps reflecting wisely, coming to recognize form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, and the truth of the emptiness of self and of dharmas, as the Bodhisattva Ground (fascicle 36) teaches that there is consciousness only and no external object.

Observing in this way again and again, one knows what to do when sitting in meditation and is not weighed down by having nothing to work on; through calm and insight one then becomes aware of principles never noticed before.

178. Section: The Three Teachings for Dwelling Summed Up

One should know these three are named the teachings on the right path for times of dwelling.

Chinese Source

當知此三是名住時正道言教。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know these three are named the teachings on the right path for times of dwelling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Dwelling means cultivating calm and insight, or simply abiding in the monastery; whether sitting, walking, or lying down, these three kinds of right reflection apply. First, regarding the truth one observes, one begins with worldly attention so that one's thinking accords with the three seals; though still unable to reflect apart from words, one must be free of perplexity and doubt about the written teaching, clearing up even the slightest unclarity, since half belief leaves one without deep faith in the dharma.

Second, one does not cling to wrong discursive thought arising from desire, ill will, and harm, and when it appears, one notices it and does not keep turning along with the affliction. Third, one gives rise to right view through hearing and reflection, that is, one cultivates the four limbs of stream-entry (四預流支), applies wise attention, builds up one's own right view, and cuts off wrong view. These are the teachings on the right path for times of dwelling (住時攝).

179. Section: The Teachings for Activity Begin — Two Named

Chinese Source

復有二種於彼行時正道言教。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are two kinds of teaching on the right path for times of activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The teachings for times of activity (行時攝) are the Buddha's instructions on the noble path for the course of daily activity, as distinct from the three for times of dwelling just concluded.

180. Section: The First Teaching for Activity — No Anger at Correction, No Vexation Over Requisites

That is, when wise fellow practitioners of the holy life explain to one what is fitting and what is not fitting, one does not give rise to anger; further, on account of coarse and poor requisites for sustaining life, whether obtained or not obtained, and on account of whatever hardships come through the precepts and the like, the mind is not vexed. This is named the first.

Chinese Source

謂諸有智同梵行者，為彼宣說處非處時，不生忿怒；又由麤弊資命眾具，若得不得，及由戒等所有災害，心不熱惱；是名第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, when wise fellow practitioners of the holy life explain to one what is fitting and what is not fitting, one does not give rise to anger; further, on account of coarse and poor requisites for sustaining life, whether obtained or not obtained, and on account of whatever hardships come through the precepts and the like, the mind is not vexed. This is named the first.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When wise fellow practitioners come to advise one who holds dharmas contrary to the provisions, pointing out what is reasonable and what is not, what is contrary to the provisions and what accords with them, the one advised should not become angry. Among the provisions for the two paths, one should be moderate in food, diligent in the yoga of wakefulness, guard the gates of the senses, examine the ultimate of wholesome dharmas, and cultivate worldly and world-transcending freedom from desire; when reminded of these, one does not turn hatred toward the one reminding.

Nor is the mind vexed whether or not one obtains coarse requisites, or when the precepts require what one must do even unwillingly. In short, one gives rise to no hatred and cultivates the six harmonies. In the Buddha's time, Dravya Mallaputra was in charge of assigning quarters and bedding, and the monk Maitrabhūmika, junior in seniority, repeatedly received the poorest; growing resentful, he believed Dravya was partial and had his sister groundlessly accuse Dravya of breaking the precepts. Yet offerings are limited, and those last in line commonly receive what is poorer; such a moment is a chance to train right mindfulness, to feel shame at one's own lack of virtue, to rejoice in the elders' virtue, or to see it all as mere dependent arising, rather than clinging to the objects of the five desires and delaying the noble path, still less doing evil over it.

181. Section: The Second Teaching for Activity — No Dejection at Another's Gain and Honor

Chinese Source

於得所勝利養恭敬，心不悒然，是名第二。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding gain and honor in which one is surpassed, the mind is not dejected. This is named the second.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When others receive more excellent gain and honor, when they receive more than oneself, or when a donor makes offerings to them and not to oneself, the mind is not downcast or unhappy. One does not envy another's gain; the mind is unmoved, since each has his own causes and conditions.

182. Section: The Teacher's Establishment Concluded, Closing "Establishing What Runs Counter to the Provisions"

Chinese Source

彼由如是住時、行時，能正修行涅槃妙道，由此不久當得涅槃，終無毀失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through such dwelling and such activity, they are able rightly to practice the wondrous path to nirvāṇa; through this, before long they will attain nirvāṇa, never ultimately failing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

In times of dwelling, the practitioner cultivates the three: freedom from perplexity and doubt through observing as heard, not clinging to wrong discursive thought about three matters, and right view born of hearing and reflection; in times of activity, the practitioner gives rise to no anger and is not dejected at others' gain. In this way one rightly practices the wondrous noble path to nirvāṇa, through these causes and conditions will before long accomplish nirvāṇa, and will never ultimately fall into ruin.

This passage, already met once in the Compendium of Synonyms (fascicle 83), is read here again. With this, establishing what runs counter to the provisions (立違糧), corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-fourth sūtra, is complete.

183. Section: Background — Sūtra 54, the Monk Tiṣya and the Two Travelers, Closing "Establishing What Runs Counter to the Provisions"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-fourth sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, a monk named Tiṣya told the monks gathered in the dining hall that he could not discern the dharma, took no delight in the holy life, was fond of sleep, and had doubts about the dharma. One monk reported this to the Buddha, who said: Tiṣya is a foolish man who does not guard his sense doors, does not know moderation in food, is not awake and alert in the first and last watches of the night, is lazy and not diligent, and does not examine wholesome dharmas — for such a one to discern the dharma, delight in the holy life, be free of sleep, and be free of doubt is impossible; for one who guards the senses, knows moderation in food, keeps vigil, and examines wholesome dharmas, it is possible. Summoned, Tiṣya admitted it. The Buddha asked him: if one is not free of greed, desire, craving, thought, and thirst for form, will sorrow, lamentation, distress, and suffering arise when that form changes? Yes, Tiṣya said; and the same for the other aggregates. And if one is free of them? No, he said. The Buddha praised him and gave a simile: of two travelers, one knows the road and one does not; the knower tells the other that ahead the road forks — leave the left and take the right; then come ravines and streams, and a thicket, and each time he should leave the left and take the right, going on step by step until he reaches the city. The one who does not know the road is the foolish ordinary being; the one who knows it is the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly awakened; the fork is beings' doubt; the left road is the three unwholesome thoughts of greed, hatred, and harm, the right road the three wholesome thoughts of renunciation, non-hatred, and non-harm; further along on the left lie wrong view, wrong intention, and the rest of the eight wrong factors, on the right right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration; the ravines and streams are hatred, concealment, and sorrow; the thicket is the five strands of sense pleasure; and the city is parinirvāṇa. The Buddha, as teacher, has done all a teacher should for his disciples, out of compassion; now, he said, you must do your own part — at the foot of a tree, on open ground, or in a mountain cave, spread grass for a seat, reflect with right mindfulness, practice non-laxity, and do not leave yourselves to regret later. Hearing this, Tiṣya rejoiced and put it into practice.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that Tiṣya was candid in confessing what the treatise calls the dharmas contrary to the provisions, and that the Buddha's diagnosis — unguarded senses, immoderate eating, no vigil, laziness, failing to examine wholesome dharmas — describes the same; discerning wholesome from unwholesome, delighting in the holy life, giving up sleep, and resolving doubt are the dharmas in accord with the provisions, the mark of a true monk. When Tiṣya, free of craving, would feel no sorrow at the aggregates' change, it is because he sees them as without self, empty, and ungraspable — through contemplating no-self or the signless, the mind does not cling when the body perishes and does not rush to take up another. The master then maps the simile onto the treatise's five right-path instructions: the Buddha as the one who knows the road, with beings using worldly attention to resolve their doubt, is the first; leaving the left road of greed, hatred, and harm for the right road of the three wholesome thoughts is the second; the eight wrong factors set against the eightfold path is the third; the ravines and streams — anger when guided, concealing one's faults, sorrow — are the fourth; and the thicket of the five strands of sense pleasure — distress at others' gain and honor, or at one's own seeming too little — is the fifth. With this, establishing what runs counter to the provisions, corresponding to sūtra 54, is complete.

184. Section: The Teacher's Own Task Begins — Five Tasks Named

Further, the great teacher, toward the śrāvakas, has in brief five kinds of tasks a teacher performs. First, rightly subduing; second, rightly embracing; third, rightly rebuking; fourth, rightly teaching defilement; fifth, rightly teaching purity.

Chinese Source

復次，大師於諸聲聞，略有五種師所作事。一者、正折伏，二者、正攝受，三者、正訶責，四者、正說雜染，五者、正說清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the great teacher, toward the śrāvakas, has in brief five kinds of tasks a teacher performs. First, rightly subduing; second, rightly embracing; third, rightly rebuking; fourth, rightly teaching defilement; fifth, rightly teaching purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The teacher's own task (師所作) explains swift penetration's tenth topic. These are the skillful means the Buddha uses in teaching the śrāvaka assembly. Rightly subduing (正折伏): when a disciple commits a grave offense or holds wrong views, a formal act of the community, carried out according to the dharma, subdues the offender so that he repents and reforms; if he will not, he is expelled. Rightly embracing (正攝受): the Buddha embraces disciples with the dharma, so there is dharma to hear and practice, and with material support, supplying robes, food, bedding, and medicine according to the dharma, attending to common understanding and fair sharing so that body and mind can dwell at ease.

Rightly rebuking (正訶責): when a disciple errs, the teacher rebukes him skillfully and correctly, so that he knows his fault and sets his mind right. Rightly teaching defilement (正說雜染): showing the causes of worldly defilement, the truths of suffering and its origin. Rightly teaching purity (正說清淨): showing the truths of cessation and the path, the method and sequence of practice, and so the attainment of quiescent, pure nirvāṇa. The first three are matters of admonition; the last two are instruction in concentration and wisdom.

185. Section: Specific Kinds Elaborated — Rightly Subduing Through Ruling Again on Disputes

Further, through two causes and conditions, regarding śrāvakas who have transgressed in matters of dispute, the matter they dispute is reopened and ruled upon again. First, because the disturbance has grown and spread; second, in order to accord with the discipline.

Chinese Source

復次，由二因緣，於諸諍事違越聲聞，覆相記別彼所諍事。一、擾亂增廣故；二、與律相應故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through two causes and conditions, regarding śrāvakas who have transgressed in matters of dispute, the matter they dispute is reopened and ruled upon again. First, because the disturbance has grown and spread; second, in order to accord with the discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) explain that disputes among śrāvakas are, in brief, of four kinds, as the matters of discipline in fascicle 99 describe: disputes from another's accusation, disputes from mutual suspicion, disputes from one's own accusation, and disputes from mutual accusation. Since such disputes involve transgressing the points of training, the treatise speaks of śrāvakas who have transgressed. Once a dispute has been settled, the offense should not be raised again; here, however, for two reasons it is raised again, and this is called reopening and ruling again on the matter disputed.

The two reasons are that the dispute has not ceased and is already disturbing the assembly's practice, and that it must be handled through a formal act of the community according to the discipline. Master Cháng Bǎi gives the Vinaya's ways of settling each kind: a dispute from another's accusation is settled in the presence of the accused (現前毘尼); one from mutual suspicion is settled through recollection (憶念毘尼), or, where the act was done in a state of insanity, through a verdict of past insanity (不癡毘尼); one from one's own accusation is settled through one's own confession (自言治); and long-running mutual accusations between two communities are settled by covering over with grass (如草覆地), each side sending an elder to represent it, so that the dispute ends like mud covered with grass. The treatise raises the offense again here because it is explaining the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-fifth sūtra, in which the Buddha, after dealing with a dispute in the community, out of compassion embraces the monks once more.

186. Section: Rightly Subduing Through Expulsion — Seven Causes

First, because they are seen to engage in wrong conduct in every respect; second, because they are seen to do so for the most part; third, on account of the wrong ways of their own assembly heads — elders, ācāryas, and upādhyāyas; fourth, because they are unfit to dwell together with others; fifth, because they have already been expelled; sixth, in order to avoid present faults; seventh, in order that future faults not arise.

Chinese Source

復次，由七因緣，大師驅擯諸聲聞眾。一者、見一切種皆行邪行故；二者、見彼多分故；三者、由彼眾首上座阿遮利耶、鄔波柁耶方便故；四者、不堪共住故；五者、被驅擯故；六者、避現前過故；七者、令不生起未來過故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through seven causes and conditions, the great teacher expels members of the śrāvaka assembly. First, because they are seen to engage in wrong conduct in every respect; second, because they are seen to do so for the most part; third, on account of the wrong ways of their own assembly heads — elders, ācāryas, and upādhyāyas; fourth, because they are unfit to dwell together with others; fifth, because they have already been expelled; sixth, in order to avoid present faults; seventh, in order that future faults not arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

First, monks who commit serious wrong conduct, such as the gravest class of offense, are expelled from the community. Second, a monk whose conduct for the most part fails to keep the precepts and the community's rules, and who repeatedly offends without repenting, may be expelled by a formal act to protect the community. Third, if the ācārya responsible for admonition or the elder upādhyāya responsible for instruction holds wrong conduct or wrong views and cannot admonish and instruct disciples according to the dharma and discipline, the Buddha, though they are senior, has them expelled by a formal act so that the community is not led astray.

Fourth, a monk or nun who commits grave offenses gains nothing from practicing together, while fellow practitioners are harmed and the community suffers, so they cannot dwell together. Fifth, one already expelled by one community may not be accepted by another. Sixth, failing to expel would bring many present faults. Seventh, expulsion is of two kinds, permanent expulsion and disciplinary expulsion, and both aim to keep the offender from repeating the fault: one who will not accept admonition is expelled permanently, while one who genuinely repents may remain in the community. In rightly subduing, the Buddha usually displays majesty and power.

187. Section: Rightly Embracing Begins — Ten Causes for Entering Villages for Almsfood, the First Four

Second, in order to lead a portion of them to go for almsfood. Third, in order to embrace a portion of them by sharing their activity. Fourth, in order to be a great illumination for beings of the future, up to causing them to give rise, for a moment, to touching and realizing.

Chinese Source

復次，由十因緣，如來入於聚落乞食。一者、當顯杜多功德故。二者、為欲引彼一分令入乞食故。三者、為欲以同事行攝彼一分故。四者、為與未來眾生作大照明故，乃至令彼暫起觸證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through ten causes and conditions, the Tathāgata enters villages for almsfood. First, in order to display the merits of the dhūta practices. Second, in order to lead a portion of them to go for almsfood. Third, in order to embrace a portion of them by sharing their activity. Fourth, in order to be a great illumination for beings of the future, up to causing them to give rise, for a moment, to touching and realizing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

Rightly embracing (正攝受) unfolds in seven parts, all ways the Buddha embraces beings. Dhūta means shaking off: rousing one's spirit to practice, refining away the afflictions within, and eliminating bad habits regarding robes, food, and bedding. The Four-Part Vinaya counts twelve dhūta practices: four concerning food, two concerning robes, and six concerning dwelling.

Some śrāvaka disciples did not like going for alms, though almsround spares one many affairs for practice and helps one leave conceit behind; seeing the Buddha himself go, they too would go rather than slack off, so he embraces them by sharing their activity. The fourth cause brings great light to beings, dispelling the darkness of ignorance, so that later beings will not disparage renunciants for begging. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) explain causing them to touch and realize as letting beings of the present directly see the Tathāgata entering the village for alms; Master Cháng Bǎi adds that some beings' wholesome roots ripen only on meeting the Buddha, whereupon they can touch and realize the true reality of dharmas.

188. Section: Entering Villages for Almsfood — the Remaining Six Causes

Fifth, in order to draw in outsiders of coarse and base conviction. Sixth, for those who, going by what they have heard, raise slander, he therefore displays his wondrous form and quiescent deportment, causing them to marvel and their minds to turn toward him for refuge. Seventh, so that beings of the middle category may, with little effort, plant much merit. Ninth, so that the many calamities of beings who are blind, deaf, deranged, or confused in mind may all be stilled. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The heavenly abiding (天住) means the four meditative absorptions, which the Buddha often enters. By his own example he leads disciples who like mixing with laypeople and monastics in idle talk toward seclusion and concentration; to those who already delight in calm and insight he shows that he delights in the same; in concentration his present aggregates abide at ease, joined with joy, pleasure, or equanimity. Entering the same state as the gods, he can meet with them and teach them; with the buddha-eye he surveys beings throughout the great trichiliocosm and goes to liberate those whose roots are about to ripen.

Seeing him in concentration, his disciples long for him and know that his concentration surpasses theirs. Some great śrāvakas, constantly cultivating calm and insight, penetrate the true reality of dharmas on hearing only a brief teaching, and the Buddha shows that this is possible because of such cultivation. Finally, he urges those attached to broad discussion without practice to give it up: the dharma in words is supreme compared with worldly things, yet compared with reality beyond words it is still proliferation. Hearing and reflection must serve as the basis, and with right view as support one must sit and cultivate repeatedly to penetrate reality.

Embracing through the dharma means teaching disciples the dharma. Embracing through material goods means the teacher provides robes, food, bedding, and medicine. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Those newly gone forth are of five kinds. Going forth late means going forth in old age. Going forth in childhood means the crow-chasing novices, aged seven or eight to thirteen. Going forth in youth means those aged fourteen to nineteen, novices fit for the dharma, able both to serve their teachers and to recite sūtras and sit in meditation. The novice (勞策) has just shaved the head and entered the order, diligently cultivating precepts, concentration, and wisdom and training the mind under the bhikṣus. Not yet accustomed to the renunciant life, such newcomers come to regret what they have done in three situations: having transgressed a point of training, doing what should not be done or failing to do what should, and then reflecting on it; having vowed to receive the Buddha's dharma and discipline, being sincere enough to notice even a small fault at once and repent of it deeply; and having just left household life with a mind set on renunciation, so that remorse arises when they err.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The very highest seat shows how honored the dharma is, and the Buddha sits upon it with solemn dignity. On the second mark, Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the Buddha does not teach disciples who fail to abide in a deportment fit for hearing the dharma: as the Vinaya says, out of respect for the dharma, one standing does not teach one who is seated, one on a lower seat does not teach one on a higher seat, and one does not teach those whose heads are covered, whose hands are on their hips, or whose arms are folded. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Rightly rebuking (正訶責) is the third of the teacher's tasks. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Rightly teaching defilement (正說雜染) sets out wrong conduct and its causes. The fault in manifest activity: greed, hatred, and delusion are actively arising, the mind has no shame or conscience, and one cannot give up evil conduct. The fault of intention: an intention that is defiled. The fault of application: either never arousing vigor, or growing lax and lazy in the midst of effort. The fault of wisdom: not diligently pursuing the three kinds of wisdom, from hearing, reflection, and cultivation, losing right mindfulness, and mostly abiding in delusion. The fault of discursive thought: the mind wandering in unwise thought, giving rise to the eight kinds of discursive thought, such as desire, ill will, and harm. The fault of basis: because little was cultivated in past lives, only feeble and few wholesome roots are accomplished in this one.

This fault chiefly stresses evil conduct produced by the active arising of greed, hatred, and delusion.

Not yet having gone deeply into the wisdom born of hearing or of reflection, one often loses right mindfulness and mostly abides in delusion; having heard the dharma without reflecting on it carefully, one's wisdom does not grow. Without vigor, calm and insight are not accomplished, the mind is neither quiet nor concentrated, and without wisdom one cannot observe matters clearly or tell right conduct from wrong, and so is easily led astray and easily errs.

When such thoughts arise much of the time, the mind is not on the right path, does not accord with the dharma and discipline, and becomes scattered and confused. Constantly thinking of family affection and worldly matters is a great fault for practice, a defiled fault of discursive thought, and a mind so confused easily commits evil conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Because such practitioners planted few wholesome roots before, resting on past lives in which the wisdom of hearing, reflection, and cultivation was not cultivated and gathered as a cause, four marks appear in this life: faith in the dharma that is slight and narrow; precepts that are weak by nature, without diligent keeping; mindfulness never trained before, so that one is absent-minded and forgetful; and an innate wisdom, the native capacity of the sixth consciousness, that is not keen.

In short, because provisions were not gathered in the past, the five wholesome roots of faith, vigor, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom accomplished in this life are all slight. With shallow roots, one easily returns to lay life, breaks precepts, or loses mindfulness, and wrong conduct arises. With this, the six marks that together encompass every fault of a practitioner are complete.

Second, one who delights in finding fault, seeking the Buddhist community's flaws and nursing grievances, is a thief within the true dharma, without real faith. These first two marks concern householders; the next two concern renunciants.

Third, one goes forth merely to sustain life, since renunciants easily obtain food and clothing; going forth with such a motive is to fill a place in the saṅgha under false pretenses and receive the offerings of the faithful without merit. Fourth, one goes forth out of fear of a king, of thieves, or of creditors; in ancient India a king could not punish a renunciant, so some who had committed crimes, were threatened by thieves, or would not repay debts went forth to escape. Because the motive is wrong, even with shaved head and dyed robe, wrong conduct easily arises.

204. Section: The Harm of Such Wrong Conduct — Held Back in Two Matters

One who engages in such wrong conduct is then held back in two matters: first, held back by ruining one's own aim as a householder; second, held back by ruining one's own aim as a renunciant.

Chinese Source

若行如是諸邪行者，便於二事有所稽留。一者、失壞在家自義稽留；二者、失壞出家自義稽留。

Ru-Yi Translation

One who engages in such wrong conduct is then held back in two matters: first, held back by ruining one's own aim as a householder; second, held back by ruining one's own aim as a renunciant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) explain that the first two marks ruin one's own aim as a householder and the last two ruin one's own aim as a renunciant. What is lovable and blameless is called one's aim (義); since the restraint of precepts can bring one's own aim about, the treatise distinguishes householder and renunciant. Master Cháng Bǎi points to the Compendium of Synonyms (fascicle 83), which calls it an aim because it is what is sought and brings benefit, being lovable and without blame; relying on the restraint of precepts to cultivate concentration and wisdom brings this lovable, blameless benefit to oneself.

A householder could have supported the Three Jewels, become a person of right faith, even practiced and realized the fruits; with an impure intention and a mind seeking faults in the noble teaching, he gains none of a lay Buddhist's benefit and stays where he is. A renunciant who practices single-mindedly could cut off the afflictions of birth and death; going forth merely to sustain life or to escape kings, thieves, or creditors, his resolve for the path is weak, he does not diligently apply himself, and he loses the renunciant's special benefit of arhatship, held back on the noble path and spending his life to no purpose.

205. Section: The Causes of Wrong Conduct Begin — Two Named

Further, such wrong conduct has two causes and conditions: wrong discursive thought regarding three matters, and the wrong perceptions that precede it.

Chinese Source

復次，如是邪行有二因緣。謂於三事不正尋思，及彼前行諸不正想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, such wrong conduct has two causes and conditions: wrong discursive thought regarding three matters, and the wrong perceptions that precede it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wrong conduct of the six or four marks just described arises from two causes: wrong discursive thought regarding three matters, and, before that thought, the grasping of signs and secondary features that gives rise to thoughts of desire, ill will, and harm. In short, wrong perception comes first and wrong discursive thought follows, and this is the cause of evil conduct.

206. Section: The Three Matters Pointed Back To

The three matters should be known as before.

Chinese Source

其三事者，如前應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The three matters should be known as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) point back to the earlier passage on not clinging to wrong discursive thought about three matters: first, the many requisites for sustaining life; second, the marks of harming others; third, either another's reviling and abuse, or any one disagreeable occurrence that manifests, a dharma out of accord with fellow practitioners of the holy life. They should be known as explained there.

207. Section: How Wrong Thought Arises From Grasping Signs

Regarding these, one gives rise to wrong perceptions, grasping at their signs and secondary features; from then on, regarding the matters that follow from them, one much pursues discursive thought and much pursues close examination.

Chinese Source

於彼發起諸不正想，隨取相好，自斯已後，於其隨法多隨尋思、多隨伺察。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding these, one gives rise to wrong perceptions, grasping at their signs and secondary features; from then on, regarding the matters that follow from them, one much pursues discursive thought and much pursues close examination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When the six faculties meet the six objects, one first grasps an object's sign (取相), its general mark, and then its secondary features (取隨好), the particular details that make it attractive or hateful; following upon these come many further matters, stirring up more and more discursive thought and examination, and the eight wrong kinds of thought, such as desire, ill will, and harm, follow. Anger at a person works the same way: recalling only the face or name is grasping the sign, then thoughts of that person's unpleasant words and acts multiply until anger blazes.

Applied to the three matters: grasping the signs and features of forms, sounds, smells, tastes, touches, food, robes, bedding, and medicine with greed gives rise to wrong thought about the requisites; a mind bent on harming others, joined with hatred, gives rise to wrong thought about harm; and wishing to retaliate against another's abuse, or being irritated by a fellow practitioner's disagreeable conduct, gives rise to wrong thought about the third matter.

208. Section: Rightly Teaching Purity Begins — Two Antidotes, the First Through the Foundations of Mindfulness

Further, in order to cut off such causes and conditions of wrong conduct, one should know there are likewise two kinds of antidote. First, in order to cut off wrong discursive thought, one settles the mind well in the foundations of mindfulness through the two practices, unerring and repeated.

Chinese Source

復次，為斷如是邪行因緣，當知亦有二種對治。一者、為斷不正尋思，以無顛倒數數二行，於諸念住善住其心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, in order to cut off such causes and conditions of wrong conduct, one should know there are likewise two kinds of antidote. First, in order to cut off wrong discursive thought, one settles the mind well in the foundations of mindfulness through the two practices, unerring and repeated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

Rightly teaching purity (正說清淨) gives the antidotes to the two causes of wrong conduct, wrong discursive thought and wrong perception. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) explain the two practices as calm and insight. To cut off wrong discursive thought, one cultivates calm and insight without error and again and again; cultivating calm and insight here means cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness, body, feeling, mind, and dharmas, settling the mind well on the dharma and turning it toward the path, so that wrong discursive thoughts no longer multiply.

209. Section: The Second Antidote — the Signless Mind-Concentration

Second, in order to cut off wrong perceptions, one cultivates the signless mind-concentration.

Chinese Source

二者、為斷諸不正想，修習無相心三摩地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, in order to cut off wrong perceptions, one cultivates the signless mind-concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Ground of Concentration (fascicle 12) defines the signless mind-concentration as reflecting on the cessation of the aggregates subject to grasping as quiescent, with the mind abiding on a single object. Here one reflects on the unconditioned, quiescent mark of the aggregates' cessation, leaving behind false grasping at signs, and the mind rests on a signless object, seeing form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, as empty and without sign.

This concentration also cuts off wrong discursive thought: perception is the grasping of signs, and where signs and secondary features are not grasped, wrong discursive thought and confused thinking do not arise.

210. Section: Examining the Marks of Cultivation Begins — Only Keen Desire Succeeds

This cultivation of the antidotes is accomplished only through keen desire within the cultivation of those antidotes — not by those whose desire is not keen.

Chinese Source

此修對治，要由於彼修對治中猛利樂欲，方得成辦，非彼樂欲不猛利者。

Ru-Yi Translation

This cultivation of the antidotes is accomplished only through keen desire within the cultivation of those antidotes — not by those whose desire is not keen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whether cultivating the signless mind-concentration against wrong perception, or calm, insight, and the four foundations of mindfulness against wrong discursive thought, success requires keen vigor and a powerful wish. One must be willing to cultivate the foundations of mindfulness and the signless concentration again and again against one's own afflictions; one who drifts along idly, seeing no harm in discursive thought, reasoning that no one else knows what one is thinking, has too weak a motive to cut it off, cannot arouse vigor, and so remains defiled.

211. Section: Keen Desire Elaborated — Two Conditions Named

This keen desire arises from two conditions: because these antidotes have a great fruit, and because they are not shared with any outsider.

Chinese Source

此猛利欲由二緣生，謂此對治有大果故，不共一切諸外道故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This keen desire arises from two conditions: because these antidotes have a great fruit, and because they are not shared with any outsider.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Why does a practitioner have a strong wholesome desire to cultivate calm and insight, the four foundations of mindfulness, and the signless concentration? First, one knows these two antidotes yield a great and special fruit, accomplishing nirvāṇa and cutting off the round of birth and death. Second, outsiders do not know how to cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness or the signless concentration. In its contaminated sense, the attainment reached through a signless concentration also extends to outsiders; the treatise here speaks of uncontaminated concentration.

212. Section: The Two Conditions Explained — Having a Great Fruit

Having a great fruit means that, while cultivating them, one is able to realize the signless mind-concentration and to abide in the two realms that are the wondrous gate of ambrosia — namely, the realm of cutting-off and the realm free of desire, toward nirvāṇa with residue and without residue. One who abides in these is near the two nirvāṇas, though not all are attained at this very time.

Chinese Source

有大果者，謂修習時，便能剋證無相心定，及住二界妙甘露門。所謂斷界及無欲界，若有餘依及無餘依。安住此者近二涅槃，未於今時一切皆得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having a great fruit means that, while cultivating them, one is able to realize the signless mind-concentration and to abide in the two realms that are the wondrous gate of ambrosia — namely, the realm of cutting-off and the realm free of desire, toward nirvāṇa with residue and without residue. One who abides in these is near the two nirvāṇas, though not all are attained at this very time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (披尋記) refer this to fascicle 85: when the formations to be cut off on the path of seeing are cut off, this is named the realm of cutting-off; when the formations to be cut off on the path of cultivation are cut off, this is named the realm free of desire; while only the remaining support is present, this is named the realm of nirvāṇa with residue; when that support ceases, this is named the realm of cessation, also called the realm of nirvāṇa without residue. Since nirvāṇa with residue and without residue both fall within the realm free of desire, the two realms are established as the wondrous gate of ambrosia. The Compendium of Synonyms (fascicle 83) says nirvāṇa is ambrosia because birth, aging, sickness, and death are all permanently exhausted; relying on the realm of cutting-off and the realm free of desire, one can realize this fruit, and so they are called its gate.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that cutting off the distinctly-arisen afflictions of craving and views, one hundred twelve in all, on the path of seeing and realizing the first fruit is the realm of cutting-off; on the path of cultivation one cuts off the innately-arisen afflictions. Following fascicle 85, he takes the realm free of desire as the non-returner's cutting off of the desire realm's afflictions, and reads the arhat, with every affliction of the three realms cut off and only the fruit of past contaminated karma remaining, as nirvāṇa with residue, and the arhat's passing at the end of life, body and mind no longer found, as the realm of cessation, nirvāṇa without residue. From the first fruit to the fourth, one cultivates the foundations of mindfulness and the signless concentration in this way; abiding in the two realms is already near the two nirvāṇas, yet they are not reached quickly, only through long and diligent practice.

213. Section: Not Shared With Others Begins — the Signless Concentration Found Only Within

Not shared means that the signless concentration exists only within the inner dharma; outsiders do not have it.

Chinese Source

言不共者，謂無相定唯內法有，諸外道無。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not shared means that the signless concentration exists only within the inner dharma; outsiders do not have it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The signless mind-concentration exists only within the Buddha's teaching, also called the inner dharma, and outsiders do not know how to cultivate it. Taken as contaminated concentration, the attainment reached through a signless concentration also extends to outsiders; the treatise here speaks of uncontaminated concentration.

214. Section: The Question — Why Outsiders Lack It

Why is this?

Chinese Source

何以故？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise asks why the signless concentration is not shared, and answers in two parts: why outsiders do not have it, and why it is found only within the inner dharma.

215. Section: The Explanation Begins — Why Outsiders Do Not Have It

Because those outsiders, if something is found, then superimpose upon it an observation not according with its measure; if nothing is found, they then falsely discriminate. Through the view of self, deluded about the formations, they give rise to clinging to self either solely regarding the body, or solely regarding the nonmaterial aggregates, or regarding both together; because they cling to self, they think, "I shall cease to be," and so their minds take no delight in nirvāṇa. They are not yet able even to enter it — how much less to abide in it; they only grow more alarmed and afraid, and their minds retreat.

Chinese Source

由彼外道，若有所得，即便增益不如量觀；若無所得，即妄分別。由我見故，愚於諸行，或唯於身、或唯無色、或總於二生我執著；以執我故，謂我當無，便於涅槃心不欣樂。尚未能入，況乎安住，唯增驚怖，其心退還。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because those outsiders, if something is found, then superimpose upon it an observation not according with its measure; if nothing is found, they then falsely discriminate. Through the view of self, deluded about the formations, they give rise to clinging to self either solely regarding the body, or solely regarding the nonmaterial aggregates, or regarding both together; because they cling to self, they think, "I shall cease to be," and so their minds take no delight in nirvāṇa. They are not yet able even to enter it — how much less to abide in it; they only grow more alarmed and afraid, and their minds retreat.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Outsiders who attain meditative concentration, when something is found, superimpose an observation that does not accord with reality, and so give rise to eternalism; when nothing is found, they falsely discriminate, and easily give rise to annihilationism. Most of the sixty-two views arise in this way within concentration, from misjudging the object seen in it. At root they do not understand no-self: from the view of a perishable self come eternalism and annihilationism and, branching from them, the sixty-two views; they are deluded about every conditioned dharma, the sentient-being world and the container world alike, and know nothing of the emptiness of self and of dharmas. Some cling to the body as self, some to one of the immaterial aggregates, feeling, perception, formation, or consciousness, as self and the rest as belonging to self, and some cling to self over name and form together.

Because they cling to self, when a state resembling the signless concentration appears they fear that the self will cease to be, and so take no delight in nirvāṇa, thinking that in nirvāṇa the self would be nothing at all. Not entering it, how could they abide in it? If outsiders truly came upon a signless state resembling nirvāṇa, they would only grow more alarmed, and their minds would retreat. With signs as their object they superimpose, clinging to form, or to feeling, perception, formation, or consciousness, or to both form and the formless, as self; facing signlessness they deny, taking it to be sheer nothingness, which is another clinging. Clinging to existence and clinging to emptiness are both imagined grasping; holding the view of self, outsiders fall into one or the other and cannot enter the uncontaminated signless concentration.

216. Section: Found Only Within the Inner Dharma — An Overall Distinction

Those who abide in the inner dharma are the contrary of them: regarding parinirvāṇa, their minds do not retreat; they understand it as solely the cessation of suffering, and see in it solely the virtue of quiescence.

Chinese Source

住內法者與彼相違，於般涅槃心無退轉，了唯苦滅，見唯靜德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who abide in the inner dharma are the contrary of them: regarding parinirvāṇa, their minds do not retreat; they understand it as solely the cessation of suffering, and see in it solely the virtue of quiescence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Holding right view of no-self, those who abide in the inner dharma do not lose heart before entering nirvāṇa. They know that there was never a self to begin with, so that realizing nirvāṇa is not the disappearance of a self; form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness were never to be found, not only once nirvāṇa is reached. They understand nirvāṇa as the complete stilling of every suffering, seeing only its virtue of quiescence and never imagining that the self is lost, for they have no clinging to self.

217. Section: Trainees and Those Beyond Training Distinguished — Those Still in Training

Those still in training seek solely inner cessation, not a path to birth, and further seek instruction and admonition from others.

Chinese Source

若諸有學唯祈內滅，非為生道更從他求教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those still in training seek solely inner cessation, not a path to birth, and further seek instruction and admonition from others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Those of the first three fruits, who have not yet fully realized nirvāṇa, do not seek a better fruit in a future birth; praying only that their own inner being reach cessation and accomplish nirvāṇa, they request instruction in concentration and wisdom and admonition in the precepts from other good friends.

218. Section: Those Beyond Training, Closing "The Teacher's Own Task" and Swift Penetration Entirely

Those beyond training delight solely in inner cessation; they never again seek to exhaust the afflictions, and there remain only the formations produced by prior causes, spontaneously returning to cessation, and so they attain parinirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

若諸無學唯欣內滅，終不更求盡諸煩惱，唯有先因所生諸行任運歸滅而般涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those beyond training delight solely in inner cessation; they never again seek to exhaust the afflictions, and there remain only the formations produced by prior causes, spontaneously returning to cessation, and so they attain parinirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An arhat beyond training is glad that inner cessation has at last been reached; every affliction already stilled, there is nothing further to cut off. The afflictions of craving and views of all three realms are entirely cut off, and only the present aggregates, the fruit body produced by past afflictions, remain, spontaneously and naturally tending toward cessation, and so the arhat enters nirvāṇa without residue.

With this, the teacher's own five tasks are complete: rightly subduing, rightly embracing, rightly rebuking, rightly teaching defilement, and rightly teaching purity. The teacher's own task, corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-fifth sūtra, is complete, and with it swift penetration's ten topics, sūtras 46 through 55. Fascicle 87 continues the aggregates section with its fifth overall topic, cause (因攝).

219. Section: Background — Sūtra 55, the Buddha Rebukes the Monks and Receives Them Again, Closing "The Teacher's Own Task"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's fifty-fifth sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, a minor dispute arose in the assembly and the Buddha rebuked the monks. He then went into the city for alms, and afterward sat alone beneath a tree in the Andha Grove, reflecting: many in the assembly are young monks, newly gone forth, who, not seeing their teacher, may come to regret it and grow sorrowful; having long felt compassion for them, I should return and receive them again. The great Brahmā, knowing the Buddha's thought, appeared before him in the time it takes a strong man to bend and straighten his arm, affirmed every point, and asked him to return and receive the monks; the Buddha consented in silence, and Brahmā bowed, circled him three times, and vanished. Returning to the Jeta Grove, the Buddha spread his sitting cloth, sat composed and upright, and showed a slight expression that let the monks dare approach; they came with shame on their faces, bowed, and sat to one side.

The Buddha then told them: one who goes forth lives humbly, head shaved, bowl in hand, begging from house to house as if under a spell — and does so for the sake of the ultimate goal, to cross beyond birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, lamentation, distress, and suffering. You did not go forth driven by a king or by thieves, nor to escape debt, nor from fear, nor for fear of losing your life, but to be freed from suffering — is that not so? It is so, they said. Then how can some foolish one among you still give rise to greed and deep attachment, anger and violence, laziness and meanness, lost mindfulness, a scattered mind, and confused faculties? It is like going from darkness into darkness, climbing out of a cesspit only to fall back in, washing blood with blood, abandoning evil only to take up evil again — or like the charred remains of a cremation fire, discarded among the graves, that no woodcutter will gather. There are three unwholesome thoughts — of greed, hatred, and harm — and they arise from perception, from the countless perceptions of greed, hatred, and harm. They are ended completely by fixing the mind on the four foundations of mindfulness and abiding in the signless concentration, cultivated and much cultivated; it is for this that sons and daughters of good family go forth, dwelling at the gate of the deathless and finally reaching the deathless, nirvāṇa. This is not reached by those holding three views: that life is the body, that life is one thing and the body another, or that form is the self, one with it and unchanging forever. The learned noble disciple reflects: is there anything in the world that can be grasped without fault? Finding nothing, seeing that grasping at form or any other aggregate brings fault, he grasps at nothing in the world and so realizes nirvāṇa for himself: birth is exhausted, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done is done, and there will be no further existence. Hearing this, the monks rejoiced and put it into practice.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that it was on reading this sūtra, seeing how the Buddha trains his disciples, that Maitreya set out the teacher's five tasks — rightly subduing, rightly receiving, rightly rebuking, rightly teaching defilement, and rightly teaching purification — and he traces the sūtra through the treatise's own items: the alms round matches the ten reasons the Tathāgata enters a village for alms; the solitary sitting, his entering the peaceful divine abiding; the young monks newly gone forth, the marks of new disciples and their arising remorse; the wish to receive them again, the five ways the teacher receives his disciples, all out of compassion; Brahmā's coming, the seven reasons Śakra, Brahmā, and other gods come before the Tathāgata, particularly the seventh, in which Brahmā knows the Tathāgata's thought; the composed sitting, the marks the Tathāgata shows before teaching; the monks' shame, the three grounds on which disciples who have transgressed approach the teacher in shame; the question of why they went forth, the three marks of rightly rebuking; the similes of darkness, the cesspit, washing blood with blood, and the cremation remains, the six faults of defilement; the three unwholesome thoughts born of perception, the treatise's wrong perceptions that seize on marks and features; and the four foundations of mindfulness and the signless concentration, the two antidotes, which bear great fruit and are not shared with outsiders — leading through the element of cutting off and the element of freedom from desire to the gate of the deathless. The three views are taking the life faculty as self, holding life and body to be different, and taking form as the self, permanent and unchanging; the noble disciple, finding nothing in the world that can be grasped without fault, grasps at nothing and so knows his own nirvāṇa. With this, the teacher's own task, corresponding to sūtra 55, is complete.

220. Section: Background — The Summary Verse for Sūtras 46-55, Closing "Swift Penetration" Entirely

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-sixth through fifty-fifth sūtras and their summary verse

A closing verse gathers the ten sūtras of swift penetration together: "what should be said" (46), "the small clay ball" (47), "foam" — form as a mass of foam (48), "the two of not knowing" (49-50), "the river" (51), "the Jeta Grove" (52), "the trees" (53), "Tiṣya rebuked" (54), and "the perceptions" (55). With this, swift penetration — the aggregates section's fourth topic, its ten parts corresponding to sūtras 46 through 55 — is entirely complete, and with it fascicle 86 concludes.
