Fascicle 86 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十六 · The Aggregates Section Continues

Perception and Formation, and Swift Penetration

Fascicle 86 continues the Compendium of Bases' aggregates section (行擇攝), covering its third and fourth of eleven overall topics: perception and formation (想行攝) and swift penetration (速通攝). Perception and formation opens with the five marks of impermanence — a formation's own impermanent nature, its lack of constancy, its inability to endure, its unreliability, and its very nature as subject to change and ruin — then examines the fool's own threefold conceit in comparing self to others, before unfolding through the eye, benefit, nine kinds of wisdom, freedom from delusion, and further advancement toward the noble path. This fascicle corresponds to the Saṃyukta Āgama's aggregates recitation, sūtras 36 through 45.

3. Section: Perception and Formation Begins — Five Marks of the Perception of Impermanence Named

Among the formations, cultivating the perception of impermanence has five kinds — namely, through the nature of impermanence, the nature of inconstancy, the nature of being unable to endure, the nature of being unreliable, and the nature of being subject to change and ruin.

Chinese Source

於諸行中，修無常想行有五種。謂由無常性、無恆性、非久住性、不可保性、變壞法性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the formations, cultivating the perception of impermanence has five kinds — namely, through the nature of impermanence, the nature of inconstancy, the nature of being unable to endure, the nature of being unreliable, and the nature of being subject to change and ruin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Perception and formation's own first section names five approaches to cultivating the perception of impermanence toward the aggregates, whether one's own body or another's, past, present, future, inner or outer, coarse or fine, far or near — each to be unpacked individually in what follows.

4. Section: The Five Marks Explained

Here, ruin moment by moment is impermanence. One's own being, bound to a limited span of life, is inconstancy. The outer world, certain to have no abode once the kalpa ends, is the inability to endure. The lifespan not yet fulfilled, still liable to be destroyed by conditions, dying before its proper time, is unreliability. And, for however long one abides, never settled in ease throughout that span, is the very nature of change and ruin.

Chinese Source

此中剎那剎那壞，故無常。自體繫屬有限住壽，故無恆。外事劫後決定無住，故非久住。壽量未滿，容被緣壞，非時而死，故不可保。乃至爾所時住，於其中間不定安樂，故變壞法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, ruin moment by moment is impermanence. One's own being, bound to a limited span of life, is inconstancy. The outer world, certain to have no abode once the kalpa ends, is the inability to endure. The lifespan not yet fulfilled, still liable to be destroyed by conditions, dying before its proper time, is unreliability. And, for however long one abides, never settled in ease throughout that span, is the very nature of change and ruin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whether one's own body or another's, past, present, or future, inner or outer, coarse or fine, far or near, every formation alike decays moment by moment — were this not so, aging and death could never occur; a body healthy today will nonetheless sicken in time, its vigor and complexion fading in increments too subtle to notice directly, hence impermanence. Even setting momentary change aside, a single lifetime itself has its own fixed limit — seventy, eighty, a hundred years, or more — never truly enduring forever, hence inconstancy, distinguished from impermanence by looking to time's larger span rather than the instant. The physical world fares no better: mountains and rivers pass through formation, continuance, decay, and emptiness in turn, gone entirely once the kalpa of decay arrives — a sudden flood or landslide can just as easily sweep away field and home within a single day, proof enough that even the physical world cannot endure. Even before one's own natural lifespan is exhausted, unexpected conditions can bring an untimely death that ought not yet have come — hence unreliability. And, finally, even granting a full and healthy life, no single day passes in unbroken ease — some ache here, some sorrow there — this too is the very nature of change and ruin. Once these five marks are genuinely seen, a practitioner gradually releases attachment to the aggregates, hastens toward wholesome practice, and moves toward escaping the three realms entirely, even unsurpassed awakening itself; for a śrāvaka specifically, this means cutting off craving and views so that no further such body need ever be taken up again.

5. Section: Delusion's Mark Begins — Three Marks of the Fool Named

Further, an ordinary being, briefly speaking, carries three marks of foolishness.

Chinese Source

復次，愚夫略有三種愚夫之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, an ordinary being, briefly speaking, carries three marks of foolishness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Perception and formation's second section, delusion's mark, names three ways an ordinary being's own foolishness manifests, each concerning the comparison of self to others, to be examined individually in what follows.

6. Section: The First Mark — Believing Oneself Superior

This means that ordinary beings, unable to reflect on the five natures of impermanence just explained regarding every formation, instead discriminate superiority and inferiority within what is not genuinely real, weighing self against other, and conclude that they themselves are superior. This is called the ordinary being's first mark.

Chinese Source

謂諸愚夫，於一切行如上所說五無常性不能思惟；於非真實勝劣性中，分別勝劣，稱量自他，謂己為勝。是名第一愚夫之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that ordinary beings, unable to reflect on the five natures of impermanence just explained regarding every formation, instead discriminate superiority and inferiority within what is not genuinely real, weighing self against other, and conclude that they themselves are superior. This is called the ordinary being's first mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

An ordinary being, still bound by self-clinging, cannot reflect on the aggregates' own fivefold impermanence, and instead endlessly measures self against other within a superiority and inferiority that was never genuinely real to begin with, concluding — however momentarily sharp their own faculties may seem — that they themselves come out ahead, never recognizing that with no genuine self at all, no one is truly superior, equal, or inferior to anyone else; the aggregates themselves are illusory, no real self to be found in them, and comparing at all is simply a dream born of ignorance. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm: every formation's own superiority or inferiority is merely a provisional distinction established through mutual comparison, never genuinely real — one may seem clever measured against a fool, yet foolish measured against someone still cleverer; each person is sharper in some respects and duller in others, and any comparison drawn depends entirely on which respect is chosen, never a fixed, objective fact. Taking such comparison as genuinely real, and taking pride in coming out ahead, is exactly the ordinary being's own first mark — and, without encountering the true dharma, this same restless, beginningless comparing continues without any hope of ever waking from it.

7. Section: The Second and Third Marks — Equal or Inferior, and the Wise One's Contrast

As with believing oneself superior, so too with believing oneself equal or inferior — extensively explained the same way. And, contrary to this, the wise one likewise carries three marks of wisdom.

Chinese Source

如謂己勝，謂等、謂劣，廣說亦爾。與此相違，當知智者亦有三種智者之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

As with believing oneself superior, so too with believing oneself equal or inferior — extensively explained the same way. And, contrary to this, the wise one likewise carries three marks of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Believing oneself merely equal to another, or genuinely inferior, is the same delusion as believing oneself superior — all three alike arise only because the self one is comparing was never real to begin with, and all three alike breed further affliction through the very act of dividing self from other. The wise one, contrariwise, understands that superiority, equality, and inferiority are all merely provisional distinctions set up through mutual comparison, never genuinely real; where some deficiency does show up, one need only gather the right conditions and apply oneself further to make it up, so no one is ever truly, fixedly superior, equal, or inferior to anyone else. Understanding no-self and no-what-belongs-to-self, the wise one never engages in such pointless comparison at all.

8. Section: The Eye Begins — Two Marks of the Purified Wisdom-Eye Named

Further, through two marks one should know a noble one's wisdom-eye to be purified — namely, through being far from dust and free of defilement.

Chinese Source

復次，由二種相，當知聖者慧眼清淨。謂由遠塵及離垢故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through two marks one should know a noble one's wisdom-eye to be purified — namely, through being far from dust and free of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eye's own section explains the "eye" in the first line of perception and formation's particular verse. Among the five eyes — flesh, heaven, wisdom, dharma, and Buddha — this concerns the wisdom-eye, purified through being far from dust and free of defilement, echoing the Saṃyukta Āgama's own line, "far from dust, free of defilement, the dharma-eye purified." A practitioner who cultivates calm and insight upon the four truths' sixteen aspects, penetrating the five aggregates' impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, enters direct realization of the noble truths and turns from ordinary being into a noble one, gaining the wisdom-eye — this is called being far from dust and free of defilement. What follows unpacks the two through two further methods.

9. Section: Far From Dust and Free of Defilement, First Method — Current Affliction and Its Latent Seed

Through being freed from the bondage of afflictions cut off by the path of seeing that manifest as actual affliction, this is called far from dust. Through being freed from the bondage of their latent tendencies, this is called free of defilement.

Chinese Source

由見所斷諸煩惱纏得離繫故，名為遠塵。由彼隨眠得離繫故，說名離垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through being freed from the bondage of afflictions cut off by the path of seeing that manifest as actual affliction, this is called far from dust. Through being freed from the bondage of their latent tendencies, this is called free of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One entering the path of seeing cuts off the discriminated afflictions proper to that path — those arising chiefly from one's own views and reasoning, clinging to the five aggregates as self or as belonging to self, and the further afflictions this spawns across the three realms. Freed from their actual manifestation, this is far from dust — dust here being the coarser, more visible kind. Freed even from their seed, the subtler defilement remaining after the coarse dust is gone, this is free of defilement.

10. Section: Far From Dust and Free of Defilement, Second Method — Conceit and the Remaining Afflictions

Further, at the time of direct realization, there is a coarse self-conceit that arises interruptedly or uninterruptedly, mixed into one's attention. If one thoroughly knows the grasped, the grasper, and their object to be equal, that conceit is then extinguished; through its extinction, this is called far from dust. Through the cutting off of the latent tendencies of all afflictions cut off by the path of seeing, this is called free of defilement.

Chinese Source

又現觀時，有麤我慢隨入作意間無間轉，若遍了知所取、能取、所緣平等，彼即斷滅；彼斷滅故，說名遠塵。一切見道所斷煩惱隨眠斷故，說名離垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, at the time of direct realization, there is a coarse self-conceit that arises interruptedly or uninterruptedly, mixed into one's attention. If one thoroughly knows the grasped, the grasper, and their object to be equal, that conceit is then extinguished; through its extinction, this is called far from dust. Through the cutting off of the latent tendencies of all afflictions cut off by the path of seeing, this is called free of defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Practicing insight within calm abiding, observing the five aggregates as without self and without what belongs to self, a coarse conceit can still slip in — a fleeting sense that "only I can practice this observation," a subtle pride arising with or without one's noticing. Once one thoroughly knows the observed aggregates, the observing mind itself, and their shared object all alike to be without self, grasper and grasped become equal and conceit no longer arises — its actual manifestation cut off, this is far from dust. Continuing into the union of calm and insight until all one hundred and twelve latent tendencies cut off by the path of seeing across the three realms are cut off at the root, this alone is properly called free of defilement.

11. Section: Benefit Begins — Ten Benefits Named and Asked

Further, being far from dust and free of defilement, when one gains the dharma-eye within all dharmas, one should know that one thereby gains ten kinds of benefit. What are the ten?

Chinese Source

復次，遠塵離垢，於諸法中得法眼時，當知即得十種勝利。何等為十？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, being far from dust and free of defilement, when one gains the dharma-eye within all dharmas, one should know that one thereby gains ten kinds of benefit. What are the ten?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Through cultivating calm and insight, a practitioner accomplishes uncontaminated wisdom, is freed from both the discriminated self-clinging's actual manifestation and its seed, and thereby gains the dharma-eye — seeing directly, through one's own experience, the five aggregates' impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self. Ten distinct benefits follow, unpacked one by one below.

12. Section: Benefits One Through Four — Seeing the Dharma, Gaining the Dharma, Knowing the Dharma, and Thorough Firmness in the Dharma

First, having thoroughly seen the four noble truths, this is called seeing the dharma. Second, attaining one of the fruits of the ascetic path accordingly, this is called gaining the dharma. Third, being able to know for oneself what one has realized — that one has now exhausted all rebirth as a hell-being, an animal, or a hungry ghost, that one has attained stream-entry, and so on at length — through this, it is called knowing the dharma. Fourth, attaining the four kinds of confirmed purity, knowing the Buddha, the dharma, and the saṅgha as they truly are, this is called being thoroughly firm in the dharma.

Chinese Source

一者、於四聖諦已善見故，說名見法。二者、隨獲一種沙門果故，說名得法。三者、於己所證能自了知：我今已盡所有那落迦、傍生、餓鬼，我證預流，乃至廣說。由如是故，說名知法。四者、得四證淨，於佛法僧如實知故，名遍堅法。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, having thoroughly seen the four noble truths, this is called seeing the dharma. Second, attaining one of the fruits of the ascetic path accordingly, this is called gaining the dharma. Third, being able to know for oneself what one has realized — that one has now exhausted all rebirth as a hell-being, an animal, or a hungry ghost, that one has attained stream-entry, and so on at length — through this, it is called knowing the dharma. Fourth, attaining the four kinds of confirmed purity, knowing the Buddha, the dharma, and the saṅgha as they truly are, this is called being thoroughly firm in the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Seeing the dharma is the first fruit, penetrating the four truths by direct uncontaminated wisdom. Gaining the dharma follows once the discriminated afflictions of the path of seeing are cut off and the dharma-eye purified. Knowing the dharma means the stream-enterer knows for themselves that this life's causes for the three lower realms are exhausted — even though seeds for those realms remain, the conditions to ripen them no longer arise — and that stream-entry, never again to fall back into ordinary being, has been attained. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain "attained stream-entry, and so on at length" as including the full exhaustion of the seven, two, or single further rebirth still owed by a once-returner or a family-to-family stream-enterer, detailed already in fascicle 85. Thoroughly firm in the dharma is the fourth benefit: uncontaminated faith in the Three Jewels arising together with uncontaminated wisdom at the moment the truths are realized, and this faith, unshakeable by any outsider, is called thorough firmness.

13. Section: Benefits Five and Six — No Confusion and No Doubt

Fifth, having no confusion regarding what one has realized oneself. Sixth, having no doubt regarding what others have realized.

Chinese Source

五者、於自所證無惑。六者、於他所證無疑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, having no confusion regarding what one has realized oneself. Sixth, having no doubt regarding what others have realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having directly realized suffering as suffering, its cause as its cause, cessation as cessation, and the path as the path, the stream-enterer has awoken from ignorance's great dream and holds no confusion at all about their own realization. And having realized this for themselves, they likewise recognize the same quality in another who has realized it, holding no doubt about it either.

14. Section: Benefits Seven Through Nine — Needing No Outside Aid, Bending to No Other's Word, and Fearlessly Declaring All One Has Realized

Seventh, when expounding the teaching corresponding to the noble truths, not relying on any outside condition. Eighth, not watching another's face, not looking to another's mouth — within this true dharma and discipline, unmoved by any outsider's doctrine whatsoever. Ninth, declaring all the liberation one has realized, with no fear at all.

Chinese Source

七者、宣說聖諦相應教時，不藉他緣。八者、不觀他面，不看他口，於此正法毗奈耶中，一切他論所不能轉。九者、記別一切所證解脫，都無所畏。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh, when expounding the teaching corresponding to the noble truths, not relying on any outside condition. Eighth, not watching another's face, not looking to another's mouth — within this true dharma and discipline, unmoved by any outsider's doctrine whatsoever. Ninth, declaring all the liberation one has realized, with no fear at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having gained fundamental wisdom, the stream-enterer needs no text, no teacher's further instruction, to expound the four truths in subsequently attained wisdom — having walked the ancient sages' own path in person, they simply relate what they themselves have verified. Their wisdom already accords with right view, so no outside doctrine, however presented, can shake their confidence in the Buddha's dharma and discipline. And, having personally realized the true character of all dharmas, they can declare exactly which afflictions among the three realms' one hundred and twelve are resolved and which remain, without any trace of dread when others come to challenge them.

15. Section: Benefit Ten — Entering the Noble Teaching, Closing "Perception and Formation, Delusion's Mark, the Eye, and Benefit"

Tenth, through two conditions, one enters into the noble teaching — namely, through correct convention and through the ultimate meaning.

Chinese Source

十者、由二因緣，隨入聖教，謂正世俗及第一義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tenth, through two conditions, one enters into the noble teaching — namely, through correct convention and through the ultimate meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Correct convention is the teaching as conveyed through language, called the wisdom that abides in the dharma; the ultimate meaning refers to nirvāṇa-wisdom. Relying on the wisdom that abides in the dharma to penetrate suffering, its cause, cessation, and the path, and cultivating calm and insight upon it, one cuts off the corresponding afflictions and accomplishes nirvāṇa-wisdom — the dharma-abiding wisdom always coming first, nirvāṇa-wisdom following after. With this tenth benefit, the four sections perception and formation, delusion's mark, the eye, and benefit — all corresponding to the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-sixth sūtra — are complete.

16. Section: Background — Sūtra 36, Śroṇa's Dialogue with Śāriputra

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-sixth sūtra

The Buddha was dwelling at Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove; the venerable Śāriputra was staying on Vulture Peak. A householder's son, Śroṇa, often came there and asked Śāriputra why ascetics and brahmins, comparing their own impermanent, ever-changing, unsettled body to another's, conclude "I am superior," "I am equal," or "I am inferior" — precisely the three foolish marks already explained above — and what clings beneath such thinking that keeps them from seeing things as they truly are.

Rather than answering directly, Śāriputra questioned Śroṇa in turn: is form permanent or impermanent? Impermanent, Śroṇa answered. Then is it suffering? It is suffering. Then would the Buddha's noble disciples, knowing form to be impermanent and therefore suffering and subject to change, cling to it as self, as other than self, or as self-in-form and form-in-self? No, Śroṇa answered — a noble disciple sees no self in form, no self apart from form, no self in form or form in self. The same holds for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Śāriputra then explained that a practitioner observes each aggregate's exhaustive nature across past, future, present, inner, outer, coarse, fine, superior, inferior, far, and near, and its shared nature — not self, not other than self, not mutually contained — and this, sustained through calm and insight, is called knowing as it truly is; from it come weariness — the fruit of the path of seeing — freedom from desire — the fruit of the path of cultivation — liberation, and the knowledge of liberation, until one knows: birth for me is exhausted, the pure life established, what had to be done is done, no further existence is to be known. Hearing this, Śroṇa himself cut off the discriminated afflictions, both their manifestation and their seed, and attained the first fruit — then rose, knelt, and took refuge in the Three Jewels as a lay follower for the rest of his life, departing in joy.

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are nine kinds of wisdom able to thoroughly know and transcend the formations — namely, the wisdom of the formations' flowing, the wisdom of the formations' reverse cessation, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of defilement, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of purification, the wisdom of purity, and the wisdom of suffering, the wisdom of its cause, the wisdom of cessation, and the wisdom of the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The nine kinds of wisdom, in "九智無癡與勝進," explain the aggregates section's third particular verse. Ordinary beings are all troubled by the aggregates' own blazing suffering; accomplishing these nine allows one to thoroughly know and transcend the formations rather than remain controlled by them.

18. Section: The First Two Kinds of Wisdom — Flowing and Reverse Cessation

Here, the wisdom of the formations' flowing is, in brief, the right wisdom regarding the gathering of every formation through three causes and conditions — namely, through the gathering of delight, the gathering of contact, and the gathering of name-and-form — and, according to what corresponds, the gathering of form, or of feeling and so on, or of consciousness. Through the cessation of these same three causes and conditions, the three kinds of formation cease — this is called the wisdom of the formations' reverse cessation.

Chinese Source

此中諸行流轉智者，略由三種因緣集故，一切行集所有正智。謂喜集故、觸集故、名色集故，隨其所應，若色集、若受等集、若識集。即此三種因緣滅故，三種行滅，是名諸行還滅智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the wisdom of the formations' flowing is, in brief, the right wisdom regarding the gathering of every formation through three causes and conditions — namely, through the gathering of delight, the gathering of contact, and the gathering of name-and-form — and, according to what corresponds, the gathering of form, or of feeling and so on, or of consciousness. Through the cessation of these same three causes and conditions, the three kinds of formation cease — this is called the wisdom of the formations' reverse cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 87)

Delight here is a kind of craving, distinct from outright greed: delight is the joy felt toward an object not yet met, as an intermediate-state being feels joy glimpsing its future parents; greed is the clinging that follows once the object is actually before one, like a cup filled till it overflows. Delight leads to craving, craving leads to grasping, grasping leads to becoming, becoming leads to birth — so delight's gathering gives rise to form's gathering, entering a mother's womb. Contact between the six sense-faculties and their six objects gives rise to the six kinds of contact, and from contact arise the corresponding feeling, perception, and formation — so contact's gathering gives rise to feeling's, perception's, and formation's gathering. And consciousness continues only because of name-and-form — the mind-dharmas and physical dharma present from conception onward — so name-and-form's gathering gives rise to consciousness's gathering. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 87) explain further: every formation's cause divides into a shared cause, delight, common to both mind and matter, whose ripening does not wait on further conditions since it was seeded by a prior joy already directed at that place of rebirth; and unshared causes proper to matter or mind separately — contact, the immediately preceding cessation of mind, co-arisen mental factors, and the ten kinds of matter — each of which still needs its own further conditions, moment by moment, before it can actually arise, even once the prior seed is present.

19. Section: The Next Three Kinds of Wisdom — Defiled Condition, Purifying Condition, and Purity

The wisdom of the causes and conditions of defilement, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of purification, and the wisdom of purity are, respectively, relish, danger, and escape, to be known as before.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The wisdom of the causes and conditions of defilement, the wisdom of the causes and conditions of purification, and the wisdom of purity are, respectively, relish, danger, and escape, to be known as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 85)

As fascicle 85 already explained: observe relish, danger, and escape through three matters and two marks to see whether one's own mind is defiled or pure. First, observe whether relish for the formations is present — relish for suffering is itself suffering, and relish keeps one from being freed from suffering, so relish is the very condition of defilement. Second, observe the aggregates' great faults and calamities — their impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self — for only by truly seeing the danger does one come to weariness, the condition of purity; ordinary beings prize the five aggregates as a treasure and cling to them all the more tightly for it, which is exactly why purity is so hard to reach, while a noble one like Maudgalyāyana, at death, let go of the aggregates as one lets go of a poisoned vessel, without the least attachment. Third, observe purity itself directly — whether desire and craving toward one's own or another's body, or toward form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, has actually been left behind, whether some degree of awakening and accordant realization has arisen. This threefold observation is relish, danger, and escape together: relish is the condition of defilement, danger's observation is the condition of purity, and escape is purity's own accomplishment, from cutting off affliction's actual manifestation through to cutting off its seed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 34, Śrāvaka Section)

Fascicle 34's Śrāvaka Section teaches ten aspects for observing the truth of suffering, entering its four aspects — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, no-self. 21. Section: Thorough Knowledge and Transcendence Explained, and the Nine Wisdoms Summarized

If the wisdom that thoroughly knows the formations through the principle of the truths is in any way lacking, one certainly cannot advance further in cultivation, employ the power of the antidote to cut off the afflictions, and transcend all formations; contrary to this, one is then able to transcend them. 22. Section: Background — Sūtras 37-38, Thorough Knowledge and Transcendence Retold to Śroṇa

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth sūtras

Śroṇa returned to Śāriputra again. In the thirty-seventh sūtra, on thorough knowledge, Śāriputra explained that any ascetic or brahmin who does not truly know form's own and shared marks, does not know its arising, does not know its cessation, and does not know the path to its cessation, lacks the capacity to actually cut off this illusory form — and the same holds for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Questioned again on whether form and the other aggregates are permanent, Śroṇa answered as before that they are impermanent, hence suffering, hence not self, not other than self, not mutually contained. Śāriputra then taught the same elevenfold observation of each aggregate's exhaustive and shared nature, leading to weariness, freedom from desire, liberation, and freedom from birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, and affliction; Śroṇa departed in joy as before.

In the thirty-eighth sūtra, on transcendence, Śāriputra explained the same five wisdoms — knowing form, knowing its gathering (from delight and craving), knowing its cessation, knowing relish toward it, knowing its danger, and knowing escape from it — as together the wisdom of the formations' flowing and reverse cessation, defiled condition, purifying condition, and purity; lacking these five, one cannot transcend form, nor, by the same reasoning, feeling, perception, formation, or consciousness. Śāriputra again led Śroṇa through the same elevenfold exhaustive-and-shared-nature observation of the five aggregates as without self and without what belongs to self, from which follows freedom from craving for anything in the world at all, non-clinging, and arhatship — the first fruit's birth exhausted, the third fruit's pure life established, the fourth fruit's task completed, and the knowledge that no further existence remains. Śroṇa again departed in joy. These passages together show that, before the noble path is realized, one must return again and again to the same truths in thought.

29. Section: Delight and Greed Distinguished, Six Ways

Delight named for gladness arising conditioned upon a support-feeling; attachment arising conditioned upon a feeling toward an object is called greed. Further, arising toward an object about to be obtained is called delight; arising toward an object already obtained is called greed. Further, at the point of having obtained an object and being about to enjoy it is called delight; at the very point of actually enjoying this matter is called greed. Further, toward the means of obtaining an object is called delight; toward the object itself is called greed. Further, toward future existence is called delight; toward a present object is called greed. Further, toward the honor and benefit of another loved being is called delight; toward honor and benefit obtained for oneself is called greed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Six angles distinguish delight from greed. First, by feeling's own basis: joy toward one's own body-based feeling — as the ease felt after a hot spring — is delight, a self-love; craving toward the feeling an outer object provokes — coming to crave the hot spring itself — is greed, an object-love. Second, by timing relative to obtaining: joy that a winning ticket has been drawn, before it is even collected, is delight; attachment to the money once collected, and to multiplying it further, is greed. Third, by timing relative to enjoying: the eager joy of a trip already booked, not yet begun, is delight; the attachment that extends the return flight once the trip is actually underway is greed. Fourth, by means versus object: enjoying gambling as a quick means to money is delight; attachment to the money itself is greed. Fifth, by future versus present: craving for a future existence is delight; craving for the present object is greed. Sixth, by self versus other: rejoicing in a loved one's own honor and gain is delight; joy in one's own honor and gain is greed.

30. Section: Background — Sūtras 39-41, Mahākauṣṭhila and Śāriputra on Ignorance and Clarity, Closing "Freedom From Delusion"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's thirty-ninth through forty-first sūtras

At Vulture Peak, the venerable Mahākauṣṭhila — himself already accomplished — asked Śāriputra, for the benefit of later generations: what is ignorance, and who has it? Śāriputra answered: ignorance is simply not knowing. Pressed further on what exactly is not known, Śāriputra explained across the thirty-ninth sūtra: not knowing form's past nature as impermanent, its present nature as a ceasing dharma, and its future nature as an arising-and-ceasing dharma — and the same for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness — is ignorance; knowing all this as it truly is, is clarity, called direct knowledge, direct seeing, brightness, awakening, wisdom, and unbroken realization.

In the fortieth sūtra, Śāriputra answered the same question through the four noble truths instead: not knowing form, form's arising, form's cessation, and the path to form's cessation — and the same for the remaining four aggregates — is ignorance; knowing all this as it truly is, is clarity. In the forty-first sūtra, Śāriputra answered through the nine kinds of wisdom already explained above: not knowing the first five (the wisdom of the formations' flowing, reverse cessation, defiled condition, purifying condition, and purity) is ignorance; and clarity requires further realizing the four truths' wisdoms of suffering, its cause, cessation, and the path. With this, freedom from delusion — corresponding to sūtras 39 through 41 — is complete: knowing the three times' impermanence brings clarity; failing to know it brings ignorance, and ignorance in turn gives rise to greed, hatred, and delusion, whose further synonyms and the distinction between delight and greed have already been explained above.

As a disease: the elements — earth, water, fire, wind, space, consciousness — falling into disorder brings bodily sickness and suffering; recognizing this danger, one cultivates weariness and turning away. As a boil: the aggregates, produced by affliction and karma, are like a poisoned growth, their very nature suffering — wherever conditions meet, affliction flows out from them, dooming one to further suffering ahead; recognizing the aggregates as carrying the seeds of every affliction, hidden trouble festering beneath the surface, one cultivates weariness and turning away. As an arrow: the aggregates' danger is like an enemy's arrow lodged deep in the body, agonizing and hard to draw out; recognizing this, one cultivates weariness and turning away.

35. Section: The Fourth Aspect — Affliction, and the Summary of Weariness and Turning Away

As an affliction is, for instance, like one whose various sufferings arise from one's own wrong reckoning amid the loss of loved ones, wealth, and the like, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away. Thus is it named: a practitioner cultivating insight cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away regarding the formations.

Chinese Source

惱害者，謂於親財等匱乏中，因自邪計所生諸苦，修厭背想。如是名為修觀行者，於諸行中修厭背想。

Ru-Yi Translation

As an affliction is, for instance, like one whose various sufferings arise from one's own wrong reckoning amid the loss of loved ones, wealth, and the like, upon which one cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away. Thus is it named: a practitioner cultivating insight cultivates the perception of weariness and turning away regarding the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

The Notes gloss "loss of loved ones, wealth, and the like" as the death or estrangement of loved relatives, the loss of one's wealth, or the loss of one's own health, and "one's own wrong reckoning" as the unwise discrimination that turns such loss into suffering. Because beings wrongly conceive of self and what-belongs-to-self within the aggregates, the ordinary losses of relatives, wealth, and health become occasions of anguish — recognizing this, one cultivates weariness and turning away, completing the fourfold contemplation.

36. Section: Danger — Impermanence and Suffering

The perception of danger has, further, two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations as impermanent and contemplating them as suffering.

Chinese Source

過患想者，復有二行。謂於諸行思惟無常及思惟苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The perception of danger has, further, two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations as impermanent and contemplating them as suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Impermanence is every conditioned dharma's shared mark: the body ages, sickens, and dies; thoughts never cease shifting; feeling swings ceaselessly between pain, pleasure, and neither; consciousness discriminates without a moment's rest. Being impermanent, none of it can hold lasting ease, hence suffering — recognizing the aggregates' great danger through these two, a practitioner advances further.

37. Section: True Meaning — Emptiness and No-Self, and the Concluding Statement on Cause and Fruit

The perception of true meaning likewise has two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations' emptiness-nature and their no-self-nature. Here, one first properly cultivates the perception of danger and the perception of true meaning, and only afterward is able to abide in the perception of weariness and turning away. One should know that here the fruit is spoken of first, and the cause afterward.

Chinese Source

實義想者，亦有二行。謂於諸行思惟空性及無我性。此中先於過患想及實義想正修習已，然後方能住厭背想。當知此中，先說其果，後說其因。

Ru-Yi Translation

The perception of true meaning likewise has two aspects — namely, contemplating the formations' emptiness-nature and their no-self-nature. Here, one first properly cultivates the perception of danger and the perception of true meaning, and only afterward is able to abide in the perception of weariness and turning away. One should know that here the fruit is spoken of first, and the cause afterward.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

No-self means there is no self within the aggregates; emptiness means there is no self apart from the aggregates either, inside or outside — hence empty. Through repeated hearing, reflection, and cultivation of calm and insight, one first cuts off the discriminated self-clinging and, further, the innate clinging to self and to what belongs to self. The treatise explains that only after cultivating danger and true meaning does weariness and turning away truly settle — the four-aspect contemplation (disease, boil, arrow, affliction) is a stream-enterer's own advancement, its fruit, described first; the impermanence/suffering/emptiness/no-self contemplation that precedes stream-entry is the cause, described after. In practice, all eight are cultivated both before and after the first fruit, only with a shifting emphasis: before stream-entry, weariness through disease-boil-arrow-affliction leads into impermanence-suffering-emptiness-no-self and the uninterrupted concentration that enters the path of seeing; after stream-entry, the same weariness-contemplation purifies what has already been seen, still resting finally on the same four marks, all the way through the fourth fruit and the present dwelling in ease.

38. Section: Background — Sūtra 42, Mahākauṣṭhila and Śāriputra on Advancement, Closing "Advancement"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-second sūtra

At Vulture Peak, Mahākauṣṭhila asked Śāriputra how one not yet directly realizing the truths should think in order to advance toward uninterrupted realization. Śāriputra answered: to attain the first fruit, diligently contemplate the five aggregates through three perceptions — weariness and turning away (as disease, boil, thorn, and slayer — corresponding to this treatise's arrow and affliction), danger (impermanence and suffering), and true meaning (emptiness and not-self). Asked the same question for the second, third, and fourth fruits, and finally for the present dwelling in ease of an arhat already accomplished, Śāriputra gave the very same answer each time — the same eightfold contemplation carrying a practitioner, ever more finely observed, from ordinary being all the way through arhatship and the present dwelling in ease. With this, advancement — corresponding to sūtra 42 — is complete.

39. Section: The View of Self Distinguished Begins — Four Kinds of Self-View Named

Further, there are four kinds of view of self serving as a basis, able to give rise to conceit.

Chinese Source

復次，有四種我見為所依止，能生我慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are four kinds of view of self serving as a basis, able to give rise to conceit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The view of self distinguished explains the third line of perception and formation's verse, "the view of self distinguished, threefold mark and formation," corresponding to sūtra 44.

40. Section: The Four Kinds of Self-View Explained

First, the discriminated view of self, which arises among outsiders. Second, the innate view of self, which arises even down among birds and beasts. Third, the view of self conditioned upon one's own basis, which arises with regard to each one's own inner body. Fourth, the view of self conditioned upon another's basis, which arises with regard to another's body.

Chinese Source

一、有分別我見，謂諸外道所起。二、俱生我見，謂下至禽獸等亦能生起。三、緣自依止我見，謂於各別內身所起。四、緣他依止我見，謂於他身所起。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the discriminated view of self, which arises among outsiders. Second, the innate view of self, which arises even down among birds and beasts. Third, the view of self conditioned upon one's own basis, which arises with regard to each one's own inner body. Fourth, the view of self conditioned upon another's basis, which arises with regard to another's body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The discriminated view of self is what outsiders' own texts and doctrines argue for explicitly, clinging to the aggregates as self or as belonging to self — though in truth any ordinary being not yet awakened to reality carries both this and the innate view, not outsiders alone; the treatise singles out outsiders here only to mark Buddhism's basic difference from them, since outsiders alone refuse to teach no-self. The innate view needs no argument at all — even an animal facing a tiger instinctively flees, a pig or sheep led to slaughter instinctively panics, proof enough that every being clings instinctively to its own life. The view conditioned on one's own basis, clinging to a real self within this present body, is called the view of a perishable aggregate; the view conditioned on another's basis takes another's aggregated body as a genuinely real other being, and from this comparison the discrimination of self, other, and others-among-others arises.

41. Section: How the Discriminated Self-View Generates Conceit — the Mirror Simile

Regarding the discriminated view of self serving as a basis to generate conceit: through this view, one observes one's own and another's body and reckons a real self to exist; through these two kinds of self-view as a basis, conceit arises. It is like the surface of a clean, round mirror, upon which, conditioned by the reflected object, an image arises; conditioned by this image, in dependence on itself it gives rise to the thought of inferior, middling, or superior. Thus, through wrong discrimination, the view of self conditioned on one's own basis serves as a condition, giving rise to the view of self conditioned on another's basis — just as an image arises in dependence on a reflected object. And this in turn serves as a condition, giving rise to conceit, which faces others and reckons oneself superior, equal, or inferior.

Chinese Source

分別我見為所依止生我慢者，謂由此見觀自、他身，計有實我；由此二種我見為依，發生我慢。譬如清淨圓鏡面上，質像為依，發生影像；影像為依，於自依止發生劣中勝想。如是由邪分別故，緣自依止我見為緣，發生緣他依止我見。如依質像，發生影像。又此為緣，發生我慢，方他謂己或勝、或等、或劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the discriminated view of self serving as a basis to generate conceit: through this view, one observes one's own and another's body and reckons a real self to exist; through these two kinds of self-view as a basis, conceit arises. It is like the surface of a clean, round mirror, upon which, conditioned by the reflected object, an image arises; conditioned by this image, in dependence on itself it gives rise to the thought of inferior, middling, or superior. Thus, through wrong discrimination, the view of self conditioned on one's own basis serves as a condition, giving rise to the view of self conditioned on another's basis — just as an image arises in dependence on a reflected object. And this in turn serves as a condition, giving rise to conceit, which faces others and reckons oneself superior, equal, or inferior.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and Master Yin Shun's Modern Treatise on the Middle Way

Standing before a clean mirror, one's reflection appears; wrongly taking that reflection to be "me" is the view of self conditioned on one's own basis. Seeing another's body then calls up a corresponding image in one's own mind, wrongly taken as another real self — the view of self conditioned on another's basis. Comparing these two, before and after, or against another, gives rise to the thought of inferior, equal, or superior — precisely conceit. Master Yin Shun distinguishes the view conditioned on one's own basis (the view of a perishable aggregate, a direct felt sense of one's own reality) from the view conditioned on another's basis (clinging to a person as a real being, called the view of a person) — the two are quite different, since the perishable-aggregate view concerns only one's own body and mind directly, while the view of a person can regard even animals as real beings without generating any sense of "self" in them.

42. Section: How the Innate Self-View Generates Conceit — the Clear-Water Simile

Regarding the innate view of self serving as a condition to generate conceit, one should know the simile differs from before — it is like a person of clear sight, standing before a vessel of clean water, observing their own eyes and ears. The rest is as before, and its mark should be known accordingly.

Chinese Source

俱生我見為緣生我慢者，當知譬喻與前差別。如明眼人，臨淨水器自觀眼、耳。所餘如前，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding the innate view of self serving as a condition to generate conceit, one should know the simile differs from before — it is like a person of clear sight, standing before a vessel of clean water, observing their own eyes and ears. The rest is as before, and its mark should be known accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary Notes, fascicle 22 (Master Tài)

Where the mirror was man-made, standing for the discriminated view comparing self against other, the water is entirely natural, standing for the innate view, which needs only one's own reflection to arise — seeing one's own eyes and ears in the water and thinking, unprompted, "how fine my eyes are," "what fortunate ears I have." The mirror-conceit arises by placing oneself above another, elevating self over other; the water-conceit arises simply by clinging fondly to one's own excellence, closer to what is elsewhere called pride — arising from one's own basis alone, without any comparison to another at all.

43. Section: How Self-View Is Cut Off — Only Through the Well-Taught Dharma, and True Gratitude

This entire perishable-aggregate view, of every kind, can be permanently cut off only by relying on the well-taught dharma and discipline, and by no other, wrong teaching. Thus does the Tathāgata, and fellow practitioners of the pure life known to the community, or a teacher's own disciples who are fellow practitioners, hold great kindness. Solely through this one condition is it named true repayment of kindness to the great teacher, or, after his passing, to one's fellow practitioners of the pure life.

Chinese Source

此一切種薩迦耶見，唯依善說法毗奈耶方能永斷，非餘邪教。如是如來，及眾共知同梵行者、或諸弟子同梵行者，有大恩德。唯由如是一因緣故，名於大師、或滅度後同梵行者真實報恩。

Ru-Yi Translation

This entire perishable-aggregate view, of every kind, can be permanently cut off only by relying on the well-taught dharma and discipline, and by no other, wrong teaching. Thus does the Tathāgata, and fellow practitioners of the pure life known to the community, or a teacher's own disciples who are fellow practitioners, hold great kindness. Solely through this one condition is it named true repayment of kindness to the great teacher, or, after his passing, to one's fellow practitioners of the pure life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Only the Buddha's own dharma and discipline — nowhere found among outsider teachings — can permanently cut off all four kinds of perishable-aggregate view; the Buddha and the noble disciples who transmit this liberation from generation to generation hold immeasurable kindness toward beings. The Buddha himself, across countless kalpas of difficult practice, sought out and realized this very dharma for the sake of beings; relying on it to attain genuine liberation is therefore called true repayment of that kindness.

44. Section: A Second Kind — Partial Repayment of Kindness, and Background on Sūtra 44

Further, through a second condition: if there is one able, in reliance on the meaning of these very distinctions, to diligently cultivate right practice for the sake of benefit, this too is named partial repayment of kindness, since what one hopes for is not yet fulfilled.

Chinese Source

又由第二，謂若有能即依如是差別句義為利益故，勤修正行，如是亦名隨分報恩，彼所希望未滿足故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through a second condition: if there is one able, in reliance on the meaning of these very distinctions, to diligently cultivate right practice for the sake of benefit, this too is named partial repayment of kindness, since what one hopes for is not yet fulfilled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 20) and on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-fourth sūtra

A practitioner still diligently cultivating toward liberation, whether for their own sake or for the sake of all beings, but not yet established in the pure life, has not yet fulfilled the hope of cutting off self-view — yet this sincere effort is itself already, by its very nature, an offering to the great teacher, and so is called partial repayment. The Notes cite the Cultivation-Perfected Ground: a teacher who rightly practices the dharma in accordance with the dharma, manifesting complete awakening only to establish the true teaching, thereby shows others how to practice rightly in turn.

The venerable Pūrṇa Maitrāyaṇīputra, foremost in teaching the dharma, once told Ānanda at Kauśāmbī's Ghoṣila Park: beings cling to arisen dharmas as self, not to the unarisen — no one clings to unconditioned nirvāṇa as self, but the moment-to-moment continuity of the arisen body is clung to as self, and likewise for feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Using the same mirror and clear-water similes just explained, Pūrṇa showed Ānanda how the discriminated and innate self-views arise. Questioned in turn, Ānanda confirmed the aggregates as impermanent, hence suffering — the truest, most ultimate suffering, known only to the noble — hence not self, not other than self, not mutually contained; observing each aggregate's exhaustive nature across past, future, present, inner, outer, coarse, fine, superior, inferior, far, and near, and its shared nature, leads to weariness, freedom from desire, and liberation, up through all four fruits. Ānanda declared that Pūrṇa's teaching this very method was what had first brought him to the dharma-eye and the first fruit, and that he himself had transmitted this same method ever since — his own true repayment of kindness, a method found nowhere outside the Buddha's own teaching. With this, the view of self distinguished, corresponding to sūtra 44, is complete.

45. Section: Threefold Mark and Formation Begins — Three Marks of the Cessation of Formations Named

Second, through the cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature. Third, through the permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions.

Chinese Source

復次，由三種相諸行滅故，說名無餘依涅槃界。一者、先所生起諸行滅故；二者、自性滅壞諸行滅故；三者、一切煩惱永離繫故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through three marks the formations cease, and this is named the realm of nirvāṇa without residue. First, through the cessation of formations previously arisen. Second, through the cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature. Third, through the permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Threefold mark and formation explains the verse's fourth line, distinguishing nirvāṇa with residue — where the aggregated body remains but affliction is already gone — from nirvāṇa without residue, where the aggregates themselves are entirely extinguished, through these three marks.

46. Section: The First Mark — Formations Previously Arisen Cease

The cessation of formations previously arisen means that the aggregates giving rise to future existence, produced by karma and affliction from a former life, and sought through a former vow, permanently cease.

Chinese Source

先所生起諸行滅者，謂於先世能感後有諸業煩惱之所造作，及由先願之所思求，令所生起諸行永滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

The cessation of formations previously arisen means that the aggregates giving rise to future existence, produced by karma and affliction from a former life, and sought through a former vow, permanently cease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This present aggregated body was produced by past karma and affliction and by a past craving for further existence; its permanent cessation, at the exhaustion of this life's span, is the first mark.

47. Section: The Second and Third Marks — Self-Ruin and the Permanent Release From Affliction

The cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature means that, having arisen, they perish of their own accord, never abiding to their ultimate extent, and the formations permanently cease. The permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions means that the afflictions are cut off entirely, without residue, and, through their present cessation, do not arise again hereafter.

Chinese Source

自性滅壞諸行滅者，謂彼生已任性滅壞，非究竟住諸行永滅。一切煩惱永離繫者，謂諸煩惱無餘斷滅，由今滅故，後不更生。

Ru-Yi Translation

The cessation of formations that perish of their own inherent nature means that, having arisen, they perish of their own accord, never abiding to their ultimate extent, and the formations permanently cease. The permanent release from the bondage of all afflictions means that the afflictions are cut off entirely, without residue, and, through their present cessation, do not arise again hereafter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Not one moment of the aggregates truly abides — arisen, they ruin themselves of their own accord; an ordinary being's afflictions, uncut, water the seeds of language and karma into a further life, so the aggregates never cease for good, only an arhat truly permanently ceasing them. And since every affliction is cut off root and branch, no further cause remains to draw a future life's aggregates into being — with these three marks together, the formations cease entirely, and this is nirvāṇa without residue.

48. Section: Concluding the Name — Nirvāṇa Is Not Utter Non-Existence, and the Refutation of Four Wrong Positions

Therefore, through these three marks of the cessation of formations, it is named quiescence — not that it is utterly without mark, but that its mark is different. If it were utterly without mark, it could not even be posited as being named quiescence.

Chinese Source

是故由此三相諸行滅故，說名寂滅。非永無相，其相異故。若永無相，不可施設說名寂滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore, through these three marks of the cessation of formations, it is named quiescence — not that it is utterly without mark, but that its mark is different. If it were utterly without mark, it could not even be posited as being named quiescence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi's own Investigation (fascicle 65)

Nirvāṇa without residue must not be clung to as utter nothingness — quiescence is inconceivable, an absolute beyond the relative, neither rightly called existent nor non-existent. The Investigation into the Ground of Wisdom Born of Reflection rejects all four possible positions as unreasonable to ask, answer, or think: that nirvāṇa is different from conditioned dharmas — impossible, since it is only what conditioned dharmas' own cessation reveals, with no nirvāṇa apart from them; that it is not different — equally impossible, since defilement and purity, the impermanent and the permanent, are simply not the same; that it is both different and not different — mere idle speculation, since nirvāṇa is realized, not spoken of; and that it is neither different nor not different — itself a form of annihilationism, equally unreasonable. Nirvāṇa's true mark is only what every wholesome dharma — precept, concentration, wisdom — reveals: quiescent, beyond all speculation, realized only within, a matter where words and mental grasping both give out, known only by direct realization, never to be settled by declaring it existent or non-existent in advance.

The Investigation further explains conditioned dharmas by five marks (the future not yet arisen, the past already ceased, the present abiding in its own mark, causes continuing in succession, and effects continuing in succession) and unconditioned dharmas by the reverse of these five — since affliction, karma, and suffering are neither identical nor separate, nirvāṇa likewise can be called neither the same as nor different from conditioned dharmas; its mark is known only through direct realization.

49. Section: Background — Sūtra 43, Śāriputra and Ānanda on Cessation, Closing Threefold Mark and Formation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-third sūtra

At Śrāvastī's Jeta Grove, Śāriputra — an arhat — humbly asked the first-fruit Ānanda: what is cessation, and who has it? Ānanda answered that the five aggregates are themselves the fruit of past karma and affliction, born of a past craving for further existence — one should blame no parent for one's birth, only one's own past delight, since "delight gathers, and so form gathers." Formations already arisen are impermanent, ceasing moment by moment; present formations likewise inevitably perish of their own nature; and once the future arising-and-ceasing dharma itself ceases — because affliction is permanently cut off — this alone is truly named cessation, nirvāṇa without residue: the sūtra's "impermanent" corresponds to the treatise's first mark, its "ceasing dharma" to the second, and "that dharma having ceased, this is named cessation" to the third. Śāriputra then repeated Ānanda's own answer back to him, adding: were the five aggregates not produced by past karma and affliction, nor drawn forth by craving for further existence, how could they ever cease at all? — precisely, "with cause and condition the world arises, with cause and condition the world ceases." The two rejoiced together, each confirming the other's understanding. With this, threefold mark and formation, corresponding to sūtra 43, is complete.

復次，由三解脫門增上力故，當知建立四種法嗢柁南。謂空解脫門、無願解脫門、無相解脫門。

51. Section: The Wishlessness Gate — Impermanence and Suffering; the Emptiness Gate — No-Self

All formations are impermanent, and all formations are suffering — these, relying on the gate of wishlessness, establish the first and second dharma-summary verses. All dharmas are without self — this, relying on the gate of emptiness, establishes the third dharma-summary verse.

Chinese Source

一切行無常、一切行苦者，依無願解脫門，建立第一、第二法嗢柁南。一切法無我者，依空解脫門，建立第三法嗢柁南。

Ru-Yi Translation

All formations are impermanent, and all formations are suffering — these, relying on the gate of wishlessness, establish the first and second dharma-summary verses. All dharmas are without self — this, relying on the gate of emptiness, establishes the third dharma-summary verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Seeing the aggregates' exhaustive and shared nature as impermanent and hence suffering brings weariness and, at last, freedom from any wish for further existence in the three realms — this is wishlessness, an arhat's own case. Seeing the aggregates, past, present, future, inner, outer, coarse, fine, superior, inferior, far, and near, as neither self nor belonging to self, no fixed ruling self ever to be found in or apart from them, is the gate of emptiness.

52. Section: The Signlessness Gate — Nirvāṇa's Quiescence

Nirvāṇa's quiescence — this, relying on the gate of signlessness, establishes the fourth dharma-summary verse.

Chinese Source

涅槃寂靜者，依無相解脫門，建立第四法嗢柁南。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nirvāṇa's quiescence — this, relying on the gate of signlessness, establishes the fourth dharma-summary verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 50's fourfold quiescence in both kinds of nirvāṇa

Having seen the aggregates as empty and without self or what-belongs-to-self, one comes to see that what the self depended on — the aggregates themselves — is equally ungraspable, and thus enters signlessness; realizing all signs as ungraspable is itself liberation. Nirvāṇa is called quiescent precisely because it carries no mark of the aggregates or the six sense-faculties at all. Fascicle 50 further distinguishes four kinds of quiescence within nirvāṇa with residue (of suffering, of affliction, of not harming beings, and of equanimity) and four within nirvāṇa without residue. Altogether: the gate of wishlessness establishes impermanence and suffering; the gate of emptiness establishes no-self; the gate of signlessness establishes nirvāṇa's quiescence — the four dharma seals in full.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The three matters spoken of are, first, the grasped; second, the grasper; third, the grasping itself. What is grasped here? The five aggregates subject to grasping. What grasps? Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes (fascicle 54) and the Compendium for Elucidation

Self-view is never a single, indivisible thing but always this threefold operation together. The grasped object is simply the five aggregates. The grasper is the four kinds of grasping: grasping through desire (chiefly a householder's clinging to bodies, one's own and another's); grasping through view (wrong views such as eternalism, annihilationism, and the sixty-two views, chiefly among outsider renunciants); grasping through rules and vows (an outsider's mistaken belief that certain observances alone bring liberation); and grasping through the assertion of self (self-clinging built into the very word "I," common to renunciant and householder alike). As for how grasping occurs: consciousness abides in and clings to form, feeling, perception, and formation — the four abodes of consciousness — taking each as its object; form thus becomes the basis of the grasped mind through being clung to and taken as an object, while the coarse residue this clinging leaves behind becomes the basis of the grasper mind, and the same holds for feeling, perception, and formation. Fascicle 89 offers a related but distinct account, worth reading alongside this one.

64. Section: Background — Sūtra 45, the Elder Chanda and Ānanda on Fear of No-Self, Closing "The Dharma Summarized" and Sūtras 36-45

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, on the Saṃyukta Āgama's forty-fifth sūtra

Shortly after the Buddha's passing, at the Deer Park in Vārāṇasī, the elder Chanda went from elder to elder seeking teaching on the dharma's true reality. Each told him the same three seals — form and the other aggregates are impermanent, all formations impermanent, all dharmas without self, nirvāṇa quiescent, corresponding respectively to the gates of wishlessness, emptiness, and signlessness. Chanda found he already knew this intellectually, yet whenever he actually contemplated all formations' impermanence, all dharmas' no-self, and the utter cessation of craving in nirvāṇa, his mind grew confused and afraid, unable to settle or accept it, wondering: if this is so, where am I, and what supports my continued existence? Unsatisfied, he thought of Ānanda, foremost in learning, and traveled to Kauśāmbī to find him — the "empowerment of the person."

Ānanda praised Chanda's honesty in admitting his fear rather than pretending understanding, and, seeing him now ready through wholesome desire's understanding-mind and pliant-mind, taught him what he himself had heard from the Buddha regarding Mahākātyāyana: ordinary people fall to one of two extremes, clinging to real existence or real non-existence; but one who neither clings to nor grasps at the aggregates as self, who simply knows suffering as it arises and ceases without doubt, sees rightly. Seeing the world's arising as it truly is, dependently originated through affliction, karma, and suffering, one does not fall into the extreme of non-existence; seeing its cessation likewise as dependently arisen, one does not fall into the extreme of existence. The Tathāgata, apart from both extremes, teaches the middle: this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises — thus with ignorance formations arise, on through birth, aging, sickness, death, and sorrow; this not existing, that does not exist; this ceasing, that ceases — thus with ignorance's cessation, formations cease, and so on to the cessation of the whole mass of suffering. Understanding dependent origination this way, one falls into neither extreme and walks the middle path — the "empowerment of the dharma," resolving exactly Chanda's own oscillation between eternalism and annihilationism, and his own "empowerment of self" in genuinely grasping it.

Chanda thereby gained the pure dharma-eye and the stream-enterer's benefits — seeing the dharma, gaining the dharma, knowing the dharma, arousing the dharma (the fourth benefit, elsewhere called thorough firmness), transcending doubt about himself and about others, needing no other's aid, and fearlessness before the teacher's own doctrine — eight of the ten benefits already explained above. Chanda declared he would never again retreat from this understanding, holding no further self or what-belongs-to-self, seeing only the true dharma; Ānanda confirmed his attainment, and the two parted in mutual joy. With this, the dharma summarized, corresponding to sūtra 45, is complete — and with it, perception and formation's own particular verse, spanning sūtras 36 through 45, is entirely closed. A closing verse gathers all ten: "Śroṇa's three" (36-38), "ignorance also three" (39-41), "uninterrupted realization" (42), "cessation" (43), "Pūrṇa" (44), and "Chanda" (45).

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Swift penetrating wisdom means the wisdom that swiftly and permanently exhausts every contamination — not a supernormal power, but wisdom into reality itself. The power of wisdom is right view, first conforming to liberation and then to decisive discernment, cultivating calm and insight upon the aggregates' exhaustive and shared nature until the truths are directly realized. The power of non-laxity is diligence, guarding the mind against greed, hatred, and delusion. The power of habituation is patient, unhurried repetition, never giving up before realization, however long it takes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Wisdom" (智) generally names understanding of past and future, "vision" (見) understanding of the present; the nine distinctions between them follow below. The power of non-laxity is simply continuing to guard the mind against the five hindrances once uncontaminated wisdom is gained. The power of habituation is patient, results-blind practice — like a seed sown in the ground: checking daily for a sprout and despairing when none appears only breeds the very weariness that causes one to quit, whereas a digger who keeps digging the same well, without giving up, eventually strikes water; digging halfheartedly in many places never does.
