Fascicle 84 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十四 · The Compendium of Synonyms Concludes

Desire's Eight Dangers, and the Unwholesome Portion

Fascicle 84 completes the Compendium of Synonyms (攝異門分), the treatise's fourth of five major divisions. Where fascicle 83 closed the wholesome portion's first three verses, this fascicle completes the fourth and final wholesome verse — desire's three kinds (opening with the Buddha's eight similes for its danger, spoken to the householder Poṭaliya: a dry bone, a piece of meat, a grass torch, a pit of coals, a venomous snake, a dream, borrowed finery, and rotten fruit at a treetop's end), invitation, the dharma, the saṅgha, the Buddha's own excellent qualities, weariness, the brahmin, impermanence, and the five aggregates compared to foam, a bubble, a mirage, a banana tree, and an illusion. It then unfolds the unwholesome portion's own single verse in full — birth, aging, and death; the many facets of clinging; what is welcomed; affliction's own twenty-four synonyms; greed, hatred, and delusion explained at exhaustive length; and scarcity and synonyms as this closing topic's final two entries — bringing the entire Compendium of Synonyms, spanning fascicles 83 and 84, to its close.

1. Section: The Compendium of Synonyms' Lower Half Opens

The Compendium of Synonyms, part two.

Chinese Source

攝異門分之下

Ru-Yi Translation

The Compendium of Synonyms, part two.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Compendium of Synonyms has an upper half, in fascicle 83, and a lower half, here in fascicle 84. The upper half contained one overall summary verse and, of the wholesome portion's four particular verses, the first three — the first of which itself further unfolded two middle-length verses on discipline and the noble path. This lower half continues the wholesome portion with its fourth and final particular verse, then adds the unwholesome portion's own single verse. The wholesome portion (白品) covers wholesome dharmas; the unwholesome portion (黑品) covers unwholesome ones.

2. Section: The Fourth Verse — Desire, Invitation, Dharma, Saṅgha, the Qualities That Give, Weariness, the Brahmin, Impermanence, and Foam

Further, the summary verse says: desire, of three kinds; invitation; the dharma; the saṅgha; the excellent qualities that give; weariness; the brahmin; impermanence; things like foam, coming last.

Chinese Source

復次，嗢柁南曰：欲三種延請　法僧惠施故　厭梵志無常　聚沫等為後

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the summary verse says: desire, of three kinds; invitation; the dharma; the saṅgha; the excellent qualities that give; weariness; the brahmin; impermanence; things like foam, coming last.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This fourth wholesome verse previews nine topics: desire's three kinds; invitation of a teacher; the dharma; the saṅgha; excellent qualities — here explained not as literal giving, but as the Buddha's own qualities, since it is by virtue of those qualities that the dharma itself is given to beings for their benefit; weariness; the brahmin; impermanence; and things like foam, appearing last.

3. Section: Desire's Three Kinds Begin — Desire as Inverted, and What It Rests On

That all desires are impermanent, false, and unreal means desire is proclaimed as inverted, since desire is the basis of four kinds of inverted view.

Chinese Source

諸欲無常虛偽不實者，謂於諸欲宣說顛倒，以是四種顛倒事故。

Ru-Yi Translation

That all desires are impermanent, false, and unreal means desire is proclaimed as inverted, since desire is the basis of four kinds of inverted view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the sub-commentary

The verse's first topic, desire's three kinds, unfolds across many sections — first the three marks of inverted, illusory, and deceiving of fools, then eight similes for its danger, then its unclean names. The Buddha regularly taught that desire is impermanent, false, and unreal, and therefore suffering: once a being clings to form, sound, scent, taste, touch, or the desire between the sexes, the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity all arise from that very clinging — form and the rest are exactly the basis on which these four inversions rest.

4. Section: Explaining the Inversion Itself — Empty, Deceptive, Painful, and Falsely Pleasurable — Closing "Desire as Inverted"

It should be known that here, empty means without self; deceptive means impure; unreal means suffering, since what is found there is falsely pleasurable, not truly so.

Chinese Source

當知此中，虛故無我，偽故不淨，不實故苦，由於是處樂非實故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that here, empty means without self; deceptive means impure; unreal means suffering, since what is found there is falsely pleasurable, not truly so.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever is loved and clung to — one's own body and mind, or any further object held and received — is empty of any true master, hence without self; it is deceptive, since ordinary beings mistake what is genuinely impure for pure, unaware of the affliction concealed within; and being unreal, arising from causes and conditions, it is ultimately suffering, its pleasure only apparent, never genuine. With this, desire's mark as inverted is closed. The treatise turns next to desire as a false dharma.

5. Section: Desire Named a False Dharma — the Simile of the Illusionist

Yet those desires appear as if permanent and the rest, and are therefore called a false dharma, being the basis of inversion. Why are desires called a false dharma? To reveal this meaning, the simile of an illusion is spoken. Though not truly permanent and the rest, they appear to be so, and are therefore alike to that illusion.

Chinese Source

然彼諸欲似常等現，說名妄法，顛倒事故。云何諸欲名為妄法？為顯此義，說幻事喻。雖非常等，然似顯現，故同彼法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet those desires appear as if permanent and the rest, and are therefore called a false dharma, being the basis of inversion. Why are desires called a false dharma? To reveal this meaning, the simile of an illusion is spoken. Though not truly permanent and the rest, they appear to be so, and are therefore alike to that illusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and the desire between the sexes appear under the guise of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity, and so are called a false dharma, giving rise to the four inversions. The Buddha often compared desire to an illusionist's conjured beauty — real-seeming at the moment it is seen, yet wholly unreal — since desire's own nature is never truly permanent, pleasurable, selfhood, or pure, only appearing to be so before the deluded mind, exactly like every other deceiving, inverting dharma in the world.

6. Section: Deceiving Fools, Never the Wise — Closing Desire's Three Marks

Deceiving fools means the unlearned fool does not truly know those desires, and is therefore forever deceived through the long night, deeply attached, touched and driven by the suffering of change; the wise are not so, since they truly know.

Chinese Source

誑惑愚夫者，謂無聞愚夫，於彼諸欲不如實知，故於長夜恆被欺誑，深生染著，為變壞苦之所逼觸；諸聰慧者則不如是，如實知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Deceiving fools means the unlearned fool does not truly know those desires, and is therefore forever deceived through the long night, deeply attached, touched and driven by the suffering of change; the wise are not so, since they truly know.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Desire, like an illusionist's conjuration, deceives only fools, never sages: one who has neither heard the dharma nor practiced it cannot truly know desire, and so is endlessly deceived through birth-and-death's long night, taking deep delight and clinging in it — and since desire is itself subject to change, that very clinging is pressed and driven by the suffering of its own eventual ruin. The wise, having accumulated the wisdom born of hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, know desire's impurity directly, and so are never so deceived; the Buddha further taught that among every kind of desire, the desire between the sexes is the most severe of all. A companion passage adds: whether of this life or the next, whether present or future, form and every desire alike are Māra's own domain, bait that lures beings back into birth-and-death, giving rise to countless unwholesome states, discursive thought, hatred, and even open strife. With this, the three marks of desire — inverted, a false dharma, and deceiving of fools — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to eight similes for desire's danger, beginning with a dry bone.

7. Section: Eight Similes Begin — Desire Compared to a Dry Bone

And those desires, compared to a dry bone, mean there is never satisfaction.

Chinese Source

又彼諸欲，喻枯骨者，令無飽故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And those desires, compared to a dry bone, mean there is never satisfaction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Basic Section and a companion sūtra

The Basic Section's Stage of Thought-Constructed Wisdom already compared a being's clinging to desire to a stray dog gnawing a dry bone — never satisfied, only ever more entangled the more it gnaws. The Madhyama Āgama's own version, spoken to the householder Poṭaliya, describes a starving dog waiting at a butcher's, gnawing the discarded bones it is thrown until its lips split and its throat is torn, never once relieved of its hunger — exactly how a being pursuing desire can never reach satisfaction, however much is gained.

8. Section: Desire Compared to a Piece of Meat — Fought Over by Many

Compared to a piece of meat means being shared by many.

Chinese Source

喻段肉者，多所共故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a piece of meat means being shared by many.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Whatever appears attractive — form, sound, scent, taste, touch — is coveted by most beings alike; whatever one being desires, another is apt to desire as well, and conflict follows. The companion sūtra tells of a scrap of meat dropped in an open field: the first bird to seize it is immediately set upon by every other bird competing for the same prize, exactly as desire, more pain than pleasure and fraught with danger, is fought over by all who see it.

9. Section: Desire Compared to a Grass Torch — the Cause of Unlawful and Evil Conduct

Compared to a grass torch means being the cause of unlawful conduct and evil conduct.

Chinese Source

喻草炬者，是非法行、惡行因故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a grass torch means being the cause of unlawful conduct and evil conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

One who clings to desire will commit no end of unlawful, evil karma seeking to satisfy it — history records kings who destroyed rival nations, even ruining their own, all for desire's sake. The companion sūtra likens desire to carrying a lit torch against the wind: unless it is quickly cast aside, it burns the hand and limbs at once — desire, too, brings far more pain than pleasure to whoever refuses to let it go.

10. Section: Desire Compared to a Pit of Coals — Increasing the Great Torment of Craving

Compared to a portion of coals means increasing the great torment of craving and desire.

Chinese Source

喻一分炭者，增長欲愛大熱惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a portion of coals means increasing the great torment of craving and desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Scripture compares desire to a smokeless pit of blazing coals — the hottest fire gives off no smoke, and touching it can take a life. One who falls into the fire-pit of craving and desire certainly suffers extreme pain, the torment of craving only increasing until it may cost life itself; the companion sūtra has the Buddha ask the householder directly whether any sane, undeluded person, loving ease and hating pain, loving life and hating death, would willingly leap into such a pit — the householder answers no, precisely the point: no wise person embraces desire once its true danger is seen.

11. Section: Desire Compared to a Venomous Snake — Shunned by Every Sage

Compared to a great venomous snake means being shunned by every sage and worthy one.

Chinese Source

喻大毒蛇者，為諸聖賢所遠離故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a great venomous snake means being shunned by every sage and worthy one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Scripture describes the great venomous snake as utterly vicious, black, and fearsome in appearance — every sage and worthy one, being free of desire, takes no pleasure in its domain. The companion sūtra asks whether any sane person would offer a hand to such a snake and invite its bite; the householder again answers no, since anyone who sees the snake clearly moves at once to escape it — exactly as the wise, once desire's danger is truly seen, move to escape it as well.

12. Section: Desire Compared to What Is Gained in a Dream — Vanishing the Instant One Wakes

Compared to what is gained in a dream means it quickly scatters and falls apart.

Chinese Source

喻夢所得者，速散壞故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to what is gained in a dream means it quickly scatters and falls apart.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Dreaming of a hoard of gold, one wakes to find not a single coin remains; the five desires seem, in the dream, capable of satisfying everything, yet nothing at all can actually be enjoyed once one wakes. Desire, too, scatters and falls apart just as quickly — the companion sūtra has the Buddha teach exactly this to the householder, that desire is like a dream, more pain than pleasure, fraught with danger, and to be abandoned.

13. Section: Desire Compared to Borrowed Finery — Resting on Manifold Conditions, Always Returned

Compared to borrowed finery means it rests on manifold conditions.

Chinese Source

喻所假借莊嚴具者，託眾緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to borrowed finery means it rests on manifold conditions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Sūtra of Past and Present Cause and Effect

A person of modest means, wishing to hold a fine banquet, borrows a house, jewelry, plates and cups from others to furnish it — and once the banquet ends, every borrowed item must be returned. Desire is exactly this kind of borrowed finery, a phenomenon resting on manifold conditions, unable to last, bound to be given back: "borrowed things must be returned; nor can they last — of a hundred years' life, sleep consumes half, and much of what remains is anxious worry, its pleasure very little."

14. Section: Desire Compared to Rotten Fruit at a Treetop's End — a Perilous, Deadly Place — Closing All Eight Similes

Compared to rotten fruit at a treetop's end means being a place of peril and death.

Chinese Source

喻諸樹端爛熟果者，危亡地故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to rotten fruit at a treetop's end means being a place of peril and death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Poṭaliya sūtra directly

A hungry traveler comes upon a fruit tree heavy with ripe fruit, but none has fallen; wanting to eat, he climbs the tree. A second hungry traveler arrives, unable to climb, and instead takes an axe to fell the whole tree — leaving the first traveler, still aloft, in mortal danger of a broken arm or worse when the tree comes down. Ripe fruit at a treetop's end is exactly such a perilous, deadly place — whoever loves and clings to desire invites exactly this kind of danger from others. With this, all eight similes for desire's danger — the dry bone, the piece of meat, the grass torch, the pit of coals, the venomous snake, the dream, borrowed finery, and rotten fruit at a treetop's end — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to desire's unclean names.

15. Section: Desire's Unclean Names — Overall and in Detail — Closing "Desire, of Three Kinds" Entirely

And uncleanliness is its overall statement. Foul stench means what is consumed as food, once spoiled, becomes this; urine and feces, spoiled and unclean, are formed this way, hence called the foul place; flesh and blood and the rest, also spoiled, are formed this way, hence called raw stench. What should be shunned with revulsion means what is formed through the practice of sexual desire, spoiled, and therefore rightly abhorred and shunned.

Chinese Source

又不淨者，是其總句。

言臭穢者，受用飲食變壞成故。屎尿不淨變壞所成，故名臭處。諸肉血等變壞所成，故名生臭。可厭逆者，受用婬欲變壞所成，可惡逆故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And uncleanliness is its overall statement.

Foul stench means what is consumed as food, once spoiled, becomes this; urine and feces, spoiled and unclean, are formed this way, hence called the foul place; flesh and blood and the rest, also spoiled, are formed this way, hence called raw stench. What should be shunned with revulsion means what is formed through the practice of sexual desire, spoiled, and therefore rightly abhorred and shunned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A body born through sexual desire is by nature unclean from the start, and must further be sustained by food, which spoils within the body — hence foul stench in general; its spoiled residue becomes urine and feces, the foul place; and its spoiled nourishment becomes flesh and blood, raw stench. Since a desire-realm body is itself formed through the practice of sexual desire spoiling and changing, it should be shunned with revulsion rather than clung to — for clinging to it only guarantees a further such body to come. Foul stench, the foul place, and raw stench all trace back to sexual desire in the end; without it, there would be no need to consume food, and no such unclean resultant body at all — which is exactly why cultivating the contemplation of uncleanliness on this very desire-realm body can free a practitioner from attachment to physical form. With this, desire's three kinds — its marks, its eight similes, and its unclean names — are closed in their entirety, completing the fourth verse's first topic. The treatise turns next to invitation, the second topic.

16. Section: Invitation Begins — Inviting to Give Up Worldly Wealth, Receiving to Exhaust Craving

Further, to properly invite means with reference to giving up worldly wealth. To properly receive means with reference to exhausting craving. Wishing to seek a resultant reward, one therefore invites; wishing to seek liberation, one therefore receives.

Chinese Source

復次，應招延者，約捨世財。應奉請者，約盡貪愛。

欲求果報，是故招延。欲求解脫，是故奉請。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, to properly invite means with reference to giving up worldly wealth. To properly receive means with reference to exhausting craving.

Wishing to seek a resultant reward, one therefore invites; wishing to seek liberation, one therefore receives.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's second topic, invitation, unfolds across three sections. To invite means giving up one's worldly wealth to make offerings to the Buddha and the noble disciples, seeking a favorable future reward; to receive means welcoming and requesting their teaching on how to cut off craving, seeking liberation itself — one act aimed at reward, the other at freedom.

17. Section: Proper Reverence — Joined Palms, and Harmonious Respect

Proper joining of palms means, in inviting for the sake of these two matters. Proper harmonious respect means one should offer worship and inquiry, and should share the same discipline and view as they do.

Chinese Source

應合掌者，即為二事而延請時。應和敬者，應設禮拜問訊等故，應可與彼戒見同故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Proper joining of palms means, in inviting for the sake of these two matters. Proper harmonious respect means one should offer worship and inquiry, and should share the same discipline and view as they do.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When inviting the Buddha and the noble disciples for the sake of reward and liberation alike, one should join the palms in reverence, bow, and inquire after their well-being — and, further, emulate their virtue by keeping pure discipline and cultivating right view oneself, matching their own qualities as closely as one can.

18. Section: The Unsurpassed Field of Merit the World Should Serve — Closing Invitation Entirely

The unsurpassed field of merit the world should serve and give to means the resultant reward of giving to them is immeasurable.

Chinese Source

無上福田世應奉施者，於彼惠施果無量故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The unsurpassed field of merit the world should serve and give to means the resultant reward of giving to them is immeasurable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

The Buddha and the noble disciples are the world's unsurpassed field of merit, the very object worldly people should serve and give to, since giving to them yields an immeasurable, boundless reward. A companion sūtra describes a monk who, seeing form with the eye, hearing sound with the ear, and so through the remaining senses, dwells always in supreme equanimity, neither pained nor pleased, mindful and clearly comprehending — one who accomplishes these six constant practices, as the venerable Śāriputra and the other great elders did, is rare in the world, worthy of the world's service, reverence, and offering, the world's own unsurpassed field of merit. With this, invitation, the fourth verse's second topic, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the dharma, the third topic.

19. Section: The Dharma Begins — Skillfully Taught, and Directly Seen

Further, well-spoken means the text and its meaning are skillful and wondrous. Directly seen means it can be realized within the very dharma now present.

Chinese Source

復次，善說者，文義巧妙故。現見者，於現法中可證得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, well-spoken means the text and its meaning are skillful and wondrous. Directly seen means it can be realized within the very dharma now present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's third topic, the dharma — explaining the third word of "the dharma, the saṅgha, and the excellent qualities that give" — unfolds first through the teaching-dharma (教法), then the realization-dharma (證法). The Buddha's teaching is good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, its text and meaning alike skillful and subtle beyond compare — well-spoken. And a practitioner who follows that teaching can, within the very aggregates present right now, truly know how affliction arises through the six faculties meeting the six objects, and can, in this very life, extinguish that affliction's blaze and realize stillness directly — directly seen.

20. Section: The Realization-Dharma Begins — Cessation's Synonyms — Free of Feverish Heat

Free of feverish heat means being free of affliction.

Chinese Source

無熱者，離煩惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of feverish heat means being free of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained the teaching-dharma, the treatise turns to the realization-dharma, opening with six synonyms for the truth of cessation. A practitioner who, facing form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and mental objects, grows free of greed, hatred, and delusion attains a mind that is still, without affliction's own blazing heat — hence free of feverish heat.

21. Section: Free of Time — Transcending the Three Times

Free of time means transcending the three times.

Chinese Source

無時者，出三世故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of time means transcending the three times.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner who realizes the unconditioned dharma thereby transcends every conditioned dharma of past, present, and future alike — hence also called that which does not wait upon time.

22. Section: Hard to Draw Onward, and Hard to See

Hard to draw onward means aging, sickness, death, and the rest cannot draw one onward further. Hard to see means it cannot be seen among the heavenly and other destinies.

Chinese Source

難引者，老病死等不能引故。

難見者，天等趣中不可見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hard to draw onward means aging, sickness, death, and the rest cannot draw one onward further.

Hard to see means it cannot be seen among the heavenly and other destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once greed, hatred, and delusion toward form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and mental objects are extinguished, the twelve-linked chain of dependent origination — aging, sickness, death, and the rest — can no longer draw a being onward into further birth-and-death. And cessation itself cannot be witnessed by heavenly beings or beings of any other destiny; only the human realm, where both pleasure and pain are directly tasted, serves as a fit vessel for realizing it.

23. Section: Realized Inwardly by Oneself — Excluding the Ordinary, Naming Only the Noble

Realized inwardly by oneself means what mere faith in another cannot realize. Those with wisdom means the trainee and the one beyond training.

Chinese Source

內自所證者，唯信他等不能證故。諸有智者者，謂學無學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Realized inwardly by oneself means what mere faith in another cannot realize.

Those with wisdom means the trainee and the one beyond training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Merely believing the teaching spoken by the Buddha or another good teacher, without ever cultivating and realizing it oneself, remains stuck at the level of hearing and reflection, never truly touching the unconditioned — excluding the ordinary being from realization. Those genuinely capable of realizing it inwardly are the trainee (the noble ones of the first three fruits) and the one beyond training (the arhat of the fourth fruit); one who has truly realized the dharma has, in that very realization, already seen the Buddha.

24. Section: Dwelling, Island, Refuge, Rescue, Destination — Closing Cessation's Six Synonyms Entirely

Dwelling, island, rescue, refuge, and destination: each later phrase explains the one before it, revealing the meaning of gradual escape.

Chinese Source

為舍、為洲、為救、為歸、為趣者，由後後句釋前前句，顯出離義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dwelling, island, rescue, refuge, and destination: each later phrase explains the one before it, revealing the meaning of gradual escape.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium and a companion sūtra directly

The Compendium explains each phrase through the one that follows it: cessation is called a dwelling because it is the basis of blameless joy — safe to rest in, like a house. It is called an island because it explains "blameless joy," an island being a sandbar that cuts a sea off from its shore, exactly as cessation cuts off the three realms, the ultimate basis of blameless joy. It is called rescue because it explains "cutting off the three realms" — cessation's own virtue can block every great suffering and calamity, thereby cutting a being off from the three realms' birth-and-death. It is called refuge because it explains "blocking every great suffering and calamity" — nirvāṇa's own state is the basis of pure aspiration and skillful means free of false discrimination, and so naturally invites no calamity at all. And it is called the destination a practitioner ultimately turns toward because it explains "the basis free of false aspiration and means" — cessation is the very basis every noble nature turns toward and relies upon, where every sage ultimately arrives. A companion sūtra lists some twenty further names alongside nirvāṇa itself — the unconditioned, hard to see, unmoving, unbending, deathless, undefiled, sheltering, an island, a crossing, a support, a protection, free of drifting, free of blazing, free of burning, flowing forth, cool, subtle and wondrous, secure, without sickness, without anything at all — of which this treatise explains only a select few. With this, cessation's own many synonyms, the first half of the realization-dharma, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the path's own gradual unfolding.

25. Section: The Path's Gradual Unfolding Begins — Naming Right View

And further, being able to truly know the four noble truths is called right view.

Chinese Source

又能了知四聖諦故，名為正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, being able to truly know the four noble truths is called right view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner who cultivates insight through the noble eightfold path taught by the Buddha can, within this very life, enter the four noble truths of suffering, its cessation, and the path, realizing the fruit of stream-entry — seeing the dharma, attaining the dharma, thoroughly penetrating the dharma, standing on ultimately firm ground, transcending every uncertain hope, crossing every doubt without depending on another's word, unshaken by any teaching outside the great teacher's own, fearless with respect to every dharma — this accomplished, undefiled right view is what the path's gradual unfolding names first.

26. Section: The Marks of Gradual Unfolding — Arising, Already Arisen, Now Arising, and Yet to Arise

Arising means it is able to arise at every time. Already arisen means abiding, in a past life, in the stage beyond training. Now arising means, in the present life, either already realized or perfected through cultivation. Yet to arise means either not yet realized, or being diligently cultivated.

Chinese Source

言生起者，於一切時容可生故。已生起者，於過去世住無學位。今生起者，於現在世或已證得，或修圓滿。當生起者，或未證得，或勤修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Arising means it is able to arise at every time. Already arisen means abiding, in a past life, in the stage beyond training. Now arising means, in the present life, either already realized or perfected through cultivation. Yet to arise means either not yet realized, or being diligently cultivated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

Undefiled right view, sorted across the three times: whoever cultivates the sixteen aspects of the four truths as the Buddha taught can realize it in any time whatsoever — arising in general. An arhat beyond training who realized it in a past life shows it already arisen; a trainee at the first fruit realizing it just now, or one newly entering the fruition beyond training, shows it now arising; and an ordinary being on the verge of realization, diligently cultivating the four truths' teaching toward a future realization, shows it yet to arise.

27. Section: What Should Be Cultivated, Practiced, and Repeatedly Practiced

What should be cultivated, practiced, and repeatedly practiced should each be understood as before, according to what is fitting.

Chinese Source

應修、應習、應多修習者，隨其所應，如前當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

What should be cultivated, practiced, and repeatedly practiced should each be understood as before, according to what is fitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 83 directly

This refers back to the seven applications of attention already explained in fascicle 83 regarding the perception of impermanence: cultivated corresponds to the application of noting characteristics, or more broadly to simply setting out toward the object to be known; practiced corresponds to the application of resolve, going beyond hearing and reflection into actual meditative resolve on the object, or to sustained, earnest exertion without interruption; and repeatedly practiced corresponds to the applications of withdrawal, gathering joy, and examination, or more broadly to long, thoroughly seasoned practice over time. Since these map onto the seven applications of attention from different angles — sometimes by the attention itself, sometimes by the exertion it produces — the text simply says "according to what is fitting."

28. Section: What Should Be Safeguarded — Warding Off Regression

What should be safeguarded means being far removed from whatever accords with regression and falling back.

Chinese Source

應隨護者，遠離隨順退墮法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What should be safeguarded means being far removed from whatever accords with regression and falling back.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the fourfold right effort

A practitioner must safeguard right view once gained — avoiding bad companions, wrong teachings, and improper attention, lest it regress or be lost. This corresponds to the third of the four right efforts, safeguarding-cutting: diligently upholding a still-unarisen wholesome dharma, such as undefiled right view not yet perfected, so that it does not regress or fall away once gained.

29. Section: What Should Be Touched, and Realized — Closing the Path's Gradual Unfolding, and the Entire Realization-Dharma

Being touched means through the body itself. Being realized as a witness means either the fruit or the surpassing wisdom, as is said: I have already realized the path.

Chinese Source

言應觸者，由身體故。應作證者，或果、或勝智，如說：我已證道故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being touched means through the body itself. Being realized as a witness means either the fruit or the surpassing wisdom, as is said: I have already realized the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases and a companion sūtra

Being touched names the six faculties meeting the six objects and thereby truly knowing one's own mind's purity or defilement in that very contact; being realized as a witness names actually attaining the noble fruit through surpassing wisdom, exactly as the Buddha's own words declare: "I have already realized the path." A companion sūtra records the Buddha teaching the monks that, before his own awakening, it was through right reflection that he came to know the noble truth of suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path leading to its cessation, right view thereby arisen — and the Compendium of Bases adds that, once the four truths are seen, undifferentiated undefiled right view arises across all three times identically: called already-arisen with respect to the past, now-arising with respect to the present, yet-to-arise with respect to the future; worldly right view should be safeguarded; a trainee's right view, together with the fruit of what it has cut off, should be touched; and a trainee-beyond-training's right view, together with its own fruit of freedom from bondage, should be realized as a witness — and liberating wisdom follows the very same fourfold pattern. With this, the path's gradual unfolding — right view, its four marks, safeguarding, touching, and witness — is closed in its entirety, completing the realization-dharma, and with it, both halves of the dharma topic's inner content. The treatise turns next to the third subtopic of the dharma: the manner of teaching it well.

30. Section: The Manner of Teaching Well Begins — Speaking at the Right Time, and a Proper Preamble

Speaking at the right time means teaching only once one truly knows the listener is eager to hear and capable of hearing it; sitting on a lower seat and the rest — this is called timeliness. One should properly offer a preamble, what should be done at the outset.

Chinese Source

若了知彼願樂欲聞及堪聞者，方可為說。坐卑座等，是名為時。

應當序說先時所作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Speaking at the right time means teaching only once one truly knows the listener is eager to hear and capable of hearing it; sitting on a lower seat and the rest — this is called timeliness.

One should properly offer a preamble, what should be done at the outset.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage and the Śrāvaka Stage

The dharma's third subtopic, the manner of teaching well, unfolds across ten sections. First, timeliness: a teacher should teach only once the listener genuinely wishes to hear and is capable of receiving it — teaching someone unready only burdens them further, benefiting neither side — and should mind proper demeanor, never sitting on a high seat while an able-bodied listener sits below, nor standing to teach a seated listener, nor teaching from behind someone walking ahead, nor teaching one whose head is covered, as the Bodhisattva Stage explains. And before the deeper teaching, a proper preamble should first draw out the listener's own interest — typically the preliminary teachings of giving, discipline, and rebirth in the heavens, broadly showing desire's own faults and the benefit of renunciation, before the four truths' own ultimate teaching follows, as the Śrāvaka Stage explains.

31. Section: Teaching Earnestly and Solemnly

Once one truly knows the listener seeks to be uplifted, one should teach earnestly and solemnly, giving everything within one's own capacity according to what the listener can receive — wishing to reveal each distinct, never-before-heard meaning, not merely delighting in flowery language for its own sake.

Chinese Source

若了知彼是增上已，即便殷重，隨其所能，盡己所有而為說法。為欲開示彼彼差別未曾有義，非直華詞樂說而已。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once one truly knows the listener seeks to be uplifted, one should teach earnestly and solemnly, giving everything within one's own capacity according to what the listener can receive — wishing to reveal each distinct, never-before-heard meaning, not merely delighting in flowery language for its own sake.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Knowing that a listener has come genuinely wishing to be uplifted, and capable of receiving the teaching, a teacher should hold nothing back, teaching earnestly and with the full measure of their own ability — never so absorbed in ornate phrasing that the teaching's actual substance is lost.

32. Section: In Sequence, Attending to Hidden Meaning, and Drawing Connections

In sequence means revealing the meaning. Attending to hidden meaning means posing objections. Drawing connections means clarifying and explaining them.

Chinese Source

次第者，開示義故。隨密者，設妨難故。隨會者，顯釋彼故。

Ru-Yi Translation

In sequence means revealing the meaning. Attending to hidden meaning means posing objections. Drawing connections means clarifying and explaining them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Teaching in sequence means the content unfolds from shallow to deep, step by step; attending to hidden meaning means drawing out a sūtra's own concealed intent, even posing hypothetical objections and answering them directly, to sharpen the listener's grasp of a difficult point; and drawing connections means showing how one teaching connects with another — the four noble truths with the four foundations of mindfulness, the three dharma seals, or the eightfold path — a teacher fluent enough to weave the whole teaching together.

33. Section: Bringing Joy, Bringing Fondness, and Bringing Delight — for Three Kinds of Listener

Bringing joy means converting one who already accepts the teaching. Bringing fondness means converting one who stands in the middle. Bringing delight means converting one who slanders it.

Chinese Source

令歡喜者，化受教者故。令愛樂者，化處中者故。令喜樂者，化誹謗者故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bringing joy means converting one who already accepts the teaching. Bringing fondness means converting one who stands in the middle. Bringing delight means converting one who slanders it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three kinds of listener call for three distinct approaches: one already confident in the dharma should be taught with skillful, varied means so their joy remains full; one neither confident nor hostile, standing in the middle, should be roused toward genuine enthusiasm and fondness for continued listening; and one who actively slanders the dharma should be brought, through the teaching itself, to such delight that the slander dissolves and they willingly return to hear more.

34. Section: Praising and Encouraging, Reproving and Expelling

Praising and encouraging means seeking out genuine virtue, and, with a mind that matches it, giving voice to praise and commendation. Reproving and expelling means observing genuine fault, and, with a mind free of hatred, giving voice to correction and reproof.

Chinese Source

讚勵者，求彼實德，以稱順心，發自言音揄揚讚美。訶擯者，觀彼實過，以無恚心，發自言音開示訶責。

Ru-Yi Translation

Praising and encouraging means seeking out genuine virtue, and, with a mind that matches it, giving voice to praise and commendation. Reproving and expelling means observing genuine fault, and, with a mind free of hatred, giving voice to correction and reproof.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A listener who has genuinely gained virtue in the dharma should be praised in words that match and encourage that virtue, so their confidence grows and they continue to study; and a listener with genuine fault should be corrected with a mind entirely free of hatred or malice, gently setting the fault right rather than attacking the person.

35. Section: The Four Kinds of Reasoning

Reasoning means possessing four kinds of reasoning: the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of function, the reasoning of intrinsic nature, and the reasoning of proof.

Chinese Source

道理者，具四道理故，謂觀待道理、作用道理、法爾道理、證成道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reasoning means possessing four kinds of reasoning: the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of function, the reasoning of intrinsic nature, and the reasoning of proof.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever is taught can be examined through four angles: dependence — this dharma is established only in dependence on that dharma; function — a dharma's own characteristic explained through what it does; intrinsic nature — a dharma's nature simply being what it is, spoken of as it naturally stands; and proof — establishing a teaching through scriptural authority, inference, and direct perception together.

36. Section: Beneficial, Unmixed, and Possessing the Dharma

Beneficial means never abandoning the place one aims to help. Unmixed means being free of disorder, each part properly connected. Possessing the dharma means being able to draw out its meaning, giving rise to boundless kinds of renunciation and detachment resting on suffering and the rest.

Chinese Source

有益者，於所為處不棄捨故。無雜者，無雜亂故，有繫屬故。有法者，能引義故，依於苦等有無量種出離遠離所生法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beneficial means never abandoning the place one aims to help.

Unmixed means being free of disorder, each part properly connected.

Possessing the dharma means being able to draw out its meaning, giving rise to boundless kinds of renunciation and detachment resting on suffering and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beneficial names never abandoning the beings one aims to help, teaching them according to the dharma without fail. Unmixed names two things at once: no disorder, meaning never teaching at the wrong time, and proper connection, meaning the earlier and later meaning taught remains coherent throughout, never contradicting itself. And possessing the dharma names the teaching's own capacity to lead a listener all the way to nirvāṇa's benefit — resting on suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path, it opens boundless paths of renunciation from affliction and its dust, freeing a listener from suffering and its arising, and leading them to realize cessation.

37. Section: Fitting the Assembly

Fitting the assembly means according with what the four kinds of assembly — khattiyas and the rest — can each receive.

Chinese Source

如眾會者，隨剎帝利等四種會眾所堪能故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fitting the assembly means according with what the four kinds of assembly — khattiyas and the rest — can each receive.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A teaching must fit the capacity of whoever gathers to hear it — in ancient India, the four castes of brahmins, khattiyas (warrior-nobles), vaiśyas, and śūdras — pitched so that each of these four different assemblies can genuinely receive and understand it.

38. Section: A Mind of Loving-Kindness, of Benefit, and of Compassion

With a mind of loving-kindness means wishing to bring them happiness. A mind of benefit means: how might one bring it about that those who listen to the true dharma earnestly all attain understanding and gain great benefit? A mind of compassion means wishing to bring them to practice the dharma in accordance with the dharma.

Chinese Source

以慈心者，為欲令彼得樂義故。利益心者，云何當令若有殷重聽聞正法，皆得悟解，獲大利益故。哀愍心者，欲令彼修法隨法行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With a mind of loving-kindness means wishing to bring them happiness. A mind of benefit means: how might one bring it about that those who listen to the true dharma earnestly all attain understanding and gain great benefit? A mind of compassion means wishing to bring them to practice the dharma in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three further attitudes a teacher should hold: loving-kindness, wishing every listener genuine happiness, worldly and beyond; benefit, actively seeking how to bring earnest listeners to real understanding and great gain; and compassion, wishing that listeners will go on to actually practice the dharma they have heard, in accordance with the dharma itself.

39. Section: Without Dependence, Without Contempt — Closing the Manner of Teaching Well, and All Ten of Its Sections

Without dependence means not for the sake of gain, honor, or renown — not relying on robes and the like, nor on receiving worship and reverence, wishing only that the listener awaken to the true dharma. Nor does one hold contempt for another, and so on at length, never exalting oneself for the sake of gain, honor, or such things, saying: "Only I can know this dharma and discipline, not people like you" — and so on at length, praising one's own virtues while speaking of another's faults.

Chinese Source

無所依者，不為利養恭敬名稱故；謂不依止衣服等事，亦不依止禮敬等事，唯欲令他悟入正法。又不於他有所輕懱，乃至廣說不自高者，不為利養恭敬事故，作如是言：唯我能知如是法律，非汝等輩；乃至廣說讚己功德，談彼過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Without dependence means not for the sake of gain, honor, or renown — not relying on robes and the like, nor on receiving worship and reverence, wishing only that the listener awaken to the true dharma. Nor does one hold contempt for another, and so on at length, never exalting oneself for the sake of gain, honor, or such things, saying: "Only I can know this dharma and discipline, not people like you" — and so on at length, praising one's own virtues while speaking of another's faults.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A true teacher does not teach for the sake of material gain, honor, or fame — not for robes, food, medicine, or lodging, and not for the worship it might bring — wishing only that the listener come to awaken in the true dharma. Nor should a teacher ever hold contempt for a listener who fails to understand or asks an off-point question, nor grow arrogant, claiming "only I truly understand this dharma and discipline, people like you never will," nor endlessly praise their own virtues while pointing out others' faults, nor claim that only their own teaching is correct and every other teaching false. With this, all ten sections of the manner of teaching well — timeliness and preamble, earnestness, sequence and hidden meaning, the three kinds of listener, praise and reproof, the four kinds of reasoning, benefit and coherence, fitting the assembly, the threefold mind, and freedom from dependence or contempt — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the manner of listening well.

40. Section: Not Disparaging the Dharma, Not Disparaging the Teacher — Closing Freedom from Contemptuous Defilement

Not disparaging the dharma means not saying: this is not embellished with fine words and phrases, since all its wording is entirely crude and shallow. Not disparaging the teacher means not saying: this person's own lineage is lowlier than mine, and so on.

Chinese Source

不輕法者，不作是言：此非綺飾文字章句，所有文句悉皆麤淺故。

不輕法師者，不作是言：彼於我所種姓卑劣等故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not disparaging the dharma means not saying: this is not embellished with fine words and phrases, since all its wording is entirely crude and shallow.

Not disparaging the teacher means not saying: this person's own lineage is lowlier than mine, and so on.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A listener should never dismiss a teaching merely for its plain wording, as though only elegant phrasing deserved respect; nor should a listener look down on a teacher for a humbler birth or lesser standing than their own — both are forms of the same contemptuous defilement. With this, freedom from contemptuous defilement, revering the teacher, not disparaging the dharma, and not disparaging the teacher together, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to freedom from timid defilement.

41. Section: Not Belittling Oneself — Closing a Mind Free of Defilement Entirely

Not belittling oneself means not saying: I have no capacity to understand the dharma — never timid or lowly toward what can be realized.

Chinese Source

不自輕者，不作是言：我於解法無有力能。於其所證無怯劣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not belittling oneself means not saying: I have no capacity to understand the dharma — never timid or lowly toward what can be realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A listener must equally avoid the opposite fault of self-doubt — never claiming incapacity to understand the dharma, never shrinking timidly before the very highest awakening the Buddha himself realized, as though it could never be one's own to attain. With this, a mind free of defilement — freedom from arrogant defilement, from contemptuous defilement, and from timid defilement — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a mind free of distraction.

42. Section: A Mind Free of Distraction Begins — a Mind That Reveres the Teaching

A mind that reveres the teaching means a mind free of any wish to disturb, seeking only to understand.

Chinese Source

奉教心者，無惱亂心，唯欲求解故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind that reveres the teaching means a mind free of any wish to disturb, seeking only to understand.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A mind free of distraction unfolds across five further sections. First, a listener should hold a mind that reveres the teaching itself, entirely free of any wish to disturb or obstruct the teacher, seeking only genuine understanding.

43. Section: A Mind Set on a Single Direction

A mind set on a single direction means wishing to grasp the distinctions of the text and its phrasing.

Chinese Source

心一趣者，為欲領解文句差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind set on a single direction means wishing to grasp the distinctions of the text and its phrasing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

While listening, the mind should hold a single direction only — wholly absorbed in grasping every distinction in the dharma's own wording, without wandering elsewhere.

44. Section: Ears Given Wholly to Listening

Ears given wholly to listening means wishing to discern the distinctions of tone and cadence.

Chinese Source

屬耳聽者，為欲了知音韻差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ears given wholly to listening means wishing to discern the distinctions of tone and cadence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The ear itself should attend closely enough to catch the teacher's own tone and cadence — the particular pronunciation given to a term, or the rise in the voice when a crucial point is being emphasized — since even these details can carry meaning.

45. Section: A Mind Cultivated and Trained

A mind cultivated and trained means wishing to awaken to the profound meaning.

Chinese Source

修治意者，為欲悟入甚深義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind cultivated and trained means wishing to awaken to the profound meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond merely hearing, a listener should further train the mind itself, working to counter its own afflictions, so that the dharma's most profound meaning can genuinely be entered.

46. Section: A Mind Never Without Mindfulness — Closing a Mind Free of Distraction, and the Manner of Listening Well Entirely — Closing the Entire Dharma Topic

A mind never without mindfulness in anything means, wishing without interruption to grasp the meaning of tone, text, and phrase alike, there is nothing not truly known, nothing not thoroughly penetrated, and no time passed in vain.

Chinese Source

於一切心無不繫念者，為欲無間領解音韻文句義故，無不了知、無不通達而空過者。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind never without mindfulness in anything means, wishing without interruption to grasp the meaning of tone, text, and phrase alike, there is nothing not truly known, nothing not thoroughly penetrated, and no time passed in vain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Finally, mindfulness should hold every dharma ever heard without gap — a listener so genuinely absorbed that understanding arises the instant it is heard, nothing left unknown, nothing left unpenetrated, no moment of listening ever wasted. With this, a mind free of distraction — reverence, single-direction focus, attentive ears, a trained mind, and unbroken mindfulness — is closed in its entirety, completing the manner of listening well. And with it, the dharma topic's full four subtopics — the teaching-dharma, the realization-dharma, the manner of teaching well, and the manner of listening well — are closed in their entirety, completing the fourth verse's third topic. The treatise turns next to the saṅgha, the fourth topic.

47. Section: The Saṅgha Begins — Right Conduct's Overall Statement

Further, so-called right conduct is the overall statement.

Chinese Source

復次，言正行者，謂是總句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, so-called right conduct is the overall statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth verse's fourth topic, the saṅgha — explaining the third word of "the dharma, the saṅgha, and the excellent qualities that give" — unfolds across five sections, opening with right conduct's own many synonyms. Right conduct, stated first as a single overall summary, names the mind and conduct every practitioner should cultivate.

48. Section: In Accord With Reason — the Trainee Abiding in a Fruit

Conduct in accord with reason belongs to the trainee abiding in a fruit.

Chinese Source

應理行者，住果有學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct in accord with reason belongs to the trainee abiding in a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Right conduct's first detailed reading names four terms. A trainee abiding in the first, second, or third fruit, whose every action accords with the true nature of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, is conduct in accord with reason — the right conduct proper to a trainee who already abides in a fruit.

49. Section: Upright and Direct — the Trainee Moving Toward a Fruit

Upright, direct conduct belongs to one abiding on the path toward a fruit.

Chinese Source

質直行者，住於向道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Upright, direct conduct belongs to one abiding on the path toward a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A trainee on the path moving toward a fruit — toward the first, second, third, or fourth fruit — is certain to realize that fruit directly, and so is called upright, direct conduct; the fifteen mind-moments preceding stream-entry's own sixteenth, for instance, all belong to this moving-toward stage, only the sixteenth moment itself actually realizing the fruit.

50. Section: Harmonious Reverence — the One Beyond Training

Harmoniously reverent conduct belongs to the one beyond training, since only that one holds deep reverence for the great teacher, the true dharma, and the training precepts alike.

Chinese Source

和敬行者，是其無學。由彼唯於大師、正法及學處等深恭敬故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Harmoniously reverent conduct belongs to the one beyond training, since only that one holds deep reverence for the great teacher, the true dharma, and the training precepts alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Only the arhat beyond training genuinely fulfills the six kinds of harmonious reverence within a saṅgha: shared view, shared inner joy, shared discipline, shared benefit, dwelling together in bodily harmony, and speech free of dispute — a fullness of harmony only complete realization can sustain.

51. Section: Practice in Accordance With the Dharma — Closing the First Reading of Right Conduct's Four Terms

Conduct that follows the dharma means, while still in the causal stage of turning toward it, practicing in accordance with the dharma — through hearing another's voice, correctly and properly reflecting within.

Chinese Source

隨法行者，於因轉時法隨法行。由聞他音，內正如理而思惟故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct that follows the dharma means, while still in the causal stage of turning toward it, practicing in accordance with the dharma — through hearing another's voice, correctly and properly reflecting within.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Śrāvaka practitioners, not yet having realized a fruit, cultivate the noble eightfold path with nirvāṇa as their aim; Mahāyāna practitioners cultivate the thirty-seven factors of awakening and the six perfections with unsurpassed awakening as their aim — these causal-stage efforts toward realizing nirvāṇa or unsurpassed awakening are together called conduct that follows the dharma. Such practice continues arising in succession because, having heard a good teacher's own voice, the mind remains in accord with the true dharma, correctly and properly reflecting exactly as the Buddha taught — from which the aspiration for unsurpassed awakening arises, and diligent practice that follows the dharma proceeds. With this, right conduct's four terms — conduct in accord with reason, upright and direct conduct, harmoniously reverent conduct, and conduct that follows the dharma — are closed under their first reading. The treatise turns next to a second reading of the same four terms.

52. Section: A Second Reading of the Same Four Terms — Closing Right Conduct's Synonyms Entirely

And further, conduct in accord with reason means the very path and the fruit's extinguishing conduct. Upright, direct conduct means practicing rightly exactly as the noble teaching directs, free of flattery and deceit, showing itself just as it truly is. Harmoniously reverent conduct means being joined together with the six firm dharmas. Conduct that follows the dharma means practicing in accordance with the dharma.

Chinese Source

又應理行者，是其正道及果滅行。質直行者，如其聖教而正修行，無諂無誑，如實顯現。和敬行者，與六堅法而共相應。

隨法行者，法隨法行。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, conduct in accord with reason means the very path and the fruit's extinguishing conduct. Upright, direct conduct means practicing rightly exactly as the noble teaching directs, free of flattery and deceit, showing itself just as it truly is. Harmoniously reverent conduct means being joined together with the six firm dharmas.

Conduct that follows the dharma means practicing in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

A second angle on the same four terms: conduct in accord with reason now names the undefiled eightfold path itself, succeeding in practice, and the power, once a fruit is realized, to extinguish affliction — this is what it truly means to accord with reason, joined to the undefiled noble path. Upright, direct conduct now names practicing exactly as the Buddha's own noble teaching directs, with no flattery or deceit performed for others' eyes, nothing dressed up to win material gain — simply shown just as it truly is. Harmoniously reverent conduct now names accord with the six firm dharmas — the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, fascicle 22, confirm the six firm dharmas are simply the six kinds of harmonious reverence themselves, body, speech, mind, discipline, view, and benefit shared with the community; a saṅgha that sustains this sixfold harmony ensures the Buddha's own teaching endures long in the world. And conduct that follows the dharma admits two further readings here: first, "the dharma" names nirvāṇa itself as the goal, and "following the dharma" names the eightfold path as the cause that leads toward it; second, "the dharma" names the sūtras, vinaya, and treatises, and "following the dharma" names practice carried out in accordance with them. With this, right conduct's own many synonyms, read through both explanations, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the many synonyms for right conduct's own fruit.

Every arhat has permanently exhausted every defilement, and so on, spoken of at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage

An arhat has permanently exhausted every defilement of craving and view alike — the full extended description, already given in fascicle 34's own account of the Śrāvaka Stage, is not repeated here, only referenced. With this, right conduct's attainment — its own synonyms across two readings, and its fruit — is closed in its entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's first section, right conduct. The treatise turns next to the saṅgha's remaining four sections.

And "sons of the great teacher" is the overall statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The saṅgha topic's second of five sections gives five names for genuine sons of the great teacher, the Buddha himself, followed by an account of their twofold perfection.

55. Section: Born of the Womb — Excluding the Ordinary Being's Lowlier Sonship

Born of the womb means excluding the lowlier sonship of the ordinary being.

Chinese Source

腹所生者，簡去異生卑劣子故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of the womb means excluding the lowlier sonship of the ordinary being.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

A genuine child of the Buddha is compared to his own true-born child, excluding the ordinary being and the two lesser vehicles alike from full sonship — a commentarial tradition names three kinds of Buddha-children: the ordinary being an outside child, the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha adopted children, and only the bodhisattva a true-born child, since a bodhisattva alone realizes the noble wisdom born directly, so to speak, from the Buddha's own womb, worthy of carrying the lineage forward.

56. Section: Born of the Mouth — Born From the Very Sound of the Teaching

Born of the mouth means being born from the very sound of the teaching spoken.

Chinese Source

口所生者，從說法音而誕生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of the mouth means being born from the very sound of the teaching spoken.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

A disciple who hears the Tathāgata's own teaching and, following it, cultivates discipline, concentration, and wisdom all the way to the realization of ultimate truth is figuratively born from the Buddha's own mouth — the wisdom born of hearing itself, arising in direct dependence on the teaching heard.

57. Section: Born of the Dharma — the Dharma Itself as the Buddha's Own Mother

Born of the dharma means being born of proper attention and practice in accordance with the dharma.

Chinese Source

法所生者，如理作意、法隨法行之所生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of the dharma means being born of proper attention and practice in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary and the Nirvāṇa Sūtra

A monastic disciple who hears the teaching, reflects on it properly, and then cultivates practice in accordance with the dharma can realize the noble path and its fruit — this is being born of the dharma itself, the wisdom of reflection and cultivation together, arising in dependence on both principle and teaching. Turned around, even the Buddha's own boundless virtues arise from no other source than the true dharma itself — hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own saying, "the dharma is the Buddha's mother; the Buddha is born from the dharma."

58. Section: Transformed by the Dharma — Established Along the Path of the Dharma-Body

Transformed by the dharma means being established along the path of the dharma-body, a dharma resembling it.

Chinese Source

法所化者，從法身路而得成立，相似法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Transformed by the dharma means being established along the path of the dharma-body, a dharma resembling it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

Once forbearance regarding the non-arising of dharmas is realized, subsequently-attained wisdom takes suchness itself, in its purity, as its object — a path by which the true character of all dharmas is progressively realized and the afflictions cut off through cultivation are gradually removed; a practitioner walking this path is likewise transformed by the dharma. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain that a bodhisattva realizes the Buddhas' own dharma-body by following the ancient path of undefiled discipline, concentration, and wisdom — a path that resembles suchness and nirvāṇa's own cessation itself, hence "a dharma resembling it."

59. Section: An Equal Portion of the Dharma — Closing All Five Terms for a True Child of the Buddha

Chinese Source

法等分者，受用無漏法之財寶，相似法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

An equal portion of the dharma means enjoying the wealth of the undefiled dharma, a dharma resembling it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

A monastic disciple enjoys the same undefiled noble wealth the Buddha himself enjoys — faith, discipline, moral shame, moral dread, learning, generosity, and wisdom, the seven noble treasures — differing only in fullness or portion, yet genuinely resembling the Buddha's own undefiled enjoyment, hence an equal portion of the dharma. With this, all five names for a true child of the Buddha — born of the womb, born of the mouth, born of the dharma, transformed by the dharma, and an equal portion of the dharma — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to their twofold perfection.

60. Section: A Twofold Perfection Named — Perfected Higher Rebirth, and Perfected Rebirth Resembling the Father's Own Dharma

These several phrases reveal perfected higher rebirth, and perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Chinese Source

如是諸句，顯示增上生圓滿，及父相似法生圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

These several phrases reveal perfected higher rebirth, and perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The five names just closed reveal two kinds of perfection together: a bodhisattva who practices as the Buddha taught gains a perfected higher rebirth — a life fit to carry the lineage forward, a genuine vessel for the great vehicle, distinct from the ordinary being's outside sonship or the two lesser vehicles' own lowlier sonship — and, in time, a perfected rebirth resembling the father's own undefiled treasures of faith, discipline, moral shame, moral dread, learning, generosity, and wisdom. Higher-rebirth perfection belongs to the basic condition of one's own being; the father-resembling perfection belongs to what is yet to be gained, the condition for future accomplishment — since a bodhisattva, too, can one day reach the very fullness the Buddha himself has already reached, this dharma is called "resembling."

61. Section: The First Three Terms Matched to Higher-Rebirth Perfection

The first phrase, within perfected higher rebirth, excludes the fault of an unfit vessel; the second phrase excludes birth from impure seed and blood; the third phrase excludes birth from desire and craving rather than the true dharma. These three phrases together reveal perfected higher rebirth.

Chinese Source

謂初句，於其增上生圓滿中，遮器過失；第二句，遮其精血不淨所生；第三句，遮其欲貪非正法生。如是三句，顯示增上生圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first phrase, within perfected higher rebirth, excludes the fault of an unfit vessel; the second phrase excludes birth from impure seed and blood; the third phrase excludes birth from desire and craving rather than the true dharma. These three phrases together reveal perfected higher rebirth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of Cultivated Wisdom

"Born of the womb" shows a true child of the Buddha free of the vessel's three faults — leaking, like a cracked cup that cannot hold the water of the dharma; covered, like a sealed cup the water cannot enter, a heart unwilling to receive the teaching; and stained, like a dirty cup that spoils even pure water poured into it, a mind too clouded by affliction to hear the dharma correctly. "Born of the mouth" excludes birth from the impure union of a father's seed and mother's blood, since the noble path a practitioner seeks is instead born from hearing the teaching's own sound. And "born of the dharma" excludes birth from mere desire and craving apart from the true dharma, since the noble path a practitioner realizes always arises through proper attention and practice in accordance with the dharma, unlike an ordinary being's desire born of improper attention. These three phrases together — free of a vessel's faults, free of impure origin, free of improper birth — are exactly what makes a life fit to be an instrument of higher rebirth, capable of practicing and realizing the fruit.

62. Section: The Remaining Two Terms Matched to Perfection Resembling the Father — Closing All the Sons of the Great Teacher Entirely

The fourth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own inherent nature; the fifth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own enjoyment. These two phrases together reveal perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Chinese Source

第四句，顯示自體相似之法；第五句，顯示受用相似之法。如是二句，顯示父相似法生圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own inherent nature; the fifth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own enjoyment. These two phrases together reveal perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Transformed by the dharma" shows that a practitioner, however shallow their present attainment, can, by following the same path step by step, eventually realize a dharma-body resembling the Buddha's own — the concealed meaning of that fourth term. "An equal portion of the dharma" shows that a practitioner who follows the teaching faithfully can likewise come to enjoy an undefiled dharma-wealth resembling the Buddha's own reward-body enjoyment — the fifth term's meaning. Together, these two reveal perfection in a dharma resembling the father's own: since the Buddha, the father, has already realized the dharma-body, a practitioner who follows his teaching can likewise realize a resembling dharma-body — no longer a life composed of a father's and mother's impure seed and blood, but a life whose very body is now the dharma itself. With this, sons of the great teacher — their five names and their twofold perfection alike — are closed in their entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's second of five sections. The treatise turns next to preface and gathering, the third section.

63. Section: Preface and Gathering Begin — Distinguished as Condition and as Cause

And preface names the condition. Gathering names the cause.

Chinese Source

又序者，是緣。集者，是因。

Ru-Yi Translation

And preface names the condition. Gathering names the cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The saṅgha topic's third of five sections distinguishes preface, the supporting condition, from gathering, the actual cause. Matched against the five names for a true child of the Buddha already closed, "cause" corresponds to being born of the womb, while "condition" corresponds to the remaining four names — since a practitioner must first genuinely be a true child, possessing the great vehicle's own lineage, before accumulating the further conditions that bring the Buddha's fruit to completion.

64. Section: Their Further Names Explained — Kind Through the Condition's Power, Born Through the Cause's Power

Through the condition's own increasing power, it is called that kind; through the cause's own increasing power, it is called being born of that.

Chinese Source

緣增上故，名彼種類。因增上故，名彼所生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the condition's own increasing power, it is called that kind; through the cause's own increasing power, it is called being born of that.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As the supporting conditions gather strength through practice, a bodhisattva comes to resemble the Buddha ever more closely — "that kind." And as the underlying cause gains strength, a bodhisattva realizes unsurpassed awakening itself — "being born of that," a great-vehicle monastic being, in the fullest sense, the Buddha's own true-born child.

65. Section: How the Cause and Condition Establish the Fruit — Closing Preface and Gathering Entirely

Though the fruit is born of the cause, it is only by relying on the condition's own power that it comes to arise, since the condition depends upon the cause. And here, the later phrase explains the one before it.

Chinese Source

雖因所生，藉緣勢力方得生起，為彼依故。又於此中後句釋前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though the fruit is born of the cause, it is only by relying on the condition's own power that it comes to arise, since the condition depends upon the cause. And here, the later phrase explains the one before it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Though a fruit is properly born of its cause, realizing the noble path still requires manifold supporting conditions cultivated with effort, layered onto that original cause, before the fruit is actually reached — cause remains fundamental, condition secondary; a two-vehicle practitioner's own lesser lineage as a starting cause, for instance, produces no true fruit no matter how many of the four remaining conditions are supplied. Matched against the earlier explanation, "gathering" is the later phrase and "preface" the earlier one — since the cause must first be present for the condition to have anything to rely upon, later explaining earlier: cause as the basis, condition as its supporting power, cause and condition uniting only then to give rise to the noble path and the Buddha's own fruit. With this, preface and gathering — their distinction, their further names, and how they jointly establish the fruit — are closed in their entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's third of five sections. The treatise turns next to skillful seeing, the fourth section.

66. Section: Skillful Seeing Begins — the Overall Statement

Ru-Yi Translation

And skillful seeing is the overall statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The saṅgha topic's fourth of five sections gives four terms for skillfully seeing the truth, moving from the wisdom of hearing through the wisdom of direct realization.

67. Section: Skillful Knowing, and Skillful Reflection

言善知者，知法義故。善思惟者，如其正理而思惟故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Skillful knowing means knowing the dharma's own meaning. Skillful reflection means reflecting exactly in accord with correct principle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Skillful knowing corresponds to the wisdom born of hearing, having penetrated both the teaching that expresses the dharma and the meaning it expresses; skillful reflection corresponds to the wisdom born of reflection, reflecting exactly as the Buddha's own truth directs — the beginning stages of the practice of skillful seeing, hearing coming first, reflection following after.

68. Section: Skillful, Sharp Wisdom, and Skillful Penetration

Chinese Source

善黠慧者，全分知故。善通達者，如實知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Skillful, sharp wisdom means knowing the whole of it. Skillful penetration means knowing it exactly as it truly is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Skillful, sharp wisdom means the true character of every dharma taught in the noble teaching is fully, completely known, accomplishing the wisdom that abides in the dharma; skillful penetration means that same true character is directly, personally realized, accomplishing nirvāṇa's own wisdom, the very fruit of the noble path realizing the truth of cessation.

69. Section: A Closing Statement — the Latter Two Terms Reveal the Nature, the Former Two the Preparation — Closing Skillful Seeing Entirely

Through the latter two phrases, the very nature of skillful seeing is revealed; through the former two phrases, its preparatory practice is revealed.

Chinese Source

由後二句，顯善見性；由前二句，顯彼加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the latter two phrases, the very nature of skillful seeing is revealed; through the former two phrases, its preparatory practice is revealed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra on dependent origination directly

Skillful, sharp wisdom and skillful penetration together reveal skillful seeing's own true nature — the emptiness of all dharmas skillfully seen, the former still at the level of knowing clearly how the path is to be cultivated, the latter having personally, directly realized it. Skillful knowing and skillful reflection together reveal the necessary preparation beforehand — hearing's own wisdom, then reflection's own wisdom, exactly according with correct principle. A companion sūtra, spoken at Rājagṛha's Bamboo Grove, teaches that this body belongs neither to oneself nor to another — that the six consciousnesses, contacts, feelings, perceptions, and volitions born through the six faculties meeting the six objects together bring about the great mass of future birth, aging, sickness, death, and sorrow, arising through cause and condition; and that once ignorance itself is absent, none of this arises, the great mass of suffering itself ceasing to accumulate — a learned noble disciple who wishes to truly know the world's arising and cessation must pass through skillful seeing, skillful awakening, and skillful entry in turn, moving from the wisdom of hearing to the wisdom of cultivation, exactly matching this passage's own four terms: skillful knowing, skillful reflection, skillful sharp wisdom, and skillful penetration. With this, skillful seeing — its four terms and their closing distinction between nature and preparation — is closed in its entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's fourth of five sections. The treatise turns next to the noble, the fifth and final section.

So-called decisive attainment means ultimate renunciation, without regression.

With this, the noble — undefiled nature, renunciation, and decisive attainment — is closed in its entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's fifth and final section. And with it, all five sections of the saṅgha topic — right conduct, sons of the great teacher, preface and gathering, skillful seeing, and the noble — are closed in their entirety, completing the fourth verse's fourth topic. The treatise turns next to the excellent qualities that give, the fifth topic — the ninth verse-word's remaining term, explained not through literal giving but through the Buddha's own qualities.

73. Section: The Excellent Qualities That Give Begin — Root, Eye, and Support

That the World-Honored One is the eye means that, having attained complete awakening, he reveals the teaching to gods, humans, and the rest. That the World-Honored One is the support means that, within the dharma he teaches, whatever doubt arises can, in every case, be dispelled.

Chinese Source

復次，諸法皆以世尊為本者，由佛世尊是其最初現等覺故。世尊為眼者，現等覺已，為諸天人等開示故。世尊為依者，所說法中，隨所生起一切疑惑皆能遣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, that every dharma takes the World-Honored One as its root means the World-Honored One was the very first to attain complete awakening. That the World-Honored One is the eye means that, having attained complete awakening, he reveals the teaching to gods, humans, and the rest. That the World-Honored One is the support means that, within the dharma he teaches, whatever doubt arises can, in every case, be dispelled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage

The verse's ninth word, "the excellent qualities that give," unfolds across five sections describing the Buddha's own qualities, since the dharma given to beings for their benefit flows entirely from those qualities. As the founding teacher Śākyamuni was the first in this world to realize complete awakening, every dharma takes him as its root; having realized it, he became the eye of gods and humans alike, seeing every knowable object exactly as it is, opening the true meaning of both worldly and transcendent reality through the five kinds of vision; and within his own teaching, every doubt that could arise for any being can be fully resolved, hence his being their support. The Bodhisattva Stage names the Buddha "teacher of gods and humans" on the strength of these very qualities: the true eye, the true wisdom, the true meaning, the true dharma, the guide to explicit meaning, the support for every meaning, the one who clarifies implicit meaning, the one who cuts off every doubt that arises, the one who reveals the most profound places, bringing purity and clarity, the root of every dharma, the guide, and the support — correctly instructing gods and humans so they escape every suffering.

74. Section: Able to Be the Eye, and Able to Be Wisdom

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage

Matched to the Bodhisattva Stage's own account: being the true eye and guiding beings to explicit meaning corresponds to being able to draw forth beings' own innate wisdom, since the Buddha's fivefold vision reveals both worldly and transcendent truth directly; being the true wisdom and the support for every meaning corresponds to being able to draw forth the wisdom gained through effort, since worldly and transcendent truth can only be disclosed through wisdom — the Buddha's own perfected fundamental and subsequently-attained wisdom together establish boundless teachings, becoming the support for every meaning there is.

75. Section: Able to Be Meaning, and Able to Be Dharma

能為義者，謂能引發思所成慧故。能為法者，謂能引發聞所成慧故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Able to be meaning means being able to draw forth the wisdom born of reflection. Able to be dharma means being able to draw forth the wisdom born of hearing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage

Being able to be meaning corresponds to being the true meaning and revealing the most profound places, bringing purity and clarity — the Buddha discloses the ultimate truth's own profundity, so that a practitioner's own reflective understanding of that profound meaning grows ever clearer, drawing forth the wisdom born of reflection. Being able to be dharma corresponds to being the true dharma and the root, guide, and support of every dharma — the Buddha teaches according to genuine meaning, establishing every name and teaching as their common root, drawing forth the wisdom born of hearing in whoever receives it. Revealing genuine meaning through the dharma taught is called guiding; taking that very teaching as the support through which genuine meaning is penetrated is called being the support.

76. Section: Able to Resolve What Is Not Yet Explicit

Being able to resolve what is not yet explicit means every doubt whatsoever can be cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage

Corresponding to being the true meaning, clarifying implicit meaning, and cutting off every doubt that arises, this names the Buddha's own capacity to disclose genuine meaning so fully that beings come to understand what was previously implicit, and every doubt that might arise in a practitioner's own mind can, without exception, be fully resolved.

77. Section: Able to Be the Support for Every Meaning — Closing the Excellent Qualities That Give, and All Five Sections of the Fourth Wholesome Verse's Nine Topics

Ru-Yi Translation

Being able to be the support for every meaning means being able to draw forth every flourishing matter, worldly and transcendent alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage

The dharma the Buddha teaches allows a practitioner to accomplish every kind of benefit — drawing forth worldly virtue, such as the eight surpassing results the Bodhisattva Stage describes as arising from cultivating the six perfections, and drawing forth the wisdom of the transcendent noble path, unsurpassed awakening itself. Every flourishing thing, whether worldly merit or the noble path's own transcendent excellence, flows from the Buddha's own teaching — hence his being the support for every possible benefit. With this, the excellent qualities that give — root, eye, and support; the eye and wisdom; meaning and dharma; resolving what is implicit; and the support for every meaning — are closed in their entirety, completing the fourth verse's fifth topic. And with it, all five of the verse's first five topics — desire, invitation, the dharma, the saṅgha, and the excellent qualities that give — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to weariness, the sixth topic.

78. Section: Weariness Begins — Sorted by Stage: the Path of Seeing, Freedom From Desire, and Cessation

Chinese Source

復次，厭者，謂於見道。言離欲者，謂於修道離欲究竟。所言滅者，謂於無學一切依滅。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's sixth topic, weariness, unfolds across four sections, first sorted by the stage of realization: weariness names the sixteen mind-moments of the path of seeing culminating in stream-entry; freedom from desire names every affliction tied to the lower realms — apart from the peak of existence — being progressively abandoned from stream-entry onward through the path of cultivation; and cessation names the arhat beyond training, who has cut off every affliction of the three realms, entering nirvāṇa without residue once this present life's resultant body is exhausted.

79. Section: A Further Distinction by Stage — the First Two Belong to the Preparatory Stage, the Last to the Stage Beyond Training — Closing "Sorted by Stage"

The first two kinds are cultivated as weariness and freedom-from-desire practice at the preparatory stage; the last kind practices cessation at the stage beyond training.

Ru-Yi Translation

The first two kinds are cultivated as weariness and freedom-from-desire practice at the preparatory stage; the last kind practices cessation at the stage beyond training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Weariness and freedom from desire both still belong to the preparatory stage, effort not yet complete — first growing weary of the distinctly-arisen craving and views, then further removing the innately-arisen ones; cessation alone belongs to the stage beyond training, where the arhat's fruit is realized and every reliance finally extinguished, entering nirvāṇa without residue. With this, weariness sorted by stage of realization is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to weariness sorted by its own characteristic marks.

80. Section: Sorted by Characteristic Mark — Weariness and Freedom From Desire

And so-called weariness means, through seeing the truths, growing entirely weary and averse toward every formation. So-called freedom from desire means, through the path of cultivation, permanently cutting off craving.

Chinese Source

又言厭者，由見諦故，於一切行皆悉厭逆。言離欲者，由於修道永斷貪故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And so-called weariness means, through seeing the truths, growing entirely weary and averse toward every formation. So-called freedom from desire means, through the path of cultivation, permanently cutting off craving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases and a companion sūtra

A second angle, sorted by mark rather than stage: weariness names a practitioner who, having personally realized the four noble truths and truly seen the three realms as a burning house and birth-and-death as an enemy, grows entirely weary and averse toward every defiled resultant formation. Freedom from desire names the noble one from stream-entry onward who continues cultivating diligently, permanently cutting off the innately-arisen craving for the desire, form, and formless realms alike, ultimately released from every desire the three realms contain. The Compendium of Bases explains freedom from desire as cultivating weariness so thoroughly that, even without deliberately applying an antidote, craving simply no longer manifests toward any defiled object — achieved through the sheer power of suppression and cutting off; freedom from desire, in this sense, names the stage where every realm below the peak of existence has been left behind through reliance on that same weariness. A companion sūtra confirms this: one who contemplates impermanence no longer clings to past form, delights in future form, or clings to present form — and in that very freedom, weariness itself arises, weariness naming the path of seeing, freedom from desire the path of cultivation, and moving toward extinction the stage beyond training.

81. Section: Liberation, and Complete Liberation — Closing Weariness Entirely

So-called liberation means, through freedom from craving, being uniformly secure, the mind liberated with respect to every remaining affliction. Complete liberation means, afflictions being cut off, being universally liberated with respect to birth and every other suffering.

Chinese Source

言解脫者，由離貪故，一向安隱，於餘煩惱心得解脫。遍解脫者，煩惱斷故，於生等苦普得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called liberation means, through freedom from craving, being uniformly secure, the mind liberated with respect to every remaining affliction. Complete liberation means, afflictions being cut off, being universally liberated with respect to birth and every other suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Liberation names a practitioner who, having grown free of craving, rests securely in profound concentration, afflictions suppressed, the mind no longer bound by their active manifestation — still only a partial liberation. Complete liberation goes further, naming affliction cut off entirely, so that birth, aging, sickness, death, and every further suffering can never arise again. With this, weariness — sorted by stage, sorted by mark, and its own liberation — is closed in its entirety, completing the fourth verse's sixth topic. The treatise turns next to the brahmin, the seventh topic.

82. Section: The Brahmin Begins — a True Brahmin, Having Ultimately Reached the Far Shore

Further, being a true brahmin means having ultimately reached the far shore, and cleared away every evil — this is its mark.

Chinese Source

復次，是為婆羅門者，究竟到彼岸故，蠲除諸惡故，是為其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, being a true brahmin means having ultimately reached the far shore, and cleared away every evil — this is its mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's seventh topic, the brahmin, first gives five terms marking a true brahmin, then further distinguishes their meaning. Having ultimately reached nirvāṇa's own far shore, clear of every evil dharma whatsoever, is the true mark of a brahmin in this treatise's own sense.

83. Section: Free of Uncertainty and the Rest

Free of uncertainty and the rest means being free of doubt regarding what one has realized oneself.

Chinese Source

無猶豫等者，於自所證離疑惑故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of uncertainty and the rest means being free of doubt regarding what one has realized oneself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Having personally realized the noble path, a practitioner is entirely free of doubt or uncertainty about that very realization, having directly witnessed the true character of all dharmas. A companion sūtra describes this one as gaining the pure dharma-eye amid every defilement, realizing the fruit and the awakened path, free of every bond and confusion, having crossed doubt's own far shore, free of further affliction, no longer following another's teaching, but practicing in accordance with the dharma itself.

84. Section: Having Cut Off Every Evil Deed

Having cut off every evil deed means there is nothing that should be done that is left undone, and nothing that should not be done that is done.

Chinese Source

斷諸惡作者，於應作事無不作故，不應作事無有作故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having cut off every evil deed means there is nothing that should be done that is left undone, and nothing that should not be done that is done.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having realized the noble path, such a one does every deed of merit and wisdom that should be done, leaving nothing undone, and commits none of the evil deeds bound up with desire that should never be done at all.

85. Section: Free of Every Craving

Free of every craving means being without love of gain, honor, or reputation.

Chinese Source

離諸貪愛者，無有利養恭敬愛故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of every craving means being without love of gain, honor, or reputation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A noble one who has realized the path no longer craves any worldly thing, holding no attachment to material gain or the honor and reverence of others — seeing such gain and honor as one would a great venomous snake, the mind forever keeping its distance.

86. Section: No Latent Tendency of Attachment to Existence or Non-Existence — Closing All Five Marks of a True Brahmin

No latent tendency of attachment to existence or non-existence means the latent tendencies are permanently cut off. It should be known that here, both present and future are called existence, while the past is called non-existence.

Chinese Source

於有非有著無有隨眠者，隨眠永斷故。

當知此中，若現在世、若未來世名之為有，其過去世名為非有。

Ru-Yi Translation

No latent tendency of attachment to existence or non-existence means the latent tendencies are permanently cut off.

It should be known that here, both present and future are called existence, while the past is called non-existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Since every seed of affliction is permanently cut off, no latent tendency of clinging follows any resultant body of the past, present, or future. The past five aggregates, already ceased, are called non-existence; the present five aggregates are called existence; and the future five aggregates, yet to come, are likewise called existence. Once every seed of the three realms' craving and view is cut off, no further aggregates will ever arise — neither clinging to past form, delighting in future form, nor grasping present form: exactly this fifth mark, no latent tendency of attachment to existence or non-existence. With this, all five marks of a true brahmin — reaching the far shore, freedom from doubt, cutting off evil deeds, freedom from craving, and freedom from the latent tendency toward existence and non-existence — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a further distinction clarifying their meaning.

87. Section: A Further Distinction Begins — Observing a Brahmin's Mark Without Inversion

Through these several phrases, a brahmin's mark can be observed without inversion.

Chinese Source

由此諸句，無倒觀察婆羅門相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through these several phrases, a brahmin's mark can be observed without inversion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage

The five marks just closed let one observe, without any distortion, whether a given person is genuinely a brahmin in this treatise's own sense. The Buddha sometimes calls an arhat who has realized the noble path a true brahmin, an ultimate-sense brahmin — brahmin being India's own most honored caste, borrowed here to convey the dignity of one beyond training. The Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 29, distinguishes three kinds of brahmin: brahmin by lineage, born into a brahmin family, the foremost of India's four castes; brahmin in name only, called so merely by convention; and brahmin by right conduct, whose every deed is ultimately resolved, having driven out every evil, unwholesome dharma — the true, ultimate-sense brahmin this treatise means.

88. Section: The First Three Marks — Revealing Learning and Clear Knowing

Through the first three phrases, learning and clear knowing are revealed as its mark.

Chinese Source

由前三句，顯示多聞及與正知觀見其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the first three phrases, learning and clear knowing are revealed as its mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first three marks — reaching the far shore, freedom from doubt, and cutting off evil deeds — show that a genuine brahmin can be recognized through learning and clear knowing: genuine mastery of the Buddha's teaching, its essential meaning firmly grasped, together with clear knowing of one's own conduct moment to moment, correctly discerning right from wrong in body, speech, and mind. Both marks together help distinguish a true noble one from an imitation.

89. Section: The Fourth Mark — Why It Further Reveals Genuine Practice

Or, it may be that one has not properly cultivated the wholesome portion, and so the fourth single phrase further reveals a mark by which to observe this.

Chinese Source

或謂不正修習善品，故復顯示第四一句觀察其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, it may be that one has not properly cultivated the wholesome portion, and so the fourth single phrase further reveals a mark by which to observe this.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One who has not genuinely cultivated calm and insight tends to fall into craving for gain and honor when such objects appear — which is exactly why the fourth mark, freedom from every craving, offers a further test: a genuine brahmin, beyond the first three marks, should also be free of attachment to gain and reverence alike.

90. Section: A Further Distinction — the Eight Kinds of Attachment Named

Here, attachment means eight kinds of attachment.

Chinese Source

此中著者，謂八種著。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, attachment means eight kinds of attachment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having given the five marks of a true brahmin, the treatise now clarifies their meaning in greater depth — first by naming eight specific kinds of attachment the fifth mark, freedom from any latent tendency toward existence or non-existence, actually excludes. These same eight serve equally as a mirror for self-examination: even one who believes their own practice mature should ask whether any of these eight still remain.

91. Section: The First Attachment — Grieving Over What No Longer Exists

Amid non-existence, one may fashion the attachment of grief.

Chinese Source

於非有中，作愁憂著。

Ru-Yi Translation

Amid non-existence, one may fashion the attachment of grief.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of the eight attachments, belonging to non-existence — the past — is grief: however excellent a past circumstance once was, it has already passed; going on grieving over it, unable to let it go, is itself a form of attachment to what no longer exists.

92. Section: Five Attachments Belonging to Existence in the Present

Among existence comprised within the present world, there are five kinds of attachment: first, fashioning upkeep; second, fashioning rescue; third, fashioning what is mine; fourth, fashioning superiority; fifth, fashioning inferiority.

Chinese Source

於現在世所攝有中，有五種著。一、作修治，二、作救護，三、作我所，四、作高勝，五、作下劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among existence comprised within the present world, there are five kinds of attachment: first, fashioning upkeep; second, fashioning rescue; third, fashioning what is mine; fourth, fashioning superiority; fifth, fashioning inferiority.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Existence in the present carries five further attachments: fashioning upkeep, endlessly laboring to nourish and prolong this present body past what practice genuinely requires; fashioning rescue, seeking relief the moment the body meets any distress, again beyond what is genuinely needed; fashioning what is mine, clinging to the aggregates or to any outer object as one's own possession; fashioning superiority, the conceit that arises from clinging to self as excellent; and fashioning inferiority, a subtler conceit that clings to self even while believing oneself merely a little behind others, when in truth far behind.

93. Section: Two Attachments Belonging to Existence in the Future — Completing All Eight Attachments Enumerated

Among existence comprised within the future world: fashioning pursuit, and fashioning motion.

Chinese Source

於未來世所攝有中，作行、作動。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among existence comprised within the future world: fashioning pursuit, and fashioning motion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Fashioning pursuit names hoping for a better circumstance in a life to come — greater wealth, greater standing than the present one affords; fashioning motion names then actually acting on that hope, cultivating merit toward that future reward, since this life's own suffering has bred a longing for further existence. Together with grief over the past and the five attachments of the present, these two attachments of the future complete all eight kinds of attachment in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a further, twofold classification of the same eight.

94. Section: A Second Classification — Heaviness Toward the Inedible, Relish Toward the Edible — Closing the Eight Attachments' Full Enumeration

Across all three domains together, one may fashion the utmost heaviness, and fashion the utmost relish.

Chinese Source

總於三處，作極厚重、作極甘味。

Ru-Yi Translation

Across all three domains together, one may fashion the utmost heaviness, and fashion the utmost relish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the sub-commentary

A further lens cuts across all three times at once, by what can and cannot be eaten: toward inedible things — gold, silver, and whatever else can be traded — a being carries an intensely heavy attachment, since such possessions can always be exchanged for further pleasing objects; and toward edible things, an intense relish, a strong craving for taste itself. Even one who insists on eating only plainly, wherever they go, may simply have shifted the same attachment onto plainness itself — clinging to either richness or plainness is still clinging. The sub-commentary glosses "all three domains" as simply the three times, past, present, and future, time itself serving as their basis. With this, the eight kinds of attachment — grief over the past, five attachments of the present, two of the future, and this further cross-cutting pair of heaviness and relish — are closed in their full enumeration. The treatise turns next to why each one arises.

95. Section: Why Each Arises Begins — Grief, Because What Was Loved Has Changed and Perished

Fashioning grief happens because what was loved has changed and perished.

Chinese Source

作愁憂者，所愛變壞故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fashioning grief happens because what was loved has changed and perished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Grief over the past arises simply because a once-pleasing circumstance has since changed and been lost — a once-prosperous person, for instance, ruined by a failed venture, mourning the wealth now gone.

96. Section: Why the Five Present Attachments Arise

Fashioning upkeep happens through nourishing and hoarding. Fashioning rescue happens through seeking rescue wherever distress presses. Fashioning what is mine happens through clinging to it as one's own. Fashioning superiority happens through the conceit that arises from reckoning self as excellent — as the World-Honored One said, the conceit of beings in this world stands like a lofty banner. Fashioning inferiority happens through the conceit that arises even while reckoning self as lowly.

Chinese Source

作修治者，養育攝藏故。作救護者，於逼惱處求作救護故。作我所者，執為我所故。作高勝者，計我為勝而起憍慢故，如世尊言：世間眾生慢為高幢故。作下劣者，計我為劣而起憍慢故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fashioning upkeep happens through nourishing and hoarding. Fashioning rescue happens through seeking rescue wherever distress presses. Fashioning what is mine happens through clinging to it as one's own. Fashioning superiority happens through the conceit that arises from reckoning self as excellent — as the World-Honored One said, the conceit of beings in this world stands like a lofty banner. Fashioning inferiority happens through the conceit that arises even while reckoning self as lowly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Upkeep arises because self-love and love of its objects run so deep that a being ceaselessly nourishes this present body and hoards ever more provisions, never once reaching genuine satisfaction. Rescue arises because pain, once it presses, drives a being to seek relief beyond what is warranted — sometimes even resorting to deceit to draw out further aid rather than simply enduring a setback with dignity. What-is-mine arises the instant anything, inner or outer, is clung to as one's own possession. Superiority arises because worldly beings almost universally believe themselves excellent, and from that belief conceit grows tall and rigid, like a banner raised high — precisely because the truth of no-self remains unseen. And inferiority arises even in one who knows they fall short of others, since a subtler conceit persists even in that self-deprecation — as when someone unable to become a school principal sneers, "there's nothing so great about being principal, I simply chose not to," a lowly conceit dressed up as indifference.

97. Section: Why the Two Future Attachments Arise

So-called fashioning pursuit is craving for the future. So-called fashioning motion is, having already hoped for it, then seeking it out through skillful means.

Chinese Source

言作行者，是其希望未來世愛。言作動者，既希望已，方便追求。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called fashioning pursuit is craving for the future. So-called fashioning motion is, having already hoped for it, then seeking it out through skillful means.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Pursuit arises from hoping a future circumstance or resultant body will exceed the present one, a craving for what is yet to come; motion arises once that craving for further existence has already taken hold, driving a being to actively pursue it through skillful means — cultivating discipline or generosity, for instance, not to realize the noble path, but merely to secure a wealthier, more fortunate rebirth among gods or humans.

98. Section: Why Heaviness and Relish Arise — Closing the Reasons for All Eight Attachments, and the Entire First Reading of the Five Marks

Fashioning the utmost heaviness applies to what is loved and delighted in yet cannot be eaten — gold, silver, and the like, fit for exchange and trade. Fashioning the utmost relish applies to what can be eaten.

Chinese Source

作極厚重者，是所愛樂，非可食用，謂金銀等應可貿易。作極甘味者，是可食用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fashioning the utmost heaviness applies to what is loved and delighted in yet cannot be eaten — gold, silver, and the like, fit for exchange and trade. Fashioning the utmost relish applies to what can be eaten.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Heaviness names an intense attachment to non-food objects across all three times — gold, silver, and other tradeable goods that can be exchanged for further pleasing things, an attachment bound up especially with gain and honor. Relish names the same intensity directed toward food itself, an equally strong craving for taste. With this, why each of the eight attachments arises is closed in its entirety — and with it, the first reading of the five marks of a true brahmin, from their overall observation through this full account of what should not be clung to, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a second reading of the same five marks.

99. Section: A Second Reading Begins — the Five Marks Reveal Attaining the Path and Witnessing Its Fruit

There is a further distinction. These five phrases briefly reveal attaining the path, and witnessing the path's own fruit.

Chinese Source

復有差別。謂此五句，略顯得道、道果作證。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further distinction. These five phrases briefly reveal attaining the path, and witnessing the path's own fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second angle reads the same five marks — reaching the far shore, freedom from doubt, cutting off evil deeds, freedom from craving, and freedom from the latent tendency toward existence and non-existence — as together showing a practitioner who has attained the noble path itself and gone on to witness that path's own fruit.

100. Section: The First Phrase Matched — Briefly Revealing Attainment of the Path

Being a true brahmin briefly reveals attaining the path.

Chinese Source

是為婆羅門者，略顯得道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being a true brahmin briefly reveals attaining the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first mark, simply naming a true brahmin, briefly points to the moment the noble path itself is attained.

101. Section: The Remaining Four Phrases Matched — Revealing Witness to the Path's Own Fruit

Freedom from uncertainty and the rest, cutting off every evil deed, freedom from every craving, and freedom from any latent tendency toward existence or non-existence — these several phrases briefly reveal witnessing the path's own fruit.

Chinese Source

無猶豫等、斷諸惡作、離諸貪愛、於有非有著無有隨眠者，如是諸句，略顯獲得道果作證。

Ru-Yi Translation

Freedom from uncertainty and the rest, cutting off every evil deed, freedom from every craving, and freedom from any latent tendency toward existence or non-existence — these several phrases briefly reveal witnessing the path's own fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The remaining four marks together describe what follows once the fruit itself is realized and witnessed: no doubt or hesitation remains about one's own attainment; no regret or evil deed troubles one's conduct; no craving for gain arises; and no affliction or attachment clings to any existing or non-existing state across the three times — the very marks a noble witness to the fruit displays.

102. Section: Explaining All Four — Closing the Second Reading's Account of Attaining the Path and Its Fruit

Doubt regarding what has been discerned and understood is cut off; the dharma of forgetfulness in all that is practiced is cut off; the cause of future suffering is cut off; the coarseness that is the cause of present suffering is cut off.

Chinese Source

於記所解疑惑斷故，於所行中一切忘失法行斷故，於未來世苦因斷故，現在苦因麤重斷故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Doubt regarding what has been discerned and understood is cut off; the dharma of forgetfulness in all that is practiced is cut off; the cause of future suffering is cut off; the coarseness that is the cause of present suffering is cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation

Freedom from uncertainty means a noble one, having discerned and confirmed the four noble truths for themselves, is left with no doubt about that very realization. Cutting off evil deeds means never losing mindfulness in body, speech, or mind, so that no lapse into a defiled deed of forgetfulness ever arises. Freedom from craving means, being free of craving for gain and honor, never again generating merit-bound, demerit-bound, or unmoving karma that would produce a future cause of suffering. And freedom from the latent tendency toward existence and non-existence means the seeds of affliction tied to past, present, and future aggregates alike are exhausted, so the coarseness that would otherwise seed present suffering is likewise cut off. A second explanatory angle draws the same four terms from the noble eightfold path directly: not clinging to any mark comprised within the desire realm removes gain; not clinging to any mark of the higher two realms' meditative states removes honor, cutting off the cause of future suffering; and contemplating the markless realm is what "non-existence" itself means — a trainee, though having cut off the manifest activity of craving, still carries its latent seed, along with the four defilements of discursive thought, idle talk, attachment, and conceptual grasping, as the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation explains, while only the arhat has cut off even those seeds entirely, leaving the present aggregates free of any coarseness born of a latent tendency — this being the second way of drawing the same distinction, between the trainee who still carries a latent tendency and the one beyond training who carries none. With this, matching the remaining four marks to attaining the path's own fruit is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a closer naming of existence, non-existence, and attachment themselves.

103. Section: Naming Existence — Attention Directed at Marks Comprised Within the Three Realms

So-called existence means, it should be known, in this sense, attention directed at marks comprised within the three realms.

Chinese Source

所言有者，謂此義中，當知於其三界所攝諸相作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called existence means, it should be known, in this sense, attention directed at marks comprised within the three realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A closer naming of the terms already used follows: existence names attention still directed at some mark within the three realms — a being attached to the desire realm attends to desire itself; one attached to the form realm attends to meditative absorption; and one attached to the formless realm attends to the four formless attainments — each realm's own characteristic object of clinging.

104. Section: Naming Non-Existence — Attention Directed at the Markless Realm

So-called non-existence means attention directed at the markless realm.

Chinese Source

言非有者，於無相界作意思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called non-existence means attention directed at the markless realm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Non-existence, by contrast, names the cultivation of insight itself: since the markless realm is free of any characteristic, attention directed toward it likewise takes the markless as its object. In short, whatever is marked is called existence; whatever is markless is called non-existence.

105. Section: Naming Attachment — Its Very Substance Is Greed, Hatred, and Delusion

So-called attachment means, in this sense, it is greed, hatred, and delusion.

Chinese Source

所言著者，謂此義中是貪瞋癡。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called attachment means, in this sense, it is greed, hatred, and delusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Attachment itself, once named plainly, is simply greed, hatred, and delusion arising from clinging — the very afflictions the treatise turns next to distinguish further, by kind and by cause.

106. Section: Attachment Distinguished — the Trainee Still Carries Its Seed, the Arhat Does Not

As with the markless concentration: every trainee still carries its latent tendency; only the one who is not an arhat has the four defilements of discursive thought, idle talk, attachment, and conceptual grasping.

Chinese Source

如無相定，諸有學者猶有隨眠，非阿羅漢得有尋思、戲論、著、想四種雜染。

Ru-Yi Translation

As with the markless concentration: every trainee still carries its latent tendency; only the one who is not an arhat has the four defilements of discursive thought, idle talk, attachment, and conceptual grasping.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Even a trainee capable of entering the markless concentration still carries affliction's own dormant seed; not yet an arhat, such a one still carries all four defilements — discursive thought, idle talk (conceptual proliferation in language), attachment, and conceptual grasping — while an arhat who enters the very same concentration is free of all four entirely.

107. Section: Sorted by Monastic and Lay Life — Closing How the Four Defilements Are Classified

The first two belong to the monastic class; the latter two belong to the lay class. It is because the latent tendency of attachment is present that these come to arise.

Chinese Source

前二是出家品，後二是在家品。由有著隨眠，故彼得生起。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first two belong to the monastic class; the latter two belong to the lay class. It is because the latent tendency of attachment is present that these come to arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discursive thought and idle talk, the first two defilements, belong to monastic life — a monastic is not directly disturbed by desire's own objects, yet the mind can still wander into discursive thought about desire and idle verbal proliferation; attachment and conceptual grasping, the latter two, belong to lay life, since a layperson meets desire's objects directly, more readily clinging and grasping at their marks. Whether monastic or lay, it is the underlying latent tendency of attachment that allows any of these four to arise at all. With this, how the four defilements are classified between monastic and lay life is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to why each pair specifically arises.

108. Section: Why the Monastic's Two Defilements Arise

Monastics, through recollecting objects once encountered, have discursive thought; through the mind's own agitated activity, have idle talk.

Chinese Source

諸出家者，由追憶念曾所更境，故有尋思；動亂現行，故有戲論。

Ru-Yi Translation

Monastics, through recollecting objects once encountered, have discursive thought; through the mind's own agitated activity, have idle talk.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

A monastic's discursive thought arises from recollecting a once-experienced object of desire; idle talk arises from the mind's own restless agitation manifesting outward. The Compendium of Bases, fascicle 95, further distinguishes six kinds of wrong idle talk: inverted idle talk, wasted idle talk, contentious idle talk, idle talk comparing one's own superiority or inferiority to another's, idle talk over cleverness and livelihood, and idle talk indulging attachment to worldly wealth and food.

109. Section: Why the Lay Person's Two Defilements Arise

Lay people, dwelling amid objects directly present, have attachment and conceptual grasping, since, through defiled clinging, they take up every mark.

Chinese Source

諸在家者，住現前境，有著、有想；由有染著，取諸相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Lay people, dwelling amid objects directly present, have attachment and conceptual grasping, since, through defiled clinging, they take up every mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Lay people carry discursive thought and idle talk too, but because they typically face form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and the desire between the sexes directly, present before the senses, defiled attachment and the grasping of marks and finer characteristics arise all the more readily — the particular affliction of lay life.

110. Section: The Two Causes and Conditions Behind All Four Defilements — Closing Attachment's Own Account, the Second Reading, and the Entire Brahmin Topic

There are further two kinds of cause and condition for defilement: improper attention, and conditions that accord with that very state. Through these causes and conditions, defilement comes to arise — hence these are said to be its cause and condition.

Chinese Source

復有二種雜染因緣，謂不如理作意及順彼處法。由此因緣，彼得生起，是故說此為彼因緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are further two kinds of cause and condition for defilement: improper attention, and conditions that accord with that very state. Through these causes and conditions, defilement comes to arise — hence these are said to be its cause and condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra directly

Beyond the latent tendency itself, two further conditions bring the four defilements into actual manifestation: improper attention, reflecting along a mistaken path rather than the Buddha's own truth, and the presence of circumstances that favor that improper attention arising — together, these two conditions are exactly why discursive thought, idle talk, attachment, and conceptual grasping come to arise at all. A companion sūtra confirms this from the Buddha's own side: whenever monastic or lay alike give rise to any of these wrong states, the Buddha takes no delight in it — since a monastic caught in discursive thought and idle talk, or a lay person caught in attachment and conceptual grasping, thereby takes no delight in the true dharma, falling instead into the eightfold wrong path rather than the eightfold noble path, and this is precisely what displeases the Buddha. With this, attachment's own account — its substance, its distinction between trainee and arhat, its classification by monastic and lay life, why each pair arises, and its two underlying causes and conditions — is closed in its entirety. And with it, naming existence, non-existence, and attachment together is closed, completing the second reading of the five marks in its entirety, and with it, the fourth verse's seventh topic, the brahmin, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to impermanence, the eighth topic.

111. Section: Impermanence Begins — the Truth of Suffering — a Question Posed: Why Is All That Is Impermanent Suffering?

Further, whatever is impermanent is entirely suffering — what does this mean?

Chinese Source

復次，所有無常皆是苦者，義何謂耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, whatever is impermanent is entirely suffering — what does this mean?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's eighth topic, impermanence, opens under the truth of suffering, examining the four aspects of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self. It begins by posing a question directly: why should everything impermanent necessarily be suffering?

112. Section: The First Answer — Impermanent Beings Share in Birth, Aging, and Every Further Suffering

If there is a being sharing in impermanence, then the many sufferings of birth, aging, and the rest arise.

Chinese Source

若有無常眾同分者，有生老等眾苦生起。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is a being sharing in impermanence, then the many sufferings of birth, aging, and the rest arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Every phenomenon is impermanent, and within that impermanent order, birth, aging, sickness, and death necessarily arise — since every formation is impermanent, no being remains forever young or healthy, but is bound to cycle through aging, sickness, death, and rebirth; hence impermanence itself is suffering.

113. Section: The Second Answer — Feelings Themselves, Arising Only to Perish, Are Entirely Suffering — Closing Both Answers

If, in dependence on contact, there are feelings, all of these change and are destroyed, ceasing the moment they arise — hence it is said that every feeling is entirely suffering.

Chinese Source

若依諸觸有諸受者，彼皆變壞，生已尋滅，故說諸受皆悉是苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, in dependence on contact, there are feelings, all of these change and are destroyed, ceasing the moment they arise — hence it is said that every feeling is entirely suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A being's six kinds of feeling, arising when the six faculties meet the six objects, are certain to change and be destroyed — indeed, each feeling ceases the very instant it arises, its own nature impermanent through and through, and therefore every feeling whatsoever is suffering. With this, both answers to why the impermanent is entirely suffering — the suffering shared by every impermanent being, and the suffering inherent in feeling's own instant arising and ceasing — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to why the impermanent is also empty and without self.

114. Section: Emptiness and No-Self — Closing the Four Aspects of the Truth of Suffering

If there is the suffering dharma of birth and the rest, and the suffering dharma of destruction and the rest, all of these are without self. Since they are themselves not self, in that very place there is likewise no self to be found — through this, the aspects of emptiness and no-self are comprised.

Chinese Source

若有生等苦法及有壞等苦法，彼皆無我。自非我故，於是處所亦無有我，由此攝受空、無我行。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is the suffering dharma of birth and the rest, and the suffering dharma of destruction and the rest, all of these are without self. Since they are themselves not self, in that very place there is likewise no self to be found — through this, the aspects of emptiness and no-self are comprised.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and a companion sūtra

A being's own resultant suffering — birth, aging, sickness, death, and the suffering of change and destruction — is, on close examination, entirely without self: form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness, arising from causes and conditions, admit no permanent, singular, sovereign self to be found anywhere within them. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain this from two angles at once: since no self can be found among the formations themselves, this is called "not self," opening the way into emptiness; and since each formation's own characteristic — matter's own resistant form, feeling's own registering, perception's own grasping of marks, volition's own constructing, consciousness's own discerning — is bound up with impermanence and suffering, all arising from conditions and thus never self-governing, none can be a self either, hence "in that place there is likewise no self." No-self stresses the absence of any governing agency; emptiness stresses the absence of any such self's own substance. A companion sūtra confirms the whole progression directly: contemplating form and the other aggregates as impermanent leads to suffering, suffering leads to knowing there is no self capable of mastery and nothing that is truly one's own, no substantial self at all — hence empty, hence without self; contemplating impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self in this way is true and correct insight, capable of liberating a being from birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, grief, and every affliction. With this, the truth of suffering's four aspects — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the wisdom and forbearance that come to fully understand them.

115. Section: Wisdom and Forbearance Begin — Understanding, Equal Understanding, and Examined Understanding

And understanding is the wisdom born of hearing, as the treatises of the wise all say. Equal understanding is the wisdom born of reflection. Examined understanding is the wisdom born of cultivation.

Chinese Source

又解了者，聞所成慧，諸智論者如是說故。等解了者，思所成慧。審解了者，修所成慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

And understanding is the wisdom born of hearing, as the treatises of the wise all say. Equal understanding is the wisdom born of reflection. Examined understanding is the wisdom born of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth topic's second major section turns to the wisdom and forbearance that come to fully understand impermanence. Understanding names the wisdom born simply of hearing the five aggregates taught as impermanent; equal understanding names the wisdom born of reflecting more deeply on that same impermanence, giving rise to genuine resolve in the mind; and examined understanding names the wisdom born of actually cultivating calm and insight, directly observing body and mind's own impermanent change.

116. Section: Joy, Equal Joy, and Complete Joy — Closing Wisdom and Forbearance, and the Entire Truth of Suffering

Within these very three kinds of wisdom-practice, whatever forbearance arises is named joy, or equal joy, or complete joy.

Chinese Source

即於如是三慧行中，所有諸忍，名為喜樂、若等喜樂、若遍喜樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within these very three kinds of wisdom-practice, whatever forbearance arises is named joy, or equal joy, or complete joy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As a practitioner's forbearance in accepting impermanence deepens through hearing, reflection, and cultivation in turn, the corresponding joy deepens too: hearing that every formation is impermanent brings joy; reflecting on it further, giving rise to genuine resolve, brings equal joy; and diligently cultivating calm and insight until impermanence is directly, bodily experienced brings complete joy, filling body and mind alike. With this, wisdom and forbearance are closed in their entirety, completing the truth of suffering's full account under the first section of the impermanence topic. The treatise turns next to impermanence traced through contemplative practice.

117. Section: Contemplative Practice Begins — the Fourfold Contemplation of Breath

And there is the contemplation of impermanence, the contemplation of cutting-off, the contemplation of freedom from desire, and the contemplation of cessation — already explained at length in the Śrāvaka Stage.

Chinese Source

又有無常隨觀、斷隨觀、離欲隨觀、滅隨觀者，如聲聞地已廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

And there is the contemplation of impermanence, the contemplation of cutting-off, the contemplation of freedom from desire, and the contemplation of cessation — already explained at length in the Śrāvaka Stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage and a companion sūtra

The impermanence topic's second major section traces the same truth through the sixteen extraordinary factors of mindfulness of breathing, already treated at length in the Śrāvaka Stage. Contemplating the breath's own impermanence opens insight into impermanence itself; contemplating that same impermanence further cuts off affliction and enters the path of seeing, called the cutting-off realm; continuing to cultivate calm and insight cuts off what the path of cultivation cuts off, called freedom from desire; and contemplating the breath's impermanence still further leads all the way to the cessation-realm, first nirvāṇa with residue, then, once this life's lifespan ends, nirvāṇa without residue, every reliance permanently stilled — through this same breath-contemplation, the sixteen extraordinary factors carry a practitioner from stream-entry all the way to arhatship. A companion sūtra confirms the same progression, and the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 27, treats it at length.

118. Section: Impermanence's Own Power to Harm, and Its Obstruction of the Noble Path — Closing the Fourfold Contemplation Entirely

And impermanence's own power does harm — and so on, spoken of at length. It should be known that here, this briefer passage reveals impermanence's own further distinctions, and the distinction of its obstruction, each accordingly.

Chinese Source

又無常力之所損害，乃至廣說。當知此中，增一略文顯無常等差別，障礙差別為後，如其所應。

Ru-Yi Translation

And impermanence's own power does harm — and so on, spoken of at length. It should be known that here, this briefer passage reveals impermanence's own further distinctions, and the distinction of its obstruction, each accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage

Impermanence's own power harms the six inner faculties and, at length, the sixteen outer things comprising the material world — the full account already given in the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicles 27 and 34. This briefer passage stands as an abbreviated restatement of the earlier extended teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, revealing impermanence through directly watching oneself or another pass from youth to old age, the body's own condition shifting moment to moment, each successive formation never quite the same as the last — proof enough of every formation's impermanent nature. It further reveals impermanence's own power to obstruct the noble path: even a practitioner who repeatedly grows weary and fearful before the truths of suffering and its arising, and repeatedly aspires deeply toward nirvāṇa, may still fail to enter it wholeheartedly, precisely because a coarse residue of self-conceit continues to arise and obstruct direct realization — only once that coarse affliction, impermanence's own persistent obstacle to the noble path, is finally cut off can a practitioner rest in effortless, unconditioned practice at last. With this, the fourfold contemplation of breath and impermanence's own power to obstruct the path are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to what contemplative practice actually realizes.

119. Section: What Is Realized Begins — Attaining What Was Not Yet Attained, and Witnessing What Lies Above

Wishing to attain what has not yet been attained means attaining it for the very first time, or having first realized something still lesser. Witnessing a further distinction above means witnessing the cutting-off itself.

Chinese Source

為欲獲得所未得者，最初得故，或先下劣有所證故。於上差別而作證者，謂於其斷而作證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wishing to attain what has not yet been attained means attaining it for the very first time, or having first realized something still lesser. Witnessing a further distinction above means witnessing the cutting-off itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

To attain the noble path never before attained, a practitioner must first realize the cutting-off realm, reaching stream-entry — but since the noble path itself depends on meditative concentration cultivated through impermanence and the other contemplations, concentration is first attained as a lesser realization relative to the noble path itself. Witnessing a further distinction above names a practitioner who, having first cultivated mindfulness of breathing and attained concentration, relies on that concentration to contemplate the breath's own impermanence, and thereby rises further to witness the cutting-off realm directly.

120. Section: Observation, Careful Reflection, and Observation in Accord With Reason

So-called observation refers to wisdom. So-called careful reflection refers to concentration. Observation in accord with reason means these two dharmas turn without any inversion.

Chinese Source

言觀察者，此說於慧。言審慮者，說三摩地。如理觀察者，此說二法無顛倒轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called observation refers to wisdom. So-called careful reflection refers to concentration. Observation in accord with reason means these two dharmas turn without any inversion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Observation names the wisdom capable of observing; careful reflection names the concentration within which impermanence is carefully, thoroughly considered, the cultivated wisdom among the three. And observation in accord with reason names both wisdom and concentration together, calm and insight paired in balanced practice, perfectly pure and complete, turning without any inversion whatsoever — the Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm this reading directly: wisdom and concentration, named together as "these two dharmas," are calm and insight applied in tandem; their perfection and purity together are what "without inversion" means.

121. Section: A Detailed Example — Freedom From the Four Inversions — Closing Impermanence's Entire Eighth Topic

Though genuinely without existing, it appears — this means, in this very place, there is truly no pleasure. Empty means being empty of self. False means being impure. Not firm means being impermanent — this reveals freedom from the four inversions.

Chinese Source

雖實無有而顯現者，謂於此中實無樂故。虛者，空無我故。偽者，不淨故。不堅者，無常故。此則顯示無四顛倒。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though genuinely without existing, it appears — this means, in this very place, there is truly no pleasure. Empty means being empty of self. False means being impure. Not firm means being impermanent — this reveals freedom from the four inversions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicles 26 and 34

Though the five aggregates possess no true nature of permanence, pleasure, self, or purity, they nonetheless appear to — the World-Honored One taught repeatedly that no genuine pleasure exists among the conditioned formations an ordinary being clings to, this very body of five aggregates being nothing but a great mass of suffering. He likewise taught them empty, since no self can be found among the aggregates, its own substance ungraspable, its own governing function equally ungraspable — hence empty and without self. He taught them false, since they falsely present as pure — "a thin skin covers what is unclean, so its filth is not seen" — when in truth, beyond the body's nine orifices ceaselessly discharging impurity, even its seed, its dwelling place, its manner of conception, and its nourishment are all impure, as fascicle 26's six kinds of impurity already explained: impurity of decay, impurity of suffering, impurity of baseness, impurity relative to comparison, impurity of affliction, and impurity of swift ruin. And every conditioned formation is certainly impermanent, as fascicle 34's own five aspects of formation — change, destruction, separation, the very nature of dharmas, and coming-together — make plain, hence not firm as well. This is the detailed account of what "without inversion" truly means: only a practitioner who cultivates the contemplation of impermanence and genuinely penetrates this can be said to be free of the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity. With this, contemplative practice — its fourfold contemplation, impermanence's power to obstruct the path, what is realized through it, and freedom from the four inversions — is closed in its entirety, completing the fourth verse's eighth topic, impermanence, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to things like foam, the ninth and final topic of the fourth verse.

122. Section: Things Like Foam Begin — Form Compared to a Mass of Foam, Its Four Reasons

Further, form is compared to a mass of foam because it swiftly increases and decreases, because it arises from the water element, because it is produced through water arising from thinking of food's own flavor, and because it cannot be kneaded and shaped.

Chinese Source

復次，色如聚沫者，速增減故，水界生故，思飲食味水所生故，不可揉捼故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, form is compared to a mass of foam because it swiftly increases and decreases, because it arises from the water element, because it is produced through water arising from thinking of food's own flavor, and because it cannot be kneaded and shaped.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra spoken beside the Ganges

The verse's ninth and final topic compares the five aggregates in turn to foam, a bubble, a mirage, a banana tree, and an illusion — a companion sūtra has the Buddha, watching foam rise on the Ganges during a sudden flood, teach the monks that one with clear sight, examining closely, finds such foam momentary, without substance, without firmness, without any solid reality at all, exactly as one should examine one's own form, past, present or future, inner or outer, coarse or fine, near or far. Form resembles foam for four reasons: it swiftly grows and shrinks, gaining or losing weight rapidly, its moment-by-moment change as fleeting as foam at a rushing river's edge; roughly seventy percent of the body is water, unable to exist apart from it, exactly as river foam cannot exist apart from the river; merely thinking of a delicious food produces saliva, water arising even from the thought of flavor, since nourishing the body always depends on this same watery process; and unlike a lump of clay, the body cannot be kneaded and reshaped at will — fragile at the first real blow, exactly like foam itself.

123. Section: What Cannot Be Kneaded — Unlike a Lump of Clay

It is not like a lump of clay that can be turned and made into some other thing — hence it is said that it cannot be kneaded and shaped.

Chinese Source

非如泥團可令轉變造作餘物，是故說言不可揉捼。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is not like a lump of clay that can be turned and made into some other thing — hence it is said that it cannot be kneaded and shaped.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregate of form, like a mass of foam, cannot withstand being kneaded and reshaped — unlike a lump of clay, freely molded by hand into a figure or any shape one likes — hence "cannot be kneaded and shaped."

124. Section: Not Truly a Mass, Yet Appearing as One — Closing Form's Comparison to Foam Entirely

And it is truly not a mass, yet appears as a mass, and is thereby able to give rise to a single being's own understanding.

Chinese Source

又實非聚，似聚顯現，能發起一有情解故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And it is truly not a mass, yet appears as a mass, and is thereby able to give rise to a single being's own understanding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The body is no single, solid, real object, yet once earth, water, fire, and wind gather through causes and conditions, something that looks like a real object appears — and seeing it, one at once mistakes it for a single sentient being. What truly makes a person a person is not this body but the eighth consciousness's own single thought; once that thought departs, as when a corpse is left in the charnel ground, insensate as a log, the truth becomes plain — the body was only ever a borrowed union of earth, water, fire, and wind, incapable of lasting or standing firm, merely wearing the appearance of solidity, which is exactly what deceives the ordinary mind. A noble one is not deceived, seeing this same body as thoughtless as a log, a mere material thing, understanding that only the mind's own grasping leads beings to mistake it for a sentient being at all. With this, form's comparison to a mass of foam — its four reasons, its inability to be reshaped, and its being no true mass despite the appearance of one — is closed in its entirety, completing the first of the five aggregates' comparisons. The treatise turns next to feeling, compared to a floating bubble.

125. Section: Feeling Compared to a Floating Bubble — Its Own Meaning Stated

Feeling is compared to a floating bubble because it arises from the union of three things, resembling a dharma that cannot remain firm for long.

Chinese Source

受喻浮泡者，三和合生，不久堅住相似法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Feeling is compared to a floating bubble because it arises from the union of three things, resembling a dharma that cannot remain firm for long.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second of the five aggregate-comparisons: when the six faculties meet the six objects, the six consciousnesses arise, and the union of faculty, object, and consciousness together brings about contact — and once there is contact, feeling follows. An ordinary being takes feeling to be solid and lasting, but in truth every feeling only resembles something real, vanishing again almost the instant it arises.

126. Section: Like Ground, Like Clouds, Like Rain

So-called like ground means the faculties, the basis on which feeling arises. So-called like clouds means the various objects. So-called like rain means the various consciousnesses.

Chinese Source

言如地者，所謂諸根，彼生依故。言如雲者，謂諸境界。言如雨者，所謂諸識。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called like ground means the faculties, the basis on which feeling arises. So-called like clouds means the various objects. So-called like rain means the various consciousnesses.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The ground is like the six faculties, the very basis on which feeling arises — without an eye, there is no eye-pain; wherever there is a faculty, feeling follows. The clouds are like the objects of the six faculties — form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and mental objects — gathering only when conditions allow, and scattering again once those conditions fail, exactly as clouds drift and disperse in the sky. And the rain is like the six consciousnesses — once clouds gather thick enough, conditions are complete, and rain falls; once faculty, object, and consciousness unite, raindrops strike the ground, and feeling arises within the body.

127. Section: Like the Rain's Own Strike, and Like the Bubble Itself — Closing Feeling's Comparison to a Bubble Entirely

Like the rain's own strike means the various contacts. Like a floating bubble means the various feelings, swiftly arising and swiftly ceasing, unable to remain firm.

Chinese Source

如雨擊者，所謂諸觸。如浮泡者，所謂諸受，速疾起謝，不堅住故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Like the rain's own strike means the various contacts. Like a floating bubble means the various feelings, swiftly arising and swiftly ceasing, unable to remain firm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Just as a raindrop strikes the ground, contact strikes when faculty meets object and consciousness arises. And like the bubble the raindrop's own strike raises on a puddle's surface, feeling arises the instant contact meets its object — and vanishes again just as quickly, never remaining firm, whether painful or pleasurable. With this, feeling's comparison to a floating bubble — its own meaning, and the ground, clouds, rain, rain's strike, and bubble it resembles — is closed in its entirety, completing the second of the five aggregates' comparisons. The treatise turns next to perception, compared to a mirage.

128. Section: Perception Compared to a Mirage — Closing the Third Comparison Entirely

Perception is like a mirage because of its whirling, unsettled nature; because it arises through boundless kinds of characteristic, ever changing; because it gives rise to inversion regarding its object; because it makes that object appear utterly vivid; and because, through this, distinctions such as male and female marks come to be discriminated.

Chinese Source

想同陽焰者，飈動性故，無量種相變易生故，令於所緣發顛倒故，令其境界極顯了故，由此分別男女等相成差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Perception is like a mirage because of its whirling, unsettled nature; because it arises through boundless kinds of characteristic, ever changing; because it gives rise to inversion regarding its object; because it makes that object appear utterly vivid; and because, through this, distinctions such as male and female marks come to be discriminated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third of the five aggregates, perception, gives rise to every kind of mental image, and so is compared to the shimmering mirage a hot sun casts across open ground — restless, whirling, forever in motion, exactly like wind. Once perception grasps a mark, further marks and finer characteristics follow in endless variation; and once grasped, the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity readily arise regarding that object, making it seem all the more vivid and real — mistaking a woman's beauty for something genuine, for instance, is already an inversion, growing only more convincing the more it is dwelt upon, though no true beauty exists at all, since the body itself is impure at its core. A mind free of perception's own grasping would never let such an image arise; but an ordinary being's ceaseless perceiving makes every object seem supremely vivid, and from this the many worldly distinctions — such as male and female marks — come to be drawn at all. With this, perception's comparison to a mirage is closed in its entirety, completing the third of the five aggregates' comparisons. The treatise turns next to volition, compared to a banana tree.

129. Section: Volition Compared to a Banana Tree Begins — a Question Posed

Why is volition classed together with a banana tree?

Chinese Source

云何行類芭蕉？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is volition classed together with a banana tree?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth aggregate-comparison, volition, is treated here mainly as mental volition, since bodily and verbal action both depend on it to occur — the treatise poses this question first before unpacking the comparison itself.

130. Section: The Clear-Eyed Man, the Sharp Blade, and Entering the Forest

So-called the clear-eyed man means the noble disciple. So-called the sharp blade means the blade of subtle wisdom. So-called entering the forest means raising the mind to grasp at any of the five destinies, giving rise to manifold, distinct sufferings each with its own nature, exactly as a forest holds many distinct trees.

Chinese Source

如明眼人者，謂聖弟子。言利刃者，謂妙慧刀。言入林者，謂於五趣舉意攀緣，種種自性眾苦差別同樹法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called the clear-eyed man means the noble disciple. So-called the sharp blade means the blade of subtle wisdom. So-called entering the forest means raising the mind to grasp at any of the five destinies, giving rise to manifold, distinct sufferings each with its own nature, exactly as a forest holds many distinct trees.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The clear-eyed man is the noble disciple entering a forest to fell a tree; the sharp blade is the wisdom that cuts. The forest itself represents the five destinies — every distinct object the mind can grasp — each grasping mentally constructed, and each producing its own distinct kind of suffering, exactly as a forest holds many distinct kinds of tree: grasping at the human realm brings human suffering; grasping at hell, the hungry ghosts, or the animal realm brings each of those realms' own distinct suffering in turn. This is the noble disciple's own act of cutting off consciousness's grasping through subtle wisdom, exactly like a clear-eyed man entering a forest with a sharp blade.

131. Section: Seeking a Straight Banana Trunk, Cutting Its Root, and Peeling Back Its Leaves

Seeking a straight banana trunk means the view of self as agent and as experiencer. Cutting its root means cutting off the view of self. Peeling back its leaves means, through careful, thorough discernment, finding only manifold volitions and other formations, each a distinct dharma.

Chinese Source

為取端直芭蕉柱者，謂為作者、受者我見。截其根者，謂斷我見。披折葉者，委細簡擇唯有種種思等諸行差別法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seeking a straight banana trunk means the view of self as agent and as experiencer. Cutting its root means cutting off the view of self. Peeling back its leaves means, through careful, thorough discernment, finding only manifold volitions and other formations, each a distinct dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Why does the noble disciple enter the forest seeking a straight banana trunk? This images a practitioner cultivating calm and insight, carefully examining the five aggregates across the five destinies, past, present, and future, good or bad, superior or inferior, searching for any true agent of karma or true experiencer of its fruit — every such view of self, whether identified with the aggregates or apart from them, is sought out and, on careful examination, nowhere found — cutting the banana's own tangled root, the view of self itself. Peeling back the leaves images the noble disciple's still finer discernment: finding no true self acting anywhere in the mind, only the activity of the mental factor of volition itself — whether delighting in merit and observing the five precepts and ten wholesome deeds toward a future human or heavenly reward, committing the ten unwholesome deeds toward a future fall into the evil destinies, or cultivating the unmoving formations of meditative absorption toward rebirth in the higher two realms, all of this is simply volition's own manifold activity, producing the five destinies' differing results — with no single person ever actually there creating karma or receiving its fruit, only volition's own distinct dharmas at work.

Ru-Yi Translation

A noble disciple searching the five aggregates for any true self finds nothing at all — this is the very truth a long search ultimately confirms: what an ordinary being takes to be self is never firm or abiding, because there simply is no self to find. Someone might claim perception itself is self — "I think, therefore I am" — yet the mental factor of thought never stops changing moment to moment; which moment's thought, then, could possibly be the self? On close examination, no self is found anywhere, let alone any permanent, truly substantial self that acts or experiences and could ever actually be seen. Most take whatever makes a decision to be the self, but even that deciding thought is constantly shifting, itself only the functioning of the mental factor of volition, dependent on manifold conditions — past experience, upbringing, social environment, inherited custom, present circumstance — never a fixed, real entity of its own. With this, volition's comparison to a banana tree — the question posed, the clear-eyed man entering the forest, seeking and cutting the trunk, and finding nothing firm within — is closed in its entirety, completing the fourth of the five aggregates' comparisons. 於過去世所有六處有顧戀故，名隨眠。於未來世所有六處有希望故，名繫屬。於現在世所有六處有耽染故，名執著。

Ru-Yi Translation

Where there is lingering attachment toward the six bases of the past, this is called the latent tendency. Where there is hope toward the six bases of the future, this is called attachment to a group. Where there is deep indulgence toward the six bases of the present, this is called clinging.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Fondly recalling one's own past faculties — how sharp the eyes once were, how youthful the body — is the latent tendency; hoping one's future faculties will be even better, and tending them carefully toward that hope, or performing generosity in hope of a better rebirth, is attachment to a group; and deep indulgence in one's present faculties, savoring them right now, is clinging.

158. Section: Clinging to What Belongs to Self, and Conceit

With respect to the six bases of one's own body, taken up as one's own, this is clung to as what belongs to self. With respect to another's six bases, not taken up as one's own, whether inferior, equal, or superior, conceit arises according to its kind.

Chinese Source

於自攝受他身六處，執為我所。於劣中勝非自攝受他身六處，依慢種類，發起於慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

With respect to the six bases of one's own body, taken up as one's own, this is clung to as what belongs to self. With respect to another's six bases, not taken up as one's own, whether inferior, equal, or superior, conceit arises according to its kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Clinging to what belongs to self is twofold: clinging to one's own aggregates as mine — this body, this feeling, this thought, this decision — and clinging to another's faculties as belonging to oneself, as when a worldly person speaks of "my husband" or "my child." And comparing one's own faculties against another's gives rise to conceit in its several kinds: looking down on one whose faculties are inferior is ordinary conceit; believing oneself superior to an equal is excessive conceit; believing oneself equal to or greater than a genuine superior is conceit beyond conceit; and believing oneself only slightly behind someone far superior is inferior conceit — each a different shape the same underlying comparison takes.

159. Section: Longing, and Heavy Attachment

Toward the desire realm, comprised within the unfixed stage, further and further longing arises. Toward the form and formless realms, comprised within the fixed stage, a heavy attachment arises accordingly, on account of their own vastness and subtlety.

Chinese Source

於不定地欲界所繫，發起後後所有希求。於其定地色無色繫，如其所應，由廣大微妙故，發起厚重。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward the desire realm, comprised within the unfixed stage, further and further longing arises. Toward the form and formless realms, comprised within the fixed stage, a heavy attachment arises accordingly, on account of their own vastness and subtlety.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Toward the desire realm's own scattered, unfixed states, a being hopes only for a somewhat better rebirth within that same realm — longing. Toward the form and formless realms' own fixed meditative states, a being who has attained the first absorption hopes for rebirth in its heaven, one who has attained the sphere of infinite space hopes for rebirth there, and so on — and because what is longed for there is so vast and subtle, this deeper attachment is called heavy attachment rather than mere longing.

160. Section: Sweet Relish, and Refusal to Let Go — Closing the Storehouses and the Rest Entirely

Resting on the lay class's form, sound, scent, taste, and touch, followed by craving for one's own kin and dependents, sweet relish arises. Resting on the monastic class's six bases, through the affliction of laziness and negligence, one is unable to let go of any of it at all.

Chinese Source

依在家品色聲香味觸，由愛味眷屬所隨逐故，發起甘味。依出家品六處，由懈怠放逸煩惱故，遍於一切不能捨離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on the lay class's form, sound, scent, taste, and touch, followed by craving for one's own kin and dependents, sweet relish arises. Resting on the monastic class's six bases, through the affliction of laziness and negligence, one is unable to let go of any of it at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra directly

A lay person's attachment centers especially on form, sound, scent, taste, and touch, above all a craving relish for one's own family and dependents; a monastic's attachment takes a different shape, the six faculties, through laziness and negligence conjoined with greed, hatred, and delusion, unable to be released from affliction across the board. A companion sūtra confirms this whole topic directly: should a monk or brahmin grow accustomed to the eye drawing near to form, that one falls under Māra's own dominion, giving rise to affliction beyond release, and the same for ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind — becoming accustomed, bound, attached, relishing, drawing near, gathering, seized, held, clung to as one's own, sought after, desired, thickened, intensified, unable to be let go — the very same teaching repeated across sūtras 360 through 373. All of this affliction, differently named across these six sections — storehouses, guarding, and concealing; relish, bondage, and union; the latent tendency, attachment to a group, and clinging; clinging to what belongs to self and conceit; longing and heavy attachment; sweet relish and inability to let go — describes a single underlying clinging viewed from many angles: wherever the six faculties cling to the six objects in any of these ways, that being falls under Māra's own dominion, unable to find liberation. With this, the storehouses and the rest — clinging's six full facets — are closed in their entirety, completing the unwholesome portion's fourth topic. The treatise turns next to what is welcomed and the rest, the fifth topic.

161. Section: Matching the Distinction — What Is Endearing, and What Is Pleasing

If it is an object of circumstance that brings delight, this is called what is endearing. If it is an object of experience that brings delight, this is called what is pleasing.

Chinese Source

若境界事可愛樂故，名為可愛。若領受事可愛樂故，名為可意。

Ru-Yi Translation

If it is an object of circumstance that brings delight, this is called what is endearing. If it is an object of experience that brings delight, this is called what is pleasing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When a pleasing circumstance itself appears, delighted in for what it is, this is what is endearing; when contact with that circumstance produces a wonderful, satisfying feeling, this is what is pleasing — the sūtras also call this latter one what is clung to.

162. Section: A Concluding Summary of Every Phrase — With a Companion Sūtra on Māra's Own Dominion

Thus, whatever is said to be pleasing — whether past, future, or present, whether a matter of circumstance or of experience — differs accordingly, and so is named what is to be longed for, or what is to be recollected, or what is to be indulged in; hence all these several distinct phrases are taught.

Chinese Source

如是所說諸可愛事，或過去、或未來、或現在、或境界、或領受，有差別故，或名可希求事、或名可尋思事、或名可耽著事，是故宣說如是一切諸句差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, whatever is said to be pleasing — whether past, future, or present, whether a matter of circumstance or of experience — differs accordingly, and so is named what is to be longed for, or what is to be recollected, or what is to be indulged in; hence all these several distinct phrases are taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra directly

Every pleasing circumstance, sorted by time and by kind, receives its own name: what is to be longed for and what is welcomed, spoken of the future; what is to be recollected and what is pleasurable, spoken of the past; and what is to be indulged in, what is endearing, and what is pleasing, spoken of the present. A companion sūtra confirms the same account, and adds a warning: a monk who delights in and praises a pleasing sight, sound, or other object, or who reacts with hatred and aversion to an unpleasing one, remains under Māra's own dominion, unable to find liberation; only one who neither clings to the pleasing as ultimately real nor reacts with aversion to the unpleasing can escape Māra's bondage and remain free of his dominion.

163. Section: A Second Reading of the Same Four Terms — Closing the Fourfold Distinction Entirely

And what is welcomed, spoken with reference to the future, means what is to be longed for. What is pleasurable, spoken with reference to the present, means what is presently desired and enjoyed, never satisfied. What is pleasing, spoken with reference to the past, means recollecting what accords with the mind's own memory. What is endearing, spoken with reference to a wondrous form's own mark, means being pleasing throughout all three times alike.

Chinese Source

又可欣者，約未來世，可希求故。可樂者，約現在世，現可欲樂無厭足故。可意者，約過去世，隨念可意而追憶故。可愛者，約妙色相，貫通三世皆可愛故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And what is welcomed, spoken with reference to the future, means what is to be longed for. What is pleasurable, spoken with reference to the present, means what is presently desired and enjoyed, never satisfied. What is pleasing, spoken with reference to the past, means recollecting what accords with the mind's own memory. What is endearing, spoken with reference to a wondrous form's own mark, means being pleasing throughout all three times alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second reading resorts the same four terms: what is welcomed now names the future, hoping to obtain something pleasing; what is pleasurable names the present, an object currently possessed yet never fully satisfying; what is pleasing names the past, recollecting a satisfying circumstance once experienced; and what is endearing names any sufficiently wondrous form, pleasing across past, present, and future alike, regardless of time. With this, the fourfold distinction of what is welcomed and the rest, read through both explanations, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a further explanation matching each term to its proper case.

164. Section: What Is Desired, and What Desire Draws Along

And what is desired means what pleases the mind and is kept in memory. What desire draws along means being bound to the desire realm, or according with the two distinct kinds of enjoying desire.

Chinese Source

又可欲者，悅意記念故。欲所引者，欲界繫故，或復隨順二種差別受用欲故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And what is desired means what pleases the mind and is kept in memory. What desire draws along means being bound to the desire realm, or according with the two distinct kinds of enjoying desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage and the Stage of Cultivated Wisdom

What is desired names recalling some once-glorious circumstance with lasting inner delight. What desire draws along names the enjoyment of the desire realm's own form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and sexual desire — the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 33, distinguishes two kinds here: affliction-desire, the inward craving and false discrimination that clings to an object once it is merely thought of, and object-desire, the outward objects themselves that the five senses actually encounter. The Stage of Cultivated Wisdom, fascicle 19, gives a verse for each: object-desire is described as "farmland, gold and silver, cattle, horses, pearls, bracelets, maidservants, and further desires — these are what people take delight in"; and affliction-desire as "clinging downward and debased, changing, spoiling, birthing every defilement, from this gathering every suffering, like a broken boat taking on water" — the inward craving and clinging that arises whenever the mind fastens onto any object at all.

165. Section: What Can Be Defiled and Clung To — Closing the Unwholesome Portion's Fifth Topic Entirely

What can be defiled and clung to means being the very place where greed operates.

Chinese Source

可染著者，貪處所故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What can be defiled and clung to means being the very place where greed operates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Pleasing form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and the desire between the sexes are exactly the ground on which the affliction of greed operates, giving rise to defiled clinging. With this, what is welcomed and the rest — the fourfold distinction, each term matched to its proper case, and desire's own further account — is closed in its entirety, completing the unwholesome portion's fifth topic. The treatise turns next to affliction, the sixth topic.

166. Section: Affliction Begins — Why It Is Named a Bond

Further, because it is able to unite with five kinds of matter, it is named a bond.

Chinese Source

復次，於五種事能和合故，說名為結。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, because it is able to unite with five kinds of matter, it is named a bond.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The unwholesome portion's sixth topic, affliction, is by far its most extensive, unfolding first through affliction's own intrinsic nature across twenty-four sections, the first of which, a bond, itself unfolds across five further parts. Affliction is named a bond because it is able to unite with five specific kinds of matter, each to be named in turn.

167. Section: The Five Kinds of Matter Listed

The five kinds of matter are: first, the matter that is bound; second, the matter that binds; third, the matter of fault; fourth, the matter of continuation in kind; fifth, the matter of destination.

Chinese Source

五種事者，一、所結事，二、能結事，三、罪過事，四、等流事，五、趣向事。

Ru-Yi Translation

The five kinds of matter are: first, the matter that is bound; second, the matter that binds; third, the matter of fault; fourth, the matter of continuation in kind; fifth, the matter of destination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These five kinds of matter name the different ways affliction unites with experience — its object, its own mutual reinforcement, the fault it produces, the momentum it carries into future lives, and the destinies it leads a being toward — each to be explained individually.

168. Section: The Matter That Is Bound — Affliction's Own Object

Whatever affliction takes as its object is called the matter that is bound. Why is this so? Because craving, hatred, and the rest each unite with their object according to its own distinct mark.

Chinese Source

諸結所緣，名所結事。所以者何？由愛恚等，各於所緣隨相差別而和合故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever affliction takes as its object is called the matter that is bound. Why is this so? Because craving, hatred, and the rest each unite with their object according to its own distinct mark.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Greed typically arises toward a pleasing object, while hatred typically arises toward an unpleasing one — different afflictions unite with different objects according to each object's own mark, and that object itself, whatever it may be, is called the matter that is bound.

169. Section: The Matter That Binds — Afflictions Drawing One Another Forth

Because those very afflictions draw one another forth in succession and unite together, this is called the matter that binds.

Chinese Source

即彼諸結展轉相引而和合故，名能結事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because those very afflictions draw one another forth in succession and unite together, this is called the matter that binds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One affliction often draws another in its wake — greed for wealth may bring stinginess, unwilling to give any of it away, and seeing another with more may then bring envy, unable to bear it. This mutual drawing-forth and uniting of afflictions is the matter that binds.

170. Section: The Matter of Fault — Affliction's Own Present Suffering

The causes and conditions of affliction, capable of producing fault in this very life, down to experiencing the mental suffering and sorrow born from it — through these causes and conditions uniting together, this is called the matter of fault.

Chinese Source

諸結因緣，於現法中能生過罪，乃至領受從彼所生心法憂苦。由此因緣，能和合故，名罪過事。

Ru-Yi Translation

The causes and conditions of affliction, capable of producing fault in this very life, down to experiencing the mental suffering and sorrow born from it — through these causes and conditions uniting together, this is called the matter of fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction leads a being to commit evil karma in this very life and thereby incur fault, down to the sorrow and suffering that fault itself produces — the matter of fault. This fault unfolds, broadly, in three further ways: by whom it harms — oneself, another, or both; by when it ripens — in this life, the next, or both; and by what it produces — present sorrow and suffering, or a future fall into the evil destinies.

171. Section: The Matter of Continuation in Kind — Fuel for Fiercer Affliction to Come

Because it unites as the cause that produces fierce craving and the rest in a future life, this is called the matter of continuation in kind.

Chinese Source

為當來世猛利貪等生成之因而和合故，名等流事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it unites as the cause that produces fierce craving and the rest in a future life, this is called the matter of continuation in kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A life steeped in greed, hatred, and delusion tends to carry that same tendency into the next life, only more forcefully — a kind of momentum passed from one life to the next, a habit continuing in kind, such that greed, hatred, and delusion tend to recur again and again across successive lives.

172. Section: The Matter of Destination — Affliction's Own Power to Drive Rebirth — Closing All Five Kinds of Matter

Because it is able to produce the five destinies, uniting a being within each of those destinies, this is called the matter of destination.

Chinese Source

能生五趣，於諸趣中能和合故，名趣向事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is able to produce the five destinies, uniting a being within each of those destinies, this is called the matter of destination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction drives karma, and karma drives birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, grief, and every further great suffering, keeping a being cycling through birth-and-death without end — through affliction's own continued momentum, a being cycles again and again through the human, heavenly, hell, hungry-ghost, and animal destinies, unable to find escape — the matter of destination. With this, all five kinds of matter that affliction unites with — the bound, the binding, fault, continuation in kind, and destination — are closed in their full enumeration. The treatise turns next to a more detailed account of the matter of fault.

173. Section: Fault in Detail Begins — Harm to Self, to Another, or to Both

Through these causes and conditions, one commits an evil deed oneself, and thereby meets with another's beating, binding, scolding, expulsion, injury, and every further manifold suffering that arises — this is called harming oneself. If one does not personally meet with this, but instead causes another to meet with it, this is called harming another. If, on account of that same cause, both oneself and another meet with it together, this is called harming both.

Chinese Source

由此因緣，自行惡行，遭他笞罰、縛錄、訶罵、驅擯、害等種種眾苦而生起故，名能自損。若不自遭，令他遭故，名能損他。若由彼故，自他俱遭，名能俱損。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through these causes and conditions, one commits an evil deed oneself, and thereby meets with another's beating, binding, scolding, expulsion, injury, and every further manifold suffering that arises — this is called harming oneself. If one does not personally meet with this, but instead causes another to meet with it, this is called harming another. If, on account of that same cause, both oneself and another meet with it together, this is called harming both.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The matter of fault is examined further, first by whom it harms: affliction may lead one to commit evil oneself and thereby suffer another's beating, imprisonment under the law, harsh scolding, or expulsion and injury from one's own community — harming oneself; or it may cause another to suffer such things without oneself being touched — harming another; or it may bring suffering to both parties together through the very same evil deed — harming both.

174. Section: Fault in the Present, in a Future Life, or in Both

Able to produce fault in this very life means, on account of it, one meets with manifold suffering exactly as described, yet is not certain to fall into any evil destiny. Able to produce fault in a future life means, on account of it, though unknown to others in this very life, it can still serve as the cause of a future fall into the evil destinies. Able to produce fault in both this life and a future one means possessing both of these together.

Chinese Source

能生現法罪者，謂由彼故，遭如所說種種苦事，然不決定往諸惡趣。能生後法罪者，謂由彼故，雖於現法他所不知，然能為因往諸惡趣。能生現法後法罪者，謂具二種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Able to produce fault in this very life means, on account of it, one meets with manifold suffering exactly as described, yet is not certain to fall into any evil destiny. Able to produce fault in a future life means, on account of it, though unknown to others in this very life, it can still serve as the cause of a future fall into the evil destinies. Able to produce fault in both this life and a future one means possessing both of these together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Second, fault is examined by when it ripens: some evil deeds bring suffering already in this life — punishment, scolding, expulsion — without necessarily leading to a future evil destiny; others remain hidden from everyone in this life, yet, being severe enough, still become the cause of a future fall into an evil destiny; and still others do both at once, bringing suffering now and a fall into an evil destiny later.

175. Section: Present Sorrow, and a Coming Evil Destiny

Filled with deep defiled attachment in the present life, one's wishes go unfulfilled, giving rise to manifold mental sorrow and suffering at length, and, further, one is bound for the evil destinies to come.

Chinese Source

於現法中多懷染著，所欲不遂，廣生種種心法憂苦，復於當來往諸惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Filled with deep defiled attachment in the present life, one's wishes go unfulfilled, giving rise to manifold mental sorrow and suffering at length, and, further, one is bound for the evil destinies to come.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Third, fault is examined by what it produces: a mind deeply attached in the present, its wishes thwarted, suffers manifold sorrow and mental anguish right now — and that very same defiled craving, left unresolved, becomes the seed of a future fall into the evil destinies as well, present sorrow and future ruin bound together as one continuous fault.

176. Section: A Concluding Statement — Nine Bonds Named as Foremost — Closing "Bond" Entirely

Though bonds are boundless in number, speaking of what is foremost among them, there are, in brief, nine bonds.

Chinese Source

結雖無量，就勝而言，略有九結。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though bonds are boundless in number, speaking of what is foremost among them, there are, in brief, nine bonds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra directly

A companion sūtra records the venerable Śāriputra's own answer to the question "what is a bond?": nine bonds — the bond of craving, the bond of hatred, the bond of conceit, the bond of ignorance, the bond of view, the bond of taking up another's view, the bond of doubt, the bond of envy, and the bond of stinginess — each of which unites a being with the five kinds of matter just explained. Asked whether any path exists to cultivate and thoroughly cultivate in order to cut off these bonds, Śāriputra answered: yes, the noble eightfold path, right view through right concentration. With this, bond — its name, the five kinds of matter it unites with, their detailed marks, fault examined in detail, and this concluding ninefold list — is closed in its entirety, completing the first of affliction's twenty-four intrinsic-nature synonyms. The treatise turns next to fetter, the second synonym.

177. Section: Fetter Begins — Three Fetters Tied to Three Feelings

And further, with reference to being unable to follow one's own wish, three fetters are spoken of: greed, hatred, and delusion, each resting on one of the three feelings.

Chinese Source

又約不隨所欲義故，說有三縛，謂貪瞋癡，依三受故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, with reference to being unable to follow one's own wish, three fetters are spoken of: greed, hatred, and delusion, each resting on one of the three feelings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's second synonym, fetter, names the way affliction prevents a being from freely pursuing giving, discipline, and cultivation as they genuinely wish — greed resting on pleasant feeling, hatred resting on painful feeling, and delusion resting on neutral feeling, the mind fettered by all three, unable to move freely toward the good.

178. Section: Why It Is Named a Fetter — Wishing to Escape, Yet Unable — Closing Fetter Entirely

Through those very causes and conditions, though wishing to be free of them, one cannot become free — hence it is named a fetter.

Chinese Source

由彼因緣，雖欲脫彼而不能脫，故名為縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through those very causes and conditions, though wishing to be free of them, one cannot become free — hence it is named a fetter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations

Bound by the causes and conditions of greed, hatred, and delusion, a being wishes to be free of these three poisons yet cannot manage it, the mind held fast by its own affliction, unable to move freely — hence fetter. The Determinations, fascicle 66, explain the three feelings' own role directly: resting on pleasant feeling, greed correspondingly increases, giving rise to defiled attachment; resting on painful feeling, hatred correspondingly increases, giving rise to resentment and anger; and resting on neutral feeling, delusion correspondingly increases, giving rise to inversion — taking the impermanent formations of one's own being as permanent, the painful as pleasurable, the impure as pure, and the selfless as possessing self. With this, fetter — its three kinds and the reason for its name — is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's second synonym. The treatise turns next to the latent tendency, the third synonym.

179. Section: The Latent Tendency Begins — Why It Is So Named

And further, because it is followed by the coarse seed proper to the class of affliction, it is named the latent tendency — meaning that which follows and binds, and meaning what is subtle.

Chinese Source

又煩惱品麤重種子之所隨逐，說名隨眠，是隨縛義、是微細義。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is followed by the coarse seed proper to the class of affliction, it is named the latent tendency — meaning that which follows and binds, and meaning what is subtle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's third synonym, the latent tendency, names the seed that always accompanies affliction's own active manifestation — coarse, meaning without capability, rigid and unyielding, since an afflicted mind cannot settle or accomplish anything. Called a seed for its own subtlety, and called a latent tendency because it lies dormant, needing only the slightest disturbance to stir into activity again, exactly as a sleeping computer wakes at the smallest touch of the mouse.

180. Section: Seven Kinds of Latent Tendency — Closing the Latent Tendency Entirely

Taking only its root, there are but seven kinds.

Chinese Source

取其根本，但有七種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Taking only its root, there are but seven kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

The Compendium of Bases, fascicle 89, explains these seven root latent tendencies through the distinction between those not yet free of desire and those already free of it: one not yet free of desire carries the latent tendencies of desire-realm craving and hatred, since wanting what cannot be had breeds resentment; one already free of desire carries the latent tendency of existence-craving instead, its object now meditative absorption itself rather than sense pleasure; and both alike, whether free of desire or not, carry four further latent tendencies common to both — conceit, ignorance, view, and doubt, since both a desire-bound being and a meditator can equally grow proud, cling rigidly to their own opinion as correct, or waver in indecision. In sum, the seven are: desire-realm craving, hatred, existence-craving (belonging to the form and formless realms), and, common to all three realms, conceit, ignorance, view, and doubt. With this, the latent tendency — its name and its seven root kinds — is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's third synonym. The treatise turns next to secondary affliction, the fourth synonym.

181. Section: Secondary Affliction Begins — Why It Is So Named

And further, because it is born from affliction, because it draws near to affliction, and because it follows affliction in disturbing the mind, it is named secondary affliction.

Chinese Source

又從煩惱生故，親近煩惱故，隨惱亂心故，名隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is born from affliction, because it draws near to affliction, and because it follows affliction in disturbing the mind, it is named secondary affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's fourth synonym, secondary affliction, names the further afflictions born from a root affliction — one affliction drawing forth another, growing intimate with it, following it to disturb the mind still further.

182. Section: Its Very Substance — Every Defiled Mental Dharma Apart From the Seven Latent Tendencies — Closing Secondary Affliction Entirely

Apart from the seven latent tendencies, every remaining defiled mental dharma is a secondary affliction.

Chinese Source

除七隨眠，所餘一切染汙心法皆隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Apart from the seven latent tendencies, every remaining defiled mental dharma is a secondary affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond the seven root latent tendencies already named — desire-realm craving, hatred, existence-craving, conceit, ignorance, view, and doubt — every other defiled mental factor counts as a secondary affliction, each arising in dependence on some root affliction, one defiled dharma giving rise to further defiled dharmas in a chain. With this, secondary affliction — its name and its own substance — is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's fourth synonym. The treatise turns next to entanglement, the fifth synonym.

183. Section: Entanglement Begins — Why It Is So Named

And further, because it manifests and continues without any break, it is named entanglement.

Chinese Source

又現起相續無斷絕義，說名為纏。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it manifests and continues without any break, it is named entanglement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's fifth synonym, entanglement, names affliction's own moment-to-moment continuity, arising and ceasing without interruption, one moment binding into the next like a rope's own strands.

184. Section: Eight Kinds of Entanglement, Each Obstructing a Different Training — Closing Entanglement Entirely

Entanglement is of eight kinds: shamelessness and the rest.

Chinese Source

纏有八種，謂無慚等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Entanglement is of eight kinds: shamelessness and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

The Compendium of Bases, fascicle 89, sorts these eight kinds of entanglement across four different trainings, each pair obstructing its own: while training in the higher discipline, shamelessness and lack of moral dread repeatedly manifest as obstacles; while training in the higher mind (meditative concentration), dullness and drowsiness repeatedly manifest as obstacles; while training in the higher wisdom, discerning the dharma, restlessness and regret repeatedly manifest as obstacles; and while sharing wealth and dharma with fellow practitioners, envy and stinginess repeatedly manifest as obstacles. With this, entanglement — its name and its eight kinds, each tied to a different training — is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's fifth synonym. The treatise turns next to the stump, the sixth synonym.

185. Section: The Stump Begins — Rendering Body and Mind Incapable, Like a Salt-Ruined Field

And further, because it is able to turn into an ever more intense continuity of the coarsest kind, and because it renders body and mind incapable, it is called the stump — like a saline field, unfit for plowing or planting.

Chinese Source

又彼能令轉成上品相續起故，能令身心無堪能故，說為株杌。如舄鹵田，不任耕植。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to turn into an ever more intense continuity of the coarsest kind, and because it renders body and mind incapable, it is called the stump — like a saline field, unfit for plowing or planting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's sixth synonym, the stump, names affliction's continuity intensifying into ever fiercer forms, leaving body and mind incapable of anything wholesome — like a stump, a root left jutting from the ground with no branches above it, ruining a field of good crops wherever it is found; or like a saline field, its soil too salt-laden to plant or harvest anything at all — affliction likewise ruins the field of merit a being might otherwise cultivate.

186. Section: Five Kinds of Stump — the Five Grounds Where Doubt Takes Root

And further, because their basis differs, the doubt each produces differs accordingly, and so five mental stumps are spoken of.

Chinese Source

又處所別故，彼所生疑有差別故，說五心株。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because their basis differs, the doubt each produces differs accordingly, and so five mental stumps are spoken of.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Determined Terms

Doubt arises from five distinct grounds: doubt toward the great teacher, from lacking pure faith; doubt toward the true dharma, from failing to diligently hear and reflect on it; doubt toward the training itself, from ignorance born of not studying it; doubt toward instruction and admonition, from an inability to trust it; and doubt toward wise, celibate practitioners, from dwelling on their apparent faults. Resting on any of these five grounds, hesitant doubt arises — hence five mental stumps, called five mental seedlings in the Compendium of Determined Terms, different in name but identical in meaning.

187. Section: Three Kinds of Stump — Sorted by Greed, Hatred, and Delusion — Closing the Stump Entirely

Because greed and the rest differ from one another, three kinds are spoken of.

Chinese Source

貪等別故，說有三種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because greed and the rest differ from one another, three kinds are spoken of.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage With Both Inquiry and Analysis

Sorted instead by greed, hatred, and delusion themselves, each its own distinct stump, three kinds of stump are named — as the Stage With Both Inquiry and Analysis, fascicle 8, likewise explains. With this, the stump — its name, its five grounds of doubt, and its three kinds sorted by the three poisons — is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's sixth synonym. The treatise turns next to stain, the seventh synonym.

188. Section: Stain — Closing the Seventh Synonym

And further, because it is able to render the mind impure, it is named stain.

Chinese Source

又彼能令不清淨故，說名為垢。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to render the mind impure, it is named stain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's seventh synonym, stain, names simply and directly affliction's own polluting nature, rendering an otherwise pure mind impure. With this, stain is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's seventh synonym. The treatise turns next to nīga, the eighth synonym.

189. Section: Nīga Begins — Its Constant Flowing

And further, because it constantly flows forth at every gate, it is named nīga.

Chinese Source

又於諸處門，常流注故，名為儞伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it constantly flows forth at every gate, it is named nīga.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's eighth synonym transliterates a Sanskrit term meaning constant flow: a being continually pours affliction in and out through the six sense-gates — drawn in from an outer object, or flowing out through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Anger, for instance, flows out visibly the instant the eyes widen and the mouth clenches — affliction's own constant flow at every gate.

190. Section: Nīga Also Names Its Constant Power to Harm — Closing Nīga Entirely

Because it is constantly able to cause harm, it is likewise named nīga.

Chinese Source

常能害故，亦名儞伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is constantly able to cause harm, it is likewise named nīga.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction constantly harms both oneself and others alike, a second reason for the same name. With this, nīga — its constant flowing and its constant power to harm — is closed in its entirety, completing affliction's eighth synonym. The treatise turns next to the arrow, the ninth synonym.

191. Section: The Arrow — Closing the Ninth Synonym

And further, because it is able to leave the mind unable to settle in stillness, it is named the arrow — like a poisoned arrow, whose wound leaves one largely unable to find stillness so long as it remains unpulled.

Chinese Source

又彼能令不寂靜故，說名為箭。如被毒箭，若未拔時多不寂靜。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to leave the mind unable to settle in stillness, it is named the arrow — like a poisoned arrow, whose wound leaves one largely unable to find stillness so long as it remains unpulled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's ninth synonym compares its presence to a poisoned arrow's own wound — body and mind remain unable to settle so long as the arrow, like the affliction itself, is not yet pulled free. With this, the arrow is closed, completing affliction's ninth synonym. The treatise turns next to possession, the tenth synonym.

192. Section: Possession — Closing the Tenth Synonym

And further, because it obstructs generosity, and because it carries idle proliferation, it is named possession.

Chinese Source

又能障捨故，有戲論故，名為所有。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it obstructs generosity, and because it carries idle proliferation, it is named possession.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

Affliction is said to take possession of the resultant body it depends on — greed, hatred, and delusion the possessor, the five aggregates the possessed — dominating a being's own body, speech, and mind toward further karma, and in doing so obstructing generosity itself. It is likewise named possession because its own conceptual proliferation leaves body, speech, and mind restless, obstructing the equanimity meditative concentration requires. The Compendium of Bases, fascicle 87, glosses possession as meaning the utterly base and filthy — affliction being exactly that.

193. Section: Evil Conduct — Closing the Eleventh Synonym

And further, because unlawful conduct and unbalanced conduct manifest before it, it is named evil conduct.

Chinese Source

又非法行、不平等行現在前故，說名惡行。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because unlawful conduct and unbalanced conduct manifest before it, it is named evil conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations

Once affliction is active, it accords with unwholesome dharmas and produces unlawful conduct — the cause; and that unlawful conduct, mistaking the wrong cause for the right one in hope of a pleasing result, instead produces an unbalanced, unpleasing result, as with certain non-Buddhist ascetic practices. The Determinations, Stage of Concentrated Equipoise, fascicle 62, confirm: unlawful conduct is so named because it is not genuinely wholesome; unbalanced conduct is so named because its result is not genuinely pleasing.

194. Section: Root — Closing the Twelfth Synonym

And further, because it is able to give rise together to every affliction and every evil conduct, it is named root.

Chinese Source

又能等起一切煩惱、諸惡行故，說名為根。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to give rise together to every affliction and every evil conduct, it is named root.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage With Both Inquiry and Analysis and the Determinations

The Stage With Both Inquiry and Analysis, fascicle 8, names three roots — greed, hatred, and delusion — from which every further affliction, and every unwholesome deed capable of leading a being to the evil destinies, ultimately springs. Why single out only these three as roots, among affliction's many kinds? The Determinations, fascicle 58, explain: karma has, in brief, three originating causes — craving for pleasure, harming another, and clinging to a false teaching as established truth — and greed, hatred, and delusion correspond exactly, each to one of these three causes.

195. Section: Outflow — Closing the Thirteenth Synonym

And further, because it is able to give rise to a life yet to come, it is named outflow.

Chinese Source

又能出生當來生故，說名為漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to give rise to a life yet to come, it is named outflow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā

Affliction gives rise to craving and grasping, craving and grasping give rise to existence, existence gives rise to birth, and birth gives rise to aging and death — tracing the chain back to its root, affliction is always the cause; an ordinary being creates karma through affliction, that karma ripens into result, and a further life is thereby drawn forth, hence affliction alone is named outflow. Why is only affliction named outflow, and not karma itself? The Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā, fascicle 47, explains: karma is not fixed in its effect — some karma keeps a being long in birth-and-death, while other karma actually counters birth-and-death's own affliction — so karma alone cannot bear this name; and further, karma is itself rooted in affliction, since karma is never truly abandoned without affliction first being cut off, which is exactly why only affliction is called outflow.

196. Section: Deprivation — Closing the Fourteenth Synonym

And further, because, once birth has occurred, aging, death, and the rest bring deprivation, it is named deprivation.

Chinese Source

又既生已，由老死等令匱乏故，說名為匱。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because, once birth has occurred, aging, death, and the rest bring deprivation, it is named deprivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's fourteenth synonym, deprivation, names the sense of lack that aging, sickness, and death bring — old age brings declining vigor, strength, faculties, enjoyment, and lifespan itself, no longer whole as before; sickness ruins a once-pleasing bodily form, deepens sorrow, denies the enjoyment of what was once loved, and forces the further unpleasantness of medical treatment; and all of this leaves most people feeling that life itself has come up short, however rich or poor, however fair or plain, however excellent or humble one may be — birth is always followed by death. Yet it is precisely affliction that keeps beings endlessly indulging in desire, never satisfied, forever troubled by this very sense of deprivation — hence affliction is also called deprivation.

197. Section: Burning — Closing the Fifteenth Synonym

And further, because the union of what is unloved, the separation of what is loved, and craving for gain all burn a being, it is named burning.

Chinese Source

又非愛合會、所愛乖離、貪求利養所燒然故，說名為燒。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because the union of what is unloved, the separation of what is loved, and craving for gain all burn a being, it is named burning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8

Whenever an unwanted circumstance is forced upon a being, a beloved one is lost, or gain is craved and not obtained, affliction manifests and burns body and mind alike — hence burning. Craving here, distinct from mere indulgence, names pursuing what has not yet been gained through active striving, as this same fascicle later explains, while indulgence names clinging firmly to what has already been gained without further striving; fascicle 8 likewise explains burning as what leaves desire perpetually deprived. This synonym roughly corresponds to the first seven of the eight sufferings: outflow corresponds to the suffering of birth; deprivation, to the sufferings of aging, sickness, and death; and burning, to the sufferings of meeting the unloved, parting from the loved, and not obtaining what is sought — affliction truly does unite with suffering at every turn.

198. Section: Distress — Closing the Sixteenth Synonym

And further, because it is able to bring sorrow, lament, grief, and distress, it is named distress.

Chinese Source

又能令愁歎憂苦惱故，說名為惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to bring sorrow, lament, grief, and distress, it is named distress.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8

When affliction arises, it plunges a being into sorrow, lamentation, and grief — hence distress; fascicle 8 explains it as bringing about decline and loss, corresponding chiefly to the suffering of change. Blazing fire, burning, and distress differ subtly: blazing fire describes the intensity of defiled clinging the moment a desired object is actually obtained; burning describes the affliction of deprivation before a desired object is obtained at all; and distress describes the suffering that follows once an already-obtained object later changes and is lost — three distinct moments in the same underlying process, to be distinguished further below.

199. Section: Torrent — Closing the Seventeenth Synonym

And further, because it is able to carry a being downstream, adrift and drowning, it is named torrent.

Chinese Source

又能令順流而漂溺故，說名暴流。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to carry a being downstream, adrift and drowning, it is named torrent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicles 8 and 17

Affliction carries a being helplessly downstream along birth-and-death's own current, adrift and drowning, scarcely able to find escape — hence torrent. Fascicle 8 names four torrents: the torrent of desire, of existence, of view, and of ignorance; fascicle 17 instead names six, sorted by the six faculties, six consciousnesses, and six objects endlessly intermingling from dawn to dusk, birth to death, giving rise to boundless affliction exactly as a waterfall's own current never ceases — two differing classifications of the very same underlying torrent.

200. Section: Yoke — Closing the Eighteenth Synonym

And further, because, resting on what has come before, it serves as the foremost means by which birth-and-death continues to flow in this present life, it is named yoke.

Chinese Source

又依前際，能為現法生死流轉勝方便故，說名為軛。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because, resting on what has come before, it serves as the foremost means by which birth-and-death continues to flow in this present life, it is named yoke.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A past life's own affliction becomes the very means by which present life continues — this present existence was itself obtained because affliction, in the past, drove karma that now bears its fruit. Hence affliction is called yoke, binding a being fast to birth-and-death's own torrent exactly as a yoke binds an ox or horse to the cart it cannot escape pulling.

201. Section: Grasping — Closing the Nineteenth Synonym

And further, resting on the present, because it serves as the foremost means toward the future, it is named grasping.

Chinese Source

又依現在，能為未來勝方便故，說名為取。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, resting on the present, because it serves as the foremost means toward the future, it is named grasping.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This present life's own unresolved affliction becomes the foremost means by which a further existence arises in the current of birth-and-death yet to come — hence affliction is also named grasping, typically divided into four: the grasping of desire, of view, of rules and vows, and of self-assertion.

202. Section: Bondage — Closing the Twentieth Synonym

And further, because it is difficult to be released from, it is named bondage.

Chinese Source

又難解故，說名為繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is difficult to be released from, it is named bondage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8

Affliction is difficult to escape — cutting off its own seed above all is no easy task — hence it is also named bondage; fascicle 8 confirms this exact wording, and further names four kinds: bodily bondage through greed, bodily bondage through hatred, bodily bondage through clinging to rules and vows, and bodily bondage through clinging to this-alone-is-true views.

203. Section: Hindrance — Closing the Twenty-First Synonym

And further, because it is able to obstruct wisdom with regard to whatever is to be known, it is named hindrance.

Chinese Source

又於所知事能障智故，說名為蓋。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to obstruct wisdom with regard to whatever is to be known, it is named hindrance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When affliction arises, it leaves a being unable to penetrate the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases clearly — obstructing wisdom itself and preventing any wholesome mind from unfolding, hence hindrance. With this, hindrance is closed, completing affliction's twenty-first synonym. The treatise turns next to the five fetters, the twenty-second synonym.

204. Section: The Five Fetters Begin — the Lower Fetters Distinguished Two Ways

And further, relative to the form and formless realms, the desire realm is the lower portion; relative to the path of cultivation, the path of seeing is the lower portion.

Chinese Source

又望色無色界，欲界為下分；望其修道，見道為下分。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, relative to the form and formless realms, the desire realm is the lower portion; relative to the path of cultivation, the path of seeing is the lower portion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's twenty-second synonym, the five fetters, unfolds in two groups, lower and upper. The lower fetters carry a twofold mark: first, relative to the higher form and formless realms, the desire realm's own affliction is the "lower" portion; second, relative to the path of cultivation, whatever affliction the path of seeing cuts off is likewise the "lower" portion.

205. Section: Why They Are Named as They Are

Resting on this twofold lower distinction, according to what applies, these are named the five lower obstructions, and likewise the five lower fetters.

Chinese Source

由約此二下分差別，隨其所應，說名五下分障，亦名五下分結。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on this twofold lower distinction, according to what applies, these are named the five lower obstructions, and likewise the five lower fetters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whichever of the two lower marks applies to a given affliction, that affliction earns the name lower obstruction or lower fetter accordingly — the naming follows whichever distinction actually fits.

206. Section: The Five Upper Fetters — Closing the Five Fetters Entirely

Contrary to this, it should be known that the five upper fetters are likewise spoken of.

Chinese Source

與此相違，當知說有五上分結。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contrary to this, it should be known that the five upper fetters are likewise spoken of.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation

Opposite the lower fetters, the five upper fetters belong to the form and formless realms and are cut off only through the path of cultivation: craving for the form realm, craving for the formless realm, restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — a noble one beyond the third fruit must cut off all five to realize arhatship, while cutting off only the five lower fetters yields merely the third fruit. The Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation clarifies which lower fetter belongs to which of the two lower marks: greed and hatred, both belonging to the desire realm, fall under the realm-based distinction, spanning both distinctly-arisen and innately-arisen forms; while the view of a perishable self, clinging to rules and vows, and doubt — three afflictions of view — fall under the path-based distinction, cut off at the path of seeing. The text's own phrase "according to what applies" simply leaves this correspondence for the reader to infer rather than spelling it out. Since the three view-afflictions are already cut off at the path of seeing, only innately-arisen greed and hatred remain to be cut off through the path of cultivation before a practitioner can fully exhaust all five lower fetters and realize the third fruit. With this, the five fetters — the two lower marks, their naming, and the five upper fetters — are closed in their entirety, completing affliction's twenty-second synonym. The treatise turns next to the forest, the twenty-third synonym.

207. Section: The Forest — Closing the Twenty-Third Synonym

And so-called forest means the very nature that gives rise to manifold aggregated suffering. Through fond attachment, it continues to grow — hence it is named a dense forest.

Chinese Source

又言林者，能生種種苦蘊體性。由親愛故，彼得增長，說名稠林。

Ru-Yi Translation

And so-called forest means the very nature that gives rise to manifold aggregated suffering. Through fond attachment, it continues to grow — hence it is named a dense forest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8

Affliction leads to karma, and karma leads to suffering's own resultant fruit — every single thought is itself a seed of cause and effect, and a single moment's affliction, left unchecked, can produce a mighty fruit of suffering; left unchecked day after day, that suffering accumulates beyond counting, tree upon tree gathering into a vast forest. And because beings hold their own affliction dear, never recognizing its danger — often not even noticing anger has arisen, believing it justified — that same affliction only continues to grow, once seen through the lens of the dharma. A bodhisattva fears the cause; an ordinary being fears only the fruit — a disciple of the Buddha should hold right mindfulness always, watching every arising thought with care, since each one, however small, bears its own fruit of suffering. Fascicle 8 confirms: countless great trees of self gathered together are called a dense forest — affliction draws a being through one resultant body after another across the five destinies, as numerous and thick as trees in a vast forest, hence also named a dense forest.

208. Section: Contention — Closing All Twenty-Four Intrinsic-Nature Synonyms of Affliction in Their Entirety

And further, because it is able to give rise to manifold disputes, lawsuits, and every kind of anger and rivalry, it is named contention.

Chinese Source

又能發起諸鬥訟等種種忿競，故名為諍。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because it is able to give rise to manifold disputes, lawsuits, and every kind of anger and rivalry, it is named contention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction unsettles the mind, and that very imbalance readily gives rise to disputes, lawsuits, anger, and rivalry — hence affliction is also named contention: driven by craving-affliction, a being commits every manner of evil in the scramble for power and gain; driven by view-affliction, a being clings stubbornly to their own opinion, breeding further dispute in its wake. With this, contention is closed, completing affliction's twenty-fourth and final intrinsic-nature synonym — and with it, all twenty-four synonyms comprising affliction's own intrinsic nature are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the meaning of reproach, affliction's second major section.

209. Section: The Meaning of Reproach Begins — Darkness

Because it is what light itself counteracts, it is named darkness.

Chinese Source

明所治故，說名為黑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is what light itself counteracts, it is named darkness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction's second major section, the meaning of reproach, unfolds across six sections, each naming a further term of censure. Wisdom counteracts affliction exactly as light counteracts darkness — a being lost in affliction cannot see the road ahead, risking every manner of danger, until wisdom's own light, like a single lamp dispelling a thousand years of darkness, or the sun of wisdom clearing away ten thousand years of confusion, frees a being from suffering and affliction's own bondage at once.

210. Section: Meaninglessness

Because it is able to draw forth suffering, it is named meaninglessness.

Chinese Source

能引苦故，說名無義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is able to draw forth suffering, it is named meaninglessness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of Cultivated Wisdom

Affliction brings no benefit whatsoever, only manifold suffering — hence meaninglessness. The Stage of Cultivated Wisdom, fascicle 19, urges swiftly abandoning the ten unwholesome deeds in this present life and every meaningless cause of affliction that leads toward the three evil destinies, cultivating instead the roots and provisions of merit that lead to the fortunate destinies of gods and humans.

211. Section: Base Inferiority

Because it is of no use, it is named base inferiority.

Chinese Source

無所用故，說名弊下。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is of no use, it is named base inferiority.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Affliction offers a being no genuine benefit at all — only ruin, burning, and manifold suffering, no help whatsoever toward rising higher or growing more wholesome — hence base inferiority.

212. Section: Culpability

Because its very nature is defiled, it is named culpability.

Chinese Source

性染汙故，說名有罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because its very nature is defiled, it is named culpability.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations

Affliction's own nature is defiled — even before any word or deed expresses it, the mind is already stained the instant it arises — hence culpability. The Determinations, Stage With Both Inquiry and Analysis, fascicle 58, pose the question directly: since the World-Honored One taught that falsely discriminated craving is called a person's own desire, why is only affliction-desire, and not object-desire, named desire in this sense? Because affliction-desire's very nature is defiled.

213. Section: What Should Be Kept Distant — Closing the First Five of the Meaning of Reproach

Because it should not be drawn near, it is named what should be kept distant.

Chinese Source

不應習近故，說名應遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it should not be drawn near, it is named what should be kept distant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner should never draw affliction close — hence what should be kept distant. Keeping it at a distance first requires recognizing it clearly; without watching one's own mind and noticing affliction the instant it stirs, there is no keeping it at bay at all — only sustained mindfulness, proper attention, and genuine recollection of the true dharma can actually hold it at a distance. With this, the first five terms under the meaning of reproach — darkness, meaninglessness, base inferiority, culpability, and what should be kept distant — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the sixth and final term, itself unfolding through several further readings.

214. Section: Breaking the Precepts Begins — the First Reading

Violating the pure precepts one has received is named breaking the precepts.

Chinese Source

毀犯所受清淨戒故，名突尸羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Violating the pure precepts one has received is named breaking the precepts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The meaning of reproach's sixth and final term unfolds across a first reading of seven further sub-terms, itself examining affliction alongside the evil karma it produces. "Duḥśīla," transliterated here, joins "duṣ," meaning to violate or forsake, with "śīla," the pure, cooling precepts — so a monastic or lay practitioner who, having received the precepts, then goes on to violate them, is named one who breaks the precepts.

215. Section: Unwholesome Dharmas — Three Broad Kinds

And unwholesome dharmas mean the most intense shamelessness and lack of moral dread, faithlessness toward the Buddha and the rest, slandering the worthy and the noble, joined with wrong view; or manifold unwholesome dharmas actually manifesting; or greed, hatred, and further such states — spoken of at length.

Chinese Source

又惡法者，謂極猛利無慚無愧，不信佛等，毀謗賢聖，邪見相應故；或復種種惡法現行故；又有貪欲、瞋恚心等，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

And unwholesome dharmas mean the most intense shamelessness and lack of moral dread, faithlessness toward the Buddha and the rest, slandering the worthy and the noble, joined with wrong view; or manifold unwholesome dharmas actually manifesting; or greed, hatred, and further such states — spoken of at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Unwholesome dharmas fall broadly into three kinds: intense shamelessness and lack of moral dread, unable to feel remorse or embarrassment even after committing a fault others have exposed, which alone makes upholding discipline impossible; faithlessness toward the three jewels and even slander of the worthy and noble, joined with distorted views of cause and effect — the root error, since wrong thought always precedes wrong action, body and speech inevitably following a corrupted mind; and the ten unwholesome deeds themselves — greed, hatred, delusion, killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and the rest — arising even after the precepts have been received, defiling body, speech, and mind alike. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss "and the rest" as including delusion as well, the three poisons together capable of drawing forth every further major, intermediate, and minor secondary affliction in an ever-expanding chain, one dharma giving rise to the next.

216. Section: Rotten Within — the Hollow Timber

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One who wears a monastic's outward form while the mind inside remains filled with greed, hatred, and delusion, utterly lacking the noble path, is like a mighty timber that looks firm and fine from without, yet has been hollowed out by insects within — a shell that appears solid but holds nothing real at all.

217. Section: Base Origin — Born From What Is Lowly

Base origin is explained at length under the topic of base and non-base origin, as elsewhere.

Chinese Source

下產生者，廣如下產及非下產法門中說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Base origin is explained at length under the topic of base and non-base origin, as elsewhere.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

One who violates the precepts without any remorse, possessing none of the seven noble treasures, only affliction and evil karma to call their own, has for their whole domain nothing but base wealth and the ongoing cycle of birth-and-death — base origin. What is not base origin is instead the noble path itself, never lowly, always excellent. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes recall the earlier distinction drawn in this same fascicle, under perfected higher rebirth: an ordinary being's lowlier sonship, born through the birth canal, through impure seed and blood, through desire and craving, is exactly base origin; while a true child of the great teacher — born of the womb, the mouth, the dharma — is non-base origin.

218. Section: A Snail in Clean Water — Fouling What Once Nourished

A snail in clean water means what one has heard resembles water, dispelling the thirst of craving. If a monastic violates the training precepts and the like, this is like a snail fouling clean water — hence, like water with a snail in it, unfit to drink, it should be shunned.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

A snail in clean water means what one has heard resembles water, dispelling the thirst of craving. If a monastic violates the training precepts and the like, this is like a snail fouling clean water — hence, like water with a snail in it, unfit to drink, it should be shunned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The dharma one hears is like pure water, capable of washing away craving, hatred, and delusion exactly as ambrosial rain extinguishes affliction's own blaze — a practitioner should drink it often. But a monastic who violates the precepts is like a snail befouling an entire basin of clean water — such a person's own broken conduct stains the whole teaching, exactly as worldly onlookers, seeing a practitioner break the precepts, come to slander the three jewels themselves; hence such conduct, like water fouled by a snail, should be shunned.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

A conch-voiced dog means a monastic who habitually practices evil conduct, and who, upon receiving offerings of bedding and the rest, boasts of holding the very highest monastic seniority.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Some monastics, habitually steeped in evil conduct, neglecting their precepts and their monastic duty, act on all manner of greed, hatred, and delusion, yet when receiving offerings of bedding, medicine, and the like from lay supporters, loudly claim the highest seniority for themselves, deserving the very best — a conch-voiced dog. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes tell of a dog whose bark rings out as clear and bright as a conch shell's own sound, yet it remains, in truth, only a dog; evil monastics are the same, speaking eloquently of the dharma while acting entirely against it — fine words in the air, base conduct on the ground, good speech joined to evil deeds.

Falsely called a renunciant means already having lost one's monastic standing while still claiming to hold it — truly harboring evil desires, yet declaring oneself the very foremost, genuine renunciant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One who has already lost the true standing of a monastic, harboring greed, hatred, and delusion within, yet claims to be a genuine renunciant realizing the ultimate truth, commits a grave falsehood. And one still bound by both affliction-desire and object-desire, never having genuinely renounced sexual impurity, who nonetheless claims to have already renounced desire, commits the very same kind of falsehood.

221. Section: Falsely Claiming Chastity, and Falsely Claiming Renunciant Status — Closing the First Reading of "Breaking the Precepts" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

And, having lost one's monastic nature, still claiming to possess it, this is named falsely claiming chastity.

And, being genuinely no renunciant, yet declaring oneself the very foremost, true renunciant, this is named falsely claiming renunciant status.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the previous two terms named the underlying fault, these two name the false claim itself directly: having lost the genuine nature of a monastic, still insisting one possesses it is falsely claiming chastity; and never having realized the noble path at all, still declaring oneself the very first, truest renunciant, is falsely claiming renunciant status. With this, breaking the precepts — all seven sub-terms of its first reading, from violating the received precepts through falsely claiming renunciant status — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a second reading of these same seven terms.

222. Section: A Second Reading Begins — Giving Back the Precepts, and Taking Back What Was Once Renounced

And further, giving up what one has received is named breaking the precepts.

And taking back what one had earlier given up as an unwholesome dharma is named an unwholesome dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Read a second way: breaking the precepts now names simply returning to lay life, giving back what was received; and an unwholesome dharma now names taking back a fault one had earlier renounced — as when someone who did not originally leave home for fame or gain later comes to crave exactly that.

223. Section: Rotten Within, and Base Origin — Read a Second Way

Where outward form and inward aspiration fail to match one another, this cause and condition is named rotten within. And following wherever one's own desire leads, in both action and rest, is named base origin.

Chinese Source

形相意樂互不相稱，由是因緣，名內朽敗。

隨其所欲而行住故，名下產生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Where outward form and inward aspiration fail to match one another, this cause and condition is named rotten within.

And following wherever one's own desire leads, in both action and rest, is named base origin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the first reading of "rotten within" described the resultant mark — a monastic form concealing an empty interior — this second reading examines its cause: one who has forgotten the original vow that inspired renunciation, no longer aspiring to cultivate discipline, concentration, and wisdom or extinguish greed, hatred, and delusion, has lost the very noble-path dharma a monastic ought to possess — outward form no longer matching inward aspiration at all. And base origin, read this second way, names simply indulging whatever desire arises, resting content in that indulgence rather than subduing it — living alone tends toward this laxity more easily than communal life, since a solitary practitioner lacks the many watchful eyes a community provides, and without especially strong mindfulness and diligence, conduct readily drifts toward unwholesome ways, indulgent desire compounding into base origin even without any formal violation of the precepts.

224. Section: Fouling What One Heard, and the Conch-Voiced Dog — Read a Second Way

Disparaging what one has heard is named a snail in clean water. Wrongfully enjoying the offerings given in faith is named a conch-voiced dog.

Chinese Source

毀辱所聞故，名水生蝸螺。

由邪受用諸信施故，名螺音狗行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Disparaging what one has heard is named a snail in clean water.

Wrongfully enjoying the offerings given in faith is named a conch-voiced dog.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Disparaging the very teaching one has received — a Buddhist disciple slandering the dharma, a Mahāyāna adherent disparaging the lesser vehicle, or a follower of the lesser vehicle claiming the Mahāyāna is not the Buddha's own word — is, whether or not any precept is technically broken, a second form of fouling the water that once nourished. And wrongfully enjoying offerings given in faith — misappropriating what was donated to the whole community for one's own private use, or hoarding improperly and excessively — is a second form of the conch-voiced dog.

225. Section: Falsely Claiming Renunciant Status and Chastity, Through Wrong Speech — Closing the Second Reading, and All Six Terms of the Meaning of Reproach Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Through wrongful speech, this is named falsely claiming renunciant status and chastity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

False speech, idle chatter, and harsh words all count as this final wrongful speech — falsely proclaiming oneself a Buddhist renunciant, telling others one is free of desire, yet never speaking a single word that actually accords with such freedom, speech remaining bound to unwholesome, meaningless talk. Genuinely desireless speech benefits both speaker and listener alike; a true renunciant's words should accord with the noble path, or else silence is the better choice. With this, the second reading of all seven sub-terms is closed in its entirety, and with it, "breaking the precepts," affliction's sixth term under the meaning of reproach, is closed across both readings in its complete entirety — completing the meaning of reproach's own full six terms, and affliction topic's second of three major sections in its entirety. The treatise turns next to affliction's third and final major section, a reference to fuller treatment elsewhere.

226. Section: A Reference to Fuller Treatment Begins — Greed, Hatred, Delusion, and the Rest

Affliction's third and final major section simply points ahead to a fuller treatment reserved for the Compendium of Bases, fascicle 89: secondary affliction rests on the three unwholesome roots of greed, hatred, and delusion, from which indignation, resentment, and every further such defiled matter arise — secondary affliction carries two senses here, first as whatever accompanies a root affliction, and second, more broadly, as any affliction whatsoever that arises from the mind. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and a companion sūtra collection

This closing reference gathers up everything already explained under the unwholesome verse's first two lines — birth, aging, death, the storehouses and the rest, what is welcomed and the rest, affliction — noting that each term finds its own proper place elsewhere, and signals that the treatise will next explain greed, hatred, and delusion at length. "Roasted chicken" carries two readings: first, a being's own life is like a chicken slow-roasted alive, forever pursued by the fire of impermanence and suffering; second, it images a monastic who has broken the precepts, whose grave fault leaves them likewise pursued by manifold suffering, restless as a chicken over the flame — the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, fascicle 22, record both readings directly. With this, a reference to fuller treatment is closed, completing affliction topic's third and final major section — and with it, affliction, the unwholesome portion's sixth and by far most extensive topic, spanning all three major sections (intrinsic nature's twenty-four synonyms, the meaning of reproach's six terms, and this closing reference) is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to greed, hatred, and delusion explained at length, the seventh topic.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The unwholesome portion's seventh topic, greed, hatred, and delusion explained at length, unfolds across three major sections, the first examining greed alone across twenty-four further distinctions — a scale matching affliction's own earlier account. A Buddhist disciple should examine their own mind carefully for each of these marks in turn. Defilement names a defiled mind failing to see desire's own danger, taking delight instead in enjoying pleasing form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and sexual desire. Attachment goes further still, sparing no expense or effort — however costly — simply to indulge in such objects freely.

229. Section: Gluttony, Swallowing Whole, and Bewilderment

Chinese Source

So-called gluttony means hoping for further enjoyment yet to come. So-called swallowing whole means being swallowed up by whatever further afflictions accompany it. Bewilderment will be explained next.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Gluttony pictures greed as an insatiable glutton, forever hoping for more still to come, never once satisfied. Swallowing whole names how one affliction draws in further companions — greed for gain readily brings stinginess in its wake, unwilling to give away what has been gained, tangled together with delusion as well, until a being is entirely swallowed up by this whole cluster of affliction. Bewilderment, the Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain, will be treated further below — failing to discern virtue from fault in what is desired, not understanding that "the world's fine objects are not desire itself; true desire is a person's own discriminating craving," a being grows lost amid mere circumstance and greed arises from that very confusion.

230. Section: Indulgence, and Craving Pursuit

Indulgence names an object already possessed, requiring no further pursuit, yet still firmly clung to, effort now spent protecting and maintaining it rather than acquiring it. Craving pursuit names the opposite: an object not yet possessed, still requiring diligent striving to obtain.

The Stage of Cultivated Wisdom likewise notes that once greed for something takes hold, a wish to draw near it and delight in it naturally follows.

233. Section: Storing Away, and Guarding

So-called storing away means love directed toward what is comprised within one's own inner being. So-called guarding means love directed toward what continues within another's own being.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Storing away names self-love, craving directed at one's own inner aggregates; guarding names craving for another, a further form of craving for external objects — self-love is love of self, while craving for another is love of what belongs to self. Since craving is what accumulates suffering, the very root of birth-and-death's own cycle, every suffering of birth, aging, death, and even the roasted chicken already named arises from nothing other than self-love or craving for its objects.

234. Section: Grasping, and Thirst

So-called grasping means love directed toward what belongs to self. So-called thirst means a redoubling of longing.

Grasping names firm, unreleasing craving toward whatever one already possesses; thirst names longing redoubled toward whatever is pleasing, never once satisfied no matter how much is gained.

235. Section: What Greed Stains, What Pride Occupies, and What Is Desired

Ru-Yi Translation

What is stained means the dwelling place of greed. What is occupied by pride means the dwelling place of seven kinds of pride.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of the Mind Ground

What greed stains names any object greed dwells on — fine clothing, food, bedding, and medicine, whatever attracts defiled clinging. What pride occupies names the seven grounds where pride takes root, as the Stage of the Mind Ground, fascicle 2, explains: pride in freedom from sickness, pride in youth, pride in long life, pride in family lineage, pride in physical beauty and strength, pride in wealth and rank, and pride in broad learning — each a flourishing circumstance that, deeply clung to, breeds an intoxicated, self-satisfied conceit and a careless, indulgent mind, whether pride in a healthy body left too complacent to diligently cut off evil and cultivate good; pride in youthful good looks and sharp faculties; pride in seniority, presuming on age; pride in vast wealth or a great family's many holdings; pride in a fine appearance or bodily strength; pride in riches and rank, whether wealth or status; or pride in broad learning and wide erudition, each swelling into its own particular arrogance.

236. Section: What Is Desired — Closing "What Greed Stains, What Pride Occupies, and What Is Desired" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called what is desired means the dwelling place of greed for every manifold kind of enjoyment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Every kind of fine enjoyment — springtime outings, delicious food, gentle words, warm hospitality, and the rest — becomes an object of desire, a wished-for dream one hopes never to wake from, each its own dwelling place for craving. With this, what greed stains, what pride occupies, and what is desired are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to what is drawn near to and what is delighted in.

237. Section: What Is Drawn Near To, and What Is Delighted In — Two Further Readings, One by Basis and One by Habituation

What is drawn near to means whatever lingering, fond craving from the past continues to follow. What is delighted in means whatever joyful craving of the present continues to follow. And further, whatever craving habitually cultivated within this very life is called what is drawn near to; whatever craving arisen through habituation cultivated in a former life is called what is delighted in.

Chinese Source

所親昵者，謂是過去諸顧戀愛所隨處故。所愛樂者，謂是現在諸欣喜愛所隨處故。

又現法中串所習愛，名為親昵。宿世串習所發生愛，名為愛樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is drawn near to means whatever lingering, fond craving from the past continues to follow. What is delighted in means whatever joyful craving of the present continues to follow.

And further, whatever craving habitually cultivated within this very life is called what is drawn near to; whatever craving arisen through habituation cultivated in a former life is called what is delighted in.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A first reading sorts these two terms by time: what is drawn near to names a fond, lingering craving for some now-past person or circumstance, the affection outliving the thing itself; what is delighted in names present joy and craving, an object currently enjoyed with real excitement. A second reading instead sorts them by habituation: craving habitually cultivated within this very life is drawn near to, while craving whose habit was already formed in a past life, now simply resurfacing, is delighted in — as when a child shows an inborn gift and love for music from earliest childhood, its root already laid in a prior existence. With this, what is drawn near to and what is delighted in, across both readings, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to what causes bewilderment.

238. Section: What Causes Bewilderment — Closing the Tenth Distinction

What causes bewilderment means being unable, regarding it, to observe its virtue and its fault.

Chinese Source

所迷悶者，不能於中觀察功德及過失故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What causes bewilderment means being unable, regarding it, to observe its virtue and its fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Facing an object without discerning its actual virtue or fault, a being grows bewildered before it, and greed arises from exactly that confusion — fulfilling the earlier-deferred promise to explain bewilderment here. With this, what causes bewilderment is closed, completing greed's tenth distinction. The treatise turns next to what is greedily clung to and what is bound by affliction.

239. Section: What Is Greedily Clung To, and What Is Bound by Affliction — With the Compendium of Mixed Topics on Threefold Bondage

What is greedily clung to means the dwelling place of an indulgent mind. What is bound by affliction means the dwelling place of greed, hatred, and delusion.

Chinese Source

所貪著者，是耽樂心所居處故。所縛著者，是貪瞋癡所居處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is greedily clung to means the dwelling place of an indulgent mind. What is bound by affliction means the dwelling place of greed, hatred, and delusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Mixed Topics

Wherever greed exists, an indulgent, pleasure-seeking mind exists alongside it — a fine object is exactly where that indulgent mind dwells. And when greed actively manifests, a being is thereby prevented from following the wholesome — bondage. The Compendium of Mixed Topics, fascicle 6, names three kinds of bondage: bondage by greed, which binds beings to the suffering of change; bondage by hatred, which binds beings to the suffering of suffering itself; and bondage by delusion, which binds beings to the suffering inherent in all conditioned formations — since greed, hatred, and delusion constantly lie latent beneath pleasant and other feelings alike, and because they leave a being unable to move freely toward wholesome means, they are named bondage, exactly as an outward physical binding leaves a person unable to wander freely or to act freely within their own dwelling; the same holds for affliction's own inward binding of greed, hatred, and delusion.

240. Section: What Is Longed For, and What Is Fettered

What is longed for means it is able to give rise to craving. What is fettered means it is the dwelling place of every fetter.

Chinese Source

所希求者，能生愛故。所繫縛者，是一切結所居處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is longed for means it is able to give rise to craving. What is fettered means it is the dwelling place of every fetter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once an object is craved, a defiled longing for it arises — what is longed for. And once the mind is fettered by that craving, every further affliction follows in its wake, one drawing forth another: craving for existence gives rise to grasping, grasping gives rise to volition and the remaining links, and existence in turn gives rise to a future life's great mass of birth, aging, death, and suffering — hence what is fettered is called the dwelling place of every fetter. With this, what is greedily clung to, what is bound by affliction, what is longed for, and what is fettered are closed in their entirety, completing greed's eleventh and twelfth distinctions. The treatise turns next to what is itself evil.

241. Section: What Is Itself Evil — Closing the Thirteenth Distinction

Ru-Yi Translation

What is itself evil means being able to unite with unwholesome dharmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Greed is able to unite with killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and every one of the ten unwholesome deeds, producing evil karma directly — hence what is itself evil. With this, what is itself evil is closed, completing greed's thirteenth distinction. The treatise turns next to joy, speech, and urgency, three marks tied to body, speech, and mind.

242. Section: Joy, Speech, and Urgency — Sorted by Mind, Speech, and Body

Chinese Source

為令現前而喜樂者，謂希望故。為令現前而言說者，謂以語言而追求故。為令證得而遽務者，謂生貪著身追求故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Joy directed at making it appear before oneself means hoping for it. Speech directed at making it appear before oneself means pursuing it through words. Urgency directed at actually obtaining it means, greed having grown, pursuing it bodily.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These three mark greed's escalation through mind, speech, and body in turn: hoping a desired object will simply appear is still purely a matter of mental aspiration; voicing that longing through actual words is discursive thought turned into speech; and once greed grows strong enough to drive urgent bodily pursuit, it has become full bodily action. With this, joy, speech, and urgency are closed in their entirety, completing greed's fourteenth distinction. The treatise turns next to indulgent dwelling.

243. Section: Indulgent Dwelling — Closing the Fifteenth Distinction

Ru-Yi Translation

Indulgent dwelling means, once obtained, holding it close and never letting go.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once form, sound, scent, taste, touch, or the desire between the sexes has been obtained, a being clings to it and refuses to release it at all — indulgent dwelling. With this, indulgent dwelling is closed, completing greed's fifteenth distinction. The treatise turns next to equal defilement, equal evil, and equal delusion, sorted by the three feelings.

244. Section: Equal Defilement, Equal Evil, and Equal Delusion — Sorted by the Three Feelings

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Equal defilement means greed arising toward pleasant feeling. Equal evil means greedy hatred arising toward painful feeling. Equal delusion means delusion arising toward all three feelings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Toward pleasant feeling, greed arises together with craving for union with what is loved; toward painful feeling, hatred arises together with craving for separation from what is unloved; and toward all three feelings alike, failing to see them as illusory and insubstantial, delusion arises together with craving neither for union nor for separation. With this, equal defilement, equal evil, and equal delusion are closed in their entirety, completing greed's sixteenth distinction. The treatise turns next to fond memory, mental attachment, and toilsome pursuit, sorted by the three times.

245. Section: Fond Memory, Mental Attachment, and Toilsome Pursuit — Sorted by the Three Times

Fond memory means with reference to the past. Mental attachment means with reference to the future. Toilsome pursuit means, resting on that very cause and condition, actively pursuing it right now.

A third sorting by the three times: fond memory clings to what is past, unable to let it go; mental attachment fixes hope on what is yet to come; and toilsome pursuit names the present labor of actually chasing after a craved object right now. With this, fond memory, mental attachment, and toilsome pursuit are closed in their entirety, completing greed's seventeenth distinction. The treatise turns next to blazing fire, burning, and distress, examined here specifically as forms of greed.

246. Section: Blazing Fire, Burning, and Distress — Examined as Greed's Own Three Moments

Chinese Source

熾然者，謂所欲果遂，起染汙心故。燒者，謂所欲衰損，起染汙心故。惱者，謂所得變壞故。

Blazing fire means, the desired result having been achieved, a defiled mind arises. Burning means, the desired result having declined and been lost, a defiled mind arises. Distress means what was obtained has changed and been ruined.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These same three terms, already met among affliction's own twenty-four synonyms, reappear here specifically as marks of greed: blazing fire names the intense defiled clinging the moment a desired object is actually obtained; burning names the affliction of deprivation when a desired object is lost before ever being obtained; and distress names the suffering that follows once an already-obtained object later changes and is lost. With this, blazing fire, burning, and distress are closed in their entirety, completing greed's eighteenth distinction. The treatise turns next to praying, touching, longing, and delighting, four ways greed's own craving is displayed.

247. Section: Praying, Touching, Longing, and Delighting — Four Ways Craving Is Displayed

為祈禱者，顯示取著吉祥愛故。為觸對者，顯示取著摩執愛故。為希求者，顯示取著與利愛故。為欣悅者，顯示取著如意思惟所有愛故。

Praying reveals craving that clings to good fortune. Touching reveals craving that clings to fondling and grasping. Longing reveals craving that clings to gain and benefit. Delighting reveals craving that clings to whatever thought accords with one's own wish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Praying for a loved object to come true reveals craving for good fortune; endlessly fondling and handling a present, cherished object reveals craving for physical touch; constantly hoping for the gain and benefit an object might bring reveals craving for advantage; and a face lighting with joy the moment something pleasing appears reveals craving for whatever matches one's own wish — greed's own mark made visible through these four kinds of outward behavior. With this, praying, touching, longing, and delighting are closed in their entirety, completing greed's nineteenth distinction. The treatise turns next to turning the mind toward purity, greed's own point of release.

248. Section: Turning the Mind Toward Purity — Freedom From Desire, Referred to an Earlier Section

And further, the mind turning toward purity with respect to desire — down through the five realms of release from desire, spoken of at length — should be understood exactly as already explained in the Stage of Concentrated Equipoise.

Chinese Source

And further, the mind turning toward purity with respect to desire — down through the five realms of release from desire, spoken of at length — should be understood exactly as already explained in the Stage of Concentrated Equipoise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

For a full account to be complete, greed's own many distinctions should include how greed is actually released — hence the treatise here simply points back to the Stage of Concentrated Equipoise's own earlier account of the five realms of release from desire, rather than repeating it. With this, turning the mind toward purity is closed, completing greed's twentieth distinction. The treatise turns next to greed's remaining distinctions.

249. Section: Intoxicated Pride, and Extreme Intoxicated Pride

So-called intoxicated pride means being joined together with three kinds of pride. So-called extreme intoxicated pride means, resting on that very pride, spreading through every unwholesome dharma and leaving the mind unguarded.

Chinese Source

言憍醉者，謂與三憍共相應故。

極憍醉者，謂依止憍，遍於諸惡不善法中，能令其心不防護故。

Ru-Yi Translation

So-called intoxicated pride means being joined together with three kinds of pride.

So-called extreme intoxicated pride means, resting on that very pride, spreading through every unwholesome dharma and leaving the mind unguarded.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Establishment of Consciousness-Only Treatise and the Śrāvaka Stage

Intoxicated pride names greed joined with three of the seven prides already named — freedom from sickness, youth, and long life — as the Establishment of Consciousness-Only Treatise defines pride itself: deep defiled attachment to one's own flourishing circumstance, an intoxicated, self-satisfied nature. The Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 23, connects this directly to greed: one who delights in desire eats and drinks precisely in hope of a sturdy, vigorous body, radiant skin, lasting freedom from sickness, prolonged youth, and a long lifespan — every such pursuit is greed's own mark, at once intoxicated by present flourishing and craving still more of it. Extreme intoxicated pride goes further still: resting on that same threefold pride, improper attention multiplies and unwholesome deeds accumulate, affliction leaving the mind unable to guard itself at all — sunk so deep in affliction that one no longer even recognizes it, affliction having become simply the new normal, a far graver state than intoxicated pride alone.

250. Section: Pursuing Intoxicated Pride, and Conceit Born From Desire — Closing These Two Distinctions

Pursuing intoxicated pride means diligently learning and adopting whatever causes and conditions produce that same intoxicated pride. Conceit born from desire, with respect to the desires, means enjoying them without ever observing their fault.

Chinese Source

趣憍醉者，謂於憍醉所有因緣受學轉故。於諸欲中生等憍者，謂不觀過受用欲故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pursuing intoxicated pride means diligently learning and adopting whatever causes and conditions produce that same intoxicated pride. Conceit born from desire, with respect to the desires, means enjoying them without ever observing their fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Pursuing intoxicated pride names actively seeking out and adopting whatever practice promises freedom from sickness, lasting youth, or a long life the moment one hears of it — itself a mark of greed. And conceit born from desire names the conceit that grows directly from indulging in desire without ever recognizing its fault — a lowlier desire breeding a lesser conceit, a middling desire a middling conceit, and a grander desire a grander conceit, each simply a further form of pride born from failing to see desire's own danger. With this, pursuing intoxicated pride and conceit born from desire are closed in their entirety, completing greed's twenty-first and twenty-second distinctions. The treatise turns next to ease, experience, and pursuit of feeling.

251. Section: Ease, Experience, and Pursuit of Feeling — Closing the Twenty-Third Distinction

Ease means pleasant feeling's own particular mark. Experience means every feeling's own shared mark. Pursuit of feeling means the causal mark underlying the remaining feelings.

Chinese Source

平安者，謂樂受自相故。領受者，謂諸受共相故。趣受者，謂餘受因相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ease means pleasant feeling's own particular mark. Experience means every feeling's own shared mark. Pursuit of feeling means the causal mark underlying the remaining feelings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ease names the contented, peaceful satisfaction felt while enjoying desire, unrecognized as impermanent and selfless, greed quietly accumulating unnoticed beneath it. Experience names every kind of feeling desire's enjoyment produces — painful feeling toward a lesser desire breeding hatred, neutral feeling toward a middling desire breeding delusion, pleasant feeling toward a superior desire breeding greed — all alike impermanent, suffering, empty, and selfless, yet, failing to recognize this shared mark, a being pursues pleasant feeling all the more, itself a further form of greed. And pursuit of feeling names actively cultivating the wholesome conditions of gods and humans — the very faculties and circumstances that produce pleasant feeling, and the karma that brings a pleasing result now and in the future — precisely in order to obtain the pleasant feeling a fine desire produces; in short, practicing worldly virtue for the sake of craving desire itself. With this, ease, experience, and pursuit of feeling are closed in their entirety, completing greed's twenty-third distinction. The treatise turns next to a concluding reference closing greed's account entirely.

252. Section: Desire-Craving, Firm Attachment, Restraint, and Gluttony — A Concluding Reference, Closing All Twenty-Four Distinctions of Greed

And further, desire-craving, firm attachment, restraint, gluttony, and further such greed should be understood exactly as already explained in the Stage of the Wisdom Born of Hearing.

Chinese Source

又欲貪、堅著、拘礙、饕餮等貪，如聞所成地已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, desire-craving, firm attachment, restraint, gluttony, and further such greed should be understood exactly as already explained in the Stage of the Wisdom Born of Hearing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of the Wisdom Born of Hearing directly

This closing reference points back to the Stage of the Wisdom Born of Hearing's own fivefold account of greed: greed for inward desire itself, hoping for a pleasing resultant reward among gods or humans and delighting in the present body already obtained, never leaving the desire realm; greed for outward sexual desire; greed for circumstantial objects; greed for form, belonging to the form realm alone; and greed for a perishable self, spanning all three realms — the first three confined to the desire realm, the fourth to the form realm, the fifth running through all three. That same source further offers six descriptive terms for how beings indulge food specifically: defiled love, indulgent craving, gluttony, bewilderment, firm clinging, and steeped attachment — useful terms for understanding restraint as well, which names being hemmed in and hindered by whatever object one has grown attached to, exactly as the saying goes, "descending from luxury to frugality is hard." And wanting differs subtly from greed, as this same Compendium of Synonyms itself already explained: wanting names hoping to obtain or enjoy something, whether already possessed or not; greed names the firmer attachment that grows once genuine joy is taken in its enjoyment. With this, greed's full twenty-four distinctions, from defilement and attachment through this concluding reference, are closed in their entirety, completing the unwholesome portion's seventh topic's first major section. The treatise turns next to hatred, examined across eight further sections.

253. Section: Hatred Begins — Inward Stain, Inward Envy, Inward Enemy, and Inward Grudge

Chinese Source

復次，言內垢者，謂於怨意樂堅持不捨故。內忌者，謂於所愛障礙住故。內敵者，謂能引發所不愛故。內怨者，謂能引發所不宜故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, so-called inward stain means holding firmly, without release, to a resentful aspiration. Inward envy means dwelling as an obstruction to what is loved. Inward enemy means being able to draw forth what is unloved. Inward grudge means being able to draw forth what is unfitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The unwholesome portion's seventh topic turns next to hatred, examined across eight sections. Hatred is named inward stain because a resentful aspiration, once firmly gripped, deeply stains the mind with affliction. It is named inward envy because it obstructs whatever one loves and pursues — even the noble path itself becomes unreachable the moment hatred arises; envy here also carries the sense of begrudging another's own good fortune, taking no joy in their success, which likewise obstructs what would otherwise be loved. It is named inward enemy because hatred, once it arises, draws forth further unloved circumstances in its wake, and sustained hatred readily breeds bodily illness too — an inward enemy steadily laying siege from within, until great harm eventually follows. And it is named inward grudge because harbored resentment, even a wish to harm another, drives one into manifold improper conduct, acting exactly like a hostile bandit within one's own mind.

254. Section: What Is Unwelcome, Unpleasurable, and Unendearing — the Reverse of What Was Welcomed

What is unwelcome, applied to the future, names dreading a coming circumstance; what is unpleasurable, applied to the past, names an unpleasant memory; what is unendearing, applied to the present, and what is displeasing, applied to feeling generally, follow the same reversal — this treatise's own earlier account of what is welcomed and the rest, simply inverted here into hatred's own mirror image.

255. Section: Suffering, Harm, Contrariness, and Displeasure

And so-called suffering means it is, by its very nature, suffering, and remains suffering even when merely recollected. Harm means suffering directly present. Contrariness means suffering reflected upon across all three times. Displeasure means present suffering itself able to cause harm.

Chinese Source

又言苦者，謂彼自性苦，亦隨憶念苦故。損害者，謂現前苦故。違逆者，謂於三世思惟苦故。不順意者，謂現有苦能損害故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And so-called suffering means it is, by its very nature, suffering, and remains suffering even when merely recollected. Harm means suffering directly present. Contrariness means suffering reflected upon across all three times. Displeasure means present suffering itself able to cause harm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Southern tradition's Sutta on the Serpent

Hatred's very nature is suffering — whether the object of displeasure is a person or a circumstance, the displeasure itself is suffering, each fresh flare of it burning the one who feels it. As the Southern tradition's Sutta on the Serpent teaches, hatred is the coarsest of afflictions, coarser even than greed — greed at least clings to something loved, briefly moistening the mind if the desired object is obtained, while hatred brings suffering from beginning to end without relief, revealing hatred's own basic nature directly. Harm names that same suffering directly present the instant hatred flares, burning and injuring like a scorching fire. Contrariness names the same suffering spanning all three times — recalling the past, dwelling on the present, or imagining the future, any thought of what displeases brings suffering, since hatred itself violates and turns against the mind's own natural peace. And displeasure names present suffering's own power to injure — displeasure breeds hatred, hatred is already suffering, and dwelling further on it only compounds that suffering, the mind caught in an unrelenting, unreleased knot.

256. Section: Suffering, Intensity, Hardness, Bitterness, and Displeasure — a Reference to Fuller Treatment — Closing the Fourth Distinction

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases directly

Hatred, once arisen, sears like a raging fire, drawing forth manifold suffering; its manifest activity is intense; joined with hatred, gentleness is lost and the mind turns hard; and hatred itself brings a bitter, aching pain, altogether unwelcome, unpleasurable, unendearing, and displeasing. The Compendium of Bases, fascicle 91, explains this same fourfold reversal directly: unwelcome names displeasure toward the future; unpleasurable names an unpleasant memory of the past; unendearing names displeasure on contact with the present; and displeasure names discomfort toward feeling generally. With this, suffering, intensity, hardness, bitterness, and displeasure are closed in their entirety, completing hatred's fourth distinction. The treatise turns next to violence, hatred's own overall statement.

又暴惡者，是其總句。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, violence is the overall statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wherever hatred is present, violent, fierce conduct of body, speech, and mind follows in its train — this overall statement is unpacked in detail across the sections that follow.

258. Section: Stinging Words, and Resentful Writing

Resentful writing names the same hatred showing through the written word — even composing an essay, one gripped by hatred can hardly avoid coarse, harsh phrasing, resentment and dissatisfaction leaking through every line.

With this, violence — stinging words and resentful writing, resentment, outburst, and violent harm, and this lingering, recurring hatred — is closed in its entirety, completing hatred's fifth distinction. The treatise turns next to suffering, its own presence, deprivation, calamity, and heat.

Ru-Yi Translation

If affliction arises, disturbing the mind, then, through this cause and condition, one comes to dwell in suffering — as is said: a monastic mired in laziness and manifold evils comes to dwell in a mass of suffering. Calamity means being able to serve as the cause of further affliction. Heat means, at a later time, giving rise to feverish distress.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Hatred's sixth distinction, examined first through cause and condition: hatred disturbs the mind directly, and a body caught in its grip suffers at once — a flushed face, a pounding heart, the whole body agitated and burning. Its own presence names hatred's power to seed a further, future suffering on top of the present one; deprivation names hatred's own power to obstruct every wholesome practice — a student who resents a teacher, for instance, finds every word that teacher speaks impossible to absorb; calamity names hatred's own power to become the seed from which yet more affliction springs, agitating body, speech, and mind alike, so that even before any actual misfortune arrives, one's own store of merit has already begun to drain away; and heat names the feverish distress that inevitably follows once hatred has taken hold.

262. Section: The Same Five Terms Examined by Feeling — With the Poisonous Abscess Simile

And further, so-called suffering is the overall statement. Its own presence means being joined with sorrow and suffering. Deprivation means pleasant feeling itself changing and being ruined. Calamity means, within neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, being unreleased from either of the other two.

Chinese Source

又言苦者，是其總句。有苦者，謂憂苦相應故。有匱者，謂樂受變壞故。有災者，謂在不苦不樂受中，於二不解脫故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, so-called suffering is the overall statement. Its own presence means being joined with sorrow and suffering. Deprivation means pleasant feeling itself changing and being ruined. Calamity means, within neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, being unreleased from either of the other two.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 5 and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Sorted instead by the three feelings: suffering is the overall statement, hatred uniting with every kind of painful feeling; its own presence names the suffering of suffering itself, sorrow directly joined to hatred; and deprivation names the suffering of change, the ruin of an already-pleasant feeling. Calamity names neutral feeling's own hidden danger — lacking any genuine insight into the true dharma, a being is never actually released from either suffering or pleasure even while resting in neutral feeling, since its own affliction lies dormant beneath it, unnoticed. Fascicle 5 offers a simile: like a poisonous, feverish abscess, which carries its own inherent heat and poison quite apart from any external touch of hot or cold, the five aggregates likewise carry an inherent, latent danger that painful or pleasant feeling's own vividness normally conceals — precisely because neutral feeling lacks that vividness, its own hidden poison and heat can be observed directly, exactly like the abscess itself. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss "unreleased from either" as being unreleased from both suffering and pleasure together.

263. Section: Heat Applied to the Three Feelings, and a Further Reading of "Its Own Presence" and "Deprivation" — Closing the Sixth Distinction

Heat means, resting on pleasant feeling and the rest, according to what applies, the fire of greed, hatred, and delusion. And, with reference to the past, there is its own presence; with reference to the future, there is deprivation.

Chinese Source

有熱者，謂於樂等，如其所應，有貪瞋癡火故。

又於過去有苦，於未來有匱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Heat means, resting on pleasant feeling and the rest, according to what applies, the fire of greed, hatred, and delusion.

And, with reference to the past, there is its own presence; with reference to the future, there is deprivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Heat, matched to each of the three feelings, names greed's fire arising toward pleasant feeling, hatred's fire toward painful feeling, and delusion's fire toward neutral feeling, each according to what applies. A further, alternate reading sorts its own presence and deprivation instead by time: its own presence names a past unpleasant experience already suffered, while deprivation names a present pleasant feeling's own coming change, a loss anticipated for the future. With this, suffering, its own presence, deprivation, calamity, and heat — examined by cause and condition, by the three feelings, and by this further temporal reading — are closed in their entirety, completing hatred's sixth distinction. The treatise turns next to harm, the enemy, and the grudge.

264. Section: Harm, the Enemy, and the Grudge — Closing the Seventh Distinction

And further, harm means revealing the presence of a superior grade of resentment and grievance. The enemy and the grudge are as already explained.

Chinese Source

又害者，謂顯示攝受上品怨嫌故。敵者、怨者，如前已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, harm means revealing the presence of a superior grade of resentment and grievance. The enemy and the grudge are as already explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Harm names a superior, more severe grade of resentment and grievance — a mind actually stirring toward inflicting injury on another. The enemy and the grudge simply repeat the inward enemy and inward grudge already explained above: the inward enemy draws forth unwelcome circumstances the moment hatred manifests; the inward grudge, harboring even a wish to harm, drives improper conduct in its wake. With this, harm, the enemy, and the grudge are closed in their entirety, completing hatred's seventh distinction. The treatise turns next to subduing, ruining, and the rest.

265. Section: Subduing, Ruining, and the Rest Begin — Four Terms Distinguished

And further, subduing means opposing the arising of an as-yet-unarisen human effort. Ruining means opposing the continuation of an already-arisen human effort. Being overcome by another means opposing the arising of an as-yet-unarisen capability. Falling behind another means opposing the continuation of an already-arisen capability.

Chinese Source

又摧伏者，謂與未生士用生相違故。破壞者，謂與已生士用住相違故。為他所勝者，謂與未生功能生相違故。落在他後者，謂與已生功能住相違故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, subduing means opposing the arising of an as-yet-unarisen human effort. Ruining means opposing the continuation of an already-arisen human effort. Being overcome by another means opposing the arising of an as-yet-unarisen capability. Falling behind another means opposing the continuation of an already-arisen capability.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Hatred's eighth and final distinction names four further marks. Subduing names hatred's power to obstruct a wholesome effort not yet arisen — exactly as the fourfold right effort teaches a practitioner to give rise to unarisen wholesome dharmas, a single flash of hatred can burn down that whole forest of merit in an instant, blocking meditative absorption on the very verge of success. Ruining names hatred's power to destroy a wholesome effort already underway — work already begun for the benefit of others, undone the moment hatred flares. Being overcome by another names affliction actually winning out over a still-unarisen wholesome capability, evil conduct arising in its place. And falling behind another names an already-arisen wholesome seed suppressed by hatred, prevented from ever manifesting.

266. Section: The Reverse of These Four — Closing Hatred's Eighth Distinction, and Hatred Entirely

And further, not being subdued, not being ruined, not being overcome, and prevailing over another — these several phrases should be understood through the meaning of the phrases just given, in reverse.

Chinese Source

又不摧伏、不破壞、非所勝、有所勝者，如是諸句，由前諸句其義應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, not being subdued, not being ruined, not being overcome, and prevailing over another — these several phrases should be understood through the meaning of the phrases just given, in reverse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

A practitioner who recognizes hatred's own uselessness and manifold faults, working diligently through insight and corrective teaching to subdue it, keeps an unarisen wholesome dharma from ever being subdued by hatred, and keeps an already-arisen wholesome dharma from ever being ruined; free of hatred, no unarisen wholesome capability is overcome, and an already-arisen one continues to grow and prevail. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes clarify that, of the four terms just given, the first two concern a wholesome dharma's own active manifestation, while the latter two concern its underlying seed — this distinction should be understood accordingly. With this, subduing, ruining, and their reverse are closed in their entirety, completing hatred's eighth and final distinction — and with it, hatred, examined across all eight of its own sections, is closed in its entirety, completing the second of greed, hatred, and delusion's three major sections. The treatise turns next to delusion, the third and final section.

267. Section: Delusion Begins — Ignorance Regarding the Prior Extreme

Further, so-called ignorance regarding the prior extreme means failing to understand the impermanent nature of past formations.

Chinese Source

復次，於前際無智者，謂於過去諸行無常法性不了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, so-called ignorance regarding the prior extreme means failing to understand the impermanent nature of past formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 9

Delusion, the third and final major section of greed, hatred, and delusion explained at length, unfolds first by simply listing seven kinds of delusion already explained in fascicle 9, then unpacking them anew. Ignorance regarding the prior extreme names failing to grasp that every past formation of the five aggregates was, without exception, impermanent — "impermanent" itself simply negating any true permanence, since no conditioned dharma has ever genuinely persisted. Fascicle 9 defines it directly: improper discrimination applied to past formations — wondering "did I exist in the past, or did I not; what nature did I have; what kind of being was I?" — constitutes this very ignorance.

268. Section: Ignorance Regarding the Latter Extreme

Ignorance regarding the latter extreme means failing to understand the nature of present formations' total cessation.

Chinese Source

於後際無智者，謂於現在諸行盡滅法性不了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding the latter extreme means failing to understand the nature of present formations' total cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 9

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the root text's own content here appears transposed with the following section: "the latter extreme" properly names the future, yet the sentence translated above actually describes the present — its content instead correctly belongs under the third term, "the prior-and-latter extreme," which names the present as the latter extreme of the past and the prior extreme of the future at once. Read as the future properly intends, ignorance regarding the latter extreme means failing to understand that future formations are bound to arise, and, once arisen, bound to cease entirely — since ignorance persists as an unbroken seed, this exists, therefore that exists, and this arises, therefore that arises, one link in dependent origination endlessly seeding the next, from ignorance through formations all the way to birth, aging, and death. Fascicle 9 confirms: improper discrimination applied to future formations — wondering "will I exist in the future, or will I not; what nature, what kind of being will I have?" — constitutes this very ignorance regarding the latter extreme.

269. Section: Ignorance Regarding the Prior-and-Latter Extreme — Closing All Three Temporal Kinds of Ignorance

Ignorance regarding the prior-and-latter extreme means failing to understand that future formations are bound to arise, and that, once arisen, they are bound to cease entirely.

Chinese Source

於前後際無智者，謂於未來諸行當生法性，及當生已當盡法性不了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding the prior-and-latter extreme means failing to understand that future formations are bound to arise, and that, once arisen, they are bound to cease entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As noted under the previous section, Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this same transposition from the other side: this sentence's own content, concerning what is yet to arise, actually belongs to the second term above, while "the prior-and-latter extreme" properly names the present moment — the latter extreme relative to the past, and the prior extreme relative to the future at once. Read as the present properly intends, ignorance regarding the prior-and-latter extreme means failing to understand that present formations, moment by moment, are already ceasing entirely, arising and perishing so swiftly that nothing whatsoever can be held onto or mastered. With this, ignorance regarding the prior extreme, the latter extreme, and the prior-and-latter extreme together — delusion's own threefold blindness across all three times — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to delusion's remaining kinds, already listed in fascicle 9.

270. Section: Worldly Delusion's Own Mark — the Improper Reflections It Produces — Closing Worldly Delusion Entirely

Not understanding in this way means, resting on the prior extreme and the rest, giving rise to improper reflection: did I exist in a past life or not — down through, at length — who am I, who will know me, where has this being now present come from, and where will it go once it perishes here?

Chinese Source

彼於如是不了知者，謂依前際等，起不如理思惟：我於過去世為曾有耶？乃至廣說我為是誰？誰當是我？今此有情從何而來？於此沒已當往何所？

Ru-Yi Translation

Not understanding in this way means, resting on the prior extreme and the rest, giving rise to improper reflection: did I exist in a past life or not — down through, at length — who am I, who will know me, where has this being now present come from, and where will it go once it perishes here?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Worldly delusion, the first of the seven kinds, names not understanding the true, selfless, impermanent nature of past, present, and future alike — and resting on that very ignorance, a whole train of improper reflection follows: which destiny did I inhabit in a past life, human, heavenly, hellish, or otherwise; what was my name then; who were my parents, my siblings; who am I now; who will ever truly know me; where has this present being come from, and where will it go once this life ends? With this, worldly delusion — its threefold temporal ignorance and the improper reflection it produces — is closed in its entirety, completing the first of the seven kinds of delusion. The treatise turns next to delusion regarding phenomena themselves, the second kind.

271. Section: Delusion Regarding Phenomena Begins — Ignorance Named

Resting in this way on improper attention toward the prior and latter extremes, one remains deluded and fails to understand this very impermanent nature, a view of self following after every formation — regarding what is inward, what is outward, and both together, unable to understand that there is only this nature of formations at all.

Chinese Source

如是依前後際不如理作意，故於如是無常法性愚癡不了，於諸行中我見隨逐，於內、於外、俱於二種唯有法性不能了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting in this way on improper attention toward the prior and latter extremes, one remains deluded and fails to understand this very impermanent nature, a view of self following after every formation — regarding what is inward, what is outward, and both together, unable to understand that there is only this nature of formations at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 9

Delusion regarding phenomena, the second kind, names the view of self that follows directly from worldly delusion's own improper reflection — clinging to one's own aggregates as a permanent, unified, governing self; clinging to outer, non-sentient things, a car, a house, as one's own possession; and clinging to another being's own five aggregates as friend, enemy, or stranger — never recognizing that inward self, outward object, and other being alike are nothing but impermanent formations, no true substance anywhere to be found. Fascicle 9 names this ignorance regarding both what is inward and what is outward together.

272. Section: Distinguishing Inward, Outward, and Both — Closing Delusion Regarding Phenomena Entirely

Inward means the inner bases. Outward means the outer trace. Both inward and outward together mean the basis the faculties depend on, together with the mental-object base — since these dharmas can be found both within, and are also comprised within the outer bases.

Chinese Source

內謂內處，外謂外跡，內外即是根所依處及以法處。由彼諸法於內可得，又是外處之所攝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Inward means the inner bases. Outward means the outer trace. Both inward and outward together mean the basis the faculties depend on, together with the mental-object base — since these dharmas can be found both within, and are also comprised within the outer bases.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Inward names one's own six inner faculties; outward names the six outer objects beyond the body; and both together names another being's own six faculties and the mental-object base — since another's faculties are, from that being's own side, an inner basis, yet from one's own side, comprised within the outer world, they carry both names at once. In sum, failing to understand the inward body, the outward world, and other beings' own inward-and-outward nature alike gives rise to self-love and craving for further existence toward one's own body, craving for its objects toward the outer world, and craving for what belongs to self toward other beings — establishing these three kinds of ignorance: inward, outward, and both together. With this, delusion regarding phenomena is closed in its entirety, completing the second of the seven kinds of delusion. The treatise turns next to delusion regarding transmigration, the third kind.

273. Section: Delusion Regarding Transmigration Begins — Ignorance Regarding Karma

Ignorance regarding karma means being unable to understand that karma has only the nature of a formation, and falsely reckoning a self to be its agent.

Chinese Source

於業無智者，謂於諸業唯有行性不能了知，而妄計度我為作者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding karma means being unable to understand that karma has only the nature of a formation, and falsely reckoning a self to be its agent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Delusion regarding transmigration, the third kind, first names ignorance regarding karma: an ordinary being fails to see that every deed of body, speech, and mind is simply a conditioned formation ceaselessly arising and passing, and instead falsely clings to a governing self as its actual agent — "I see, I hear, I think," never recognizing that seeing, hearing, and cognizing all depend on the coming together of manifold conditions, nine for the eye consciousness alone, none of it self-directed at all. Clinging to a self as the one who acts breeds only further feverish affliction — this belongs to delusion regarding transmigration, since karma is simply karma, yet an ordinary being clings to a self behind it.

274. Section: Ignorance Regarding Ripened Result

Ignorance regarding ripened result means, with respect to the world of sentient beings, the world of their environment, and every further circumstance arisen from karma as its cause, falsely reckoning a sovereign lord, an agent, or one who is born.

Chinese Source

於異熟無智者，謂於有情世間及器世間，若餘境界，業因所起，妄計自在、作者、生者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding ripened result means, with respect to the world of sentient beings, the world of their environment, and every further circumstance arisen from karma as its cause, falsely reckoning a sovereign lord, an agent, or one who is born.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ripened result names karma's own fruit, ripening only after time has passed, in a different form, at a different moment than the karma that produced it. Both the world of sentient beings and their surrounding environment — the house one lives in, the things one uses — arise from karma; one whose past merit was great enjoys a correspondingly finer environment now. Failing to understand that this whole process, sentient world and environment alike, arises from karma without any true governing self, an ordinary being falsely imagines a great sovereign lord as its creator, or a single agent who creates life and a single being who is born to receive it — this too belongs to delusion regarding transmigration, ignorance that clings to a self as the very one who experiences karma's fruit.

275. Section: Ignorance Regarding Karma and Its Ripened Result Together — Closing Delusion Regarding Transmigration Entirely

Ignorance regarding karma and its ripened result together means being deluded about everything at once, arriving at the wrong view that slanders karma's own fruit — this refers to the ignorance held by non-Buddhist ascetics and ordinary beings alike, regarding every dharma.

Chinese Source

於業異熟無智者，謂遍愚一切，獲得誹謗業果邪見。此即宣說外道異生，於諸法中所有無智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding karma and its ripened result together means being deluded about everything at once, arriving at the wrong view that slanders karma's own fruit — this refers to the ignorance held by non-Buddhist ascetics and ordinary beings alike, regarding every dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Where the first two kinds concerned ignorance about karma alone and about its ripened result alone, this third kind concerns ignorance about the relationship between the two entirely, giving rise to the wrong view that a wholesome cause brings no pleasant fruit and an unwholesome cause brings no painful fruit — the wrong view that denies karma and its fruit altogether. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain: saying there is no excellent conduct, no evil conduct, and no fruit or ripened result belonging to either kind of karma is exactly this wrong view that denies both cause and effect — non-Buddhist ascetics who deny that keeping discipline and cultivating concentration counter affliction, or that such affliction brings any evil result, and who deny that generosity, wholesome in itself, brings any merit, or that stinginess brings any fault, exemplify this very denial of cause and effect. With this, delusion regarding transmigration — ignorance regarding karma, regarding its ripened result, and regarding the two together — is closed in its entirety, completing the third of the seven kinds of delusion. The treatise turns next to delusion regarding the supreme, the fourth kind.

276. Section: Delusion Regarding the Supreme Begins — Ignorance Regarding the Buddha

Ignorance regarding the Buddha means failing to understand the Tathāgata's own dharma-body and every mark of his conduct.

Chinese Source

於佛無智者，謂不了知如來法身及諸行相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding the Buddha means failing to understand the Tathāgata's own dharma-body and every mark of his conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Delusion regarding the supreme, the fourth kind, names ignorance regarding the three jewels, beginning with the Buddha himself — failing to understand the Tathāgata's own dharma-body, his conduct, and his marks. Even a Buddhist follower who has not studied the scriptures deeply may remain ignorant of the Buddha's own ten powers, four fearlessnesses, eighteen unique qualities, thirty-two major marks, and eighty minor marks, and how they were accomplished — reciting the Buddha's name without truly grasping his greatness, his compassion, or the power of his vow, such a follower cannot easily feel a genuine, resonant connection with the Buddha at all, since without real faith there can be no true closeness.

277. Section: Ignorance Regarding the Dharma

Ignorance regarding the dharma means failing to understand its mark of being well spoken, and the rest.

Chinese Source

於法無智者，謂不了知善說等相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding the dharma means failing to understand its mark of being well spoken, and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ignorance regarding the dharma names failing to reflect deeply on the true dharma's own goodness at the beginning, middle, and end — good at the beginning, since hearing it first clears away affliction; good in the middle, since practicing it meets no obstruction, following the middle way free of both extremes; and good at the end, since practicing in accordance with it certainly leads to unsurpassed awakening or nirvāṇa itself — whether through never studying it, studying without genuine reflection, misunderstanding it, indulging the five desires instead of diligent study, remaining mired in unresolved doubt, or even slandering the true dharma outright. The dharma itself is twofold, conventional and ultimate — the conventional true dharma being the sūtras, vinaya, and treatises expressed through language, and the ultimate true dharma being the very truth those conventional teachings lead to once the noble path is realized and non-discriminating wisdom attained; failing to properly reflect on, let alone realize, either constitutes this ignorance.

278. Section: Ignorance Regarding the Saṅgha — Closing Delusion Regarding the Supreme Entirely

Ignorance regarding the saṅgha means failing to understand its mark of right conduct, and the rest.

Chinese Source

於僧無智者，謂不了知正行等相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding the saṅgha means failing to understand its mark of right conduct, and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ignorance regarding the saṅgha names failing to understand what right conduct actually asks of a monastic — the Buddha established that a monk trains in the precepts for five years, a nun for six, learning the monastic life and its discipline from the ground up before turning to the study of the teachings, the practice of meditation, and the cultivation of calm and insight; a newly ordained monastic who instead grows lazy, unwilling to diligently pursue any of this, embodies exactly this ignorance, letting the time given for practice slip by empty. With this, ignorance regarding the Buddha, the dharma, and the saṅgha together is closed in its entirety, completing delusion regarding the supreme, the fourth of the seven kinds of delusion. The treatise turns next to delusion regarding the truths, the fifth kind.

279. Section: Delusion Regarding the Truths — Closing the Fifth Kind

Ignorance regarding suffering and the rest means failing to understand the marks distinguished throughout the sūtras, and failing to understand the sixteen aspects.

Chinese Source

於苦等無智者，謂如諸經所分別相，及十六行中不了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding suffering and the rest means failing to understand the marks distinguished throughout the sūtras, and failing to understand the sixteen aspects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A śrāvaka practitioner takes liberation as the very aim of practice, and the four noble truths' own sixteen aspects are the basic teaching that aim rests on — familiarity with these terms must come first, through hearing, before reflection and cultivation can follow toward genuine liberation. A practitioner who cannot apply real effort to this teaching, who fails to understand and put into practice the sixteen aspects of suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path, is, however clever by worldly measure, no wise one in the Buddha's own eyes — only diligent practice through hearing, reflection, and cultivation makes one a genuine knower of the world's true nature. With this, delusion regarding the truths is closed in its entirety, completing the fifth of the seven kinds of delusion. The treatise turns next to delusion regarding defilement and purity, the sixth kind.

280. Section: Delusion Regarding Defilement and Purity Begins — Ignorance Regarding the Cause

Ignorance regarding the cause means failing to understand, among ignorance and the remaining links of existence, each one's own causal capacity to give rise to formations and the rest.

Chinese Source

於因無智者，謂於無明等諸有支中，能為行等所有因性不了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding the cause means failing to understand, among ignorance and the remaining links of existence, each one's own causal capacity to give rise to formations and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations

Delusion regarding defilement and purity, the sixth kind, names failing to understand how each link of dependent origination, beginning with ignorance, serves as the cause of the link that follows it. The Determinations, fascicle 56, explain: the cause that draws every suffering onward is ignorance conditioning formations, on through contact conditioning feeling; the cause that gives rise to suffering is feeling conditioning craving, craving conditioning grasping, grasping conditioning existence — the cause of every suffering yet to come; and suffering's own arising is existence conditioning birth, birth conditioning aging and death. Failing to understand this generative cause, and the resultant formations and aggregates it gives rise to, constitutes delusion regarding defilement and purity.

281. Section: Ignorance Regarding What the Cause Produces — Examined Link by Link

Ignorance regarding what the cause produces means failing to understand, among formations and the remaining links, their own nature as produced from ignorance and the rest as their cause.

Chinese Source

於因所生無智者，謂於行等諸有支中，從無明等因所生性不了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance regarding what the cause produces means failing to understand, among formations and the remaining links, their own nature as produced from ignorance and the rest as their cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 9

Where the previous section examined dependent origination from the side of the cause, this one examines it from the side of the result — failing to understand that formations and the remaining links are themselves the result produced by ignorance and the links before them. Fascicle 9 describes two further wrong views this same ignorance can produce: mistakenly holding that birth-and-death's own dependent origination arises without any cause at all, the view of no cause; or clinging to some other, improper cause as the creator of life — a sovereign lord, primal nature, a cosmic person, or some intermediate agent — the view of an unequal cause. Ignorance about the generative cause itself is called ignorance regarding the cause; ignorance about the resultant formations it produces is called ignorance regarding what the cause produces.

282. Section: Examined Through Defiled and Pure Dharmas, and Threefold Karma — Closing Delusion Regarding Defilement and Purity Entirely

And further, regarding defiled and pure dharmas — the unwholesome, the wholesome, the culpable, the blameless, fault, and virtue, each accordingly — and regarding what accords with the dark and the bright, namely ignorance and its bright counterpart: dark-dark ripening; bright-bright ripening; and the portion that is mixed, namely dark-and-bright, bright-and-dark ripening — all of this, arising entirely from causes and conditions, is called dependently arisen. Failing to understand any of it is called ignorance.

Chinese Source

又於雜染、清淨品法，謂不善、善、有罪、無罪、過患、功德相應故。隨順黑白，謂無明、明分故。黑黑異熟；白白異熟；及有對分，謂即黑白黑白異熟。如是一切，皆從因緣之所生故，名為緣生。於彼一切不了知故，名為無智。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, regarding defiled and pure dharmas — the unwholesome, the wholesome, the culpable, the blameless, fault, and virtue, each accordingly — and regarding what accords with the dark and the bright, namely ignorance and its bright counterpart: dark-dark ripening; bright-bright ripening; and the portion that is mixed, namely dark-and-bright, bright-and-dark ripening — all of this, arising entirely from causes and conditions, is called dependently arisen. Failing to understand any of it is called ignorance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Defiled dharmas — affliction, karma, and birth — are unwholesome, culpable, and full of fault; pure dharmas — hearing, reflection, cultivation, discipline, concentration, and wisdom — are wholesome, blameless, and full of virtue; a practitioner should learn to distinguish them clearly. Karma performed in ignorance, unaware that dark deeds bring suffering, is dark karma; karma performed with the understanding that evil brings suffering, and so restrained toward good, is bright karma. Three kinds of ripening follow: dark-dark ripening, unwholesome, demerit-bound karma whose cause is dark and whose fruit — rebirth in the various hells — is dark as well; bright-bright ripening, unmoving karma such as the meditative absorption that leads to the form and formless heavens, whose cause and fruit are both bright; and the mixed portion, dark-and-bright, bright-and-dark ripening, the karma of desire-realm gods and humans, whose good deeds are mixed with occasional evil, bringing a correspondingly mixed result — some suffering even amid a fortunate human or heavenly birth, some small pleasure even amid an evil destiny's own particular karma. All of this — every karmic result of every kind of life — arises entirely through causes and conditions, hence "dependently arisen"; failing to understand any part of this whole process is delusion regarding defilement and purity. With this, delusion regarding defilement and purity — ignorance regarding the cause, regarding what the cause produces, and regarding defiled and pure dharmas together with threefold karmic ripening — is closed in its entirety, completing the sixth of the seven kinds of delusion. The treatise turns next to delusion regarding overestimation, the seventh and final kind.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, being unable to fully and truly penetrate the six bases of contact means: among what arises through contact at the six bases according with pleasant feeling and the rest, failing to truly and fully understand that its cessation is itself peaceful stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Delusion regarding overestimation, the seventh and final kind, names a subtle failure specific to meditators: contact with a pleasing object gives rise to pleasant feeling and greed; contact with a displeasing object gives rise to painful feeling and hatred; and contact with a neutral object gives rise to neutral feeling and delusion — yet none of these feelings is truly self-born, other-born, jointly born, or causelessly born; they simply arise dependent on contact, with no permanent, unchanging, substantial entity to be found, feeling itself being empty and still by nature. Failing to truly penetrate this emptiness, a meditator who, upon entering absorption, experiences the temporary cessation of painful feeling — the body and mind growing light, at ease, and joyful as greed, hatred, and the rest fall temporarily silent — may mistake that temporary stillness for the noble path itself actually attained, a serious misreading of what meditative practice has really accomplished: delusion regarding overestimation.

With this, delusion regarding overestimation, examined through both readings, is closed in its entirety, completing the seventh and final kind of delusion — and with it, all seven kinds of delusion already explained in fascicle 9, now unpacked here in full, are closed in their entirety. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Darkness names simple failure to correctly grasp what is genuinely real. Foolishness names the opposite error, falsely superimposing real existence onto what has no true nature at all — an ordinary being's habitual over-imputation, mistaking mere convention for genuine substance, clinging to the imagined and the dependently-arisen alike as though self-born, other-born, jointly born, or causelessly real, or denying the perfected nature's own genuine reality and collapsing it into mere nihilistic emptiness. Benighted ignorance names an inability to skillfully grasp whatever is to be known, whether body, feeling, mind, or dharma, whether the aggregates, elements, or sense-bases — the Yogācārabhūmi Notes clarify this as failing to understand both the meaning taught and the very words that express it. And darkening names the settled wrong view that goes so far as to slander the true dharma outright — often born from a comparative mind that reckons one's own teaching supreme and every other teaching inferior. With this, darkness, foolishness, benighted ignorance, and darkening — delusion's six further names in their entirety — are closed. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Failing to abide in a reverent, teachable mind names one who studies calm and insight, or the practice of the pure land, at great length, yet has no genuine wish to put any of it into practice, content merely to study doctrine while neglecting calm, insight, and mindfulness of the Buddha alike. Failing to practice rightly names failing to genuinely aspire toward practice in accordance with the dharma — "the dharma" here meaning nirvāṇa itself, and practice in accordance with it meaning the noble eightfold path or the thirty-seven factors of awakening; or, read another way, "the dharma" meaning the sūtras, vinaya, and treatises, and practice in accordance with it meaning conduct that follows them. 296. Section: Wasted With No Fruit, and Devoid of Meaning and Benefit — Closing the Fourth Obstruction Entirely

Chinese Source

唐捐者，不能修往善趣因故。無果者，不能得彼善趣果故。無義者，不能修得涅槃因故。無利者，不能得彼涅槃果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wasted means being unable to cultivate the cause of a fortunate destiny. Devoid of fruit means being unable to attain a fortunate destiny's own result. Devoid of meaning means being unable to cultivate nirvāṇa's own cause. Devoid of benefit means being unable to attain nirvāṇa's own fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra on the two defilements directly

Indulging in sleep beyond what practice requires wastes the time that could cultivate the causes of a fortunate rebirth, and, through laziness and negligence, forfeits that fortunate result entirely; the same indulgence likewise wastes the time that could cultivate nirvāṇa's own cause, forfeiting nirvāṇa's own fruit as well — devoid of meaning in this life, devoid of benefit in the life to come. A companion sūtra teaches that a monastic should, in every posture, whether walking or sitting in stillness, keep proper attention ever-present as a refuge, thereby escaping two defilements. Excessive sleep is contrasted here with excessive discursive thought: even monastics who might otherwise doze rather than let the mind run wild through endless discrimination over form, sound, scent, taste, and touch understand that such discrimination breeds entanglement and dispute, harming both self and others; seeing a form or hearing a sound and grasping at its mark and its finer characteristics gives rise to manifold affliction and karma leading to the evil destinies, when in truth eye, form, and the eye-consciousness born from their contact are all impermanent, conditioned, and dependently arisen, feeling itself arising and passing in exactly the same way. A learned noble disciple reflects: I shall observe the body as impermanent, every dharma as mind-generated, and mind alone as the true root of all things; feeling itself, born from contact, is likewise impermanent, conditioned, and dependently arisen. Such a disciple, knowing sleep's proper time and measure, never indulges it to excess — excessive sleep is a foolish way to sustain a life, gaining neither the benefit of nirvāṇa nor the cultivation of merit — and remains equally free of clinging to mere discrimination, since habitual grasping at marks and finer characteristics breeds affliction, entanglement, and dispute that rob many others of nirvāṇa's own benefit and ease. Growing weary of attachment to the eye and its form, its contact, and the feeling born from that contact, weary of every thought and feeling arising from it, no longer delighting in pleasing form, sound, scent, taste, or touch, nor clinging to the faculties or their objects — this very weariness is liberation, and liberation brings the knowledge of liberation itself: for the noble disciple, birth is exhausted, the chaste life established, what was to be done is done, no further existence to be known — the same cultivated restraint applying equally to ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. A companion passage adds a further caution on two additional dangers requiring proper attention: first, that one who carefully sustains their own life — through food, sleep, and every other necessity — may nonetheless meet sudden, unexpected death, and so must never neglect contemplating impermanence, since a single breath not returning can end everything; and second, that one must guard right mindfulness so that death, should it come, does not arrive amid an unwholesome state of mind and thereby cast one into the evil destinies, since escape from the three evil paths, once entered, is exceedingly hard to win back. The remedy for both dangers is proper attention itself: better to endure another's beating, even the loss of a limb or of life itself, than to die amid an unwholesome mind — and cultivating constant joy in the true dharma, especially joy in contemplating impermanence, is precisely what keeps unwholesome states from arising at the moment death actually comes. Two defilements are named here as arising from grasping at marks during ordinary activity and from distracting thoughts during seated practice, one a "resembling cause" for the other, urging a practitioner to notice how daytime habits of grasping return as evening's own distracted thoughts, and to remain watchful, ashamed of laxity, and continuous in mindfulness throughout. With this, delighting in sleep and squandering life — wasted, devoid of fruit, devoid of meaning, and devoid of benefit — is closed in its entirety, completing delusion's fourth obstruction. The unwholesome verse's fourth line's third word, scarcity and the rest, forms the unwholesome portion's eighth topic, naming three ways affliction stunts wisdom. Scarcity names wisdom left unable to grow lofty or expansive — whenever affliction actively manifests, the mind's own clarity narrows at once, and a practitioner who habitually indulges defilement never allows mindfulness the room to grow either high or wide, unable to accord with the noble teaching's own vast measure. Smallness names wisdom confined to a lowly, narrow measure — ignorance clouds its own light, leaving a practitioner content with a little learning or a lesser meditative attainment, unaware that further diligence is needed, unable to rise higher. And meagerness names wisdom reduced to accepting only whatever bare convention worldly speech allows — unable even to cultivate right view within ordinary worldly life, let alone approach any transcendent wisdom at all. With this, scarcity, smallness, and meagerness — three marks of affliction's own power to stunt wisdom — are closed in their entirety, completing the unwholesome portion's eighth topic. The treatise turns next to synonyms and the rest, the ninth and final topic.

304. Section: Synonyms, Intent, and Distinctiveness — Closing the Ninth and Final Topic, and the Entire Unwholesome Portion

Further, synonyms mean a dharma's own distinct mark, considered in itself. Intent means a dharma's own conventional mark, considered as distinct. Distinctiveness means a dharma's own causal mark, considered as distinct.

Chinese Source

復次，或異門者，自相差別故。或意趣者，俗相差別故。或殊異者，因相差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, synonyms mean a dharma's own distinct mark, considered in itself. Intent means a dharma's own conventional mark, considered as distinct. Distinctiveness means a dharma's own causal mark, considered as distinct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The unwholesome verse's fourth line's final phrase, synonyms and the rest, closes the unwholesome portion with three terms for how many names can serve one meaning. Synonyms names a dharma's own distinct mark expressed through different wording: affliction is also called a bond, on through contention, because affliction's own nature can unite with fault and sorrow, or give rise to dispute and rivalry — each name grasping the same underlying nature from its own particular angle. Intent names a dharma's own worldly, conventional mark, considered as distinct: greed, for instance, unfolds into defilement, attachment, restraint, and gluttony precisely because each names a different worldly circumstance greed can take — the distinction between a dharma's own intrinsic nature and its worldly circumstance is exactly the distinction between a lamp's own essential nature, dispelling darkness, and the countless different lamps that circumstance produces, long or short, white or yellow. And distinctiveness names how different causal marks of affliction produce different resultant circumstances: hatred's own cause produces inward stain, subduing, and ruin among its many marks; delusion's own cause obstructs a practitioner's wisdom and produces disrespect through failing to reflect among its own improper conduct — every dharma's own intrinsic mark and worldly mark alike shift accordingly with its cause. With this, synonyms, intent, and distinctiveness are closed in their entirety, completing the unwholesome portion's ninth and final topic — and with it, the entire unwholesome portion (黑品) of the Compendium of Synonyms, its full nine topics from birth through this closing account of many names for one meaning, is closed in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement closing the Compendium of Synonyms as a whole.

305. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Compendium of Synonyms in Its Entirety

This is called the Compendium of Synonyms. These synonyms, among all those scattered throughout the sūtras that are coarser, more evident, and more frequently used, have been briefly gathered here to display their distinct meanings. As for the boundless remaining synonyms and distinctions of meaning the buddhas and world-honored ones have spoken, following this same direction, this same body of learning, and this same manner of teaching, one should diligently and individually reflect upon and discern each further synonym and each further meaning, bringing out and establishing them in turn.

Chinese Source

如是名為攝異門分。如是異門，於諸經中，隨其麤顯言多用者，略已采集示差別義。其餘無量諸佛世尊所說異門及義差別，由此方隅、由此所學、由此言教，應當精勤別別思擇異門、異義顯示安立。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called the Compendium of Synonyms. These synonyms, among all those scattered throughout the sūtras that are coarser, more evident, and more frequently used, have been briefly gathered here to display their distinct meanings. As for the boundless remaining synonyms and distinctions of meaning the buddhas and world-honored ones have spoken, following this same direction, this same body of learning, and this same manner of teaching, one should diligently and individually reflect upon and discern each further synonym and each further meaning, bringing out and establishing them in turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise closes the entire Compendium of Synonyms with this final summary: everything explained across fascicles 83 and 84 gathers only the more prominent, more commonly used synonyms scattered throughout the sūtras, sorted here to display their distinct meanings clearly. Countless further synonyms and distinctions of meaning remain, spoken by the buddhas across immeasurable further teachings, which this treatise has not attempted to exhaust — but the direction it has charted, the body of learning it has modeled, and the manner of teaching it has demonstrated together offer a method: a practitioner who has followed this compendium's own reasoning should now be equipped to diligently reflect upon and discern any further synonym or distinction of meaning encountered elsewhere, bringing each one's own significance clearly to light in turn. With this, the Compendium of Synonyms (攝異門分) — the treatise's fourth of five major divisions, spanning fascicles 83 and 84 in their entirety, from the wholesome portion's five particular verses through the unwholesome portion's single verse and this closing statement — is closed in its complete entirety. Fascicle 84 concludes here; the treatise's fifth and final division, the Compendium of Bases (攝事分), begins in fascicle 85.
