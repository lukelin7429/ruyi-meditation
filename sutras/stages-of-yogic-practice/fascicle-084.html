Fascicle 84 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十四 · The Compendium of Synonyms Concludes

Desire's Eight Dangers, and the Unwholesome Portion

Fascicle 84 completes the Compendium of Synonyms (攝異門分), the treatise's fourth of five major divisions. Where fascicle 83 closed the wholesome portion's first three verses, this fascicle opens with the fourth and final wholesome verse — desire's three kinds, invitation, the dharma, the saṅgha, the excellent qualities that make giving possible, weariness, the brahmin, impermanence, and things like foam — before turning to the unwholesome portion's own single verse, covering the many names for unwholesome states. The fourth verse opens by showing desire as inverted, illusory, and deceiving of fools, then unfolds eight vivid similes for its danger: a dry bone, a piece of meat, a grass torch, a pit of coals, a venomous snake, a dream, a borrowed ornament, and rotten fruit at a treetop's end — the very similes the Buddha spoke to the householder Poṭaliya.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 84 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Compendium of Synonyms' Lower Half Opens

The Compendium of Synonyms, part two.

Chinese Source

攝異門分之下

Ru-Yi Translation

The Compendium of Synonyms, part two.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Compendium of Synonyms has an upper half, in fascicle 83, and a lower half, here in fascicle 84. The upper half contained one overall summary verse and, of the wholesome portion's four particular verses, the first three — the first of which itself further unfolded two middle-length verses on discipline and the noble path. This lower half continues the wholesome portion with its fourth and final particular verse, then adds the unwholesome portion's own single verse. The wholesome portion (白品) covers wholesome dharmas; the unwholesome portion (黑品) covers unwholesome ones.

2. Section: The Fourth Verse — Desire, Invitation, Dharma, Saṅgha, the Qualities That Give, Weariness, the Brahmin, Impermanence, and Foam

Further, the summary verse says: desire, of three kinds; invitation; the dharma; the saṅgha; the excellent qualities that give; weariness; the brahmin; impermanence; things like foam, coming last.

Chinese Source

復次，嗢柁南曰：欲三種延請　法僧惠施故　厭梵志無常　聚沫等為後

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the summary verse says: desire, of three kinds; invitation; the dharma; the saṅgha; the excellent qualities that give; weariness; the brahmin; impermanence; things like foam, coming last.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This fourth wholesome verse previews nine topics: desire's three kinds; invitation of a teacher; the dharma; the saṅgha; excellent qualities — here explained not as literal giving, but as the Buddha's own qualities, since it is by virtue of those qualities that the dharma itself is given to beings for their benefit; weariness; the brahmin; impermanence; and things like foam, appearing last.

3. Section: Desire's Three Kinds Begin — Desire as Inverted, and What It Rests On

That all desires are impermanent, false, and unreal means desire is proclaimed as inverted, since desire is the basis of four kinds of inverted view.

Chinese Source

諸欲無常虛偽不實者，謂於諸欲宣說顛倒，以是四種顛倒事故。

Ru-Yi Translation

That all desires are impermanent, false, and unreal means desire is proclaimed as inverted, since desire is the basis of four kinds of inverted view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the sub-commentary

The verse's first topic, desire's three kinds, unfolds across many sections — first the three marks of inverted, illusory, and deceiving of fools, then eight similes for its danger, then its unclean names. The Buddha regularly taught that desire is impermanent, false, and unreal, and therefore suffering: once a being clings to form, sound, scent, taste, touch, or the desire between the sexes, the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity all arise from that very clinging — form and the rest are exactly the basis on which these four inversions rest.

4. Section: Explaining the Inversion Itself — Empty, Deceptive, Painful, and Falsely Pleasurable — Closing "Desire as Inverted"

It should be known that here, empty means without self; deceptive means impure; unreal means suffering, since what is found there is falsely pleasurable, not truly so.

Chinese Source

當知此中，虛故無我，偽故不淨，不實故苦，由於是處樂非實故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that here, empty means without self; deceptive means impure; unreal means suffering, since what is found there is falsely pleasurable, not truly so.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever is loved and clung to — one's own body and mind, or any further object held and received — is empty of any true master, hence without self; it is deceptive, since ordinary beings mistake what is genuinely impure for pure, unaware of the affliction concealed within; and being unreal, arising from causes and conditions, it is ultimately suffering, its pleasure only apparent, never genuine. With this, desire's mark as inverted is closed. The treatise turns next to desire as a false dharma.

5. Section: Desire Named a False Dharma — the Simile of the Illusionist

Yet those desires appear as if permanent and the rest, and are therefore called a false dharma, being the basis of inversion. Why are desires called a false dharma? To reveal this meaning, the simile of an illusion is spoken. Though not truly permanent and the rest, they appear to be so, and are therefore alike to that illusion.

Chinese Source

然彼諸欲似常等現，說名妄法，顛倒事故。云何諸欲名為妄法？為顯此義，說幻事喻。雖非常等，然似顯現，故同彼法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet those desires appear as if permanent and the rest, and are therefore called a false dharma, being the basis of inversion. Why are desires called a false dharma? To reveal this meaning, the simile of an illusion is spoken. Though not truly permanent and the rest, they appear to be so, and are therefore alike to that illusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and the desire between the sexes appear under the guise of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity, and so are called a false dharma, giving rise to the four inversions. The Buddha often compared desire to an illusionist's conjured beauty — real-seeming at the moment it is seen, yet wholly unreal — since desire's own nature is never truly permanent, pleasurable, selfhood, or pure, only appearing to be so before the deluded mind, exactly like every other deceiving, inverting dharma in the world.

6. Section: Deceiving Fools, Never the Wise — Closing Desire's Three Marks

Deceiving fools means the unlearned fool does not truly know those desires, and is therefore forever deceived through the long night, deeply attached, touched and driven by the suffering of change; the wise are not so, since they truly know.

Chinese Source

誑惑愚夫者，謂無聞愚夫，於彼諸欲不如實知，故於長夜恆被欺誑，深生染著，為變壞苦之所逼觸；諸聰慧者則不如是，如實知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Deceiving fools means the unlearned fool does not truly know those desires, and is therefore forever deceived through the long night, deeply attached, touched and driven by the suffering of change; the wise are not so, since they truly know.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Desire, like an illusionist's conjuration, deceives only fools, never sages: one who has neither heard the dharma nor practiced it cannot truly know desire, and so is endlessly deceived through birth-and-death's long night, taking deep delight and clinging in it — and since desire is itself subject to change, that very clinging is pressed and driven by the suffering of its own eventual ruin. The wise, having accumulated the wisdom born of hearing, reflecting, and cultivating, know desire's impurity directly, and so are never so deceived; the Buddha further taught that among every kind of desire, the desire between the sexes is the most severe of all. A companion passage adds: whether of this life or the next, whether present or future, form and every desire alike are Māra's own domain, bait that lures beings back into birth-and-death, giving rise to countless unwholesome states, discursive thought, hatred, and even open strife. With this, the three marks of desire — inverted, a false dharma, and deceiving of fools — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to eight similes for desire's danger, beginning with a dry bone.

7. Section: Eight Similes Begin — Desire Compared to a Dry Bone

And those desires, compared to a dry bone, mean there is never satisfaction.

Chinese Source

又彼諸欲，喻枯骨者，令無飽故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And those desires, compared to a dry bone, mean there is never satisfaction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Basic Section and a companion sūtra

The Basic Section's Stage of Thought-Constructed Wisdom already compared a being's clinging to desire to a stray dog gnawing a dry bone — never satisfied, only ever more entangled the more it gnaws. The Madhyama Āgama's own version, spoken to the householder Poṭaliya, describes a starving dog waiting at a butcher's, gnawing the discarded bones it is thrown until its lips split and its throat is torn, never once relieved of its hunger — exactly how a being pursuing desire can never reach satisfaction, however much is gained.

8. Section: Desire Compared to a Piece of Meat — Fought Over by Many

Compared to a piece of meat means being shared by many.

Chinese Source

喻段肉者，多所共故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a piece of meat means being shared by many.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Whatever appears attractive — form, sound, scent, taste, touch — is coveted by most beings alike; whatever one being desires, another is apt to desire as well, and conflict follows. The companion sūtra tells of a scrap of meat dropped in an open field: the first bird to seize it is immediately set upon by every other bird competing for the same prize, exactly as desire, more pain than pleasure and fraught with danger, is fought over by all who see it.

9. Section: Desire Compared to a Grass Torch — the Cause of Unlawful and Evil Conduct

Compared to a grass torch means being the cause of unlawful conduct and evil conduct.

Chinese Source

喻草炬者，是非法行、惡行因故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a grass torch means being the cause of unlawful conduct and evil conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

One who clings to desire will commit no end of unlawful, evil karma seeking to satisfy it — history records kings who destroyed rival nations, even ruining their own, all for desire's sake. The companion sūtra likens desire to carrying a lit torch against the wind: unless it is quickly cast aside, it burns the hand and limbs at once — desire, too, brings far more pain than pleasure to whoever refuses to let it go.

10. Section: Desire Compared to a Pit of Coals — Increasing the Great Torment of Craving

Compared to a portion of coals means increasing the great torment of craving and desire.

Chinese Source

喻一分炭者，增長欲愛大熱惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a portion of coals means increasing the great torment of craving and desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Scripture compares desire to a smokeless pit of blazing coals — the hottest fire gives off no smoke, and touching it can take a life. One who falls into the fire-pit of craving and desire certainly suffers extreme pain, the torment of craving only increasing until it may cost life itself; the companion sūtra has the Buddha ask the householder directly whether any sane, undeluded person, loving ease and hating pain, loving life and hating death, would willingly leap into such a pit — the householder answers no, precisely the point: no wise person embraces desire once its true danger is seen.

11. Section: Desire Compared to a Venomous Snake — Shunned by Every Sage

Compared to a great venomous snake means being shunned by every sage and worthy one.

Chinese Source

喻大毒蛇者，為諸聖賢所遠離故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to a great venomous snake means being shunned by every sage and worthy one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Scripture describes the great venomous snake as utterly vicious, black, and fearsome in appearance — every sage and worthy one, being free of desire, takes no pleasure in its domain. The companion sūtra asks whether any sane person would offer a hand to such a snake and invite its bite; the householder again answers no, since anyone who sees the snake clearly moves at once to escape it — exactly as the wise, once desire's danger is truly seen, move to escape it as well.

12. Section: Desire Compared to What Is Gained in a Dream — Vanishing the Instant One Wakes

Compared to what is gained in a dream means it quickly scatters and falls apart.

Chinese Source

喻夢所得者，速散壞故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to what is gained in a dream means it quickly scatters and falls apart.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Dreaming of a hoard of gold, one wakes to find not a single coin remains; the five desires seem, in the dream, capable of satisfying everything, yet nothing at all can actually be enjoyed once one wakes. Desire, too, scatters and falls apart just as quickly — the companion sūtra has the Buddha teach exactly this to the householder, that desire is like a dream, more pain than pleasure, fraught with danger, and to be abandoned.

13. Section: Desire Compared to Borrowed Finery — Resting on Manifold Conditions, Always Returned

Compared to borrowed finery means it rests on manifold conditions.

Chinese Source

喻所假借莊嚴具者，託眾緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to borrowed finery means it rests on manifold conditions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Sūtra of Past and Present Cause and Effect

A person of modest means, wishing to hold a fine banquet, borrows a house, jewelry, plates and cups from others to furnish it — and once the banquet ends, every borrowed item must be returned. Desire is exactly this kind of borrowed finery, a phenomenon resting on manifold conditions, unable to last, bound to be given back: "borrowed things must be returned; nor can they last — of a hundred years' life, sleep consumes half, and much of what remains is anxious worry, its pleasure very little."

14. Section: Desire Compared to Rotten Fruit at a Treetop's End — a Perilous, Deadly Place — Closing All Eight Similes

Compared to rotten fruit at a treetop's end means being a place of peril and death.

Chinese Source

喻諸樹端爛熟果者，危亡地故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Compared to rotten fruit at a treetop's end means being a place of peril and death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Poṭaliya sūtra directly

A hungry traveler comes upon a fruit tree heavy with ripe fruit, but none has fallen; wanting to eat, he climbs the tree. A second hungry traveler arrives, unable to climb, and instead takes an axe to fell the whole tree — leaving the first traveler, still aloft, in mortal danger of a broken arm or worse when the tree comes down. Ripe fruit at a treetop's end is exactly such a perilous, deadly place — whoever loves and clings to desire invites exactly this kind of danger from others. With this, all eight similes for desire's danger — the dry bone, the piece of meat, the grass torch, the pit of coals, the venomous snake, the dream, borrowed finery, and rotten fruit at a treetop's end — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to desire's unclean names.

15. Section: Desire's Unclean Names — Overall and in Detail — Closing "Desire, of Three Kinds" Entirely

And uncleanliness is its overall statement. Foul stench means what is consumed as food, once spoiled, becomes this; urine and feces, spoiled and unclean, are formed this way, hence called the foul place; flesh and blood and the rest, also spoiled, are formed this way, hence called raw stench. What should be shunned with revulsion means what is formed through the practice of sexual desire, spoiled, and therefore rightly abhorred and shunned.

Chinese Source

又不淨者，是其總句。

言臭穢者，受用飲食變壞成故。屎尿不淨變壞所成，故名臭處。諸肉血等變壞所成，故名生臭。可厭逆者，受用婬欲變壞所成，可惡逆故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And uncleanliness is its overall statement.

Foul stench means what is consumed as food, once spoiled, becomes this; urine and feces, spoiled and unclean, are formed this way, hence called the foul place; flesh and blood and the rest, also spoiled, are formed this way, hence called raw stench. What should be shunned with revulsion means what is formed through the practice of sexual desire, spoiled, and therefore rightly abhorred and shunned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A body born through sexual desire is by nature unclean from the start, and must further be sustained by food, which spoils within the body — hence foul stench in general; its spoiled residue becomes urine and feces, the foul place; and its spoiled nourishment becomes flesh and blood, raw stench. Since a desire-realm body is itself formed through the practice of sexual desire spoiling and changing, it should be shunned with revulsion rather than clung to — for clinging to it only guarantees a further such body to come. Foul stench, the foul place, and raw stench all trace back to sexual desire in the end; without it, there would be no need to consume food, and no such unclean resultant body at all — which is exactly why cultivating the contemplation of uncleanliness on this very desire-realm body can free a practitioner from attachment to physical form. With this, desire's three kinds — its marks, its eight similes, and its unclean names — are closed in their entirety, completing the fourth verse's first topic. The treatise turns next to invitation, the second topic.

16. Section: Invitation Begins — Inviting to Give Up Worldly Wealth, Receiving to Exhaust Craving

Further, to properly invite means with reference to giving up worldly wealth. To properly receive means with reference to exhausting craving. Wishing to seek a resultant reward, one therefore invites; wishing to seek liberation, one therefore receives.

Chinese Source

復次，應招延者，約捨世財。應奉請者，約盡貪愛。

欲求果報，是故招延。欲求解脫，是故奉請。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, to properly invite means with reference to giving up worldly wealth. To properly receive means with reference to exhausting craving.

Wishing to seek a resultant reward, one therefore invites; wishing to seek liberation, one therefore receives.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's second topic, invitation, unfolds across three sections. To invite means giving up one's worldly wealth to make offerings to the Buddha and the noble disciples, seeking a favorable future reward; to receive means welcoming and requesting their teaching on how to cut off craving, seeking liberation itself — one act aimed at reward, the other at freedom.

17. Section: Proper Reverence — Joined Palms, and Harmonious Respect

Proper joining of palms means, in inviting for the sake of these two matters. Proper harmonious respect means one should offer worship and inquiry, and should share the same discipline and view as they do.

Chinese Source

應合掌者，即為二事而延請時。應和敬者，應設禮拜問訊等故，應可與彼戒見同故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Proper joining of palms means, in inviting for the sake of these two matters. Proper harmonious respect means one should offer worship and inquiry, and should share the same discipline and view as they do.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

When inviting the Buddha and the noble disciples for the sake of reward and liberation alike, one should join the palms in reverence, bow, and inquire after their well-being — and, further, emulate their virtue by keeping pure discipline and cultivating right view oneself, matching their own qualities as closely as one can.

18. Section: The Unsurpassed Field of Merit the World Should Serve — Closing Invitation Entirely

The unsurpassed field of merit the world should serve and give to means the resultant reward of giving to them is immeasurable.

Chinese Source

無上福田世應奉施者，於彼惠施果無量故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The unsurpassed field of merit the world should serve and give to means the resultant reward of giving to them is immeasurable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

The Buddha and the noble disciples are the world's unsurpassed field of merit, the very object worldly people should serve and give to, since giving to them yields an immeasurable, boundless reward. A companion sūtra describes a monk who, seeing form with the eye, hearing sound with the ear, and so through the remaining senses, dwells always in supreme equanimity, neither pained nor pleased, mindful and clearly comprehending — one who accomplishes these six constant practices, as the venerable Śāriputra and the other great elders did, is rare in the world, worthy of the world's service, reverence, and offering, the world's own unsurpassed field of merit. With this, invitation, the fourth verse's second topic, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the dharma, the third topic.

19. Section: The Dharma Begins — Skillfully Taught, and Directly Seen

Further, well-spoken means the text and its meaning are skillful and wondrous. Directly seen means it can be realized within the very dharma now present.

Chinese Source

復次，善說者，文義巧妙故。現見者，於現法中可證得故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, well-spoken means the text and its meaning are skillful and wondrous. Directly seen means it can be realized within the very dharma now present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's third topic, the dharma — explaining the third word of "the dharma, the saṅgha, and the excellent qualities that give" — unfolds first through the teaching-dharma (教法), then the realization-dharma (證法). The Buddha's teaching is good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, its text and meaning alike skillful and subtle beyond compare — well-spoken. And a practitioner who follows that teaching can, within the very aggregates present right now, truly know how affliction arises through the six faculties meeting the six objects, and can, in this very life, extinguish that affliction's blaze and realize stillness directly — directly seen.

20. Section: The Realization-Dharma Begins — Cessation's Synonyms — Free of Feverish Heat

Free of feverish heat means being free of affliction.

Chinese Source

無熱者，離煩惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of feverish heat means being free of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained the teaching-dharma, the treatise turns to the realization-dharma, opening with six synonyms for the truth of cessation. A practitioner who, facing form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and mental objects, grows free of greed, hatred, and delusion attains a mind that is still, without affliction's own blazing heat — hence free of feverish heat.

21. Section: Free of Time — Transcending the Three Times

Free of time means transcending the three times.

Chinese Source

無時者，出三世故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of time means transcending the three times.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner who realizes the unconditioned dharma thereby transcends every conditioned dharma of past, present, and future alike — hence also called that which does not wait upon time.

22. Section: Hard to Draw Onward, and Hard to See

Hard to draw onward means aging, sickness, death, and the rest cannot draw one onward further. Hard to see means it cannot be seen among the heavenly and other destinies.

Chinese Source

難引者，老病死等不能引故。

難見者，天等趣中不可見故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hard to draw onward means aging, sickness, death, and the rest cannot draw one onward further.

Hard to see means it cannot be seen among the heavenly and other destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once greed, hatred, and delusion toward form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and mental objects are extinguished, the twelve-linked chain of dependent origination — aging, sickness, death, and the rest — can no longer draw a being onward into further birth-and-death. And cessation itself cannot be witnessed by heavenly beings or beings of any other destiny; only the human realm, where both pleasure and pain are directly tasted, serves as a fit vessel for realizing it.

23. Section: Realized Inwardly by Oneself — Excluding the Ordinary, Naming Only the Noble

Realized inwardly by oneself means what mere faith in another cannot realize. Those with wisdom means the trainee and the one beyond training.

Chinese Source

內自所證者，唯信他等不能證故。諸有智者者，謂學無學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Realized inwardly by oneself means what mere faith in another cannot realize.

Those with wisdom means the trainee and the one beyond training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Merely believing the teaching spoken by the Buddha or another good teacher, without ever cultivating and realizing it oneself, remains stuck at the level of hearing and reflection, never truly touching the unconditioned — excluding the ordinary being from realization. Those genuinely capable of realizing it inwardly are the trainee (the noble ones of the first three fruits) and the one beyond training (the arhat of the fourth fruit); one who has truly realized the dharma has, in that very realization, already seen the Buddha.

24. Section: Dwelling, Island, Refuge, Rescue, Destination — Closing Cessation's Six Synonyms Entirely

Dwelling, island, rescue, refuge, and destination: each later phrase explains the one before it, revealing the meaning of gradual escape.

Chinese Source

為舍、為洲、為救、為歸、為趣者，由後後句釋前前句，顯出離義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dwelling, island, rescue, refuge, and destination: each later phrase explains the one before it, revealing the meaning of gradual escape.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium and a companion sūtra directly

The Compendium explains each phrase through the one that follows it: cessation is called a dwelling because it is the basis of blameless joy — safe to rest in, like a house. It is called an island because it explains "blameless joy," an island being a sandbar that cuts a sea off from its shore, exactly as cessation cuts off the three realms, the ultimate basis of blameless joy. It is called rescue because it explains "cutting off the three realms" — cessation's own virtue can block every great suffering and calamity, thereby cutting a being off from the three realms' birth-and-death. It is called refuge because it explains "blocking every great suffering and calamity" — nirvāṇa's own state is the basis of pure aspiration and skillful means free of false discrimination, and so naturally invites no calamity at all. And it is called the destination a practitioner ultimately turns toward because it explains "the basis free of false aspiration and means" — cessation is the very basis every noble nature turns toward and relies upon, where every sage ultimately arrives. A companion sūtra lists some twenty further names alongside nirvāṇa itself — the unconditioned, hard to see, unmoving, unbending, deathless, undefiled, sheltering, an island, a crossing, a support, a protection, free of drifting, free of blazing, free of burning, flowing forth, cool, subtle and wondrous, secure, without sickness, without anything at all — of which this treatise explains only a select few. With this, cessation's own many synonyms, the first half of the realization-dharma, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the path's own gradual unfolding.

25. Section: The Path's Gradual Unfolding Begins — Naming Right View

And further, being able to truly know the four noble truths is called right view.

Chinese Source

又能了知四聖諦故，名為正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, being able to truly know the four noble truths is called right view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner who cultivates insight through the noble eightfold path taught by the Buddha can, within this very life, enter the four noble truths of suffering, its cessation, and the path, realizing the fruit of stream-entry — seeing the dharma, attaining the dharma, thoroughly penetrating the dharma, standing on ultimately firm ground, transcending every uncertain hope, crossing every doubt without depending on another's word, unshaken by any teaching outside the great teacher's own, fearless with respect to every dharma — this accomplished, undefiled right view is what the path's gradual unfolding names first.

26. Section: The Marks of Gradual Unfolding — Arising, Already Arisen, Now Arising, and Yet to Arise

Arising means it is able to arise at every time. Already arisen means abiding, in a past life, in the stage beyond training. Now arising means, in the present life, either already realized or perfected through cultivation. Yet to arise means either not yet realized, or being diligently cultivated.

Chinese Source

言生起者，於一切時容可生故。已生起者，於過去世住無學位。今生起者，於現在世或已證得，或修圓滿。當生起者，或未證得，或勤修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Arising means it is able to arise at every time. Already arisen means abiding, in a past life, in the stage beyond training. Now arising means, in the present life, either already realized or perfected through cultivation. Yet to arise means either not yet realized, or being diligently cultivated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

Undefiled right view, sorted across the three times: whoever cultivates the sixteen aspects of the four truths as the Buddha taught can realize it in any time whatsoever — arising in general. An arhat beyond training who realized it in a past life shows it already arisen; a trainee at the first fruit realizing it just now, or one newly entering the fruition beyond training, shows it now arising; and an ordinary being on the verge of realization, diligently cultivating the four truths' teaching toward a future realization, shows it yet to arise.

27. Section: What Should Be Cultivated, Practiced, and Repeatedly Practiced

What should be cultivated, practiced, and repeatedly practiced should each be understood as before, according to what is fitting.

Chinese Source

應修、應習、應多修習者，隨其所應，如前當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

What should be cultivated, practiced, and repeatedly practiced should each be understood as before, according to what is fitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 83 directly

This refers back to the seven applications of attention already explained in fascicle 83 regarding the perception of impermanence: cultivated corresponds to the application of noting characteristics, or more broadly to simply setting out toward the object to be known; practiced corresponds to the application of resolve, going beyond hearing and reflection into actual meditative resolve on the object, or to sustained, earnest exertion without interruption; and repeatedly practiced corresponds to the applications of withdrawal, gathering joy, and examination, or more broadly to long, thoroughly seasoned practice over time. Since these map onto the seven applications of attention from different angles — sometimes by the attention itself, sometimes by the exertion it produces — the text simply says "according to what is fitting."

28. Section: What Should Be Safeguarded — Warding Off Regression

What should be safeguarded means being far removed from whatever accords with regression and falling back.

Chinese Source

應隨護者，遠離隨順退墮法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What should be safeguarded means being far removed from whatever accords with regression and falling back.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the fourfold right effort

A practitioner must safeguard right view once gained — avoiding bad companions, wrong teachings, and improper attention, lest it regress or be lost. This corresponds to the third of the four right efforts, safeguarding-cutting: diligently upholding a still-unarisen wholesome dharma, such as undefiled right view not yet perfected, so that it does not regress or fall away once gained.

29. Section: What Should Be Touched, and Realized — Closing the Path's Gradual Unfolding, and the Entire Realization-Dharma

Being touched means through the body itself. Being realized as a witness means either the fruit or the surpassing wisdom, as is said: I have already realized the path.

Chinese Source

言應觸者，由身體故。應作證者，或果、或勝智，如說：我已證道故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being touched means through the body itself. Being realized as a witness means either the fruit or the surpassing wisdom, as is said: I have already realized the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases and a companion sūtra

Being touched names the six faculties meeting the six objects and thereby truly knowing one's own mind's purity or defilement in that very contact; being realized as a witness names actually attaining the noble fruit through surpassing wisdom, exactly as the Buddha's own words declare: "I have already realized the path." A companion sūtra records the Buddha teaching the monks that, before his own awakening, it was through right reflection that he came to know the noble truth of suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path leading to its cessation, right view thereby arisen — and the Compendium of Bases adds that, once the four truths are seen, undifferentiated undefiled right view arises across all three times identically: called already-arisen with respect to the past, now-arising with respect to the present, yet-to-arise with respect to the future; worldly right view should be safeguarded; a trainee's right view, together with the fruit of what it has cut off, should be touched; and a trainee-beyond-training's right view, together with its own fruit of freedom from bondage, should be realized as a witness — and liberating wisdom follows the very same fourfold pattern. With this, the path's gradual unfolding — right view, its four marks, safeguarding, touching, and witness — is closed in its entirety, completing the realization-dharma, and with it, both halves of the dharma topic's inner content. The treatise turns next to the third subtopic of the dharma: the manner of teaching it well.

30. Section: The Manner of Teaching Well Begins — Speaking at the Right Time, and a Proper Preamble

Speaking at the right time means teaching only once one truly knows the listener is eager to hear and capable of hearing it; sitting on a lower seat and the rest — this is called timeliness. One should properly offer a preamble, what should be done at the outset.

Chinese Source

若了知彼願樂欲聞及堪聞者，方可為說。坐卑座等，是名為時。

應當序說先時所作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Speaking at the right time means teaching only once one truly knows the listener is eager to hear and capable of hearing it; sitting on a lower seat and the rest — this is called timeliness.

One should properly offer a preamble, what should be done at the outset.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage and the Śrāvaka Stage

The dharma's third subtopic, the manner of teaching well, unfolds across ten sections. First, timeliness: a teacher should teach only once the listener genuinely wishes to hear and is capable of receiving it — teaching someone unready only burdens them further, benefiting neither side — and should mind proper demeanor, never sitting on a high seat while an able-bodied listener sits below, nor standing to teach a seated listener, nor teaching from behind someone walking ahead, nor teaching one whose head is covered, as the Bodhisattva Stage explains. And before the deeper teaching, a proper preamble should first draw out the listener's own interest — typically the preliminary teachings of giving, discipline, and rebirth in the heavens, broadly showing desire's own faults and the benefit of renunciation, before the four truths' own ultimate teaching follows, as the Śrāvaka Stage explains.

31. Section: Teaching Earnestly and Solemnly

Once one truly knows the listener seeks to be uplifted, one should teach earnestly and solemnly, giving everything within one's own capacity according to what the listener can receive — wishing to reveal each distinct, never-before-heard meaning, not merely delighting in flowery language for its own sake.

Chinese Source

若了知彼是增上已，即便殷重，隨其所能，盡己所有而為說法。為欲開示彼彼差別未曾有義，非直華詞樂說而已。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once one truly knows the listener seeks to be uplifted, one should teach earnestly and solemnly, giving everything within one's own capacity according to what the listener can receive — wishing to reveal each distinct, never-before-heard meaning, not merely delighting in flowery language for its own sake.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Knowing that a listener has come genuinely wishing to be uplifted, and capable of receiving the teaching, a teacher should hold nothing back, teaching earnestly and with the full measure of their own ability — never so absorbed in ornate phrasing that the teaching's actual substance is lost.

32. Section: In Sequence, Attending to Hidden Meaning, and Drawing Connections

In sequence means revealing the meaning. Attending to hidden meaning means posing objections. Drawing connections means clarifying and explaining them.

Chinese Source

次第者，開示義故。隨密者，設妨難故。隨會者，顯釋彼故。

Ru-Yi Translation

In sequence means revealing the meaning. Attending to hidden meaning means posing objections. Drawing connections means clarifying and explaining them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Teaching in sequence means the content unfolds from shallow to deep, step by step; attending to hidden meaning means drawing out a sūtra's own concealed intent, even posing hypothetical objections and answering them directly, to sharpen the listener's grasp of a difficult point; and drawing connections means showing how one teaching connects with another — the four noble truths with the four foundations of mindfulness, the three dharma seals, or the eightfold path — a teacher fluent enough to weave the whole teaching together.

33. Section: Bringing Joy, Bringing Fondness, and Bringing Delight — for Three Kinds of Listener

Bringing joy means converting one who already accepts the teaching. Bringing fondness means converting one who stands in the middle. Bringing delight means converting one who slanders it.

Chinese Source

令歡喜者，化受教者故。令愛樂者，化處中者故。令喜樂者，化誹謗者故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bringing joy means converting one who already accepts the teaching. Bringing fondness means converting one who stands in the middle. Bringing delight means converting one who slanders it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three kinds of listener call for three distinct approaches: one already confident in the dharma should be taught with skillful, varied means so their joy remains full; one neither confident nor hostile, standing in the middle, should be roused toward genuine enthusiasm and fondness for continued listening; and one who actively slanders the dharma should be brought, through the teaching itself, to such delight that the slander dissolves and they willingly return to hear more.

34. Section: Praising and Encouraging, Reproving and Expelling

Praising and encouraging means seeking out genuine virtue, and, with a mind that matches it, giving voice to praise and commendation. Reproving and expelling means observing genuine fault, and, with a mind free of hatred, giving voice to correction and reproof.

Chinese Source

讚勵者，求彼實德，以稱順心，發自言音揄揚讚美。訶擯者，觀彼實過，以無恚心，發自言音開示訶責。

Ru-Yi Translation

Praising and encouraging means seeking out genuine virtue, and, with a mind that matches it, giving voice to praise and commendation. Reproving and expelling means observing genuine fault, and, with a mind free of hatred, giving voice to correction and reproof.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A listener who has genuinely gained virtue in the dharma should be praised in words that match and encourage that virtue, so their confidence grows and they continue to study; and a listener with genuine fault should be corrected with a mind entirely free of hatred or malice, gently setting the fault right rather than attacking the person.

35. Section: The Four Kinds of Reasoning

Reasoning means possessing four kinds of reasoning: the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of function, the reasoning of intrinsic nature, and the reasoning of proof.

Chinese Source

道理者，具四道理故，謂觀待道理、作用道理、法爾道理、證成道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reasoning means possessing four kinds of reasoning: the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of function, the reasoning of intrinsic nature, and the reasoning of proof.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever is taught can be examined through four angles: dependence — this dharma is established only in dependence on that dharma; function — a dharma's own characteristic explained through what it does; intrinsic nature — a dharma's nature simply being what it is, spoken of as it naturally stands; and proof — establishing a teaching through scriptural authority, inference, and direct perception together.

36. Section: Beneficial, Unmixed, and Possessing the Dharma

Beneficial means never abandoning the place one aims to help. Unmixed means being free of disorder, each part properly connected. Possessing the dharma means being able to draw out its meaning, giving rise to boundless kinds of renunciation and detachment resting on suffering and the rest.

Chinese Source

有益者，於所為處不棄捨故。無雜者，無雜亂故，有繫屬故。有法者，能引義故，依於苦等有無量種出離遠離所生法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beneficial means never abandoning the place one aims to help.

Unmixed means being free of disorder, each part properly connected.

Possessing the dharma means being able to draw out its meaning, giving rise to boundless kinds of renunciation and detachment resting on suffering and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beneficial names never abandoning the beings one aims to help, teaching them according to the dharma without fail. Unmixed names two things at once: no disorder, meaning never teaching at the wrong time, and proper connection, meaning the earlier and later meaning taught remains coherent throughout, never contradicting itself. And possessing the dharma names the teaching's own capacity to lead a listener all the way to nirvāṇa's benefit — resting on suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path, it opens boundless paths of renunciation from affliction and its dust, freeing a listener from suffering and its arising, and leading them to realize cessation.

37. Section: Fitting the Assembly

Fitting the assembly means according with what the four kinds of assembly — khattiyas and the rest — can each receive.

Chinese Source

如眾會者，隨剎帝利等四種會眾所堪能故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fitting the assembly means according with what the four kinds of assembly — khattiyas and the rest — can each receive.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A teaching must fit the capacity of whoever gathers to hear it — in ancient India, the four castes of brahmins, khattiyas (warrior-nobles), vaiśyas, and śūdras — pitched so that each of these four different assemblies can genuinely receive and understand it.

38. Section: A Mind of Loving-Kindness, of Benefit, and of Compassion

With a mind of loving-kindness means wishing to bring them happiness. A mind of benefit means: how might one bring it about that those who listen to the true dharma earnestly all attain understanding and gain great benefit? A mind of compassion means wishing to bring them to practice the dharma in accordance with the dharma.

Chinese Source

以慈心者，為欲令彼得樂義故。利益心者，云何當令若有殷重聽聞正法，皆得悟解，獲大利益故。哀愍心者，欲令彼修法隨法行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

With a mind of loving-kindness means wishing to bring them happiness. A mind of benefit means: how might one bring it about that those who listen to the true dharma earnestly all attain understanding and gain great benefit? A mind of compassion means wishing to bring them to practice the dharma in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three further attitudes a teacher should hold: loving-kindness, wishing every listener genuine happiness, worldly and beyond; benefit, actively seeking how to bring earnest listeners to real understanding and great gain; and compassion, wishing that listeners will go on to actually practice the dharma they have heard, in accordance with the dharma itself.

39. Section: Without Dependence, Without Contempt — Closing the Manner of Teaching Well, and All Ten of Its Sections

Without dependence means not for the sake of gain, honor, or renown — not relying on robes and the like, nor on receiving worship and reverence, wishing only that the listener awaken to the true dharma. Nor does one hold contempt for another, and so on at length, never exalting oneself for the sake of gain, honor, or such things, saying: "Only I can know this dharma and discipline, not people like you" — and so on at length, praising one's own virtues while speaking of another's faults.

Chinese Source

無所依者，不為利養恭敬名稱故；謂不依止衣服等事，亦不依止禮敬等事，唯欲令他悟入正法。又不於他有所輕懱，乃至廣說不自高者，不為利養恭敬事故，作如是言：唯我能知如是法律，非汝等輩；乃至廣說讚己功德，談彼過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Without dependence means not for the sake of gain, honor, or renown — not relying on robes and the like, nor on receiving worship and reverence, wishing only that the listener awaken to the true dharma. Nor does one hold contempt for another, and so on at length, never exalting oneself for the sake of gain, honor, or such things, saying: "Only I can know this dharma and discipline, not people like you" — and so on at length, praising one's own virtues while speaking of another's faults.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A true teacher does not teach for the sake of material gain, honor, or fame — not for robes, food, medicine, or lodging, and not for the worship it might bring — wishing only that the listener come to awaken in the true dharma. Nor should a teacher ever hold contempt for a listener who fails to understand or asks an off-point question, nor grow arrogant, claiming "only I truly understand this dharma and discipline, people like you never will," nor endlessly praise their own virtues while pointing out others' faults, nor claim that only their own teaching is correct and every other teaching false. With this, all ten sections of the manner of teaching well — timeliness and preamble, earnestness, sequence and hidden meaning, the three kinds of listener, praise and reproof, the four kinds of reasoning, benefit and coherence, fitting the assembly, the threefold mind, and freedom from dependence or contempt — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the manner of listening well.

40. Section: Not Disparaging the Dharma, Not Disparaging the Teacher — Closing Freedom from Contemptuous Defilement

Not disparaging the dharma means not saying: this is not embellished with fine words and phrases, since all its wording is entirely crude and shallow. Not disparaging the teacher means not saying: this person's own lineage is lowlier than mine, and so on.

Chinese Source

不輕法者，不作是言：此非綺飾文字章句，所有文句悉皆麤淺故。

不輕法師者，不作是言：彼於我所種姓卑劣等故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not disparaging the dharma means not saying: this is not embellished with fine words and phrases, since all its wording is entirely crude and shallow.

Not disparaging the teacher means not saying: this person's own lineage is lowlier than mine, and so on.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A listener should never dismiss a teaching merely for its plain wording, as though only elegant phrasing deserved respect; nor should a listener look down on a teacher for a humbler birth or lesser standing than their own — both are forms of the same contemptuous defilement. With this, freedom from contemptuous defilement, revering the teacher, not disparaging the dharma, and not disparaging the teacher together, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to freedom from timid defilement.

41. Section: Not Belittling Oneself — Closing a Mind Free of Defilement Entirely

Not belittling oneself means not saying: I have no capacity to understand the dharma — never timid or lowly toward what can be realized.

Chinese Source

不自輕者，不作是言：我於解法無有力能。於其所證無怯劣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not belittling oneself means not saying: I have no capacity to understand the dharma — never timid or lowly toward what can be realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A listener must equally avoid the opposite fault of self-doubt — never claiming incapacity to understand the dharma, never shrinking timidly before the very highest awakening the Buddha himself realized, as though it could never be one's own to attain. With this, a mind free of defilement — freedom from arrogant defilement, from contemptuous defilement, and from timid defilement — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a mind free of distraction.

42. Section: A Mind Free of Distraction Begins — a Mind That Reveres the Teaching

A mind that reveres the teaching means a mind free of any wish to disturb, seeking only to understand.

Chinese Source

奉教心者，無惱亂心，唯欲求解故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind that reveres the teaching means a mind free of any wish to disturb, seeking only to understand.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A mind free of distraction unfolds across five further sections. First, a listener should hold a mind that reveres the teaching itself, entirely free of any wish to disturb or obstruct the teacher, seeking only genuine understanding.

43. Section: A Mind Set on a Single Direction

A mind set on a single direction means wishing to grasp the distinctions of the text and its phrasing.

Chinese Source

心一趣者，為欲領解文句差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind set on a single direction means wishing to grasp the distinctions of the text and its phrasing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

While listening, the mind should hold a single direction only — wholly absorbed in grasping every distinction in the dharma's own wording, without wandering elsewhere.

44. Section: Ears Given Wholly to Listening

Ears given wholly to listening means wishing to discern the distinctions of tone and cadence.

Chinese Source

屬耳聽者，為欲了知音韻差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ears given wholly to listening means wishing to discern the distinctions of tone and cadence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The ear itself should attend closely enough to catch the teacher's own tone and cadence — the particular pronunciation given to a term, or the rise in the voice when a crucial point is being emphasized — since even these details can carry meaning.

45. Section: A Mind Cultivated and Trained

A mind cultivated and trained means wishing to awaken to the profound meaning.

Chinese Source

修治意者，為欲悟入甚深義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind cultivated and trained means wishing to awaken to the profound meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond merely hearing, a listener should further train the mind itself, working to counter its own afflictions, so that the dharma's most profound meaning can genuinely be entered.

46. Section: A Mind Never Without Mindfulness — Closing a Mind Free of Distraction, and the Manner of Listening Well Entirely — Closing the Entire Dharma Topic

A mind never without mindfulness in anything means, wishing without interruption to grasp the meaning of tone, text, and phrase alike, there is nothing not truly known, nothing not thoroughly penetrated, and no time passed in vain.

Chinese Source

於一切心無不繫念者，為欲無間領解音韻文句義故，無不了知、無不通達而空過者。

Ru-Yi Translation

A mind never without mindfulness in anything means, wishing without interruption to grasp the meaning of tone, text, and phrase alike, there is nothing not truly known, nothing not thoroughly penetrated, and no time passed in vain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Finally, mindfulness should hold every dharma ever heard without gap — a listener so genuinely absorbed that understanding arises the instant it is heard, nothing left unknown, nothing left unpenetrated, no moment of listening ever wasted. With this, a mind free of distraction — reverence, single-direction focus, attentive ears, a trained mind, and unbroken mindfulness — is closed in its entirety, completing the manner of listening well. And with it, the dharma topic's full four subtopics — the teaching-dharma, the realization-dharma, the manner of teaching well, and the manner of listening well — are closed in their entirety, completing the fourth verse's third topic. The treatise turns next to the saṅgha, the fourth topic.

47. Section: The Saṅgha Begins — Right Conduct's Overall Statement

Further, so-called right conduct is the overall statement.

Chinese Source

復次，言正行者，謂是總句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, so-called right conduct is the overall statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth verse's fourth topic, the saṅgha — explaining the third word of "the dharma, the saṅgha, and the excellent qualities that give" — unfolds across five sections, opening with right conduct's own many synonyms. Right conduct, stated first as a single overall summary, names the mind and conduct every practitioner should cultivate.

48. Section: In Accord With Reason — the Trainee Abiding in a Fruit

Conduct in accord with reason belongs to the trainee abiding in a fruit.

Chinese Source

應理行者，住果有學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct in accord with reason belongs to the trainee abiding in a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Right conduct's first detailed reading names four terms. A trainee abiding in the first, second, or third fruit, whose every action accords with the true nature of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, is conduct in accord with reason — the right conduct proper to a trainee who already abides in a fruit.

49. Section: Upright and Direct — the Trainee Moving Toward a Fruit

Upright, direct conduct belongs to one abiding on the path toward a fruit.

Chinese Source

質直行者，住於向道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Upright, direct conduct belongs to one abiding on the path toward a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A trainee on the path moving toward a fruit — toward the first, second, third, or fourth fruit — is certain to realize that fruit directly, and so is called upright, direct conduct; the fifteen mind-moments preceding stream-entry's own sixteenth, for instance, all belong to this moving-toward stage, only the sixteenth moment itself actually realizing the fruit.

50. Section: Harmonious Reverence — the One Beyond Training

Harmoniously reverent conduct belongs to the one beyond training, since only that one holds deep reverence for the great teacher, the true dharma, and the training precepts alike.

Chinese Source

和敬行者，是其無學。由彼唯於大師、正法及學處等深恭敬故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Harmoniously reverent conduct belongs to the one beyond training, since only that one holds deep reverence for the great teacher, the true dharma, and the training precepts alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Only the arhat beyond training genuinely fulfills the six kinds of harmonious reverence within a saṅgha: shared view, shared inner joy, shared discipline, shared benefit, dwelling together in bodily harmony, and speech free of dispute — a fullness of harmony only complete realization can sustain.

51. Section: Practice in Accordance With the Dharma — Closing the First Reading of Right Conduct's Four Terms

Conduct that follows the dharma means, while still in the causal stage of turning toward it, practicing in accordance with the dharma — through hearing another's voice, correctly and properly reflecting within.

Chinese Source

隨法行者，於因轉時法隨法行。由聞他音，內正如理而思惟故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct that follows the dharma means, while still in the causal stage of turning toward it, practicing in accordance with the dharma — through hearing another's voice, correctly and properly reflecting within.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Śrāvaka practitioners, not yet having realized a fruit, cultivate the noble eightfold path with nirvāṇa as their aim; Mahāyāna practitioners cultivate the thirty-seven factors of awakening and the six perfections with unsurpassed awakening as their aim — these causal-stage efforts toward realizing nirvāṇa or unsurpassed awakening are together called conduct that follows the dharma. Such practice continues arising in succession because, having heard a good teacher's own voice, the mind remains in accord with the true dharma, correctly and properly reflecting exactly as the Buddha taught — from which the aspiration for unsurpassed awakening arises, and diligent practice that follows the dharma proceeds. With this, right conduct's four terms — conduct in accord with reason, upright and direct conduct, harmoniously reverent conduct, and conduct that follows the dharma — are closed under their first reading. The treatise turns next to a second reading of the same four terms.

52. Section: A Second Reading of the Same Four Terms — Closing Right Conduct's Synonyms Entirely

And further, conduct in accord with reason means the very path and the fruit's extinguishing conduct. Upright, direct conduct means practicing rightly exactly as the noble teaching directs, free of flattery and deceit, showing itself just as it truly is. Harmoniously reverent conduct means being joined together with the six firm dharmas. Conduct that follows the dharma means practicing in accordance with the dharma.

Chinese Source

又應理行者，是其正道及果滅行。質直行者，如其聖教而正修行，無諂無誑，如實顯現。和敬行者，與六堅法而共相應。

隨法行者，法隨法行。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, conduct in accord with reason means the very path and the fruit's extinguishing conduct. Upright, direct conduct means practicing rightly exactly as the noble teaching directs, free of flattery and deceit, showing itself just as it truly is. Harmoniously reverent conduct means being joined together with the six firm dharmas.

Conduct that follows the dharma means practicing in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

A second angle on the same four terms: conduct in accord with reason now names the undefiled eightfold path itself, succeeding in practice, and the power, once a fruit is realized, to extinguish affliction — this is what it truly means to accord with reason, joined to the undefiled noble path. Upright, direct conduct now names practicing exactly as the Buddha's own noble teaching directs, with no flattery or deceit performed for others' eyes, nothing dressed up to win material gain — simply shown just as it truly is. Harmoniously reverent conduct now names accord with the six firm dharmas — the Yogācārabhūmi Notes, fascicle 22, confirm the six firm dharmas are simply the six kinds of harmonious reverence themselves, body, speech, mind, discipline, view, and benefit shared with the community; a saṅgha that sustains this sixfold harmony ensures the Buddha's own teaching endures long in the world. And conduct that follows the dharma admits two further readings here: first, "the dharma" names nirvāṇa itself as the goal, and "following the dharma" names the eightfold path as the cause that leads toward it; second, "the dharma" names the sūtras, vinaya, and treatises, and "following the dharma" names practice carried out in accordance with them. With this, right conduct's own many synonyms, read through both explanations, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the many synonyms for right conduct's own fruit.

53. Section: The Fruit of Right Conduct — the Arhat's Every Defilement Permanently Exhausted — Closing Right Conduct's Attainment Entirely

Every arhat has permanently exhausted every defilement, and so on, spoken of at length.

Chinese Source

諸阿羅漢諸漏永盡，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every arhat has permanently exhausted every defilement, and so on, spoken of at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage

An arhat has permanently exhausted every defilement of craving and view alike — the full extended description, already given in fascicle 34's own account of the Śrāvaka Stage, is not repeated here, only referenced. With this, right conduct's attainment — its own synonyms across two readings, and its fruit — is closed in its entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's first section, right conduct. The treatise turns next to the saṅgha's remaining four sections.

54. Section: Sons of the Great Teacher Begin — the Overall Statement

And "sons of the great teacher" is the overall statement.

Chinese Source

又大師子者，是其總句。

Ru-Yi Translation

And "sons of the great teacher" is the overall statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The saṅgha topic's second of five sections gives five names for genuine sons of the great teacher, the Buddha himself, followed by an account of their twofold perfection.

55. Section: Born of the Womb — Excluding the Ordinary Being's Lowlier Sonship

Born of the womb means excluding the lowlier sonship of the ordinary being.

Chinese Source

腹所生者，簡去異生卑劣子故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of the womb means excluding the lowlier sonship of the ordinary being.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

A genuine child of the Buddha is compared to his own true-born child, excluding the ordinary being and the two lesser vehicles alike from full sonship — a commentarial tradition names three kinds of Buddha-children: the ordinary being an outside child, the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha adopted children, and only the bodhisattva a true-born child, since a bodhisattva alone realizes the noble wisdom born directly, so to speak, from the Buddha's own womb, worthy of carrying the lineage forward.

56. Section: Born of the Mouth — Born From the Very Sound of the Teaching

Born of the mouth means being born from the very sound of the teaching spoken.

Chinese Source

口所生者，從說法音而誕生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of the mouth means being born from the very sound of the teaching spoken.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

A disciple who hears the Tathāgata's own teaching and, following it, cultivates discipline, concentration, and wisdom all the way to the realization of ultimate truth is figuratively born from the Buddha's own mouth — the wisdom born of hearing itself, arising in direct dependence on the teaching heard.

57. Section: Born of the Dharma — the Dharma Itself as the Buddha's Own Mother

Born of the dharma means being born of proper attention and practice in accordance with the dharma.

Chinese Source

法所生者，如理作意、法隨法行之所生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of the dharma means being born of proper attention and practice in accordance with the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary and the Nirvāṇa Sūtra

A monastic disciple who hears the teaching, reflects on it properly, and then cultivates practice in accordance with the dharma can realize the noble path and its fruit — this is being born of the dharma itself, the wisdom of reflection and cultivation together, arising in dependence on both principle and teaching. Turned around, even the Buddha's own boundless virtues arise from no other source than the true dharma itself — hence the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's own saying, "the dharma is the Buddha's mother; the Buddha is born from the dharma."

58. Section: Transformed by the Dharma — Established Along the Path of the Dharma-Body

Transformed by the dharma means being established along the path of the dharma-body, a dharma resembling it.

Chinese Source

法所化者，從法身路而得成立，相似法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Transformed by the dharma means being established along the path of the dharma-body, a dharma resembling it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

Once forbearance regarding the non-arising of dharmas is realized, subsequently-attained wisdom takes suchness itself, in its purity, as its object — a path by which the true character of all dharmas is progressively realized and the afflictions cut off through cultivation are gradually removed; a practitioner walking this path is likewise transformed by the dharma. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain that a bodhisattva realizes the Buddhas' own dharma-body by following the ancient path of undefiled discipline, concentration, and wisdom — a path that resembles suchness and nirvāṇa's own cessation itself, hence "a dharma resembling it."

59. Section: An Equal Portion of the Dharma — Closing All Five Terms for a True Child of the Buddha

An equal portion of the dharma means enjoying the wealth of the undefiled dharma, a dharma resembling it.

Chinese Source

法等分者，受用無漏法之財寶，相似法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

An equal portion of the dharma means enjoying the wealth of the undefiled dharma, a dharma resembling it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra sub-commentary

A monastic disciple enjoys the same undefiled noble wealth the Buddha himself enjoys — faith, discipline, moral shame, moral dread, learning, generosity, and wisdom, the seven noble treasures — differing only in fullness or portion, yet genuinely resembling the Buddha's own undefiled enjoyment, hence an equal portion of the dharma. With this, all five names for a true child of the Buddha — born of the womb, born of the mouth, born of the dharma, transformed by the dharma, and an equal portion of the dharma — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to their twofold perfection.

60. Section: A Twofold Perfection Named — Perfected Higher Rebirth, and Perfected Rebirth Resembling the Father's Own Dharma

These several phrases reveal perfected higher rebirth, and perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Chinese Source

如是諸句，顯示增上生圓滿，及父相似法生圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

These several phrases reveal perfected higher rebirth, and perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The five names just closed reveal two kinds of perfection together: a bodhisattva who practices as the Buddha taught gains a perfected higher rebirth — a life fit to carry the lineage forward, a genuine vessel for the great vehicle, distinct from the ordinary being's outside sonship or the two lesser vehicles' own lowlier sonship — and, in time, a perfected rebirth resembling the father's own undefiled treasures of faith, discipline, moral shame, moral dread, learning, generosity, and wisdom. Higher-rebirth perfection belongs to the basic condition of one's own being; the father-resembling perfection belongs to what is yet to be gained, the condition for future accomplishment — since a bodhisattva, too, can one day reach the very fullness the Buddha himself has already reached, this dharma is called "resembling."

61. Section: The First Three Terms Matched to Higher-Rebirth Perfection

The first phrase, within perfected higher rebirth, excludes the fault of an unfit vessel; the second phrase excludes birth from impure seed and blood; the third phrase excludes birth from desire and craving rather than the true dharma. These three phrases together reveal perfected higher rebirth.

Chinese Source

謂初句，於其增上生圓滿中，遮器過失；第二句，遮其精血不淨所生；第三句，遮其欲貪非正法生。如是三句，顯示增上生圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first phrase, within perfected higher rebirth, excludes the fault of an unfit vessel; the second phrase excludes birth from impure seed and blood; the third phrase excludes birth from desire and craving rather than the true dharma. These three phrases together reveal perfected higher rebirth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of Cultivated Wisdom

"Born of the womb" shows a true child of the Buddha free of the vessel's three faults — leaking, like a cracked cup that cannot hold the water of the dharma; covered, like a sealed cup the water cannot enter, a heart unwilling to receive the teaching; and stained, like a dirty cup that spoils even pure water poured into it, a mind too clouded by affliction to hear the dharma correctly. "Born of the mouth" excludes birth from the impure union of a father's seed and mother's blood, since the noble path a practitioner seeks is instead born from hearing the teaching's own sound. And "born of the dharma" excludes birth from mere desire and craving apart from the true dharma, since the noble path a practitioner realizes always arises through proper attention and practice in accordance with the dharma, unlike an ordinary being's desire born of improper attention. These three phrases together — free of a vessel's faults, free of impure origin, free of improper birth — are exactly what makes a life fit to be an instrument of higher rebirth, capable of practicing and realizing the fruit.

62. Section: The Remaining Two Terms Matched to Perfection Resembling the Father — Closing All the Sons of the Great Teacher Entirely

The fourth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own inherent nature; the fifth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own enjoyment. These two phrases together reveal perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Chinese Source

第四句，顯示自體相似之法；第五句，顯示受用相似之法。如是二句，顯示父相似法生圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own inherent nature; the fifth phrase reveals a dharma resembling his own enjoyment. These two phrases together reveal perfected rebirth in a dharma resembling the father's own.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Transformed by the dharma" shows that a practitioner, however shallow their present attainment, can, by following the same path step by step, eventually realize a dharma-body resembling the Buddha's own — the concealed meaning of that fourth term. "An equal portion of the dharma" shows that a practitioner who follows the teaching faithfully can likewise come to enjoy an undefiled dharma-wealth resembling the Buddha's own reward-body enjoyment — the fifth term's meaning. Together, these two reveal perfection in a dharma resembling the father's own: since the Buddha, the father, has already realized the dharma-body, a practitioner who follows his teaching can likewise realize a resembling dharma-body — no longer a life composed of a father's and mother's impure seed and blood, but a life whose very body is now the dharma itself. With this, sons of the great teacher — their five names and their twofold perfection alike — are closed in their entirety, completing the saṅgha topic's second of five sections. The treatise turns next to preface and gathering, the third section.
