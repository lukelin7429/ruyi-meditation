Fascicle 93 · 瑜伽師地論卷第九十三 · Complete

Dependent Origination and the Rest, Five Topics Complete

Fascicle 93 opens with the treatise's own confirmation that the sense-bases section (處擇攝, fascicles 89–92) has been fully explained, and turns to the third of sūtra matters' four major divisions: dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements (緣起食諦界擇攝), spanning fascicles 93–96 and decisively explaining four topics from the Saṃyukta Āgama through seven gates. This fascicle works through establishing and the rest (立等), the first of these gates' own ten-topic summary verse, in all ten of its topics, among them establishing (立), the full classical twelve-links exposition across the three time periods; suffering (苦), the nine-mark tree of future suffering; accumulation (聚), the bodhisattva's own reverse-and-forward contemplation leading to awakening; contemplating the truths (諦觀), the Buddha's rediscovery of the ancient eightfold path; and embracing the noble teaching (攝聖教), the doctrine that dependently-arisen dharmas have no true agency of their own. It then takes up subtle wisdom and the remaining topics, followed by the entire second particular verse, the two truths and the rest (二諦等). Fascicle 94 continues with the third particular verse, contact as condition and the rest (以觸為緣等), which the treatise's own framework places there.

1. Section: The Sense-Bases Section Closes; Dependent Origination and the Rest Begins

Thus the sense-bases section has already been explained. Dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements — I shall now speak of these.

Chinese Source

如是已說處擇攝。緣起、食、諦、界擇攝，我今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the sense-bases section has already been explained. Dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements — I shall now speak of these.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Sūtra matters' decisive analysis (擇攝) of the Saṃyukta Āgama has four divisions: the aggregates section (行擇攝, fascicles 85–88), the sense-bases section (處擇攝, fascicles 89–92) — both now complete — the section on dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements (緣起食諦界擇攝, fascicles 93–96), and the factors-of-awakening section (菩提分法擇攝, fascicles 97–98). This third section works through seven gates: establishing and the rest, the two truths and the rest, and contact as condition and the rest, and cessation and the rest (these four decisively explain dependent origination; the first two are in this fascicle, the latter two in fascicle 94); the embrace of food (fascicle 94, the four foods); the embrace of the truths (fascicle 95, the four noble truths); and the embrace of the elements (fascicle 96, the eighteen elements).

4. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — Establishing's Three Marks, Briefly Marked and Listed

Briefly, by three marks, one should know the distinctions in establishing dependent origination: first, arising from the prior period into the middle period; second, arising from the middle period into the latter period; third, within the middle period, once arisen, either continuing to flow onward, or turning toward purity.

Chinese Source

略由三相，應知建立緣起差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly, by three marks, one should know the distinctions in establishing dependent origination.

Chinese Source

一、從前際中際得生；二、從中際後際得生；三、於中際生已隨轉及趣清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, arising from the prior period into the middle period; second, arising from the middle period into the latter period; third, within the middle period, once arisen, either continuing to flow onward, or turning toward purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens the long-form explanation (長行釋), whose ten sections match the summary verse's ten topics, beginning with the first, establishing (立), itself opening with briefly marking and listing (略標列) three marks. The 披尋記 notes this three-mark analysis closely follows the Basic Section's own treatment in fascicle 9. This treatise's own causal scheme (unlike the śrāvaka three-lives/two-round-of-cause-and-effect model) uses a two-lives/one-round scheme: ignorance and formations as the drawing cause; the seeds of consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, and feeling as what is drawn; craving, grasping, and existence as the generating cause; and birth, aging, and death as what is generated.

8. Section: The Middle Period's Embrace Begins — Its Stage of Arising

Thus, turning onward, there comes to be craving both inward and outward... When consciousness is about to generate its fruit, this craving arises before it as an accompanying condition. Once life has ended, by the prior period's cause, one's own embodiment arises in the present world.

Chinese Source

如是展轉有內外愛，

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, turning onward, there comes to be craving both inward and outward —

Chinese Source

識生果時，能為助伴現前而起。既命終已，由前際因，於現在世自體得生。

Ru-Yi Translation

When consciousness is about to generate its fruit, this craving arises before it as an accompanying condition. Once life has ended, by the prior period's cause, one's own embodiment arises in the present world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens the middle period's embrace (中際攝), beginning with its stage of arising (生起位). Inward craving is craving for one's own embodiment, born of the delusion that fails to see non-self; outward craving is craving for the objective world, born of the delusion that fails to see conditions as illusion-like. Both, present at the moment of death, moisten the seeds of consciousness, name-and-form, the six bases, contact, and feeling laid down in the prior period, turning them into existence and giving rise to the present embodiment.

9. Section: Its Stage of Growth, Closing "The Middle Period's Embrace" and "Arising from the Prior Period" Entirely

Once born, gradually within the mother's womb, by consciousness as condition, the continuing resultant consciousness turns on without interruption, sustaining the kalala and the rest. Name-and-form's stages grow ever more distinguished, from the womb onward, right up to old age.

Chinese Source

生已漸次於母腹中，因識為緣，續生果識隨轉不絕，任持所有羯羅藍等。名色分位後後殊勝，始從胎藏，乃至衰老。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once born, gradually within the mother's womb, by consciousness as condition, the continuing resultant consciousness turns on without interruption, sustaining the kalala and the rest. Name-and-form's stages grow ever more distinguished, from the womb onward, right up to old age.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The embryo passes through the eight classical stages — kalala, arbuda, peśī, ghana, praśākhā, then hair-and-nails, the sense-faculties, and their full form — each seven days marking a further transformation, through birth, growth, and on to old age. With this, its stage of growth (增長位) closes entirely, closing the middle period's embrace (中際攝2: 生起位/增長位) entirely, which closes arising from the prior period (從前際生2: 前際攝/中際攝) entirely.

10. Section: Continuing to Flow Onward Once Arisen Begins — Bringing Forth the Two Mutual Supports, Name-and-Form Conditions Consciousness

Further, this very consciousness, when about to continue arising, can bring about the karma that generates and its resultant fruit. The resultant, arisen consciousness in turn relies on name-and-form to continue turning — namely, turning by relying on the eye and the rest, its six supports... By this it is said: name-and-form conditions consciousness.

Chinese Source

又即此識當續生時，能感生業與異熟果。異熟生識復依名色相續而轉，謂依眼等六依轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, this very consciousness, when about to continue arising, can bring about the karma that generates and its resultant fruit. The resultant, arisen consciousness in turn relies on name-and-form to continue turning — namely, turning by relying on the eye and the rest, its six supports.

Chinese Source

由是說言：名色緣識。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this it is said: name-and-form conditions consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens continuing to flow onward once arisen (生已隨轉), beginning with bringing forth the two mutual supports (出二依止) — consciousness relying on name-and-form, name-and-form relying on consciousness. The consciousness that enters the womb in its first moment is called true resultant, the ālaya-consciousness; the six operating consciousnesses it subsequently gives rise to, drawn out by that same karmic power, are called resultant-born consciousness, and these rely on name-and-form (the six bases) to keep turning.

11. Section: Explaining the Mark of Name-and-Form Conditioning Consciousness

The co-arisen five faculties are called form; the uninterrupted-cessation and the rest are called name — each, as fitting, able to serve as consciousness's support. Consciousness relies on these, and thus, right up to the end of life, turns again and again.

Chinese Source

俱生五根說名為色，無間滅等說名為名，隨其所應，能與六識作所依止。識依彼故，乃至命終數數隨轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

The co-arisen five faculties are called form; the uninterrupted-cessation and the rest are called name — each, as fitting, able to serve as consciousness's support. Consciousness relies on these, and thus, right up to the end of life, turns again and again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

"Uninterrupted-cessation and the rest" names two aspects of the mental faculty: the uninterrupted-cessation mental faculty (each prior moment of consciousness ceasing as the condition for the next), common to all three vehicles, and the afflicted mental faculty, the seventh consciousness, found only in the Mahāyāna account — hence the "and the rest."

12. Section: Consciousness Conditions Name-and-Form, Closing "Bringing Forth the Two Mutual Supports" — Distinguishing Each Individually — Entirely

Further, the five form faculties — the forms produced by the great elements the faculties rely on, and the forms produced by the great elements the faculties occupy, together with every other name — are all held and sustained by that consciousness, so that the faculties fall within a continuum, turning on without interruption... By this it is said: consciousness conditions name-and-form, name-and-form conditions consciousness.

Chinese Source

又五色根，根依大種、根處大種所生諸色及諸餘名，由彼執持，所有根等墮在相續，流轉不絕。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the five form faculties — the forms produced by the great elements the faculties rely on, and the forms produced by the great elements the faculties occupy, together with every other name — are all held and sustained by that consciousness, so that the faculties fall within a continuum, turning on without interruption.

Chinese Source

由是故言：識緣名色，名色緣識。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this it is said: consciousness conditions name-and-form, name-and-form conditions consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Mahāvibhāṣā

With this, consciousness conditions name-and-form (識緣名色) closes entirely, closing distinguishing each individually (別辨2: 名色緣識/識緣名色) entirely. The Mahāvibhāṣā distinguishes the two formulas six ways — displaying consciousness's function versus name-and-form's function; each as the other's mutual support, like two bundled reeds leaning on each other, or an elephant, horse, or boat together with its driver; the moment of rebirth versus the sustained life that follows; consciousness generating name-and-form versus consciousness abiding by relying on it; what is generated versus what generates; and prior-to-posterior sequence versus simultaneous co-arising.

16. Section: Distinguishing Egg-Birth and Moisture-Birth

Egg-birth and moisture-birth have no further distinction apart from what occurs within the mother's womb.

Ru-Yi Translation

Egg-birth and moisture-birth have no further distinction apart from what occurs within the mother's womb.

17. Section: Distinguishing Transformation-Birth Individually Begins — the Desire and Form Realms

Beings possessing form who undergo transformation-birth in the desire realm or form realm, at the very moment of birth, have their faculties fully complete — this differs from the rest.

Chinese Source

有色有情，在欲、色界受化生者，於初生時諸根圓滿，與餘差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beings possessing form who undergo transformation-birth in the desire realm or form realm, at the very moment of birth, have their faculties fully complete — this differs from the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens distinguishing transformation-birth individually (別辨化生), beginning with the desire and form realms (欲色界) — unlike womb-, egg-, or moisture-birth, where faculties may be incomplete, transformation-birth (found among the gods, some humans at the aeon's start, and certain hell-, hungry-ghost-, and animal-realm beings) always brings the six faculties into being all at once, fully formed.

18. Section: The Formless Realm, Closing "Distinguishing Transformation-Birth Individually" and "By the Four Kinds of Birth" Entirely

Among the class of beings in the formless realm, consciousness relies on name and the seed of form, and name and the seed of form turn by relying on consciousness. Because that consciousness contains the seed of form, form — though interrupted — will arise again later. Such is the distinction found here.

Chinese Source

在無色界諸有情類，識依於名及色種子，名及色種依識而轉。由彼識中有色種故，色雖間斷，後當更生。如是名為此中差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among the class of beings in the formless realm, consciousness relies on name and the seed of form, and name and the seed of form turn by relying on consciousness. Because that consciousness contains the seed of form, form — though interrupted — will arise again later. Such is the distinction found here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

In the formless realm, form does not manifest, but its seed remains held within the ālaya-consciousness; once the formless realm's lifespan ends, that being can still take rebirth in a realm possessing form. With this, the formless realm (無色界) closes entirely, closing distinguishing transformation-birth individually (別辨化生2: 欲色界/無色界) entirely, closing by the four kinds of birth (約四生辨3: 指依胎生/簡卵濕生/別辨化生) entirely.

Namely, such a person, having arisen within the middle period, experiences two fruits obtained from prior karma. First, experiencing the internal resultant fruit; second, experiencing the dominant fruit born of feeling toward objective conditions.

Chinese Source

謂彼如是於中際生補特伽羅，領受先業所得二果。一者、領受內異熟果，二者、領受境界所生受增上果。

22. Section: Explaining Its Cause Individually Begins — the Drawing Cause: What Draws — Ignorance Conditions Formations

Because of hearing improper teachings, or because of the power of habituation from former lives, one gives rise to delusion regarding these two fruits. Because of delusion regarding the internal resultant fruit, one fails to truly know the suffering of future rebirth to come. By this power of ignorance regarding both the prior and latter periods, one creates and augments formations just as before.

Chinese Source

彼由聽聞不正法故，或由先世串習力故，於二種果發起愚癡。彼由於內異熟果中有愚癡故，不能如實了知當來後有生苦。由此前際、後際無明增上力故，如前造作增長諸行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of hearing improper teachings, or because of the power of habituation from former lives, one gives rise to delusion regarding these two fruits. Because of delusion regarding the internal resultant fruit, one fails to truly know the suffering of future rebirth to come. By this power of ignorance regarding both the prior and latter periods, one creates and augments formations just as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining its cause individually (別釋其因), beginning with the drawing cause (引因), distinguishing its marks (辨相), and, within it, what draws (能引) — ignorance conditions formations (無明緣行). Not understanding that the resultant embodiment is not self, nor its surrounding conditions self's-possession, one continues creating wholesome, unwholesome, and unmoving karma exactly as before, laying down further seeds for a future fruit.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

By this new karma perfuming and transforming consciousness, one acts in accordance with karma within the present dharma. Thus, ignorance serving as condition, formations arise; formations serving as condition, consciousness is caused to transform.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens formations condition consciousness (行緣識), beginning with clarifying its arising (明緣起) — new karma perfumes the ālaya-consciousness, planting seeds for the destinies of the three realms to come.

Together these nine marks establish the tree of suffering.

With this, benefiting others (利他行) closes entirely, closing clarifying the twofold beneficial practice (明二利行2: 自利行/利他行) entirely, closing contemplating the truths (諦觀2: 出觀四諦/明二利行), the fourth of establishing and the rest's ten topics, entirely. The Abhidharmakośa's opening verse of homage praises the Buddha's three virtues found here: the virtue of wisdom (breaking through all ignorance to unsurpassed awakening) and the virtue of severance (cutting off all affliction to unsurpassed nirvāṇa) — both self-benefiting — and the virtue of compassion (saving all beings) — benefiting others.

A long companion sūtra has the Buddha recount his own pre-awakening contemplation: reasoning backward from aging-and-death through birth, existence, grasping, craving, feeling, contact, the six bases, and name-and-form to consciousness — unable to go further back than consciousness, since consciousness and name-and-form mutually condition each other — then reasoning that ignorance's absence removes formations, removing consciousness, name-and-form, and so on down to the cessation of the whole mass of suffering. Reflecting that he had thereby rediscovered "the ancient path of the ancient sages," he compares himself to a traveler in the wilderness who stumbles upon a long-overgrown road leading to a forgotten, thriving city, and reports the discovery to a king who then resettles and revives it — just so, the Buddha rediscovered the noble eightfold path leading to the cessation of suffering, taught by no one before him yet walked by every awakened one, and, having realized it himself, taught it in turn to his fourfold assembly and to renunciants and householders of other paths alike, that the Dharma might spread for the benefit of all. As Venerable Yin Shun observes on this passage: dependent origination's rising and ceasing is not something the Buddha invented, but a truth as it naturally is, discovered by his equanimous great wisdom peering into life's deepest mystery.

A companion sūtra teaches the same: foolish, unlearned beings may weary of the body of the four great elements, yet cannot weary of mind, thought, and consciousness, which they have long cherished as self. It would be better to take the body as self than the mind, for the body may last ten, twenty, or a hundred years, while mind changes day and night, like a monkey that lets go of one branch and seizes another. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Per the 披尋記: seeing outer forms by the eye and on that account speaking to others is speech of seeing; speaking on account of what one has inwardly felt, realized, touched, and obtained is speech of knowing — these two rest on direct perception. Neither seeing nor hearing, but thinking, weighing, and examining on one's own and on that account speaking is speech of cognizing, resting on inference. Hearing a sound from another and on that account speaking is speech of hearing, resting on scriptural authority. All this should be understood according to the Stage of Mind. Knowing here covers what nose, tongue, and body consciousness directly know of scent, taste, and touch. Though the hearing of sound is itself also direct perception, it is assigned to scriptural authority because the holy teaching is received through the ear. The Yogācārabhūmi Commentary counts direct perception more widely: eye consciousness seeing, the mind consciousness simultaneous with the five consciousnesses, mind consciousness in concentration, and the taking of objects by nose, tongue, and body consciousness.

A companion sūtra has the Buddha teach the inner touching of dharma (內觸法). A monk claims to have grasped it, but the Buddha is displeased, and at Ānanda's request he teaches that one should reflect: suffering has upadhi (億波提) as its cause, origin, arising, and turning; upadhi has craving as its cause; and craving arises, is bound, and abides in the world's lovely, comely forms. Upadhi is read in two ways: as basis, matching the treatise's two bases of suffering, or, per Venerable Yin Shun, as grasping. Those who regard such forms as permanent, lasting, safe, free of sickness, self, and self's-possession see craving grow, and with it upadhi and suffering. They are like travelers in the spring heat who drink from a cool, fragrant roadside pool that has been poisoned, despite a warning. Those who see such forms as a sickness, a boil, a thorn, a killer, impermanent, suffering, empty, and not-self are like travelers who heed the warning, bear their thirst, and eat dry barley meal instead. The Buddha closes by telling Ānanda to see, hear, cognize, and know this dharma in just this way, and likewise for past and future.

A companion sūtra has the Buddha declare that he has crossed all doubt, drawn out the thorn of wrong view, and has no clinging, and teach the monks the noble, transcendent, emptiness-joined dependent origination: because this exists, that exists. Some monks grow doubtful and distressed, because they had taken what they had not attained to be attained. Why? Because dependent origination is profound and hard to see, and doubly profound is the cessation of all grasping, the exhaustion of craving, freedom from desire, nirvāṇa. These two are the conditioned, which has arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing, and the unconditioned, which has none of them. Because the cause gathers, suffering gathers; because the cause ceases, suffering ceases; severing every pathway and ending continuity is called the limit of suffering. What remains is the remaining suffering of the residual body; when that too ceases at the end of life, there is stillness, coolness, and nirvāṇa. Master Cháng Bǎi gives two corresponding sūtras, the Sūtra on the Great Conditions (大緣方便經) of the Dīrgha Āgama, which also corresponds to the Great Discourse on Causes (大緣經) of the Dīgha Nikāya, and the Great Cause Sūtra (大因經), no. 97 of the Madhyama Āgama; since the two are very alike, he presents only the first. Ānanda, reflecting in seclusion, finds the twelve links as clear as if before his eyes and asks why the World-Honored One calls them profound. The Buddha replies: do not say so. Dependent origination is utterly profound, hard to see and hard to know; gods, Māra, Brahmā, renunciant wanderers, and brahmins who have not seen it become lost when they try to reason it out. The Buddha then traces the links backward and forward, asking at each step whether, if the condition were absent, the result could exist, with Ānanda answering it could not. He expands craving into a further chain — seeking, gain, use, desire, attachment, envy, guarding, protecting — ending in weapons, disputes, and countless evil deeds. Consciousness and name-and-form support each other like reeds leaning together. A monastic who truly contemplates this is liberated by wisdom, knowing and seeing with the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising.

This first part corresponds to the subtle causes and effects hard to understand. The next corresponds to no-self hard to understand and to beings free of bondage yet bound. Some hold feeling to be the self; some hold that the self is what experiences feeling; some hold that feeling as a dharma is the self; some hold that craving is the self. The Buddha shows each view at fault: the three feelings do not occur together; each arises from its own contact and ceases when that contact ceases, as fire arises from two rubbed sticks and is gone when they are separated; they are conditioned, impermanent, and perishing. Some take form or the formless, limited or extensive, as seen in concentration, to be the self, each holding only their own view to be true. Outsiders take the seven abodes of consciousness and the two bases (non-perception and neither-perception-nor-non-perception) to be safety and refuge; a monastic who truly knows their arising, ceasing, relish, danger, and escape says, "These are not me, I am not these." Whoever moves forward and backward at will through the eight liberations is a both-ways-liberated arhat. The master adds that the sūtra's last two passages do not match the treatise directly on beings bound yet free of bondage, the treatise being an insight developed in reliance on them.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the second topic, conventional and ultimate (世俗勝義), beginning with marking skill (標善巧). The conventional truth is the dependently-arisen realm established by verbal expression; the ultimate truth is the realm of non-discriminating wisdom gained through realization. Master Cháng Bǎi gives the two truths' common meaning: truth (諦) is the principle that is real and not false. Per the Mahāvibhāṣā (fascicle 77), what worldly understanding grasps and what convention accepts is conventional truth, and the reality thoroughly seen by the noble wisdom free of contaminants is ultimate truth.

He also cites the Sūtra on the Initial Excellent Dharma-Gate of Distinguishing Dependent Origination (分別緣起初勝法門經): within the four noble truths, what is the domain of the wisdom that abides in the Dharma is conventional truth; what is the domain of the supreme wisdom realized inwardly, and not of established wisdom, is called ultimate truth. In brief, the conventional affirms the principle by which things exist, the ultimate denies that they have substance, and the Mahāyāna holds that one must recognize both sides to see the true mark of dharmas.

275. Section: The General Conclusion Gaining the Name, Closing "Differentiation" — the Sixth of Seven Topics — Entirely

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, the general conclusion gaining the name (總結得名) closes entirely, closing differentiation (分別3: 標列因緣/隨釋差別/總結得名), the sixth of the seven topics, entirely.

The companion sūtra, set at the Kuru town of Kalmāṣadamya, has the Buddha teach dependent origination as dharma-teaching and meaning-teaching (法說、義說). The dharma-teaching is the law that because this exists, that exists, the treatise's nature of things as they are. The meaning-teaching goes through each link, the treatise's nature of the full extent of what exists. Ignorance is explained in twenty-nine items: not knowing the prior period, the latter period, and both; the inner, the outer, and both; karma, ripening, and both; the Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha; suffering, origin, cessation, and path; cause and what arises from cause; the wholesome and unwholesome; the blameworthy and blameless; what is to be practiced and not; the inferior and superior; the defiled and pure; and not truly knowing the six bases of contact. Master Cháng Bǎi matches these to the groupings of fascicle 9.

Formations are of body, speech, and mind; consciousness is the six consciousness-bodies, body meaning accumulation; name is the four formless aggregates and form is the four great elements and what they make; next come the six inner sense-bases, the six bodies of contact, and the three feelings; craving is for desire, form, and the formless; grasping is of four kinds; existence is karmic existence of the three realms. Birth is the attaining of aggregates, elements, sense-bases, and life faculty; aging is white hair, bent back, leaning on a staff, and failing faculties; death is passing away, the breakup of the body, the end of life, warmth leaving, and the life faculty ceasing.

279. Section: Listing Begins — the First Cause

First, because formations, as said before, have no agency.

Chinese Source

一者、諸行，如前所說無作用故。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, because formations, as said before, have no agency.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens listing (列). Per the 披尋記, what was said before is the passage on dependently-arisen dharmas being well established as lacking agency: from future suffering one contemplates in reverse the present and prior suffering and origin; name-and-form conditions consciousness and consciousness conditions name-and-form, like bundled reeds leaning on each other; and within this no dependently-arisen dharma is self-made, other-made, both-made, or causeless.

Master Cháng Bǎi recalls why: dharmas arise from their own seeds but need other dominant conditions, so they are not self-made; they require their own seeds, so they are not other-made; seed and condition have no real making, so they are not both-made; yet cause and condition have causal function, and dharmas do arise when these come together, so they are not causeless.

280. Section: The Second Cause, Closing "Listing" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, because there is no further maker, no further being, to be found.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, listing (列) closes entirely. Dharmas arise only through conditions; the claim that formations' causes and effects depend on some further being as maker, such as Īśvara, finds nothing to support it.

281. Section: Explaining Begins — Not Self-Made

Here, because formations have no agency, that this one experiences and this one undergoes self-made suffering and pleasure is not reasonable.

Chinese Source

此中諸行無作用故，此受此領自作苦樂，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, because formations have no agency, that this one experiences and this one undergoes self-made suffering and pleasure is not reasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining (釋), beginning with not self-made (非自作). Feeling has as its cause contact, the union of faculty, object, and consciousness. Master Cháng Bǎi cites fascicle 91: outsiders, overstepping direct perception, whether by the prior period or the present, hold firmly to four wrong theories of suffering and pleasure. Falsely conjecturing past deeds as cause, they establish suffering and pleasure as wholly self-made; or, conjecturing about the present, they take them to follow one's own wish and effort. Neither is reasonable.

282. Section: Not Other-Made

Further, because that further maker, that further being, cannot be found, that another experiences and another undergoes is not reasonable.

Chinese Source

又彼有餘作者有情不可得故，餘受餘領，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, because that further maker, that further being, cannot be found, that another experiences and another undergoes is not reasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This covers not other-made (非他作). Outsiders conjecture, by the prior period, that suffering and pleasure have Īśvara as their cause, holding them to be wholly other-made; or, by the present, that what does not follow one's own wish or effort is brought on by others. Neither is reasonable.

283. Section: Not Both-Made

Feeling being thirsted for by oneself, to take up another's making and experience it is likewise not reasonable.

Chinese Source

受所渴愛，攝受他受亦不應理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Feeling being thirsted for by oneself, to take up another's making and experience it is likewise not reasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This covers not both-made (非俱作). Per the 披尋記, the sense here shows that feeling is not made by both. Feeling of one's own being or of objects is thirsted for by oneself alone; one should not take another's making as a cause in order to experience it, and therefore both-made is likewise not reasonable.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that outsiders conjecture, by the prior period, that suffering and pleasure come from Īśvara and then from past deeds, or, by the present, from one's own wish together with what others bring on. Yet feelings are only one's own mind's discrimination; conditioned by them arises the affliction of thirst, and apart from the mental factors of feeling and craving no self exists.

Ru-Yi Translation

Because there are conditions, feelings arise; therefore arising without cause is likewise not reasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, not arising without cause (非無因生) closes entirely, closing explaining (釋4: 非自作/非他作/非俱作/非無因生) entirely, closing bringing forth what cannot be declared (出不可記4: 標/徵/列/釋) entirely. Outsiders conjecture that suffering and pleasure are causeless; or, when such feelings arise without prior effort of self or other, simply through an object appearing, and they fail to discern the subtle cause, contact, they wrongly conclude there is no cause.

285. Section: Concluding by Relying on the Middle Way, Closing "Self-Made" — the Seventh of Seven Topics — "The Long-Form Explanation," and "The Two Truths and the Rest" — the Second Particular Verse — Entirely

Therefore, staying far from the former three extremes of the theory of wrong cause and the latter single extreme of the theory of no cause, awakening to the teaching of the middle-way practice as before, and diligently cultivating right practice, one is able to exhaust the many sufferings.

Chinese Source

是故遠離前之三種惡因論邊、後之一種無因論邊，覺了如前中道行教，勤修正行，能盡眾苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore, staying far from the former three extremes of the theory of wrong cause and the latter single extreme of the theory of no cause, awakening to the teaching of the middle-way practice as before, and diligently cultivating right practice, one is able to exhaust the many sufferings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, concluding by relying on the middle way (結依中道) closes entirely, closing self-made (自作2: 出不可記/結依中道), the seventh of the seven topics, entirely, closing the long-form explanation (長行釋7) entirely, closing the two truths and the rest (二諦等2: 頌標列/長行釋), dependent origination's second particular verse, entirely. Per the 披尋記, what was said before is: staying far from these two extremes of wrong view and seeing only cause and effect is called the practice of the middle way; and all that teaching of no-self should be known as the teaching of the middle-way practice. The three extremes of wrong cause are self-made, other-made, and both-made; the single extreme is arising without cause.

Five companion sūtras follow. A monastic asks whether dependent origination was made by the Buddha or by others; the Buddha answers that it was made by neither: whether or not tathāgatas appear, the dharma realm stands thus, and he awakened to it and taught it. A brahmin asks whether one makes and experiences for oneself, or another does; the Buddha leaves both undeclared, the first falling into eternalism and the second into annihilationism, and teaches from the middle. Kātyāyana asks what right view is: one who does not grasp, who knows the arising and ceasing of affliction and has no doubt about dependent origination, realizing it himself and not through another, has right view; seeing the world's origin removes the denial of cause and effect, seeing its cessation removes the superimposition of a real self, and hearing this he is freed. Acela Kāśyapa asks whether suffering is self-made, other-made, both, or causeless; each is set aside, yet suffering is not nonexistent, and the Buddha teaches the middle that because this exists, that exists. Soon after, a cow protecting its calf gores him; his faculties were clear at death, and the Buddha declares that he had seen the Dharma and entered nirvāṇa. The outsider Timbaruka asks the same about suffering and pleasure and receives the same reply. Master Cháng Bǎi concludes that one who penetrates no-self will not falsely hold results to be self-made or other-made, and that the Buddha teaches from the middle, free of annihilation and permanence.

With this, fascicle 93 comes to its close. The next fascicle continues with the third particular verse of dependent origination's embrace.
