Fascicle 83 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十三 · The Compendium of Synonyms Begins

A Buddhist Dictionary: Many Names, One Meaning

With the Compendium of Explanations now closed across fascicles 81–82, the treatise opens its fourth of five major divisions: the Compendium of Synonyms (攝異門分), spanning fascicles 83 and 84. Where the previous division taught how to explain a sūtra, this one gathers and sorts the many different names and shades of meaning scattered throughout the āgama sūtras for the same underlying realities — one term opening onto many, one meaning bearing many names. An overall summary verse divides the whole division into a wholesome portion (four further verses) and an unwholesome portion (one more), covering some fifty-one distinct topics between them. Fascicle 83 opens the wholesome portion with its first verse: the many kinds of teacher, the Buddha as foremost among all beings, the vast synonyms of wisdom, and the four kinds of skillful teaching.

1. Section: The Compendium of Synonyms Opens — Closing Explanations, and an Overall Verse on the Wholesome and Unwholesome Portions

The Compendium of Synonyms, part one. Thus has the Compendium of Explanations already been spoken. What, now, is the Compendium of Synonyms? The overall summary verse says: The wholesome portion and the unwholesome portion — their synonyms and the rest are proclaimed, in order to awaken understanding of their meaning; this brief overall verse should be known.

Chinese Source

攝異門分之上。如是已說攝釋。云何攝異門？總嗢柁南曰：白品與黑品，異門等宣說，為開悟義覺，略總頌應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Compendium of Synonyms, part one.
Thus has the Compendium of Explanations already been spoken. What, now, is the Compendium of Synonyms?
The overall summary verse says: The wholesome portion and the unwholesome portion — their synonyms and the rest are proclaimed, in order to awaken understanding of their meaning; this brief overall verse should be known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

The treatise now opens its fourth of five major divisions. Where the Determinations reworked what the Basic Section left incomplete, and the Compendium of Explanations taught how a sūtra's own text should be handled, this Compendium of Synonyms goes further still, sorting out both text and meaning together — one term opening onto many doors, one meaning bearing many names, hence "synonyms." Spanning fascicles 83 and 84, it divides into a wholesome portion (白品, four further verses, three of them in this fascicle) and an unwholesome portion (黑品, one further verse, in fascicle 84) — fifty-one topics in all, some terms (wisdom, for instance) unfolding into fifty or sixty distinct synonyms apiece. The whole division exists, the summary explains, because presenting the same truth through many different names lets many different minds each find their own way to awakening — what fails to land through one term may land at once through another.

2. Section: The First Wholesome Verse — Teacher, Foremost, the Two Wisdoms, and Four Kinds of Skillful Teaching

The particular summary verse says: Teacher, foremost, the two wisdoms; the four kinds of skillful teaching, and the rest; also having cause and occasion, and the rest; giving, discipline, and the path, explained at length.

Chinese Source

別嗢柁南曰：師第一二慧，四種善說等，亦有因緣等，施戒道廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The particular summary verse says: Teacher, foremost, the two wisdoms; the four kinds of skillful teaching, and the rest; also having cause and occasion, and the rest; giving, discipline, and the path, explained at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This first verse previews eight terms to be unpacked: teacher — every kind of person a disciple might rightly rely on and be relied upon by; foremost — the Buddha as first, most honored, highest, and unsurpassed among all beings; the two wisdoms — wisdom's own vast synonyms, gained wisdom, vast wisdom, and pure wisdom among some twenty-two further terms; the four kinds of skillful teaching — perfection in text, in meaning, in result, and in the teacher's own conduct; having cause and occasion — the five excellences of the true dharma already met in fascicle 82 (having cause, having leading-out, having what is relied upon, having boldness, having miraculous transformation); and giving, discipline, and the path, each explained at length — generosity's own many synonyms, discipline's many distinctions, and the many components of the noble path (the foundations of mindfulness, right efforts, bases of power, faculties, powers, factors of awakening, and path-factors). Two further middle-length verses on discipline and the path, embedded beneath this first verse, will unfold within this same fascicle.

3. Section: Teacher Begins — the Great Teacher, the Foremost Disciples, and Those Who Carry the Teaching Forward

Here, the great teacher is the Tathāgata himself. The foremost disciples are the first among his disciples, such as the venerable Śāriputra and the rest. As for those who carry on the teaching: this means preceptors, instructing teachers, fellow practitioners of the dharma, those who awaken understanding, and those who prompt recollection.

Chinese Source

此中大師，所謂如來。紹師即是第一弟子，如彼尊者舍利子等。

言襲師者，謂軌範師、若親教師、若同法者、能開悟者、令憶念者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the great teacher is the Tathāgata himself. The foremost disciples are the first among his disciples, such as the venerable Śāriputra and the rest.

As for those who carry on the teaching: this means preceptors, instructing teachers, fellow practitioners of the dharma, those who awaken understanding, and those who prompt recollection.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Three tiers of teacher, each named for its relation to the Buddha's own teaching. The great teacher is the Buddha himself. The foremost disciples — Śāriputra foremost in wisdom, Maudgalyāyana foremost in spiritual power, and, more broadly, any arhat who has accomplished their own goal, from Piṇḍola-bhāradvāja to Cūḍapanthaka — earn the name "foremost" precisely by having realized nirvāṇa, their own self-benefit complete. And those who carry the teaching forward into later generations take five forms: the preceptor who models and teaches proper conduct; the instructing teacher, particularly the precept-master at full ordination; a fellow practitioner accomplished enough in hearing, practicing, or realizing the dharma to serve as an example; one able to open a disciple's understanding through skillful, timely instruction; and one able to remind a disciple of a past transgression or a forgotten point of dharma. Together these five keep the teaching alive long after the Buddha's own direct instruction has passed.

4. Section: Establishing, Transmitting, and Following the Sacred Teaching — the Three Tiers of Teacher by Their Function

The great teacher is the one who establishes the sacred teaching; the foremost disciples are those who transmit the sacred teaching; those who carry it on are those who follow the sacred teaching. Permitting and prohibiting all that should and should not be done, and instructing again and again from time to time — one should know these are the ones who proclaim, transmit, and follow it, respectively.

Chinese Source

大師即是立聖教者，紹師即是傳聖教者，襲師即是隨聖教者。開許制止一切應作、不應作故，時時教授教誡轉故。當知即是能說、傳說及隨說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

The great teacher is the one who establishes the sacred teaching; the foremost disciples are those who transmit the sacred teaching; those who carry it on are those who follow the sacred teaching. Permitting and prohibiting all that should and should not be done, and instructing again and again from time to time — one should know these are the ones who proclaim, transmit, and follow it, respectively.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Only the Buddha himself permits what is blameless and prohibits what is not, establishing the sacred teaching from nothing — hence "one who proclaims it." His noble disciples transmit that same teaching through discipline, concentration, and wisdom taught again and again — hence "one who transmits it." And later teachers, though not necessarily themselves realized, faithfully instruct according to that same teaching as it has been passed down — hence "one who follows it." All three, together, instruct disciples through eight approaches of practice (contemplating impurity, loving-kindness, dependent origination, the elements, breath, and the antidotes to eternalism, annihilationism, and overweening conceit) and five kinds of correction (restraining, permitting, admonishing, expelling, and comforting). With this, the three tiers of teacher, examined by their function in establishing, transmitting, and following the sacred teaching, are closed. The treatise turns next to the same three tiers examined by how they correct a disciple's conduct.

5. Section: One Who Corrects, One Who Rewards, and One Who Does Both — Expelling Wrongdoing, Encouraging Right Conduct

Expelling one who does what should not be done — this is called one who corrects. Comforting and encouraging one who does what should be done — this is called one who rewards. Being able to reveal both of these two matters — this is called one who does both.

Chinese Source

驅擯造作不應作故，名能獎者。慶慰造作應作事故，名勝獎者。於前二事能開示故，名至獎者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Expelling one who does what should not be done — this is called one who corrects. Comforting and encouraging one who does what should be done — this is called one who rewards. Being able to reveal both of these two matters — this is called one who does both.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A teacher who rightly expels a disciple guilty of serious misconduct — whether temporarily, with compassion, for a lesser fault, or for life, in the gravest cases — acts out of the same underlying kindness as one who instead praises and encourages a disciple's genuine progress in discipline, concentration, and wisdom; both discipline and praise serve the same end of moving a disciple out of what is unwholesome and into what is wholesome. A teacher able to skillfully show a disciple which response fits which occasion embodies both correction and reward at once.

6. Section: One Who Guides, One Who Guides Well, and One Who Guides Completely — Dispelling Doubt, Regret, and Every Affliction

Being able to dispel whatever doubt arises — this is called one who guides. Being able to dispel remorse and regret — this is called one who guides well. Being able to dispel every root affliction and secondary affliction — this is called one who guides completely.

Chinese Source

隨所生起一切疑惑皆能遣故，名能導者。惡作憂悔皆能遣故，名勝導者。一切煩惱及隨煩惱皆能遣故，名至導者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being able to dispel whatever doubt arises — this is called one who guides. Being able to dispel remorse and regret — this is called one who guides well. Being able to dispel every root affliction and secondary affliction — this is called one who guides completely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Three depths of guidance, each addressing a deeper layer of disturbance. Dispelling doubt about a teacher, a teaching, a practice, or an instruction settles the mind's uncertainty. Dispelling remorse addresses something subtler still — the mental factor of regret itself, which broods over a past deed, good or bad, and which the treatise elsewhere defines by its very nature: a mind that keeps returning to what was done, obstructing the calm needed for meditation. And dispelling every root and secondary affliction reaches furthest of all, guiding a disciple out of greed, hatred, delusion, and their whole family of derivative afflictions entirely.

7. Section: What Guidance Actually Removes — Doubt Unresolved, Understanding Undeepened, Profundity Unpenetrated

Being able to sever all doubts means this: what has not yet been made evident, one is able to make evident; what has already been made evident, one causes to become clear and pure; and the profound meaning of a passage, through wisdom, one is able to fully understand and widely reveal.

Chinese Source

於諸疑惑能斷除者，謂未顯義能顯發故，已顯發義令明淨故，甚深義句以慧通達廣開示故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being able to sever all doubts means this: what has not yet been made evident, one is able to make evident; what has already been made evident, one causes to become clear and pure; and the profound meaning of a passage, through wisdom, one is able to fully understand and widely reveal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Severing doubt itself unfolds in three stages of increasing depth: drawing out a meaning still hidden in the text; refining a meaning already drawn out until it is fully clear and pure; and, for the most profound passages of all — teachings such as consciousness-only or dependent origination's own emptiness — penetrating them through the wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, and then unfolding them widely for the disciple in turn.

8. Section: A Second Companion, a Good Friend, and an Old Acquaintance

One vowed to serve as preceptor, model, and support to rely upon — this is called a second companion. One who accompanies a disciple along the path itself — this is called a good friend. One who once shared happy household days together in the past — this is called merely an acquaintance.

Chinese Source

誓許為作軌範尊重所依止故，名第二伴。隨轉伴故，名為善友。宿昔同處居家樂故，名為知識。

Ru-Yi Translation

One vowed to serve as preceptor, model, and support to rely upon — this is called a second companion. One who accompanies a disciple along the path itself — this is called a good friend. One who once shared happy household days together in the past — this is called merely an acquaintance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

A ship crossing open water needs a guiding light; a "second companion" is exactly that light — a teacher who has vowed to serve as preceptor and model, supporting a practitioner dwelling alone in solitude for the sake of calm-abiding and insight. A "good friend" walks the noble path alongside a disciple, companion in practice rather than merely in vow. And "acquaintance" names only a shared past — friends or family from lay life, with no claim on spiritual guidance implied by the word alone.

9. Section: One Who Pities, and One Who Has Bestowed Kindness — Closing the Sixfold Account of Persons

Parents and kin bound to one another by mutual ties — these are called those who pity. One who is not family yet bestows kindness — this is called one who has bestowed kindness.

Chinese Source

父母宗親互相繫屬，名憐愍者。若非眷屬而施恩惠，名有恩者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Parents and kin bound to one another by mutual ties — these are called those who pity. One who is not family yet bestows kindness — this is called one who has bestowed kindness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Direct family and wider kin, bound by natural mutual care, are those who pity; a stranger who nonetheless offers material or spiritual benefit, unasked, is one who has bestowed kindness. With this, all six categories of person a disciple might rely upon — great teacher and lineage, transmitters, correctors, guides, companions, and kin — are closed. The treatise turns next to the same territory examined by its own inherent virtues.

10. Section: Distinctions of Virtue Begin — "Benefit" Itself: What Is Sought Because It Draws Out Real Good

The word "benefit" names the sought-after matter that draws out real good. Delighting in this is called delighting in benefit.

Chinese Source

言義利者，名所求事能引義利。樂為此故，名樂義利。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "benefit" names the sought-after matter that draws out real good. Delighting in this is called delighting in benefit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Buddha-Ground Treatise

Nirvāṇa itself is the sought-after matter — delighting in the noble path and its fruit is delighting in benefit. The commentarial tradition distinguishes benefit four ways: present good versus future good, worldly good versus transcendent good, freedom from evil versus taking up what is wholesome, and merit versus wisdom — all converging on what the Bodhisattva Ground names simply as settling beings, once and for all, in ultimate nirvāṇa and the Tathāgata's own vast wisdom.

11. Section: "Benefit" as Wholesome Conduct — the Cause of Ease

The word "benefit" also names wholesome conduct. Delighting in this is called delighting in what is beneficial.

Chinese Source

言利益者，名為善行。樂為此故，名樂利益。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "benefit" also names wholesome conduct. Delighting in this is called delighting in what is beneficial.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground

Read this way, "benefit" names not the goal itself but the wholesome bodily and verbal conduct — giving, discipline, and cultivation — that a joyful mind naturally produces; equally, it names rescuing beings from what is unwholesome and settling them in what is wholesome. Benefit here is the cause, and ease, as the Bodhisattva Ground puts it, is its fruit: whoever is given real benefit is thereby given real ease as well.

12. Section: "Ease" Itself — Body and Mind at Rest, in This Life and Beyond — Closing Distinctions of Virtue

The word "ease" names abiding secure, benefiting body and mind. Delighting in this is called delighting in ease. Resting on the pleasure of this present life is called delighting in security; resting on the pleasure of future lives is called delighting in the security that accords with it.

Chinese Source

言安樂者，名安隱住益身心義。樂為此故，名樂安樂。

依現法樂，名樂安隱。依後法樂，說名為樂相應安隱。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "ease" names abiding secure, benefiting body and mind. Delighting in this is called delighting in ease.

Resting on the pleasure of this present life is called delighting in security; resting on the pleasure of future lives is called delighting in the security that accords with it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Nirvāṇa itself is called "the sought-after matter" precisely because it is lovable and blameless; seeking it first is what draws out every further benefit. Ease then divides simply by timeframe — security enjoyed in this very life, and the security that accords with it, carried forward into future lives as well. With this, distinctions of virtue — benefit as goal, benefit as wholesome cause, and ease as its fruit both now and hereafter — are closed, and with them, the account of those a disciple may rely upon, examined by person and by virtue alike, is complete. The treatise turns next to the disciple's own side: the gradual sequence of faith, effort, and right conduct that makes reliance on such a teacher actually work.

13. Section: Those Who Rely Begin — the Sequence of Faith: Trusting Compliance

Presently and correctly following in every matter — this is called trusting compliance.

Chinese Source

於一切事現正隨從，故名信順。

Ru-Yi Translation

Presently and correctly following in every matter — this is called trusting compliance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having examined the teacher's own side, the treatise now turns to the disciple who relies on such a teacher, beginning with faith's own gradual sequence. Trusting compliance is simply undistorted, correct conduct toward everything a good friend teaches — giving, discipline, cultivation, hearing, reflection, discernment, and benefiting beings alike — a mind genuinely willing to obey, visiting and honoring the teacher whenever occasion allows.

14. Section: Faith Itself — Arising with Respect to a Person

Arising specifically with respect to that person — this is called faith.

Chinese Source

若即於彼補特伽羅處所而起，故名為信。

Ru-Yi Translation

Arising specifically with respect to that person — this is called faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This names faith taken toward the Buddha, a good friend, or another person directly — faith grounded in a person, also called the faith of one who follows through faith.

15. Section: Pure Faith — Hearing of Virtue and Power, Trusting the Dharma Itself, Down to the Hair Standing on End

Having heard of that one's virtue, power, and surpassing wisdom, faith then arises with respect to the dharma itself, according with its own principle — this is called pure faith. And by the sheer force of this, the hair on the body may stand on end, and tears may fall in weeping — marks such as these are marks of pure faith.

Chinese Source

聞彼功德及與威力、殊勝慧已，即於彼法處所而起，隨順理門，故名淨信。即由如是增上力故，身毛為豎、悲泣墮淚，如是等事是淨信相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having heard of that one's virtue, power, and surpassing wisdom, faith then arises with respect to the dharma itself, according with its own principle — this is called pure faith. And by the sheer force of this, the hair on the body may stand on end, and tears may fall in weeping — marks such as these are marks of pure faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where ordinary faith takes a person as its object, pure faith takes the dharma itself, arising once the Three Jewels' own virtue, power, and wisdom are truly heard and understood, according with truth's own principle — the faith of one who follows through dharma. Its outward marks are unmistakable: hearing a teaching or a chant and finding the hair rise on one's own arms, or tears falling unbidden — genuine signs of faith reaching all the way through.

16. Section: Sustained Faith — Trusting the Three Jewels' Real Virtue Through Every Posture — Closing the Sequence of Faith

Having heard of that one's virtue and power, then, throughout walking, standing, and every other deportment, constantly trusting that these virtues are genuinely real — this is called sustained faith.

Chinese Source

聞彼功德、威力等已，於行住等諸威儀中，恆常信彼實有功德，故名信述。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having heard of that one's virtue and power, then, throughout walking, standing, and every other deportment, constantly trusting that these virtues are genuinely real — this is called sustained faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary and the Śrāvaka Ground

Sustained faith carries the same trust in the Three Jewels' genuine virtue through every posture of daily life, constantly recollected rather than a single momentary arising. The Śrāvaka Ground sorts faith overall into two aspects (trusting compliance and purity) and two grounds it rests on (observing the principle of dharmas, and trusting a person's own spiritual power). With this, the fourfold sequence of faith — trusting compliance, faith itself, pure faith, and sustained faith — is closed. The treatise turns next to the sequence of effort.

17. Section: The Sequence of Effort Begins — Aspiration: Delighting to Act, Delighting to Attain, in Four Kinds

The word "aspiration" means delighting to act and delighting to attain in a given matter.

Chinese Source

所言欲者，若於是處樂作、樂得。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "aspiration" means delighting to act and delighting to attain in a given matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

Trusting what fascicle 82 already explained — that abiding in the benefit of training, wisdom foremost, and firm liberation genuinely leads to what should be attained — gives rise to real delight in the wholesome dharmas that lead there, gathering in both effort and skillful means. The Śrāvaka Ground names four kinds of this aspiration: aspiring to realize the higher liberation one longs for; aspiring to ask questions, seeking out accomplished companions in the monastic community to hear what has not yet been heard, fully; aspiring to gather the necessary provisions, growing ever more careful in discipline, in guarding the senses, in moderate eating, and in reduced sleep; and aspiring to accord with yogic practice itself, delighting in uninterrupted, earnest effort in the very course of cultivation.

18. Section: Effort Itself — Bold and Fierce Application, Fourfold

The word "effort" means arousing actual application, the mind bold and fierce.

Chinese Source

言精進者，發起加行，其心勇悍。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "effort" means arousing actual application, the mind bold and fierce.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Consciousness-Only Treatise and the Śrāvaka Ground

Where aspiration is the wish, effort is the wish carried into actual bodily, verbal, and mental action — boldness meaning steady advance in wholesome dharmas without ever slipping back, fierceness meaning genuinely wholesome rather than merely neutral exertion, which is why effort belongs to the wholesome mental factors alone. The Śrāvaka Ground names four kinds: effort toward hearing, resolving to hear fully what has not yet been heard; effort toward reflection, pondering alone in solitude the meaning of what was heard; effort toward cultivation, practicing calm-abiding and insight at set times in stillness; and effort toward purifying obstacles, diligently walking and sitting by day and night to cleanse the mind of whatever hinders it.

19. Section: Spurring Oneself On — Sustained Diligent Cultivation, and the Bodhisattva's Four All-Encompassing Efforts

The word "spurring oneself on" means, having become bold, then diligently cultivating that very application.

Chinese Source

言策勵者，既勇悍已，於彼加行正勤修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "spurring oneself on" means, having become bold, then diligently cultivating that very application.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground

Once bold effort has begun — reciting sūtras, prostrating, keeping discipline, cultivating calm-abiding and insight — spurring oneself on keeps that boldness diligently sustained. The Bodhisattva Ground names four all-encompassing efforts of this kind: effort freeing one from defilement, preventing every unarisen fetter and affliction and cutting off what has already arisen; effort drawing in wholesome dharmas, causing every unarisen wholesome dharma to arise and every arisen one to remain, grow, and never be forgotten; effort purifying karma, keeping body, speech, and mind entirely pure; and effort increasing wisdom, gathering and growing the wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation.

20. Section: Firm Resolve, and Going Beyond — Never Faltering, and Sparing No Effort

The word "firm resolve" means, once effort has been aroused, never once slackening or abandoning it, unbroken and never regressing. The word "going beyond" means earnest, thoroughgoing effort.

Chinese Source

言剛決者，發精進已，終無懈廢，不壞不退。

言超越者，殷重精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "firm resolve" means, once effort has been aroused, never once slackening or abandoning it, unbroken and never regressing.

The word "going beyond" means earnest, thoroughgoing effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Firm resolve is the endurance to see effort through — bearing whatever bodily fatigue or mental strain hearing, reflection, cultivation, or benefiting others may bring, without collapsing halfway. Going beyond then adds thoroughness to endurance: a bodhisattva does not stop at one virtue practiced well, but pursues discipline together with generosity and the whole range of perfections, and benefits beings as opportunity allows, comprehensively rather than partially — effort complete enough to genuinely surpass where one presently stands.

21. Section: Majestic Force — Rising Before Dawn as Though Donning Armor for Battle

The word "majestic force" means: past the middle watch of the night, or in the first watch, donning armor, one should arouse effort.

Chinese Source

言威勢者，謂過夜分，或前一更，被服鎧甲，當發精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "majestic force" means: past the middle watch of the night, or in the first watch, donning armor, one should arouse effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground

Of the night's three watches — the first from six to nine, the middle from nine to three (reserved for rest), the last from three to six — "past the middle watch" means the pre-dawn hours, "the first watch" the early evening; either way, a practitioner takes up calm-abiding and insight as though arming for war. The Bodhisattva Ground's own account of "armored effort" makes the image vivid: before any actual application begins, the mind first dons a vow of its own — willing to spend a full day and night in hell for each being rescued from suffering, for as many great eons as it takes, without the resolve ever weakening, and pressing forward regardless of how much longer buddhahood itself might take — armor donned in spirit before a single step of practice is taken.

22. Section: Rousing Oneself Further, and Sharp, Piercing Resolve — Advancing Beyond the Armor, Seeing Clearly the Fruit's Own Benefit

The word "rousing oneself further" means, as one is thus armored, arousing diligent effort, or advancing still further, majestic and bold, arousing diligent effort. Seeing deeply the surpassing benefit belonging to that fruit — this is called sharp, piercing resolve.

Chinese Source

言奮發者，如所被服發勤精進，或更昇進，威猛勇悍發勤精進。

深見彼果所有勝利，故名勇銳。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "rousing oneself further" means, as one is thus armored, arousing diligent effort, or advancing still further, majestic and bold, arousing diligent effort.

Seeing deeply the surpassing benefit belonging to that fruit — this is called sharp, piercing resolve.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the Compendium of Bases

Rousing oneself further carries the armored resolve into actual practice, majestic and bold rather than merely determined in the abstract, echoing the Compendium of Bases' description of a bodhisattva unafraid before the vast scope of the Dharma. Sharp, piercing resolve then adds a clear-sighted reason to persevere: seeing plainly the great benefit already gained, and the greater benefit still to come, sustains uninterrupted effort toward it.

23. Section: Bold Endurance — Bearing Cold, Heat, and Drenching Rain Without Wavering

Being able to bear cold and the like, even drenching rain, while diligently cultivating — this is called bold endurance.

Chinese Source

於勤修時，堪能忍受寒等淋瀝，故名勇悍。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being able to bear cold and the like, even drenching rain, while diligently cultivating — this is called bold endurance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground's account of "unmoved effort"

Real forbearance shows itself against real hardship — heat, cold, mosquitoes, hunger, thirst, wind and sun, even spirits or demonic interference — none of which shakes a bodhisattva's resolve or tempts a fall back to a lesser vehicle or an ordinary pursuit of worldly comfort. With this, the marks of unshakable bold effort are shown.

24. Section: Hard to Overcome, and Never Satisfied with Little — Trusting Ever-Higher Realization, Refusing to Settle for a Small Gain

Skillfully knowing the distinctions between earlier and later stages, and, with respect to ever more excellent distinctions still to be realized, generating deep trust and effort accordingly — this is called hard to overcome. Not being timid amid whatever small or inferior distinction has already been realized, but continuing to cultivate what is wholesome — this is called never satisfied with little.

Chinese Source

由善了知前後差別，於其勝上差別證中，深生信順所有精進，名難制伏。

於少下劣差別所證進修善中，無怯劣故，名無喜足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Skillfully knowing the distinctions between earlier and later stages, and, with respect to ever more excellent distinctions still to be realized, generating deep trust and effort accordingly — this is called hard to overcome.

Not being timid amid whatever small or inferior distinction has already been realized, but continuing to cultivate what is wholesome — this is called never satisfied with little.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground

Hard to overcome describes a practitioner who, hearing of realizations vast, profound, and hard to fathom, never belittles their own capacity or grows timid, confident there is more still to reach. Never satisfied with little describes the same confidence turned toward what has already been gained: no small attainment is mistaken for the whole path, and effort continues undiminished rather than resting content — the very "unsatisfied, unretreating effort" the Bodhisattva Ground and Compendium of Bases both describe.

25. Section: Steadying the Mind — Repeatedly Watching for Affliction and Demonic Interference, and Calming What Is Found

The word "steadying the mind" means, amid every obstruction to effort — every root affliction, secondary affliction, and demonic disturbance — repeatedly watching for it, and settling the mind to stillness.

Chinese Source

言勵心者，謂於精進所有障處，一切煩惱及隨煩惱諸魔事中，頻頻覺察，令心靜息。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "steadying the mind" means, amid every obstruction to effort — every root affliction, secondary affliction, and demonic disturbance — repeatedly watching for it, and settling the mind to stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground's account of "equanimous effort"

Outside formal meditation, mindfulness simply guards against greed, hatred, and delusion arising at all; within meditation, the same vigilance treats the five hindrances and their kin as the very faults they are, purifying the mind of whatever obstructs before it takes root — together sustaining the mindful, equanimous abiding the Bodhisattva Ground calls even cultivation across every wholesome dharma alike.

26. Section: Constancy — Never Laying Down the Yoke — Closing All Thirteen Marks of the Sequence of Effort

The word "constancy" means, within this very right application, being able to practice it constantly, never laying down the yoke.

Chinese Source

言常恆者，謂即於此正加行中，能常修作，能不捨軛。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "constancy" means, within this very right application, being able to practice it constantly, never laying down the yoke.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Constancy carries wholesome practice unbroken across past, present, and future, the yoke of discipline and diligence never once set down. With this, all thirteen marks of the sequence of effort — aspiration, effort, spurring oneself on, firm resolve, going beyond, majestic force, rousing oneself further, sharp resolve, bold endurance, being hard to overcome, never being satisfied with little, steadying the mind, and constancy — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to the sequence of right conduct itself.

27. Section: The Sequence of Right Conduct Begins — Right Faith: Pure Trust Arising from Hearing the Great Teacher's True Dharma

The word "right faith" means, when the great teacher speaks the true dharma, having heard this true dharma, gaining pure faith.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "right faith" means, when the great teacher speaks the true dharma, having heard this true dharma, gaining pure faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A third sequence, right conduct, unfolds in twelve stages, beginning where all genuine practice begins: faith itself, without which wholesome aspiration and bold effort have nowhere to take root — faith is rightly called the very source of the path and the mother of all virtue.

28. Section: Heedfulness — Guarding the Mind Against Whatever Obstructs Renunciation, and Constantly Cultivating What Is Wholesome

Heedfulness means, having gained faith, guarding the mind against dharmas that obstruct delight in renunciation, and constantly arousing the cultivation of wholesome dharmas.

Chinese Source

不放逸者，謂得信已，於樂出離障礙法中，防護其心，恆常發起善法修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Heedfulness means, having gained faith, guarding the mind against dharmas that obstruct delight in renunciation, and constantly arousing the cultivation of wholesome dharmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once faith is established, heedfulness guards it in practice — keeping the mind clear of the five desires and five hindrances that obstruct renunciation, and constantly taking up calm-abiding, insight, and every other wholesome cultivation in their place.

29. Section: Yoga Itself — Retaining, Reciting, Questioning, Discerning, and Rightly Cultivating

The word "yoga" means receiving and retaining, reading and reciting, questioning and discussing, discernment, and rightly cultivating application.

Chinese Source

言瑜伽者，受持、讀誦、問論、決擇、正修加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "yoga" means receiving and retaining, reading and reciting, questioning and discussing, discernment, and rightly cultivating application.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All three vehicles' practice of calm-abiding and insight share the name yoga, meaning simply union with the dharma. Five steps carry a practitioner there: receiving and retaining what is heard in mind; reading and reciting it aloud until it is memorized; questioning and discussing it with a teacher or fellow practitioner, since a question honestly asked is itself a kind of wisdom; discernment, thinking the matter through with inference once scriptural authority has been received; and rightly cultivating application, tasting the truth directly through calm-abiding and insight. The first three belong to the wisdom born of hearing, discernment to the wisdom born of reflection, and right cultivation to the wisdom born of practice — yoga's true practice spans all three trainings and all three wisdoms at once.

30. Section: Reflection — Carefully Observing the Ultimate Meaning Already Retained

The word "reflection" means carefully and truly observing the ultimate meaning of the dharma one has retained.

Chinese Source

言思惟者，隨所受持究竟法義，審諦觀察。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "reflection" means carefully and truly observing the ultimate meaning of the dharma one has retained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Holding the ultimate teachings — emptiness, the dharma-realm, reality's own limit — in mind, a practitioner examines them carefully by scriptural authority, by inference, and by direct observation in turn, until the mind itself accords with reality as it truly is.

31. Section: Recollection — Never Forgetting, and Rightly Remembering Even the Distant Past

The word "recollection" means being unable to forget any meaning of dharma once observed, and rightly recollecting even what was long ago done or said.

Chinese Source

言憶念者，於所觀察一切法義，能不忘失；於久所作、久所說中，能正隨念。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "recollection" means being unable to forget any meaning of dharma once observed, and rightly recollecting even what was long ago done or said.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Daily habituation to hearing and reflecting on the dharma from morning to night builds a genuine power of recollection — contemplations on impurity or impermanence held firmly in mind, and even distant past words and deeds accurately remembered, so that mindfulness and clear comprehension pervade every present moment.

32. Section: Investigation — Thoughts of Renunciation Arising from Undistorted Dharma

The word "investigation" means, relying on this very undistorted meaning of dharma, giving rise to thoughts of renunciation and the rest.

Chinese Source

言尋思者，即依如是無倒法義，起出離等所有尋思。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "investigation" means, relying on this very undistorted meaning of dharma, giving rise to thoughts of renunciation and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Grounded in correct understanding, wholesome investigation takes the form of thoughts free of ill will and harm, aimed at renouncing every defiled dharma and realizing every pure one — a constant self-check on whether one's own mind still runs with affliction or has genuinely turned toward the wholesome.

33. Section: Knowledge, and Understanding — the Wondrous Wisdom of Preparation, and the Wondrous Wisdom of Direct Realization

The word "knowledge" means the wondrous wisdom of transcendent application. The word "understanding" means the wondrous wisdom of transcendent direct realization.

Chinese Source

所言智者，謂出世間加行妙慧。

所言解者，謂出世間正體妙慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "knowledge" means the wondrous wisdom of transcendent application. The word "understanding" means the wondrous wisdom of transcendent direct realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first six marks all belong to the stage of accumulation; here, at "knowledge," the sequence advances to the stage of preparation — the non-discriminating wisdom that arises approaching realization, though technically still contaminated, already oriented toward what is uncontaminated. "Understanding" then names the fruit that preparation leads to: the fundamental non-discriminating wisdom itself, realized at stream-entry for a śrāvaka or the first ground for a bodhisattva — mind and truth finally united without mediation.

34. Section: Wisdom Itself, and Observation — the Worldly Wisdom Gained After Realization, and Honest Self-Examination of What Remains

The word "wisdom" means the wondrous worldly wisdom subsequently gained, once transcendent wisdom has already been realized. The word "observation" means, through undistorted, observing attention, carefully and truly examining what has been cut off, what has not yet been cut off, what still remains, and what remains no longer.

Chinese Source

所言慧者，謂已證得出世間慧，後時所得世間妙慧。

言觀察者，謂由無倒觀察作意，審諦觀察已斷、未斷、有餘、無餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "wisdom" means the wondrous worldly wisdom subsequently gained, once transcendent wisdom has already been realized. The word "observation" means, through undistorted, observing attention, carefully and truly examining what has been cut off, what has not yet been cut off, what still remains, and what remains no longer.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once fundamental non-discriminating wisdom is realized, a noble one goes on to reflect on worldly and transcendent dharmas alike through subsequently gained wisdom — still discriminating in form, unlike an ordinary being's discrimination, but free of the grasping that makes ordinary thought impure. Observation then keeps this same honesty turned inward: checking, by scriptural authority, inference, and direct perception together, exactly which afflictions are cut off and which remain — no self-deception permitted.

35. Section: Pure Conduct — the Noble Eightfold Path, and Freedom from Sexual Misconduct

The word "pure conduct" means the noble eightfold path, together with freedom from the practice of sexual desire that is not genuine pure conduct.

Chinese Source

言梵行者，謂八聖支道，及與遠離非正梵行習婬欲法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "pure conduct" means the noble eightfold path, together with freedom from the practice of sexual desire that is not genuine pure conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eightfold path earns the name "pure conduct" (brahmacarya) three ways: it is conduct that leads to brahma — nirvāṇa itself, free of defilement; it is conduct free of what is not brahma — affliction; and it arises from the Tathāgata's own pure, brahma-like voice. Pure conduct in this sense also means simply freedom from craving-bound sexual desire.

36. Section: Abiding in the Remaining Pure Conduct — the Thirty-Seven Factors of Awakening, Sorted by Calm-Abiding, Insight, and Mindfulness of Body — Closing the Sequence of Right Conduct, and All Three Sequences, Entirely

Further, "abiding in the remaining pure conduct" means the thirty-seven factors of awakening, gathered under three headings: through calm-abiding, through insight, and through mindfulness of the body. As is fitting, each belongs to its own nature or its own category. Here, faith and mindfulness are common to both categories.

Chinese Source

又言安住餘梵行者，謂三十七菩提分法，彼由三處之所攝受。

謂由奢摩他故，由毗鉢舍那故，由修身念故。

如其所應，彼自性故、彼品類故。

此中信念，俱通二品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, "abiding in the remaining pure conduct" means the thirty-seven factors of awakening, gathered under three headings: through calm-abiding, through insight, and through mindfulness of the body.

As is fitting, each belongs to its own nature or its own category.

Here, faith and mindfulness are common to both categories.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary and the Great Commentary on the Abhidharma

Beyond the eightfold path already named, the remaining pure conduct comprises the four foundations of mindfulness, four right efforts, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, and seven factors of awakening — thirty-seven in all, none falling outside three headings: calm-abiding, insight, or mindfulness of the body, itself the very foundation the whole path builds on, since it is named first among the four foundations of mindfulness. Some factors belong purely to calm-abiding's own nature (the four bases of power, for instance), some purely to insight's (the factor of investigating dharmas), and some to both categories at once — mindfulness of the body chief among these. Faith and mindfulness both run through every stage regardless: as the Great Commentary explains, faith is named a factor of awakening not because it awakens directly but because it is the very foundation every wholesome practice rests on — without first trusting that the path leads somewhere, no one would ever take the first step — and mindfulness runs through both calm-abiding, which needs it to hold a single object steady, and insight, which needs it to discern dharmas at all. With this, all twelve marks of the sequence of right conduct — right faith, heedfulness, yoga, reflection, recollection, investigation, knowledge, understanding, wisdom, observation, pure conduct, and abiding in the remaining pure conduct — are closed, and with them, all three sequences of the disciple's own reliance — faith, effort, and right conduct — and the entire account of the teacher, the very first topic of the particular summary verse, are now fully explained. The treatise turns next to the Buddha as foremost among all beings, the verse's second topic.

37. Section: The Buddha as Foremost Begins — "First," Because His Own Goal Is Accomplished

Further, this same great teacher is also called first, because his own goal is accomplished.

Chinese Source

復次，即此大師亦稱第一，自義行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, this same great teacher is also called first, because his own goal is accomplished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's second topic turns from the teacher himself to the Buddha as foremost among all beings, unpacked across three complementary explanations. In the first, "first" simply names the Buddha's own accomplished goal — unsurpassed, unconditioned nirvāṇa realized — making him first throughout the three realms.

38. Section: "Honored," Because He Accomplishes Others' Goals; "Excellent," Because He Accomplishes Both

He is also called honored, because he accomplishes the goals of others. He is also called excellent, because he accomplishes both goals together.

Chinese Source

亦稱為尊，他義行故。

亦稱為勝，俱義行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

He is also called honored, because he accomplishes the goals of others. He is also called excellent, because he accomplishes both goals together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where "first" named self-benefit alone, "honored" names the Buddha's complete success in benefiting others — helping every being toward nirvāṇa and wholesome practice, the most honored being among gods and humans. "Excellent" then names both together, self-benefit and other-benefit perfected as one — no being in the three realms surpasses him.

39. Section: "Highest," Surpassing Every Outside Doctrine; "Unsurpassed," Surpassing Even Śrāvakas and Solitary Buddhas — Closing the First Explanation

He is also called highest, because he outshines all outside doctrines. He is also called unsurpassed, because he outshines the lesser and middle vehicles of śrāvakas and solitary buddhas alike.

Chinese Source

亦稱為上，映蔽一切諸外道故。

亦稱無上，映蔽一切聲聞、獨覺下中乘故。

Ru-Yi Translation

He is also called highest, because he outshines all outside doctrines. He is also called unsurpassed, because he outshines the lesser and middle vehicles of śrāvakas and solitary buddhas alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Highest" places the Buddha above every non-Buddhist teaching, which cannot approach his wisdom and merit; "unsurpassed" places him above even the lesser and middle vehicles within Buddhism itself, the summit of every path. With this, the first of three explanations of the Buddha's fivefold "foremost" status — first, honored, excellent, highest, and unsurpassed, read through the lens of accomplished goals — is closed. The treatise turns next to the same five terms read through the lens of obstructions overcome.

40. Section: A Second Explanation — the Same Five Terms Read Through Perfected Goodness Shared with Worldlings, Śrāvakas, and Solitary Buddhas, and Both Obstructions Purified

There is a further distinction. "First" means goodness shared with the whole world is perfected. "Honored" means goodness shared with all śrāvakas is perfected. "Excellent" means goodness shared with all solitary buddhas is perfected. "Highest" means purity from the affliction obstruction is attained. "Unsurpassed" means purity from the obstruction to what is known is attained.

Chinese Source

復有差別。言第一者，共諸世間善圓滿故。所言尊者，共諸聲聞善圓滿故。所言勝者，共諸獨覺善圓滿故。所言上者，於煩惱障得清淨故。言無上者，於所知障得清淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further distinction. "First" means goodness shared with the whole world is perfected. "Honored" means goodness shared with all śrāvakas is perfected. "Excellent" means goodness shared with all solitary buddhas is perfected. "Highest" means purity from the affliction obstruction is attained. "Unsurpassed" means purity from the obstruction to what is known is attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This second explanation reads the same five terms as an ascending scale of shared and unshared virtue: goodness the Buddha shares with worldlings first, then with śrāvakas, then with solitary buddhas, and finally two unshared achievements — the affliction obstruction cleared (freeing him from birth-and-death, as it does an arhat) and, beyond even that, the obstruction to what is known cleared as well (opening the way to unsurpassed awakening, which afflictions alone being cleared could never reach). With this, the second explanation, distinguished by obstructions overcome, is closed. The treatise turns next to a third explanation.

41. Section: A Third Explanation — the Same Five Terms Read Through the Desire, Form, and Formless Realms, and Worldly Versus Transcendent Goodness — Closing Every Explanation by Wording

There is a further distinction still. "First" means goodness of conduct in the desire realm is perfected. "Honored" means goodness of conduct in the form realm is perfected. "Excellent" means goodness of conduct in the formless realm is perfected. "Highest" means goodness surpassing the whole of worldly, threefold existence is perfected. "Unsurpassed" means transcendent goodness itself is perfected.

Chinese Source

復有差別。言第一者，於欲行善得圓滿故。所言尊者，於色行善得圓滿故。所言勝者，無色行善得圓滿故。所言上者，超過一切三界世間善圓滿故。言無上者，出世間善得圓滿故。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further distinction still. "First" means goodness of conduct in the desire realm is perfected. "Honored" means goodness of conduct in the form realm is perfected. "Excellent" means goodness of conduct in the formless realm is perfected. "Highest" means goodness surpassing the whole of worldly, threefold existence is perfected. "Unsurpassed" means transcendent goodness itself is perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

This third and final explanation climbs through the three realms themselves — the ten wholesome deeds of the desire realm perfected, the meditative goodness of the form realm perfected, the attainments of the formless realm perfected — and then breaks free of all three: "highest" naming the subsequently gained wisdom that surpasses every worldly realm, and "unsurpassed" naming the fundamental wisdom itself, transcendent goodness in its own fully perfected right. With this, all three explanations of the Buddha's fivefold foremost status by wording alone are closed. The treatise turns next to the same status demonstrated by comparison with different kinds of beings.

42. Section: Comparison by Kind Begins — Creatures Without Feet, with Two, with Four, and with Many

無足有情者，如蛇等。二足有情者，謂人等。四足有情者，如牛等。多足有情者，如百足等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Footless beings are such as snakes; two-footed beings are such as humans; four-footed beings are such as cattle; many-footed beings are such as centipedes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having shown the Buddha's foremost status through wording, the treatise now demonstrates the same status by simple comparison — beginning with desire-realm creatures sorted by the crudest possible measure, how many legs they walk on: none, like snakes or earthworms; two, like humans, gods, or birds; four, like cattle, dogs, pigs, or sheep; and many, like centipedes, cockroaches, or spiders.

43. Section: Beings with and Without Form, and Beings with, Without, or Neither With nor Without Conception — Closing Every Kind Compared

Formless beings range from the sphere of infinite space up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. Beings with conception range from the desire realm up through the sphere of nothingness, excluding the heaven of no conception. Beings without conception are those of the heaven of no conception. Beings neither with nor without conception are those born in the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

Beings with form range from the desire realm up through the fourth meditative absorption. Formless beings range from the sphere of infinite space up through the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

Beings with conception range from the desire realm up through the sphere of nothingness, excluding the heaven of no conception. Beings without conception are those of the heaven of no conception. Beings neither with nor without conception are those born in the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second comparison sorts beings by whether they retain physical form at all — the desire realm and the four meditative absorptions of the form realm still embodied, the four formless spheres beyond it having shed materiality entirely. A third comparison sorts by conception itself — nearly all beings retain it, except those born in the heaven of no conception (within the fourth absorption), whose perception and feeling are wholly stilled, and those in the subtlest formless sphere of neither perception nor non-perception, where only the faintest trace of conception remains. With this, every kind of being compared — by limb, by form, and by conception — is closed. The treatise turns next to a concluding summary of what this threefold comparison shows.

44. Section: A Concluding Summary — the Buddha's Threefold Foremost Status, Surpassing Every Kind of Being — Closing the Entire Account of "Foremost"

Such is the brief account of these kinds compared, revealing the Tathāgata's threefold foremost status: by limb, by physical support, and by mind.

Chinese Source

如是略說品類差別，顯示如來三種第一，謂由蠢動故、由依止故、由心故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such is the brief account of these kinds compared, revealing the Tathāgata's threefold foremost status: by limb, by physical support, and by mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the account of the teacher stated the Buddha's own virtues directly, this account of foremost status proves the same point by contrast — comparing him against every restless creature crawling the desire realm, every being bound by a physical form or freed of one, and every mind whether fully conceiving, unconceiving, or barely conceiving at all, the Tathāgata stands beyond and above every one of them. With this, the entire account of the Buddha as foremost, the summary verse's second topic, is closed in full. The treatise turns next to its third topic: the vast synonyms of wisdom, unfolding across twenty-two further sections.

45. Section: The Two Wisdoms Begin — Wisdom That Gains, Wisdom That Is Vast, Wisdom That Is Pure

Chinese Source

復次，能得慧者，謂總攝一切能引義利所有善慧生長增益。廣大慧者，謂軟中上品增進差別。清淨慧者，謂宿世串習，經歷多時其慧成熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, gaining wisdom means, in general, every wholesome wisdom that draws out benefit, growing and increasing without end. Vast wisdom means the distinction of advancing from a lesser to a middling to a superior grade. Pure wisdom means wisdom habituated across past lives, matured over a long span of time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

The verse's third topic, wisdom, unfolds into some fifty-four distinct synonyms across twenty-two sections — the largest single term explored in this entire compendium. This first section names three: gaining wisdom, wisdom that draws toward nirvāṇa and keeps growing, whether at the preparatory or subsequently gained stage; vast wisdom, simply the relative comparison between a lesser, middling, and superior grade of the same wisdom; and pure wisdom, wisdom cultivated across many past lifetimes until it fully matures and can finally cut off affliction.

46. Section: Wisdom That Accomplishes, Wisdom That Perfects, and Wisdom That Never Regresses

Accomplishing wisdom means thoroughly knowing and permanently cutting off every affliction. Perfecting wisdom means this same wholesome wisdom has reached the very end. Non-regressing wisdom means this same wholesome wisdom has become a dharma of no regression, ultimate renunciation.

Chinese Source

成辦慧者，謂於諸煩惱遍知永斷。圓滿慧者，謂即此善慧已到究竟。無退慧者，謂即此善慧成無退法，究竟出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Accomplishing wisdom means thoroughly knowing and permanently cutting off every affliction. Perfecting wisdom means this same wholesome wisdom has reached the very end. Non-regressing wisdom means this same wholesome wisdom has become a dharma of no regression, ultimate renunciation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Accomplishing wisdom marks stream-entry and beyond, affliction genuinely cut off; perfecting wisdom marks the very summit — buddhahood in the Mahāyāna, arhatship for a śrāvaka; and non-regressing wisdom marks the security that comes with genuine realization, never again falling back to an ordinary being's condition.

47. Section: Nimble Wisdom, Swift Wisdom, and Sharp Wisdom

Nimble wisdom means understanding swiftly and at once. Swift wisdom means wisdom free of any obstruction or delay. Sharp wisdom means thoroughly understanding a matter both in its full extent and as it truly is.

Chinese Source

言捷慧者，速疾了知故。言速慧者，慧無滯礙故。言利慧者，盡其所有、如其所有皆善了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nimble wisdom means understanding swiftly and at once. Swift wisdom means wisdom free of any obstruction or delay. Sharp wisdom means thoroughly understanding a matter both in its full extent and as it truly is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three images of quickness and keenness: nimble wisdom grasps a hundred things from hearing one; swift wisdom moves without any hesitation or blockage; and sharp wisdom cuts all the way through, taking in both a dharma's full particular extent and its shared, unchanging nature at once.

48. Section: Emerging Wisdom — Skillfully Knowing the Worldly Path of Renunciation — Closing Nimble, Swift, and Sharp Wisdom

The word "emerging wisdom" means skillfully knowing renunciation's own worldly path free of desire.

Chinese Source

言出慧者，於出離法世間離欲能善了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "emerging wisdom" means skillfully knowing renunciation's own worldly path free of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Buddha-Ground Treatise

The Buddha-Ground Treatise glosses nimble, swift, and sharp wisdom in turn: swiftly entering the meaning of the Buddha's own teaching and discipline is nimble wisdom; entering it more fully still, able to actually practice accordingly, is swift wisdom; and penetrating dependent origination's own subtlest meaning is sharp wisdom. Emerging wisdom, here, names the worldly meditative wisdom that achieves absorption itself, understanding renunciation's own path even before transcendent realization is reached.

49. Section: Discerning Wisdom — Knowing the Transcendent Dharmas of Renunciation

Discerning wisdom means being able to know the transcendent dharmas of renunciation.

Chinese Source

決擇慧者，於出世間諸離欲法能了知故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Discerning wisdom means being able to know the transcendent dharmas of renunciation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground and the Buddha-Ground Treatise

Once access concentration is reached, two paths diverge: the worldly path just named, requiring emerging wisdom, and the transcendent path, requiring discerning wisdom — penetrating the four noble truths directly and realizing the fruits from stream-entry to arhatship. This non-discriminating wisdom serves as the very cause that brings nirvāṇa about, earning it the further name "supreme discerning wisdom."

50. Section: Profound Wisdom — Emptiness and Dependent Origination Known as They Truly Are, and Only the Tathāgata Establishes What Others Merely Follow — Closing Section Four

Profound wisdom means knowing dharmas that accord with profound emptiness and dependent origination, and truly and skillfully penetrating every profound phrase of meaning without exception. Here, the Tathāgata's own wisdom is able to establish the teaching; the wisdom of śrāvakas and others is only able to follow and awaken to what has been established.

Chinese Source

甚深慧者，於甚深空相應、緣起隨順諸法能了知故，又於一切甚深義句皆能如實善通達故。

此中如來慧能制立，聲聞等慧於所制立能隨覺了。

Ru-Yi Translation

Profound wisdom means knowing dharmas that accord with profound emptiness and dependent origination, and truly and skillfully penetrating every profound phrase of meaning without exception.

Here, the Tathāgata's own wisdom is able to establish the teaching; the wisdom of śrāvakas and others is only able to follow and awaken to what has been established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Two kinds of profundity meet here — the profundity of emptiness (self and dharmas both empty of inherent nature) and the profundity of dependent origination (every phenomenon throughout the dharma-realm arising interdependently) — grasped only by wisdom that has already realized fundamental non-discrimination directly. A bodhisattva's own profound wisdom deepens by stages, from the first ground's initial touch of emptiness, through the sixth ground's dawning wisdom of dependent origination, the seventh ground's still-effortful profound insight, the eighth ground's effortless stability, and onward to buddhahood, where fundamental and subsequently gained wisdom both reach final, unconditioned completion — only there does profound wisdom become truly ultimate. And only the Buddha's wisdom actually establishes the teaching from nothing; even the wisdom of śrāvaka disciples can only follow what he has already laid down. With this, the fourth section — emerging, discerning, and profound wisdom — is closed. The treatise turns next to great wisdom and vast wisdom.

51. Section: Great Wisdom and Vast Wisdom — Long Habituation, and a Boundless Field of Objects

Further, great wisdom means this very wisdom habituated over a long span of time. Vast wisdom means this very wisdom whose field of objects is measureless and boundless.

Chinese Source

又大慧者，謂即此慧長時串習故。其廣慧者，謂即此慧無量無邊所行境故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, great wisdom means this very wisdom habituated over a long span of time. Vast wisdom means this very wisdom whose field of objects is measureless and boundless.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Buddha-Ground Treatise

"Great wisdom" is called great because it can resolve any question whatsoever, without exhaustion; "vast wisdom" is called vast because its field of objects — the entire trichiliocosm, and the whole of the twelve divisions of scripture — is thoroughly, deeply, and completely penetrated.

52. Section: Wisdom Without Equal, and the Jewel of Wisdom — Foremost Among the Five Faculties, Like a Gem That Makes a King's Own Jewel Shine

Wisdom without equal means no other wisdom equals it. The word "jewel of wisdom" means, among the faculties, wisdom is most excellent — like the wish-fulfilling gem that makes a wheel-turning king's own beryl jewel shine with radiance; joined with it, that jewel's own power comes to full fruition.

Chinese Source

無等慧者，其餘諸慧無與等故。言慧寶者，於諸根中，慧最勝故。如末尼珠，顯發輪王毗琉璃寶，令光淨故。與彼相應，故名慧寶皆得成就。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wisdom without equal means no other wisdom equals it. The word "jewel of wisdom" means, among the faculties, wisdom is most excellent — like the wish-fulfilling gem that makes a wheel-turning king's own beryl jewel shine with radiance; joined with it, that jewel's own power comes to full fruition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Buddha-Ground Treatise

Even a dull-faculty śrāvaka's wisdom already surpasses every ordinary being's own — hence "without equal" among worldly wisdom generally. Among the five faculties (faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom), wisdom is foremost, the very jewel that makes the other four shine — the three concentrations of emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, once joined to wisdom, are what actually bring a śrāvaka's own attainment to completion, hence "the jewel of wisdom accomplished."

53. Section: The Eye of Wisdom and the Light of Wisdom — Innate Wisdom, and Wisdom Drawn Out by a Teacher

Further, the eye of wisdom means wisdom born together with a being. The light of wisdom means wisdom drawn out by another — the wisdom of skillful application drawn out by another.

Chinese Source

又慧眼者，謂俱生慧。言慧明者，謂他所引，則他所引善加行慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the eye of wisdom means wisdom born together with a being. The light of wisdom means wisdom drawn out by another — the wisdom of skillful application drawn out by another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eye of wisdom is innate, the fruit of wisdom cultivated across past lives and carried forward unbidden into this one; the light of wisdom is instead drawn out through a good teacher's own instruction, the wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation kindled by another's guidance.

54. Section: The Glow of Wisdom and the Radiance of Wisdom — Wisdom Born of Hearing and Reflection, and Wisdom Born of Cultivation

The word "glow of wisdom" means the wisdom accomplished through hearing and reflection, by way of application. The word "radiance of wisdom" means the wisdom accomplished through cultivation, arising from this very glow.

Chinese Source

言慧光者，謂即加行聞思成慧。言慧曜者，謂即由此修所成慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "glow of wisdom" means the wisdom accomplished through hearing and reflection, by way of application. The word "radiance of wisdom" means the wisdom accomplished through cultivation, arising from this very glow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where "the light of wisdom" named wisdom kindled by a teacher generally, glow and radiance distinguish its two stages more finely: the wisdom born of hearing and reflection is the first glimmer, the "glow," and the wisdom born of cultivation that grows from it is the fuller "radiance."

55. Section: The Lamp of Wisdom and the Torch of Wisdom — Revealing the Profound Teaching, and Following It at Every Time and Measure

The word "lamp of wisdom" means revealing the Tathāgata's own sūtras, their profound establishments, and the rest. The word "torch of wisdom" means being able to follow the dharma teaching according to whatever measure and time is fitting.

Chinese Source

言慧燈者，謂於如來所說經典，甚深建立等開示故。言慧炬者，謂於法教，隨量隨時能隨轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "lamp of wisdom" means revealing the Tathāgata's own sūtras, their profound establishments, and the rest. The word "torch of wisdom" means being able to follow the dharma teaching according to whatever measure and time is fitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

A teacher who can reveal a sūtra's own profound doctrines — emptiness, dependent origination — evenly and clearly is rightly called a lamp of wisdom. And whoever follows the sacred teaching faithfully at every time, whether taking a single passage, the whole teaching, or its measureless breadth as their own object of practice, carries that same light forward like a torch, never departing from the Buddha's own authoritative word.

56. Section: Illuminating Wisdom and Wisdom Without Darkness — Understanding Still Received from Another, and Understanding Personally Verified

The word "illuminating wisdom" means being able, through excellent wisdom, to skillfully know this or that dharma — yet though skillfully known, this understanding still follows another's guidance, and has not yet been personally verified. "Wisdom without darkness" means personal verification itself.

Chinese Source

言慧照者，謂於彼彼所有諸法，以其妙慧能善了知；雖善了知，猶隨他轉，而未身證。

慧無闇者，謂身作證。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "illuminating wisdom" means being able, through excellent wisdom, to skillfully know this or that dharma — yet though skillfully known, this understanding still follows another's guidance, and has not yet been personally verified.

"Wisdom without darkness" means personal verification itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Illuminating wisdom genuinely understands the dharma, yet only through what has been heard from a teacher — real understanding, but not yet direct, personal realization of emptiness or reality itself. Wisdom without darkness names the moment that gap closes: ignorance's long night finally ended, the mind itself tasting reality directly rather than through another's report.

57. Section: The Faculty of Wisdom and the Power of Wisdom — Following Others' Realization, and Never Faltering in One's Own

The faculty of wisdom means, by the power of thoroughly understanding what another has realized, whatever wisdom this yields. The power of wisdom means, by the power of thoroughly understanding one's own earlier and later distinctions of realization, wisdom that, by the true principle of dharma, never falters or shrinks back.

Chinese Source

其慧根者，謂於他所證，能遍了知增上力故，諸所有慧。言慧力者，謂於自先後差別所證，能遍了知增上力故，由法道理無退屈慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

The faculty of wisdom means, by the power of thoroughly understanding what another has realized, whatever wisdom this yields. The power of wisdom means, by the power of thoroughly understanding one's own earlier and later distinctions of realization, wisdom that, by the true principle of dharma, never falters or shrinks back.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The faculty of wisdom still learns by following what the Buddha or a good teacher has realized, not yet one's own direct realization, though the resolve it produces is already strong enough to nourish further growth toward the path. The power of wisdom builds on that same root once it has grown firm — like a well-rooted tree finally able to withstand wind and storm — carrying a practitioner into the seven factors of discernment near the very threshold of realization, strong enough now to actually cut off affliction rather than merely grow toward doing so.

58. Section: The Wealth of Wisdom and the Jewel of Wisdom — Drawing In Every Mastery, and the Very Root of Worldly Riches

The word "wealth of wisdom" means it is able to draw in every mastery and supreme prosperity, freely obtained according to the mind's own wish. This jewel of wisdom is, among all forms of wealth, the most excellent, being the very root and cause of every worldly treasure.

Chinese Source

言慧財者，謂能招引一切自在最勝富貴，隨獲自心自在轉故。

又此慧寶，於一切財最為殊勝，能為一切世間珍財根本因故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "wealth of wisdom" means it is able to draw in every mastery and supreme prosperity, freely obtained according to the mind's own wish. This jewel of wisdom is, among all forms of wealth, the most excellent, being the very root and cause of every worldly treasure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Wisdom is one of the seven kinds of noble wealth, untouched by flood, fire, or storm, and carried forward into future lives — a mastery that lets a wise person control their own mind, cultivate concentration, and freely manifest whatever provisions they need through mastery in meditation. Every outward, worldly treasure ultimately traces back to this inward wealth as its root cause.

59. Section: The Sword of Wisdom and the Blade of Wisdom — Permanently Severing Every Fetter

As it is said, the sword of wisdom and the blade of wisdom mean being able to permanently sever every fetter.

Chinese Source

如說慧劍及慧刀者，謂能永斷一切結故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As it is said, the sword of wisdom and the blade of wisdom mean being able to permanently sever every fetter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra

The Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra ranks the two images by depth: a bodhisattva who perfects the perfection of wisdom is said to have great power; having that power, to hold a sharp blade; and only once fundamental and subsequently gained wisdom operate together — as a two-edged sword cuts where a single-edged blade cannot — is that same wisdom said to hold a sharp sword, capable of subduing every army of Māra rather than merely wielding a single cutting edge.

60. Section: The Staff, the Reins, and Wisdom That Never Falls — Warding Off Every Affliction, Guiding the Mind Like a Steed, and a Body That Cannot Be Broken

The word "staff of wisdom" means being able to ward off every affliction and every heavenly demon from afar. The word "reins of wisdom" means giving free rein to the horse of the mind-faculty, letting it gallop across the ground of wholesome conduct. "Wisdom that never falls" means causing the limbs of the body never to scatter or decay.

Chinese Source

言慧杖者，謂能遠防一切煩惱、天惡魔故。言慧轡者，縱意根馬於善行地而馳驟故。慧無墮者，令諸身分不散壞故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "staff of wisdom" means being able to ward off every affliction and every heavenly demon from afar. The word "reins of wisdom" means giving free rein to the horse of the mind-faculty, letting it gallop across the ground of wholesome conduct. "Wisdom that never falls" means causing the limbs of the body never to scatter or decay.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A staff wards off affliction and every kind of demon — the aggregates, affliction, death, and the sixth-heaven god alike; reins let the mind's own faculty run freely, yet always across wholesome ground rather than astray; and "never falling" describes a body no longer subject to the five aggregates' own decay, having realized instead the indestructible body born of the dharma's own true nature.

61. Section: The Wall of Wisdom, the Stairway of Wisdom, and the Hall of Wisdom — One Gate for the Whole House, Preparation's Ascent, and Wisdom's Own Final Room

The wall of wisdom means it pervades every dharma through a single gate. The stairway of wisdom means the path of preparation itself. The hall of wisdom means having reached the very end.

Chinese Source

慧垣牆者，遍於一切一門轉故。

慧階陛者，加行道故。慧堂殿者，到究竟故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wall of wisdom means it pervades every dharma through a single gate. The stairway of wisdom means the path of preparation itself. The hall of wisdom means having reached the very end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Lotus Sūtra

Like the great house in the Lotus Sūtra, vast and old, sheltering hundreds within a single gate — a śrāvaka's wisdom surrounds every dharma while taking nirvāṇa alone as its one entrance, and a bodhisattva's surrounds every dharma while taking the aspiration to help all beings reach unsurpassed awakening as that same single gate. The stairway then names the wisdom of the path of preparation, climbing toward realization; and the hall names arriving at last in the innermost room — fundamental non-discriminating wisdom itself realized, stream-entry for a śrāvaka, the first ground for a bodhisattva, a disciple now truly received into the house.

62. Section: A Second Explanation — Wall, Stairway, and Hall Restated as Wisdom of the Elements, Manifold Elements, and All Elements — Closing Section Fifteen

In order to reveal these same three — wall, stairway, and hall — it is further said there are three kinds: wisdom of the elements, wisdom of manifold elements, and wisdom of more than a single element.

Chinese Source

為欲顯示垣牆等三，復說三種。所謂界智、種種界智、非一界智。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to reveal these same three — wall, stairway, and hall — it is further said there are three kinds: wisdom of the elements, wisdom of manifold elements, and wisdom of more than a single element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the Yogācārabhūmi's own restatement in section 17

The same three images recur under new names, to be unpacked fully two sections ahead: the wall corresponds to wisdom of the elements, since it enters, like the wall's single gate, through the mind-consciousness element alone; the hall corresponds to wisdom of manifold elements, gathering everything the wall and stairway together enclose; and the stairway corresponds to wisdom of more than a single element, climbing step by step through many. With this, the fifteenth section — wall, stairway, and hall, and their restatement as elements, manifold elements, and more than a single element — is closed. The treatise turns next to right view and the wisdom of learners and adepts.

63. Section: Right View Begins — Skillfully Penetrating the Truth of Dharmas

Further, right view means being able to skillfully penetrate the true dharma.

Chinese Source

又正見者，能善通達真實法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, right view means being able to skillfully penetrate the true dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Worldly right view names the ordinary capacity to skillfully understand what the Buddha has taught — a first step the treatise now distinguishes from the wisdom of learners and of those beyond learning.

64. Section: The Wisdom of a Learner — Shared with Ordinary Beings, and Belonging Only to the First Three Fruits

The wisdom of a learner means, through proper attention, being able to draw forth a mind well liberated and a wisdom well liberated. And further, in later stages, the wisdom of a learner belongs to the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, and non-returning.

Chinese Source

有學慧者，如理作意，復能引發心善解脫、慧善解脫。

又於後時諸有學慧，謂預流果及一來果、不還果攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wisdom of a learner means, through proper attention, being able to draw forth a mind well liberated and a wisdom well liberated. And further, in later stages, the wisdom of a learner belongs to the fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, and non-returning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Abhidharma

"Learner" is a broader term than it first appears — the Compendium of Abhidharma defines it simply as the wholesome dharma of one still seeking liberation, spanning every practitioner from the stage of accumulation onward, ordinary being and noble one alike. The wisdom of a learner in its earliest form, at the stages of accumulation and preparation, is proper attention that gradually frees mind and wisdom from affliction; in its later, properly noble form, it belongs specifically to the first three fruits — stream-entry, once-returning, and non-returning — genuine but not yet complete.

65. Section: The Wisdom of One Beyond Learning — the Awakening of an Arhat, a Solitary Buddha, or a Fully Awakened Buddha — Closing Section Sixteen

Every wisdom of one beyond learning belongs either to an arhat's own awakening, or to a solitary buddha's own awakening, or to the unsurpassed, most excellent awakening of the tathāgatas.

Chinese Source

諸無學慧，謂阿羅漢菩提所攝，若諸獨覺菩提所攝，若諸如來最勝無上菩提所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every wisdom of one beyond learning belongs either to an arhat's own awakening, or to a solitary buddha's own awakening, or to the unsurpassed, most excellent awakening of the tathāgatas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Where the wisdom of a learner still has further to travel, the wisdom of one beyond learning has arrived — complete in each of the three vehicles' own terms, an arhat's realization the most complete a śrāvaka can reach, a solitary buddha's own independent awakening, and a tathāgata's unsurpassed awakening surpassing them both. With this, the sixteenth section — right view, and the wisdom of learners and of those beyond learning — is closed. The treatise turns next to the wisdom of elements, manifold elements, and more than a single element, promised two sections earlier.

66. Section: Wisdom of the Elements, of More Than a Single Element, and of Manifold Elements — Knowing Cause, the Eighteen Elements, and Every Realm's Own Kind

What is wisdom of the elements? It means being able to know the various elements. Being able to know all eighteen elements is called wisdom of more than a single element. Knowing the manifold kinds within those elements is called wisdom of manifold elements — thoroughly knowing and understanding the distinctions in kind among realms, destinies, grounds, and persons.

Chinese Source

云何界智？謂能了知種種界故。若能了知十八界者，名非一界智。了知彼界種種品類，名種種界智，通達了知彼界、趣、地、補特伽羅品類差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is wisdom of the elements? It means being able to know the various elements. Being able to know all eighteen elements is called wisdom of more than a single element. Knowing the manifold kinds within those elements is called wisdom of manifold elements — thoroughly knowing and understanding the distinctions in kind among realms, destinies, grounds, and persons.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Element" here means cause or condition — a human being, for instance, composed of the elements of earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness; understanding causes in this basic sense is wisdom of the elements. Extending that same understanding across all eighteen elements — the six sense faculties, six objects, and six consciousnesses that together make up the five aggregates — is wisdom of more than a single element. And extending it further still, distinguishing the manifold particular kinds within those causes — the three realms, five destinies, and nine grounds each with their own distinct place, lifespan, food, pleasure and pain, and manner of birth — is wisdom of manifold elements. With this, the seventeenth section is closed. The treatise turns next to subtle and thorough wisdom.

67. Section: Subtle Wisdom and Thorough Wisdom — Entering the Profound Truth, and Fully Realizing Every Meaning

Chinese Source

又微細者，能入真實甚深義故。言審悉者，具能證入一切義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, "subtle" means being able to enter the true, profound meaning. The word "thorough" means being able to fully realize every meaning without exception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground

The Bodhisattva Ground pairs these exactly: subtle wisdom enters what is knowable as it truly is, its unchanging nature; thorough wisdom enters what is knowable in its full extent, every particular content without remainder — the same as-it-is and full-extent pairing met throughout this treatise, now given two further names.

68. Section: Perceptive and Keen — Wisdom Drawn Out Through Practice, and Wisdom Born Together with a Being

言聰明者，謂與引發慧相應故。言叡哲者，謂與俱生慧相應故。

The word "perceptive" means being associated with wisdom drawn out through practice. The word "keen" means being associated with wisdom born together with a being.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Perceptive" describes someone whose sharpness comes from cultivated practice, proper attention still at the stage of an ordinary being; "keen" describes someone whose sharpness is innate, the fruit of wisdom already gathered across past lives and carried forward unbidden.

69. Section: A Second Explanation — Perceptive and Keen, Reversed

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, alternatively, the reverse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second, equally valid reading simply swaps the two: "keen" associated instead with drawn-out wisdom, "perceptive" with innate wisdom — the treatise leaving both pairings open rather than insisting on one alone.

70. Section: The Eye and Knowledge — Grasping What Is Directly Seen, and What Is Not

The "eye" means being able to grasp a matter directly seen. "Knowledge" means being able to grasp a matter not directly seen.

Chinese Source

眼者，能取現見事故。智者，能取不現見事故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The "eye" means being able to grasp a matter directly seen. "Knowledge" means being able to grasp a matter not directly seen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Following the sixteen aspects of the four noble truths: the "eye" names dharma-wisdom, the contaminant-free understanding that directly sees the desire realm's own four truths and thereby cuts off its afflictions of view; "knowledge" names inferential wisdom, understanding the form and formless realms' own four truths by analogy with the desire realm's, and thereby cutting off their afflictions of view in turn.

71. Section: Clarity and Realization — Entering Every Particular Matter, and Every Matter's Shared Nature

Ru-Yi Translation

"Clarity" means entering every matter in its full extent. "Realization" means entering every matter as it truly is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Clarity" names wisdom bright enough to enter the particular characteristics of every dharma — dependent origination, the aggregates, elements, and bases, their full extent taken in; "realization" names wisdom that instead enters their shared, unchanging nature.

The word "conduct of meaning" means comprised within wholesome dharmas accomplished through reflection. "Gentle conduct" means comprised within wholesome dharmas accomplished through cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four final wisdoms, each tied to one source of wholesome accomplishment: conduct of meaning grasps a text's inner meaning through reflection; conduct of dharma grasps its outer letter through hearing; wholesome conduct arises from generosity and discipline together; and gentle conduct — the mind softened and made pliable — arises from cultivation itself. With this, all twenty-two sections and roughly fifty-four synonyms of wisdom are now fully explained, closing "teacher, foremost, and the two wisdoms" — the first topic of this fascicle's opening verse — entirely. The treatise turns next to its second topic: the four kinds of skillful teaching.

73. Section: The Four Kinds of Skillful Teaching Begin — Well Spoken, and Well Realized

Further, "well spoken" means every phrase is perfectly complete. "Well realized" means being able to skillfully manifest the meaning of equal, complete awakening.

Chinese Source

復次，言善說者，謂諸文句善圓滿故。

言善覺者，謂能善現等覺義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, "well spoken" means every phrase is perfectly complete. "Well realized" means being able to skillfully manifest the meaning of equal, complete awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary and the Ground of Thought Cultivation

The verse's second topic, four kinds of skillful teaching, opens with completeness in text and meaning together. Well spoken names text perfected — the Ground of Thought Cultivation gives three marks of genuinely skillful speech: pleasing to hear, free of any taint, and simply good — twelve qualities in all, from being unagitated and gentle to being boundless and inexhaustible. Well realized names meaning perfected — the very meaning of unsurpassed, complete awakening itself skillfully manifested, since only a buddha who has fully realized reality's own ultimate truth can speak it with both text and meaning equally complete.

74. Section: Renunciation and Realizing Awakening — the Fruit of the Worldly Path, and the Fruit of the Transcendent Path — Closing Perfection in Fruit

The word "renunciation" means the worldly path, cutting off manifold suffering and thereby attaining renunciation. "Realizing awakening" means the transcendent path, genuinely and directly realizing awakening in order to transcend manifold suffering.

Chinese Source

言出離者，謂世間道，斷除眾苦得出離故。

趣等覺者，謂出世道，為超眾苦而能真實現等覺故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "renunciation" means the worldly path, cutting off manifold suffering and thereby attaining renunciation. "Realizing awakening" means the transcendent path, genuinely and directly realizing awakening in order to transcend manifold suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second kind of skillful teaching is perfection in fruit: renunciation names the fruit of worldly meditative absorption, freeing a practitioner from desire's own suffering, while realizing awakening names the greater fruit of the transcendent path — the fruits of stream-entry through arhatship, up to unsurpassed awakening itself, directly and truly realized. Whether the fruit taught is worldly or transcendent, both are equally complete. With this, perfection in fruit is closed. The treatise turns next to perfection in conduct.

75. Section: Perfection in Conduct Begins — No Difference Between Teacher and Disciple, and a Stūpa No One Can Shake

"No difference" means the teacher's and the disciple's own text and meaning nourish each other, never contradicting one another. "Having a stūpa" means no outside doctrine, heavenly demon, or other worldly power can shake it.

Chinese Source

無差別者，師與弟子所說文義相滋潤故，不相違故。

有窣堵波者，一切外道、天魔及餘世間不能傾動故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"No difference" means the teacher's and the disciple's own text and meaning nourish each other, never contradicting one another. "Having a stūpa" means no outside doctrine, heavenly demon, or other worldly power can shake it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third kind of skillful teaching, perfection in conduct, opens with two marks: a disciple who faithfully teaches according to the sūtras, discipline, and treatises never contradicts what the great teacher himself taught, each reinforcing the other; and genuine practice of the noble eightfold path stands as firm and unshakable as a stūpa, immovable by any outside doctrine, demonic power, or worldly opinion.

76. Section: Having Reliance — the Four Reliances Complete and Unbroken — Closing Perfection in Conduct

The word "having reliance" means being complete in the four reliances, without fault or breakage.

Chinese Source

言有依者，具足四依，無失壞故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "having reliance" means being complete in the four reliances, without fault or breakage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary and the Elucidation of the Sacred Teaching Treatise

The Buddha's own four reliances complete perfection in conduct: relying on the dharma, not the person, following the teaching itself rather than any particular teacher's standing; relying on the meaning, not the letter, valuing a text's genuine content over decorative language; relying on sūtras of fully drawn-out meaning, not those left unclear, favoring texts that plainly reveal emptiness and dependent origination over those requiring further interpretation; and relying on wisdom, not ordinary consciousness, pursuing direct realization through practice rather than resting content with mere hearing. The first three are what is relied upon; the fourth, wisdom, is what does the relying. Together these four reliances free a practitioner from greed, hatred, and every other fault, keeping conduct genuinely unbroken. With this, perfection in conduct — no difference from the teacher, unshakable as a stūpa, and complete in the four reliances — is closed. The treatise turns next to the fourth and final kind: perfection in the teacher himself.

77. Section: Perfection in the Teacher — Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Awakened — Closing All Four Kinds of Skillful Teaching

The great teacher, Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened — this means the teaching he speaks is skillfully pure.

Chinese Source

大師如來應正等覺者，謂所說教善清淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The great teacher, Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Awakened — this means the teaching he speaks is skillfully pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground

The fourth and final kind of skillful teaching rests entirely on who is teaching: Tathāgata, since his every word is true without exception; Worthy One, since he has realized nirvāṇa and is fit to receive the world's own highest offerings; and Perfectly and Fully Awakened, since he has genuinely awakened to reality's own ultimate truth, both the emptiness of self and of dharmas fully realized. Because the teacher himself is complete in these ways, whatever he teaches is necessarily skillful and pure. With this, all four kinds of skillful teaching — perfection in text and meaning, in fruit, in conduct, and in the teacher — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to a summary of how each phrase demonstrates its own perfection.

78. Section: A Summary — Which Phrases Show Which Perfection — Closing the Four Kinds of Skillful Teaching Entirely

Among these phrases, in brief, four kinds of perfectly skillful teaching of the dharma and discipline are revealed: the first two phrases reveal perfection in text and meaning; the next two reveal perfection in fruit; the next three reveal perfection in conduct; and the last phrase reveals perfection in the teacher.

Chinese Source

此中諸句，略顯四種善說法律最極圓滿。謂初二句顯文義圓滿，次二句顯果圓滿，次三句顯行圓滿，後一句顯師圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these phrases, in brief, four kinds of perfectly skillful teaching of the dharma and discipline are revealed: the first two phrases reveal perfection in text and meaning; the next two reveal perfection in fruit; the next three reveal perfection in conduct; and the last phrase reveals perfection in the teacher.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A tidy accounting closes the topic: well spoken and well realized (two phrases) show text and meaning perfected; renunciation and realizing awakening (two phrases) show the fruit perfected; no difference, having a stūpa, and having reliance (three phrases) show conduct perfected; and the teacher himself, named directly in the last phrase, shows the teacher perfected. Whatever the Buddha teaches, from the eighty-four thousand dharma gates down to a single sentence, is unsurpassed truth precisely because both the one who speaks and what is spoken are equally, completely pure. With this, "the four kinds of skillful teaching," the summary verse's second topic, is closed entirely. The treatise turns next to its third topic: having cause and occasion, and the rest.

79. Section: "Having Cause and Occasion" Begins — Every Training-Ground Established Only Upon an Actual Occasion

Further, as for the Bhagavān's own dharma: having cause and occasion means that a cause always arises, and only then is any training-ground established.

Chinese Source

復次，佛世尊法，有因緣者，謂有緣起，制立一切所學處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, as for the Bhagavān's own dharma: having cause and occasion means that a cause always arises, and only then is any training-ground established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The verse's third topic revisits, on this same term, the five excellences of the true dharma already met in fascicle 82's account of the listener's fifth reason — here presented as their own primary topic. Having cause and occasion means the Buddha's precepts are never issued in the abstract: only after an actual instance of misconduct — the group of six monks' own failings supplying the classic example — does the Buddha lay down the corresponding rule, discipline in particular being the clearest case of this pattern.

80. Section: "Having Leading-Out" — a Lawful Path Back to Purity Once a Violation Has Occurred

"Having leading-out" means that once a violation has occurred, a lawful method is established for leading one back out of it.

Chinese Source

有出離者，謂有犯已，制立如法還出離故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having leading-out" means that once a violation has occurred, a lawful method is established for leading one back out of it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Since discipline exists to benefit beings, the Buddha likewise established a lawful path of confession, so a disciple who has broken a precept still has a genuine way back to purity rather than being left with no recourse at all.

81. Section: "Having What Is Relied Upon" — the Four Reliances That Keep a Practitioner Beyond Every Broken Precept

"Having what is relied upon" means that, through the four reliances, one is established beyond every violation of corrupted discipline.

Chinese Source

言有依者，謂由四依，制立超越一切惡戒諸毀犯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having what is relied upon" means that, through the four reliances, one is established beyond every violation of corrupted discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Here the "four reliances" name something more concrete than the fourfold reliance on dharma over person met earlier — the four basic requisites of monastic life kept deliberately simple: robes (patched, dyed, or gathered from refuse), food (received and eaten mindfully, never for indulgence), bedding (as plain as a seat beneath a tree for the strongest, minimal even for the frail), and medicine (used only when actually needed, humble rather than costly). Living within these simple reliances, a renunciant is thereby established beyond the whole range of corrupted precepts and their violations — a disciple who takes up full ordination on this basis genuinely "has what is relied upon."

82. Section: "Having What Transcends" — Precepts That Steer Clear of Indulgence and Fruitless Austerity Alike

"Having what transcends" means precepts established to keep one free from the extreme of indulging in sensual pleasure and the extreme of self-mortification, according with what actually accomplishes the goal.

Chinese Source

有超越者，制立遠離受用欲樂、自苦行邊，隨順士用令成就故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having what transcends" means precepts established to keep one free from the extreme of indulging in sensual pleasure and the extreme of self-mortification, according with what actually accomplishes the goal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Some outside teachings mistake pleasure itself for nirvāṇa; others mistake self-inflicted suffering — starvation, drowning, fire — for the cure. The Buddha's own precepts transcend both extremes, keeping to the middle path that genuinely prevents wrongdoing and actually accomplishes purity, rather than either indulgence or needless harm.

83. Section: "Having Miraculous Transformation" — Three Kinds, Established So Practitioners Gain Swift Spiritual Mastery — Closing "Having Cause and Occasion" Entirely

"Having miraculous transformation" means that, through three kinds of manifested miraculous transformation, in order to bring about swift spiritual mastery and wisdom, right instruction is uninterruptedly established.

Chinese Source

有神變者，謂由三種所現神變，為令獲得速疾神通，無間制立正教授故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Having miraculous transformation" means that, through three kinds of manifested miraculous transformation, in order to bring about swift spiritual mastery and wisdom, right instruction is uninterruptedly established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground and the Bodhisattva Precept Sūtra

The Buddha once prohibited displaying spiritual powers before the laity, after Piṇḍola-bhāradvāja's own improper display — yet the Bodhisattva Precept Sūtra also names it a fault to withhold spiritual power from someone who genuinely needs to be awed or drawn in by it. The reconciliation lies here: the three miraculous transformations the Śrāvaka Ground describes — of spiritual power, evoking reverence that makes a disciple listen with utmost respect; of mind-reading, discerning another's inner state; and of instruction, teaching exactly as a disciple's own faculties and conduct allow them to enter the path — are permitted precisely because they serve real, uninterrupted instruction toward swift realization, not display for its own sake. With this, "having cause and occasion," together with having leading-out, having what is relied upon, having what transcends, and having miraculous transformation — the summary verse's third topic — is closed entirely. The treatise turns next to its fourth topic: generosity, explained at length.

84. Section: Generosity Explained at Length Begins — Liberating Giving: Dedicated to Nirvāṇa, Unattached to Its Own Fruit

Further, liberating giving means giving dedicated toward nirvāṇa, unattached to the fruit that giving brings.

Chinese Source

復次，解脫捨者，迴向涅槃故，於施果中無繫著故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, liberating giving means giving dedicated toward nirvāṇa, unattached to the fruit that giving brings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

The verse's fourth topic, "giving, discipline, and the path, explained at length," opens with giving. The Śrāvaka Ground defines blameless giving as giving that adorns and supports the mind, aids yogic practice, and aims at the supreme good — body, speech, and mind all pure, joined with freedom from greed, hatred, and delusion, and with diligence. Practiced this way, giving subdues stinginess, provisions calm-abiding and insight, and is dedicated not toward a pleasant rebirth but toward liberation itself — hence "liberating" giving.

85. Section: An Ever-Open Hand, and Joy in Letting Go — Before, During, and After Giving

An ever-open hand means earnest, extensive giving. Joy in letting go means the mind is glad and pure before giving, during giving, and after giving, without any regret.

Chinese Source

常舒手者，殷重廣施故。

樂棄捨者，施前、正施及與施後意悅清淨，無追悔故。

Ru-Yi Translation

An ever-open hand means earnest, extensive giving. Joy in letting go means the mind is glad and pure before giving, during giving, and after giving, without any regret.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An ever-open hand pictures a giver whose generosity is habitual and wholehearted, not grudging or occasional. Joy in letting go names three moments of purity together: gladness anticipating the gift, purity in the act itself — no clinging to a giver, a receiver, or a gift, the three circles empty — and freedom from regret afterward, since regretted giving, even if it still ripens into merit, brings no real enjoyment when that merit finally arrives.

86. Section: Sacrificial Giving — Lawfully Gathered Wealth, Offered Again and Again

Sacrificial giving means giving that is entirely lawful, wealth never gathered through violence, offered again and again, repeatedly and completely, whatever is given.

Chinese Source

祠祀施者，一向如法，不以凶暴積集財物，時時數數周遍捨施所施物故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sacrificial giving means giving that is entirely lawful, wealth never gathered through violence, offered again and again, repeatedly and completely, whatever is given.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Even ritual giving, familiar in ordinary practice, must be lawfully sourced — wealth never gained through cruelty — and offered regularly and fully, like a monastery's own daily offerings, rather than as a one-time gesture.

87. Section: Complete Giving, and Giving Shared Among Family — Offered to a Field of Merit, and Distributed Evenly to Kin

Complete giving means offering to a field of merit. Sharing generosity among family means giving evenly and repeatedly to parents, spouse, and children.

Chinese Source

捨圓滿者，謂於福田而奉獻故。

於惠施中樂分布者，謂於父母、妻子等所，時時平等而分布故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Complete giving means offering to a field of merit. Sharing generosity among family means giving evenly and repeatedly to parents, spouse, and children.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise and the Sūtra on the Field of Merit

Fields of merit are threefold — the field of reverence (the Three Jewels), the field of gratitude (parents and teachers), and the field of compassion (the poor and suffering) — and giving joyfully to any of them, sowing seeds that ripen into merit as a field ripens into harvest, is complete giving. The Sūtra on the Field of Merit names five virtues that make the monastic community itself such a field: renouncing the world for the path, discarding worldly adornment for the robe, cutting off attachment to kin, giving up bodily comfort for the sake of good, and seeking the Mahāyāna in order to liberate others. And giving is complete in a final, humbler sense too — shared evenly and repeatedly among one's own parents, spouse, children, and kin, not withheld from those closest simply because they are familiar.

88. Section: All Six Kinds of Giving Gathered into Three — by Category, by Timing, and by Place — Closing Generosity Explained at Length

All these, in sum, make six kinds of giving: first, unattached giving; second, extensive giving; third, joyful giving; fourth, repeated giving; fifth, giving to a worthy field; sixth, giving that gathers in one's own family. Here, giving is spoken of according to category, timing, and place.

Chinese Source

如是一切總有六施。一、無所依施，二、廣大施，三、歡喜施，四、數數施，五、田器施，六、攝受眷屬施。此中依止品類、時、處布施而說。

Ru-Yi Translation

All these, in sum, make six kinds of giving: first, unattached giving; second, extensive giving; third, joyful giving; fourth, repeated giving; fifth, giving to a worthy field; sixth, giving that gathers in one's own family. Here, giving is spoken of according to category, timing, and place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

The six kinds just explained sort neatly into three pairs: liberating giving and the ever-open hand describe giving's own category; joy in letting go and sacrificial giving describe its timing; and complete giving and giving shared among family describe the place, or field, it reaches. With this, generosity explained at length, the first element of the verse's fourth topic, is closed. The treatise turns next to discipline, explained at length in turn.

89. Section: Discipline Explained at Length Begins — a Middle Verse: Discipline, the Dharma, Killing, and Complete Discipline

Further, discipline explained at length: the middle summary verse says: Discipline, the dharma, killing; complete discipline, and the rest, explained at length.

復次，廣說戒者，中嗢柁南曰：尸羅法殺生，具戒等廣說。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline, the second element of "giving, discipline, and the path, explained at length," unfolds under its own middle-length verse into four sections: distinctions of discipline itself, distinctions of the dharma and discipline as categories, distinctions concerning killing, and distinctions of complete discipline.

90. Section: Distinctions of Discipline Begin — Discipline Itself, and Its Six Traditional Names: Root, Ornament, Fragrant Balm, Incense, Excellent Conduct, and Restraint

The word "discipline" means it is able to still the fever of the offense that comes from violating pure precepts, and further, it accords with the meaning of coolness. The word "restraint" means its own nature is precisely renunciation.

Chinese Source

言尸羅者，謂能寂靜毀犯淨戒罪熱惱故，又與清涼義相應故。言律儀者，謂是遠離自體相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "discipline" means it is able to still the fever of the offense that comes from violating pure precepts, and further, it accords with the meaning of coolness. The word "restraint" means its own nature is precisely renunciation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

The Śrāvaka Ground names discipline six further ways: the root, since it establishes and sustains every worldly and transcendent blameless happiness; an ornament, beautiful on anyone who wears it, in any age; a fragrant balm, cooling the fevered distress that broken precepts bring, as sandalwood cools the body's own heat; an incense, whose fragrance — a practitioner's own good reputation — travels far on the wind; excellent conduct, since its purity leads toward wondrous ease and a wondrous heavenly destiny; and restraint itself, guarding, stilling, and renouncing every unwholesome deed at the root.

91. Section: Completeness and Purity — Freedom from Regret, and the Ground of Concentration

The word "completeness" means rightly receiving discipline, free of regret and the rest. The word "purity" means gathering in the concentration that arises from it, present and manifest.

Chinese Source

言具足者，謂正攝受無悔等故。言清淨者，攝受現行三摩地故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "completeness" means rightly receiving discipline, free of regret and the rest. The word "purity" means gathering in the concentration that arises from it, present and manifest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

The Śrāvaka Ground traces a clear chain: discipline well purified first brings freedom from regret; freedom from regret brings genuine joy and ease; that ease brings the mind to right concentration; and right concentration finally brings true seeing and knowing. "Completeness" names discipline's own gathering-in of regret-free joy; "purity" names its further role as the very ground concentration is built upon.

92. Section: Wholesome and Blameless; Harmless and Accordant — Drawing In a Pleasing Fruit, and Benefiting Both Self and Other

Further, "wholesome" means being able to draw in a pleasing fruit. "Blameless" means being able to draw in benefit for both self and other. Chinese Source

言無害者，謂能違拒執持刀杖鬥諍等事。言隨順者，隨順證得諸沙門果，及餘所有勝功德故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, "wholesome" means being able to draw in a pleasing fruit. "Blameless" means being able to draw in benefit for both self and other. "Harmless" means resisting and rejecting weapons, quarrels, and the like. "Accordant" means according with the realization of the fruits of a renunciant, and every other excellent virtue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline is wholesome because it ripens into a pleasing rebirth, and blameless because it harms no one, self or other. It is harmless because a disciplined practitioner, body and speech restrained, never resorts to weapons or quarrels and so is not attacked in turn; and it is accordant because it grounds the fruits of stream-entry through arhatship, and further excellences such as the concentration free of contention or the concentration of aspiration-knowledge.

93. Section: Concealing and Revealing; Dignity and a Field of Merit — Hiding One's Own Virtue, Confessing One's Own Faults

"Dignity" means being complete with every adornment of a renunciant, such as having few desires. "Field of merit" means gathering in right view, proper conduct, and the perfected virtue of a pure livelihood.

言隱覆者，謂常隱覆自善法故。言顯發者，謂常發露自惡法故。

言端嚴者，謂具攝受諸少欲等所有沙門莊嚴具故。言福田者，攝受正見、軌範、淨命圓滿德故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Concealing" means constantly hiding one's own wholesome dharmas. "Revealing" means constantly disclosing one's own unwholesome dharmas. "Dignity" means being complete with every adornment of a renunciant, such as having few desires. "Field of merit" means gathering in right view, proper conduct, and the perfected virtue of a pure livelihood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A disciplined practitioner hides their own virtue rather than displaying it for praise, content with little, and just as readily discloses their own faults rather than concealing them, correcting course through honest shame. Seventeen further qualities — contentment, diligence, wisdom, and the rest — together make up a renunciant's own natural dignity; and a livelihood free of the five wrong forms of gain, joined to right view and proper conduct, makes such a person a genuine field of merit for others to plant seeds in.

94. Section: Free of Fever, Free of Distress, Free of Regret — Steering Clear of Austerity, Indulgence, and Defiled Remorse — Closing All Seven Distinctions of Discipline

"Free of fever" means rightly keeping free of the extreme of self-mortification. "Free of distress" means keeping free of the extreme of indulging in sensual pleasure. "Free of regret" means rightly keeping free of defiled, joyless, sorrowful matters.

Chinese Source

言無熱者，謂正遠離自苦邊故。言無惱者，遠離受用欲樂邊故。言無悔者，謂正遠離染汙、不樂、憂慼事故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Free of fever" means rightly keeping free of the extreme of self-mortification. "Free of distress" means keeping free of the extreme of indulging in sensual pleasure. "Free of regret" means rightly keeping free of defiled, joyless, sorrowful matters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Abhidharmakośa

The Abhidharmakośa lists the pointless austerities of various outside ascetics — going naked, tearing out one's own hair, carrying staffs wrapped in animal hide, smearing ashes, and the like — all of which discipline kept purely simply has no need for, hence "free of fever." Freedom from craving material comfort while still using what is genuinely needed, without attachment, is "free of distress." And never committing grave wrongs, never behaving deceitfully, and never breaking pure conduct leaves nothing to regret — hence "free of regret." With this, all seven distinctions of discipline — discipline itself, completeness and purity, wholesome and blameless, harmless and accordant, concealing and revealing, dignity and field of merit, and freedom from fever, distress, and regret — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to distinctions of the dharma and discipline as categories.

95. Section: Distinctions of the Dharma Begin — the Dharma and the Discipline: Comprised of Principle, Sustaining Excellence, and Stilling Every Affliction

Further, the well-taught dharma is comprised of principle, and sustains excellent virtue. The discipline accords with the cessation of every affliction.

Chinese Source

復次，善說法者，道理所攝故，任持勝德故。毗奈耶者，隨順一切煩惱滅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the well-taught dharma is comprised of principle, and sustains excellent virtue. The discipline accords with the cessation of every affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases

Though "dharma" broadly spans all three trainings, the sūtras typically reserve "discipline" (Vinaya) for the training-grounds specifically, leaving "dharma" here to mean concentration and wisdom — comprised of vast principle and sustaining excellent virtue. The Compendium of Bases names four marks of a truly well-taught teaching: it leads to stillness, nirvāṇa with residue; it leads to final nirvāṇa, without residue; it leads to awakening, the unsurpassed complete awakening of śrāvakas and solitary buddhas alike; and it is well-gone discernment, taught by the unsurpassed great teacher's own direct, ultimate realization. "Discipline" (Vinaya), also translated "cessation," names the precepts that subdue and extinguish affliction.

96. Section: Noble and Wholesome — Freedom from Every Defilement, and a Blameless, Pleasing Fruit

The conduct of wisdom means the superior discrimination gained through receiving and retaining, reading and reciting, questioning and discussing, and supreme discernment, and the ever-clearer understanding of that meaning brought about through diligent cultivation of wisdom. Insight means carefully observing this very meaning already discerned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The conduct of wisdom traces the same three wisdoms met throughout this treatise: receiving, reciting, and discussing belong to hearing; supreme discernment — determining a dharma's genuine, ultimate meaning — belongs to reflection; and understanding growing ever clearer through diligent practice points toward cultivation still to come, since only continued calm-abiding and insight bring genuine liberation. Insight itself then names that final observation — grounded in at least access concentration, carefully examining what hearing and reflection have already discerned until real conviction in the dharma is won. With this, all four aspects of right view — astuteness and penetration, careful examination and keen insight, awakened discernment and cultivated clarity, and the conduct of wisdom together with insight — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to right intention.

121. Section: Right Intention Begins — Engagement and Absorption: the Mind Entering Its Object, and Fully Receiving It

Engagement means first investigating, the mind entering into and applying attention toward its object through reflection. Absorption means being able to receive that very object fully.

Chinese Source

涉入者，謂先尋思，於所緣境作意思惟心涉入故。納受者，謂即於彼能攝受故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Engagement means first investigating, the mind entering into and applying attention toward its object through reflection. Absorption means being able to receive that very object fully.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Engagement names the application of resolve — investigating, say, the body's impurity, and letting the mind actually settle into that object without wandering. Absorption then names the fuller reception of both its particular and shared character, spanning the successive applications from withdrawal through the very fruit of that culmination.

122. Section: Investigation, Deeper Investigation, and Deepest Investigation — Grasping Overall Marks or the Finest Details, and Coarse or Subtle Reflection

Investigation means grasping a dharma's own marks. Deeper investigation means grasping their finer, subsidiary features. There is a further distinction: investigation means a searching mind; deeper investigation means a scrutinizing mind.

Chinese Source

推尋者，謂取彼諸相故。極推尋者，謂取彼隨好故。

復有差別。推尋者，謂尋求心。極推尋者，謂伺察心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Investigation means grasping a dharma's own marks. Deeper investigation means grasping their finer, subsidiary features. There is a further distinction: investigation means a searching mind; deeper investigation means a scrutinizing mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Two explanations of the same pair. First, by scope: investigation grasps the overall marks — the Buddha's thirty-two major marks, say — while deeper investigation grasps the finer eighty subsidiary excellences within them. Second, by depth of reflection: investigation is the coarser searching mind, noting impermanence in the passing of seasons or the blooming and fading of flowers; deeper investigation is the finer scrutinizing mind, tracking that same impermanence moment to moment, even instant to instant.

123. Section: Deepest Investigation, and Investigation, Reflection, and Discrimination Grounded in Scripture, Direct Perception, and Inference — Closing Right Intention Entirely

Deepest investigation means thoroughly calculating and investigating gain and loss to the utmost degree. Relying on the sacred teaching and giving rise to a search is called investigation; relying on direct perception is called reflection; relying on inference is called discrimination.

Chinese Source

最極推尋者，謂於得失推構尋思極校計故。

聖教為依而起尋求，說名尋思；現量為依，說名思惟；比量為依，說名分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Deepest investigation means thoroughly calculating and investigating gain and loss to the utmost degree.

Relying on the sacred teaching and giving rise to a search is called investigation; relying on direct perception is called reflection; relying on inference is called discrimination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Deepest investigation carries the same scrutiny into calculating gain and loss with the utmost thoroughness, per the Śrāvaka Ground's own sixfold analysis of insight's object. A further threefold distinction then sorts reflection itself by its ground: investigation grounded in scriptural authority alone, as when contemplating the fifteen inner and sixteen outer forms of impermanence the Śrāvaka Ground names; reflection grounded in direct perception — here meaning the purified inner perception attained through worldly concentration, actually observing a given quality arise and pass; and discrimination grounded in inference, reasoning through the impermanence of the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements by logical necessity. With this, right intention — engagement and absorption, investigation at three depths, and its threefold grounding — is closed in full. The treatise turns next to right speech, right action, and right livelihood together.

124. Section: Right Speech, Action, and Livelihood Begin — Renunciation, Reception, Sustained Guarding, and Returning to Purity

Sustained guarding means, having received it, being able to continuously protect that discipline. Returning to purity means, having mistakenly violated it, being able to lawfully confess and remove the fault.

Chinese Source

厭離者，增上意樂，於遠離中起決定故。遠離者，謂從他邊受遠離故。隨離者，謂受已後，能隨守護彼尸羅故。還離者，謂誤犯已，即能如法而悔除故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Renunciation means a superior aspiration that settles decisively on renunciation. Reception means receiving renunciation from another. Sustained guarding means, having received it, being able to continuously protect that discipline. Returning to purity means, having mistakenly violated it, being able to lawfully confess and remove the fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Right speech, action, and livelihood — the discipline-factors of the eightfold path — unfold across five sections, tracing a practitioner's whole relationship to discipline from beginning to end: first the wholehearted decision to renounce wrongdoing, then actually receiving the vow from a preceptor, then guarding it continuously once received, and finally, should a lapse occur despite every care, confessing and returning to purity rather than concealing the fault.

125. Section: Stillness, Restraint, and Closely Guarding the Sense Gateways — Patience, Contentment, and a Discipline That Becomes Second Nature

From this point onward: stillness, restraint, and guarding discipline through sustained protection. Stillness means being complete with patience and a gentle disposition. Restraint means being complete with few desires, loving-kindness, and the rest. Closely guarding the sense gateways means wrongdoing no longer arises even spontaneously.

Chinese Source

從此已後，寂止律儀隨護尸羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point onward: stillness, restraint, and guarding discipline through sustained protection. Stillness means being complete with patience and a gentle disposition. Restraint means being complete with few desires, loving-kindness, and the rest. Closely guarding the sense gateways means wrongdoing no longer arises even spontaneously.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three further stages describe discipline maturing in community life: stillness arises from the patience and gentleness that let disagreements with fellow practitioners simply pass without friction; restraint arises from genuine contentment with little — never seeking recognition for one's own virtue — joined with a compassionate spirit toward the precepts themselves; and closely guarding the sense gateways, sustained long enough, makes right conduct effortless, wrongdoing no longer even tempting.

126. Section: Not Committing, Not Practicing, and Not Violating — a Vow Kept from the Start, Understanding That Prevents Wrong, and Mindfulness That Prevents Forgetting

"Not committing" means it is simply not committed, on another's authority. "Not practicing" means, through correct understanding, it does not manifest. "Not violating" means it does not manifest through any lapse of memory.

Chinese Source

不作者，由他不作故。

不行者，由正了知不現行故。不犯者，不由失念而現行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Not committing" means it is simply not committed, on another's authority. "Not practicing" means, through correct understanding, it does not manifest. "Not violating" means it does not manifest through any lapse of memory.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three further guarantees against wrongdoing: the vow itself, taken from a preceptor, already commits body, speech, and mind against it; correct understanding of what is permitted and what is not keeps it from ever manifesting; and sustained mindfulness — guarding attention so carefully that forgetfulness has no opening — keeps it from manifesting even by accident.

127. Section: A Bridge, and a Raft — Crossing Over Wrongdoing, and Ferrying the Deluded Across What Obstructs the Path

A bridge means relying on this to cross over wrongdoing. A raft means, relying on this remedy, vowing to carry the deluded and path-lost across whatever dharma obstructs and opposes the path.

Chinese Source

橋梁者，由此為依，渡惡法故。船筏者，謂依對治，誓能運彼癡狂失道，令渡相違障礙法故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bridge means relying on this to cross over wrongdoing. A raft means, relying on this remedy, vowing to carry the deluded and path-lost across whatever dharma obstructs and opposes the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline pictured as a bridge spans the flood of wrongdoing directly beneath it; pictured as a raft on the great river of birth-and-death, it carries even the most confused, path-lost being across whatever blocks the way, all the way to the far shore of nirvāṇa.

128. Section: Utterly Content, Never Transgressing, Never the Slightest Breach — Closing Right Speech, Action, and Livelihood Entirely

"Never the slightest breach" means not the smallest portion is compromised, and none of it is abandoned.

Chinese Source

"Utterly content" means the superior aspiration toward renunciation is completely fulfilled. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Three final marks describe discipline held to completion: genuine, full satisfaction in renunciation itself; unwavering fidelity even to the point of risking one's life rather than breaking a precept; and completeness down to the smallest detail, not even a minor rule left unobserved. With this, right speech, right action, and right livelihood — renunciation and reception, sustained guarding, three further safeguards, the bridge and the raft, and complete fidelity — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to right mindfulness.

129. Section: Right Mindfulness — Settling the Mind, and Settling It Evenly, Matched to the Nine Stages of Mental Abiding — Closing the Path-Factors, the Path Explained at Length, and the Entire First Verse

The word "mindfulness" means settling the mind. "Even mindfulness" means settling the mind evenly. Explained at length in this way, one should know its marks match the distinctions of the nine stages of mental abiding, exactly as in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Chinese Source

所言念者，謂住其心故。言等念者，謂等住其心故。

如是廣說，應隨九種心住差別，如聲聞地當知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "mindfulness" means settling the mind. "Even mindfulness" means settling the mind evenly.

Explained at length in this way, one should know its marks match the distinctions of the nine stages of mental abiding, exactly as in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Right mindfulness, the concentration-factor examined here, begins with inward settling — drawing the mind back from sound, sight, and every other outward object onto its own single object, such as the breath — and even settling, sustaining that same attention continuously rather than only at the first moment. The 披尋記 lists nine names for mindfulness altogether — mindfulness, even mindfulness, recollection, particular recollection, unforgetting mindfulness, clear mental recording, freedom from loss, freedom from forgetting, and the dharma of no loss — matched one to one against the nine stages of mental abiding: inward settling, even settling, repeated settling, close settling, taming, stilling, utmost stilling, single-pointed focus, and full absorption. With this, right mindfulness closes the path-factors entirely — right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, and right mindfulness together spanning the eightfold path — and with them, the path explained at length, and with that, giving, discipline, and the path together, close the entire first verse of the wholesome portion: teacher, foremost, the two wisdoms, the four kinds of skillful teaching, having cause and occasion, and giving, discipline, and the path all explained at length. The treatise turns next to the second verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having closed the first verse's eight great topics in full, the treatise now opens the wholesome portion's second verse, previewing a fresh set of terms to be unfolded in turn: wisdom and proclaiming the dharma; wholesome aspiration; the blazing energy of diligence, solitude, and freedom from the dust of affliction; a set of comparisons — the five aggregates likened to sickness and the rest; the many senses in which "I" is spoken of, and cessation, exhaustion, and their kin; the heavens, the world, and sentient beings as further categories; reliance and its own distinctions; and finally the self and what belongs to the self, closing the verse.

131. Section: Wisdom Begins — Knowledge, Sight, Awareness, and Cognizance: Four Wisdoms Preceding the Four Modes of Speech

"Knowledge" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in hearing. "Sight" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in seeing. "Awareness" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in reflective awareness. "Cognizance" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in direct cognizance.

Chinese Source

智者，謂聞言說為先慧。見者，謂見言說為先慧。覺者，謂覺言說為先慧。知者，謂知言說為先慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Knowledge" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in hearing. "Sight" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in seeing. "Awareness" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in reflective awareness. "Cognizance" means wisdom preceded by speech grounded in direct cognizance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Basic Section's Ground of Mind

The verse's second topic, wisdom, opens with four wisdoms matched to the four grounds from which speech itself arises: speech grounded in seeing, reporting what the eye has directly witnessed; speech grounded in hearing, reporting what was heard from another; speech grounded in awareness, reporting one's own private reflection, neither seen nor heard; and speech grounded in cognizance, reporting what was inwardly felt, realized, touched, or attained. A person's wisdom shows itself precisely through the speech these four grounds give rise to — someone who observes the seasons turning or flowers blooming and fading may awaken insight into impermanence through sight alone; someone who tastes, touches, or smells directly awakens it through the three sense-consciousnesses paired always with the fifth, accompanying mental consciousness, called cognizance here.

132. Section: Knowledge and Sight — Grasping What Is Not Directly Present, and Grasping What Is

"Knowledge" means knowing an object not directly present. "Sight" means seeing an object directly present before one.

Chinese Source

智者，謂知不現見境。見者，謂見現見現在前境。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Knowledge" means knowing an object not directly present. "Sight" means seeing an object directly present before one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second pairing of the same two terms: knowledge here reaches toward the past and future, not directly witnessed — corresponding to inferential wisdom of a kind; sight reaches only toward what stands directly before the mind right now — corresponding to direct dharma-wisdom of the desire realm's own truths.

133. Section: Clarity, Realization, and Realized Wisdom — the Opposite of Ignorance, Reality Itself, and Wisdom That Adds Nothing Unreal

"Clarity" means understanding directly opposed to ignorance. "Realization" means the truly existent meaning itself. "Realized wisdom" means wisdom that does not superimpose what is not truly existent.

Chinese Source

明者，謂無明相違解。覺者，謂實有義。覺智者，謂不增益非實有智。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Clarity" means understanding directly opposed to ignorance. "Realization" means the truly existent meaning itself. "Realized wisdom" means wisdom that does not superimpose what is not truly existent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Ground and the Ground of Mind

Clarity is contaminant-free wisdom's own direct opposition to ignorance, whether innate or acquired through wrong views. Realization takes as its object reality's own two aspects — a dharma's unchanging nature, and its full particular extent — named the Bodhisattva Ground's "truly existent meaning." And realized wisdom neither adds what is not truly there (the four distortions: mistaking the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, the impure for pure, and the selfless for a self) nor subtracts what genuinely is there (as wrong views that deny giving and its fruit do) — wisdom exactly matched to reality, neither embellishing nor denying it.

134. Section: Wisdom, Clarity, and Direct Realization — Innate Wisdom, Wisdom Cultivated Through Practice, and Wisdom Neither Unseen Nor Borrowed from Another — Closing All Twelve Angles on Wisdom

"Wisdom" means wisdom born together with a being. "Clarity" means wisdom accomplished through practice and habituation. "Direct realization" means, having already directly realized the dharma within, being neither un-witnessing nor dependent on another's wisdom regarding any dharma.

Chinese Source

慧者，謂俱生生得慧。明者，謂由加行習所成慧。現觀者，謂於內現觀法已，於諸法中非不現見、非緣他智。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Wisdom" means wisdom born together with a being. "Clarity" means wisdom accomplished through practice and habituation. "Direct realization" means, having already directly realized the dharma within, being neither un-witnessing nor dependent on another's wisdom regarding any dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Innate wisdom is simply born with a being; clarity is instead earned through cultivated practice; and direct realization surpasses both, naming the contaminant-free wisdom of one who has genuinely realized the path — witnessing reality directly rather than through inference, and knowing it from one's own inner certainty rather than borrowing another's report. With this, all twelve angles on wisdom — knowledge, sight, awareness, and cognizance; knowledge and sight again; clarity, realization, and realized wisdom; and wisdom, clarity, and direct realization — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to proclaiming the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 81's own gloss of "intent"

Four stages of a single teaching act: proclaiming answers a listener's own question; establishing arranges the actual words in proper sequence — "intent" here meaning simply the words themselves, as fascicle 81 already explained, the very path by which a teaching gets set down; setting forth delivers that arrangement briefly; and unpacking follows with the fuller explanation, drawing out whatever deeper intent the brief statement left implicit.

136. Section: Disclosing and Revealing — Dispelling Doubts Passed Down Through Others, and Showing What One Has Personally Penetrated

137. Section: Instructing Unprompted, and Disclosing Without Reserve — Compassion That Speaks Before Being Asked, and Truth Withheld from No One — Closing Proclaiming Entirely

A teacher moved by compassion sometimes speaks before being asked — observing in meditation, say, that a disciple's uncertain practice is actually correct, and offering encouragement unprompted. And a genuinely complete teacher holds nothing back, unlike a closed fist concealing something in reserve, laying out the deepest truths of emptiness and no-self fully and continuously. With this, proclaiming the dharma — its four stages, disclosing and revealing, and instructing without reserve — is closed. The treatise turns next to wholesomeness.

140. Section: Singular, Complete, Pure, and Bright — Shared with No Outside Doctrine, Boundless, Intrinsically Free, and Free Through Practice — Closing Wholesomeness's First Four Marks

"Singular" means it is shared with no outside doctrine at all. "Complete" means it is without limit or measure, and most excellent of all. "Pure" means it is intrinsically liberated. "Bright" means it is liberated through the practice sustained moment to moment.

Chinese Source

純一者，謂不與一切外道共故。圓滿者，謂無限量故，最尊勝故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Singular" means it is shared with no outside doctrine at all. "Complete" means it is without limit or measure, and most excellent of all. "Pure" means it is intrinsically liberated. "Bright" means it is liberated through the practice sustained moment to moment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of the Mahāyāna

Singular because the dharma shares nothing with outside teachings; complete because its scope is boundless and it stands supreme, benefiting both self and other without limit. Pure and bright then trace back to the Compendium of the Mahāyāna's own two purities: intrinsic purity — every dharma's own nature already suchness, emptiness, reality's limit, signlessness, ultimate truth, the dharma-realm itself, however deeply afflictions may seem to obscure it, since even affliction is, at bottom, empty — and purity from defilement, gained only by realizing that same intrinsic purity and thereby freeing the mind of every obscuring trace. Purity in this sense is called "intrinsic liberation," the self-nature nirvāṇa always already present; brightness is called "liberation sustained through practice," the nirvāṇa with residue, without residue, or beyond all abiding, actually won through cultivation.

With this, well-spoken dharma — good in beginning, middle, and end; wondrous meaning and skillful text; singular, complete, pure, and bright; and pure conduct itself — is closed. 142. Section: Well-Heard Dharma — Careful Listening, and Earnest, Undistorted, Uninterrupted Reflection — Closing Wholesomeness Entirely

"Careful listening" means being urged to attentively hear such marked dharmas. "Genuinely earnest" means being urged to reflect on them properly, without distortion, without interruption, and with due seriousness.

Chinese Source

諦聽者，謂於如是相法勸令審聽。

應善懇到者，謂勸令無倒、無間、殷重如理思惟。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Careful listening" means being urged to attentively hear such marked dharmas. "Genuinely earnest" means being urged to reflect on them properly, without distortion, without interruption, and with due seriousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Just as the Buddha's own teaching is well spoken, a disciple's listening should be equally well done — attending carefully at the moment of hearing, then reflecting on what was heard without distortion, without gaps, and in earnest. With this, wholesomeness — well-spoken dharma on the teacher's side, and well-heard dharma on the listener's — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to aspiration, the verse's fourth topic.

143. Section: Aspiration Begins — Intense Aspiration: Longing for What an Arhat Has Fully Attained

Further, intense aspiration means: when shall I, in that very place, and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

復次，猛利欲者，謂我何當於彼處所，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, intense aspiration means: when shall I, in that very place, and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Ground of Cultivation

The Ground of Cultivation pictures a stream-enterer already tasting vast dharma-joy, longing intensely for arhatship still ahead — wondering, again and again, when they too might fully abide in that noble state as an arhat does, the three knowledges and six spiritual powers complete, every affliction of the three realms exhausted, never to be reborn again. Without such longing driving continued effort, a practitioner easily slackens, treating the goal as optional rather than urgent.

144. Section: Intense Fondness — Genuine Delight in One's Own Actual Practice

Intense fondness means, with respect to the right application one actually cultivates.

Chinese Source

猛利愛者，謂於所修正加行中。

Ru-Yi Translation

Intense fondness means, with respect to the right application one actually cultivates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

Where intense aspiration is the wish, intense fondness is genuine delight in the actual doing — the Śrāvaka Ground's own "right application," repeatedly forming resolve toward an object and repeatedly clearing away what obstructs it, insight and calm-abiding alternating in proper practice, and a practitioner who truly loves this work rather than merely tolerating it.

145. Section: Intense Joy and Intense Faith — Delighting in Those Worthy of Reverence, and Trusting the Teaching Completely — Closing Aspiration Entirely

Intense joy means, toward the teacher and the great teacher's own venerable place, and the like. Intense faith means, toward the teaching, its instruction, and its guidance.

Chinese Source

猛利樂者，謂於說者及與大師尊重處等。

猛利信者，謂於教法教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Intense joy means, toward the teacher and the great teacher's own venerable place, and the like. Intense faith means, toward the teaching, its instruction, and its guidance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Intense joy delights in whoever is worthy of reverence — buddhas, bodhisattvas, the noble saṅgha, and every elder teacher — and in what they have taught or accomplished; intense faith trusts, without a trace of doubt, the instruction and guidance such teachers offer, since that teaching ultimately traces back to the Buddha himself. With this, all four marks of aspiration — intense longing, intense fondness for practice, intense joy in what is worthy of reverence, and intense faith in the teaching — are closed. The treatise turns next to blazing diligence.

146. Section: Blazing Diligence Begins — Never Postponing, Diligently Pressing Toward Swift Spiritual Mastery

Further, blazing diligence means, in order to realize swift spiritual mastery and wisdom, never granting oneself idle leisure or postponing to some later time, but arousing diligent effort.

Chinese Source

復次，能熾然者，謂為證得速疾通慧，終不自暇，推延後期，發勤精進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, blazing diligence means, in order to realize swift spiritual mastery and wisdom, never granting oneself idle leisure or postponing to some later time, but arousing diligent effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A genuinely urgent practitioner never tells themselves "tomorrow" — constant awareness of impermanence keeps effort blazing rather than deferred, unlike the deliberate, well-earned rest that supports a longer journey.

147. Section: According with Yoga — Matching or Exceeding, Never Falling Short of What Is Taught

According with yoga means following one's venerable teaching, cultivating application equal to it or surpassing it, never once falling short.

Chinese Source

順瑜伽者，謂隨尊教若等、若勝而修加行，終不減劣。

Ru-Yi Translation

According with yoga means following one's venerable teaching, cultivating application equal to it or surpassing it, never once falling short.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever the practice taught — the six perfections, the four noble truths — a diligent practitioner's actual effort should match or exceed what was asked, never quietly settling for less.

148. Section: Permanent Severance, and Solitary Dwelling — Cultivating Affliction's Remedy, and Retreating to Bring Concentration and Insight to Bear — Closing Blazing Diligence Entirely

Permanent severance means being able to cultivate the remedy for affliction. Solitary dwelling means, relying on the barest, simplest bedding, dwelling apart in seclusion, bringing concentration into present manifestation, and, relying on that concentration, cultivating the remedy.

Chinese Source

能永斷者，謂能修習煩惱對治。

能閑居者，謂依所有邊際臥具遠離而居，修三摩地令現在前，依三摩地修習對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

Permanent severance means being able to cultivate the remedy for affliction. Solitary dwelling means, relying on the barest, simplest bedding, dwelling apart in seclusion, bringing concentration into present manifestation, and, relying on that concentration, cultivating the remedy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Repeated cultivation of affliction's remedy eventually cuts off not just its active manifestation but its very seeds, permanently. And solitary dwelling — the barest bedding, both body and mind withdrawn from distraction — is the practical setting that makes this possible: concentration actually established, and insight then applied on that foundation to uproot affliction at its root. With this, all four marks of blazing diligence — never postponing, according with or exceeding the teaching, permanently severing affliction, and dwelling in solitude to make this possible — are closed. The treatise turns next to solitude itself, the verse's next topic.

149. Section: Solitude Begins — Dwelling with the Barest Possessions, Alone, Without a Second

Further, solitude means dwelling in a place of retreat with only the barest bedding, abiding without a second companion.

Chinese Source

復次，獨者，謂處遠離邊際臥具，無有第二而安住故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, solitude means dwelling in a place of retreat with only the barest bedding, abiding without a second companion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Solitude here means outward simplicity taken to its limit — a single set of robes, no spare belongings, no one else sharing the dwelling — the physical circumstance that supports genuine retreat.

150. Section: Renunciation Itself — a Mind Where Defiled and Neutral Attention No Longer Arise at All

The word "renunciation" means that defiled and indeterminate attention no longer manifest.

Chinese Source

言遠離者，謂諸染汙、無記作意不現行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The word "renunciation" means that defiled and indeterminate attention no longer manifest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where solitude named the outward circumstance, renunciation names the corresponding inward state — a mind touched by neither greed, hatred, nor delusion, and free even of the dull, uncomprehending neutrality that accompanies delusion, only wholesome attention remaining.

151. Section: Freedom from Indulgence — Guarding the Mind Against Intrusive Thought, and Settling It Securely in the Wholesome

Freedom from indulgence means guarding the mind against unwholesome investigative thoughts such as desire, and further, settling oneself securely within what is wholesome.

Chinese Source

無縱逸者，謂於欲等尋思惡法防護心故，又於善中自安處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Freedom from indulgence means guarding the mind against unwholesome investigative thoughts such as desire, and further, settling oneself securely within what is wholesome.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Constant inward watching catches desire, ill will, or harmful intent — and their subtler cousins, thoughts of homeland, of immortality, of family status, of contempt for others — the instant they arise, checking them before they take hold; and that same vigilance actively settles the mind in hearing, reflection, cultivation, or the three trainings instead, rather than merely suppressing what is unwholesome.
