Fascicle 58 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十八 · Deciding the Three Stages of Examination and Reflection Begins

Why King Yama Is Called the Dharma King

Having fully determined all six kinds of skillful understanding across seven fascicles, the treatise opens an entirely new topic — deciding discernment for the three stages of examination and reflection — beginning with hell-beings' own basis, and the Buddha's own surprising answer to why King Yama, lord of the underworld, deserves to be called a benefactor rather than a tormentor.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 58 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Topic Opens — Deciding the Three Stages of Examination and Reflection

With the skillful understanding of the aggregates, the elements, the sense-fields, dependent origination, the valid and the invalid, and the faculties all now fully determined across the preceding seven fascicles, the treatise turns to an entirely new topic within this same chapter of deciding discernment: the stage possessing both examination and reflection, the stage free of examination yet still possessing reflection, and the stage free of both — three ways of classifying the entire universe of beings according to the coarseness or refinement of their own mental activity, the first spanning the desire realm together with the first meditative absorption, the second spanning the intermediate meditative state between the first and second absorptions, associated with the great Brahmā realm, and the third spanning everything from the second absorption upward, including the third and fourth absorptions and the four formless attainments beyond them. Before formally opening this new topic, the treatise first addresses two matters left incompletely covered by an earlier discussion of these same three realms — the specific basis belonging to beings born in the hells, and the specific basis belonging to animals — devoting only a brief stretch of text to these two matters before turning to a far more extensive treatment of three kinds of defilement that will occupy most of this fascicle and the three that follow: the defilement of affliction, the defilement of karma, and the defilement of rebirth itself, examined here with a thoroughness this treatise has not yet applied to these particular topics anywhere else.

Chinese Source

如是已說五識身相應地、意地決擇。有尋有伺等三地決擇，我今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the deciding discernment of the stage associated with the five sense-consciousness-bodies and the stage of mind has been explained. The deciding discernment of the stages of examination and reflection shall now be explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this fascicle opens a genuinely new phase of the treatise's own chapter of deciding discernment, moving from the six kinds of skillful understanding just completed toward a different organizing framework entirely — sorting the whole of existence by the coarseness or subtlety of examination and reflection, the very same threefold classification this treatise used many fascicles earlier in its own foundational account of the three realms. He highlights that this fascicle's own most substantial contribution will not be its brief opening remarks on hell-beings and animals, but the extensive, systematic treatment of the three defilements — affliction, karma, and rebirth — that begins here and continues across the next three fascicles, promising a level of detail on these three topics beyond anything offered elsewhere in this treatise.

2. Section: Not a Tormentor But a Teacher — Why King Yama Deserves the Name "Dharma King"

The treatise opens its brief discussion of hell-beings' own basis with a striking question: why is King Yama called the Dharma King — is it because he harms beings, or because he benefits them? If it were because he harms them, the treatise objects, that title would make no sense at all; if instead it is because he benefits them, an explanation of exactly how he does so is called for. The answer is unambiguous: it is through benefit, not harm, that King Yama earns this title. When beings are first dragged before him, he causes an image resembling whatever being they once harmed to appear before them, precisely in order to jog their own memory, and tells them plainly that they are simply receiving the fruit of actions they themselves once committed. Through this very reminder, such beings come to understand for themselves that this suffering is the direct result of their own past deeds, ripening now through their own accumulated karmic force — and, understanding this, they no longer regard King Yama's own attendants, who are not truly independent sentient beings at all but simply a karmic manifestation conjured by the collective force of the tormented beings' own past actions, with any thought of retaliation, any anger, or any lingering resentment whatsoever. And it is precisely because of this shift — ceasing to generate fresh hatred toward what are, in truth, illusory apparitions rather than real persons — that such beings stop accumulating any further karma capable of prolonging their stay in hell, so that once their existing karma has finally run its course, they are genuinely released from that realm for good.

Chinese Source

問：何故焰摩名為法王？為能損害諸眾生故？為能饒益諸眾生故？若由損害眾生，名為法王，不應道理。若由饒益眾生，今應當說云何饒益？答：由能饒益，不由損害。何以故？若諸眾生執到王所，令憶念故，遂為現彼相似之身。告言：汝等自所作業，當受其果。由是因緣，彼諸眾生各自了知，自所作業，還自受果。便於焰摩使者，眾生業力增上所生，猶如變化，非眾生所，無反害心、無瞋恚心，不懷怨恨。乃由此故，感那落迦新業更不積集，故業盡已，脫那落迦趣。是故焰摩由能饒益諸眾生故，名為法王。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: why is Yama called the Dharma King — because he harms beings, or because he benefits them? If because he harms them, this title makes no sense; if because he benefits them, how does he benefit them? Answer: through benefit, not through harm. Why? When beings are dragged before him, in order to help them remember, he causes an image resembling the being they harmed to appear, telling them: you are receiving the fruit of what you yourselves have done. Through this cause, such beings each come to understand that they are receiving the fruit of their own actions, and toward Yama's attendants — a manifestation produced by the increasing force of beings' own karma, like a magical transformation, not truly sentient beings — they give rise to no thought of retaliation, no hatred, and no lingering resentment. Precisely because of this, they cease accumulating any new karma capable of drawing further hellish rebirth, and once their existing karma is exhausted, they are released from the hell realms. It is therefore because he benefits beings that Yama is called the Dharma King.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this reframing of King Yama as one of this fascicle's own most striking opening images: a figure ordinarily imagined purely as a fearsome judge of the dead is here revealed instead as something closer to a compassionate teacher, deliberately engineering a moment of genuine self-recognition precisely so that suffering beings can stop making their own situation worse. He highlights the psychological precision of the mechanism described — recognizing one's own suffering as the fruit of one's own past action defuses the very anger that would otherwise generate fresh karma and prolong that suffering indefinitely — as a teaching with real application well beyond the hell realms themselves, the same principle underlying any suffering compounded by resentment rather than genuine acceptance. He notes the scholarly debate, cited at length in the surrounding commentary, over whether hell's own attendants are genuinely sentient beings or purely karmic apparitions, as this tradition's own careful insistence that not even hell's most fearsome figures are exempted from its rigorous analysis of what is, and is not, ultimately real.

3. Section: Three Kinds of Forgetting, and Why the Ocean Tastes of Salt

The treatise names three kinds of being who, upon arriving in hell, do not spontaneously remember their own past lives, and for whom King Yama's own intervention therefore becomes genuinely necessary. The first is extreme ignorance: someone born in a remote borderland utterly lacking any exposure to genuine teaching, unable to reflect on or investigate anything at all, simply following whatever wrongdoing happens to be modeled around them. The second is extreme indulgence: someone thoroughly given over to sensory pleasure, so powerfully absorbed in craving that no reflection or investigation ever interrupts that same indulgence, following the wrongdoing of like-minded companions without question. And the third is extreme wrong view: someone who has adopted a comprehensive, confidently held set of views actively denying cause and effect altogether, again incapable of genuine reflection, and so likewise following others into wrongdoing. Since none of these three types can naturally recall their own past karma unaided, King Yama specifically helps them remember. The treatise then turns briefly to animals' own basis, opening with a question many students have likely wondered about: why is the ocean's water salty? Two causes are named. First, the very saltiness of the water itself keeps human beings from wading or dwelling within it, thereby protecting the countless minute creatures living there from being caught and killed — this saltiness itself being the fruit of those sea-dwelling beings' own collective merit. Second, the ocean also contains many kinds of genuine treasure, yet its salt water makes reaching that treasure exceptionally difficult for land-dwelling beings, this same difficulty being the fruit of land-dwellers' own partial demerit. The lecture supplements this with three further explanations offered in the Dīrgha Āgama: that clouds periodically rain down over the entire universe, washing every impurity from the heavenly palaces on downward until it finally collects in the ocean as a single briny mixture; that an ancient sage once cursed the ocean's water to remain permanently salty and undrinkable; and that the ocean is also home to enormous creatures, some measured in the hundreds of leagues, whose own bodily waste contributes to the sea's own bitter, salty taste.

Chinese Source

略有三種補特伽羅，生那落迦不憶宿命。一、極愚癡。謂生邊地，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。二、極放逸。謂受欲者，於諸欲中增上耽著，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。三、極邪見。謂成就一切誹謗邪見，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。由彼不能自然憶念，故令憶念。復次，二因緣故，大海水鹹。一、生彼眾生福增上故；二、陸地眾生一分非福增上故。所以者何？由水鹹故，非人所涉，生彼無量微細眾生不被採害。又大海中，種種珍寶差別可得，由水鹹故，陸地眾生一分難得。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, three kinds of beings who, born in the hells, do not remember their past lives. First, extreme ignorance — born in a borderland, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Second, extreme indulgence — one given to sensory pleasure, powerfully absorbed in craving, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Third, extreme wrong view — one who has adopted comprehensive views denying cause and effect, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Since they cannot naturally remember, he causes them to remember. Next, for two reasons the ocean's water is salty: first, because of the increasing merit of beings born there; second, because of a portion of demerit belonging to land-dwelling beings. Why? Because the water's saltiness keeps it from being waded through by humans, so the countless minute beings born there are not caught and killed. Also, the ocean contains all manner of distinct treasures, yet because the water is salty, a portion of land-dwelling beings find them difficult to obtain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three kinds of forgetting as a subtle but important qualification to the earlier King Yama story: even his own compassionate intervention has its limits, working specifically for those whose forgetting stems from ignorance, indulgence, or wrong view, rather than suggesting every hell-being automatically receives this same reminder regardless of their own underlying disposition. He treats the ocean's salinity explanation as a lovely example of this treatise's own consistent instinct to find karmic explanation even in the most mundane natural facts, an everyday feature of the physical world reframed as the visible fruit of countless beings' own accumulated merit and demerit working together. He notes the citation of alternate explanations from the Dīrgha Āgama as this tradition's own comfortable pluralism on cosmological detail — several accounts can coexist without one needing to be declared the sole truth, the underlying karmic principle mattering more than the precise mechanism.

4. Section: Sharp Fetters, Dull Fetters — Opening the Vast Study of Affliction

With the brief discussion of hell-beings and animals now complete, the treatise turns to its truly major undertaking for this fascicle and the next: deciding the defilement of affliction, established through five distinct aspects — its intrinsic nature, the specific varieties within that nature, the distinction between its defiled and pure forms, the distinction between what confuses it and what severs it, and the distinction among the various antidotes capable of countering it. Opening with intrinsic nature, the treatise names two basic kinds of affliction. View-natured afflictions are the five sharper fetters — the view of a self within the aggregates, the extreme view of permanence or annihilation, wrong view itself, attachment to one's own view as supreme, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances — each a genuinely mistaken conviction actively adopted about the nature of reality, sharp in the sense of being confidently, deliberately held. Non-view-natured afflictions are instead the five duller fetters — greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt — arising not from any actively adopted wrong conviction but from a more basic, sluggish confusion about ordinary experience itself, doubt included among these five specifically because its own hesitating, undecided quality lacks the same sharp, confident edge that defines the view-natured afflictions.

Chinese Source

復次，煩惱雜染決擇，我今當說。如先所說煩惱雜染義當知。此煩惱由五種相建立差別。何等為五？一、自性故；二、自性差別故；三、染淨差別故；四、迷斷差別故；五、對治差別故。云何自性？略有二種。一、見性煩惱，二、非見性煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the deciding discernment of the defilement of affliction shall now be explained. Its meaning should be understood as already explained earlier. This affliction is established through five distinguishing aspects. What are the five? First, intrinsic nature; second, its specific varieties; third, the distinction between defiled and pure; fourth, the distinction between confusion and severance; fifth, the distinction among antidotes. What is its intrinsic nature? In brief, two kinds: view-natured affliction, and non-view-natured affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the view-natured versus non-view-natured distinction as a genuinely useful lens for recognizing one's own afflictions in daily life: the five sharper fetters are actively held convictions someone would readily defend if challenged, while the five duller fetters are more like background static, rarely examined or even noticed, yet still shaping behavior continuously beneath conscious awareness. He notes the classification of doubt among the duller fetters, despite technically also concerning the four truths, as a fine but important distinction — doubt's own hesitating, unresolved quality makes it categorically different from a confidently, wrongly held view, even though both ultimately obstruct the same clear seeing of reality. He flags that this opening definition is only the very beginning of an extensive, multi-fascicle treatment of affliction to follow, promising considerably more depth on both categories in the sections ahead.
