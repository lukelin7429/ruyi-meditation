Fascicle 58 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十八 · Deciding the Three Stages of Examination and Reflection Begins

Why King Yama Is Called the Dharma King

Having fully determined all six kinds of skillful understanding across seven fascicles, the treatise opens an entirely new topic — deciding discernment for the three stages of examination and reflection — beginning with hell-beings' own basis, and the Buddha's own surprising answer to why King Yama, lord of the underworld, deserves to be called a benefactor rather than a tormentor.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 58 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Topic Opens — Deciding the Three Stages of Examination and Reflection

With the skillful understanding of the aggregates, the elements, the sense-fields, dependent origination, the valid and the invalid, and the faculties all now fully determined across the preceding seven fascicles, the treatise turns to an entirely new topic within this same chapter of deciding discernment: the stage possessing both examination and reflection, the stage free of examination yet still possessing reflection, and the stage free of both — three ways of classifying the entire universe of beings according to the coarseness or refinement of their own mental activity, the first spanning the desire realm together with the first meditative absorption, the second spanning the intermediate meditative state between the first and second absorptions, associated with the great Brahmā realm, and the third spanning everything from the second absorption upward, including the third and fourth absorptions and the four formless attainments beyond them. Before formally opening this new topic, the treatise first addresses two matters left incompletely covered by an earlier discussion of these same three realms — the specific basis belonging to beings born in the hells, and the specific basis belonging to animals — devoting only a brief stretch of text to these two matters before turning to a far more extensive treatment of three kinds of defilement that will occupy most of this fascicle and the three that follow: the defilement of affliction, the defilement of karma, and the defilement of rebirth itself, examined here with a thoroughness this treatise has not yet applied to these particular topics anywhere else.

Chinese Source

如是已說五識身相應地、意地決擇。有尋有伺等三地決擇，我今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the deciding discernment of the stage associated with the five sense-consciousness-bodies and the stage of mind has been explained. The deciding discernment of the stages of examination and reflection shall now be explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this fascicle opens a genuinely new phase of the treatise's own chapter of deciding discernment, moving from the six kinds of skillful understanding just completed toward a different organizing framework entirely — sorting the whole of existence by the coarseness or subtlety of examination and reflection, the very same threefold classification this treatise used many fascicles earlier in its own foundational account of the three realms. He highlights that this fascicle's own most substantial contribution will not be its brief opening remarks on hell-beings and animals, but the extensive, systematic treatment of the three defilements — affliction, karma, and rebirth — that begins here and continues across the next three fascicles, promising a level of detail on these three topics beyond anything offered elsewhere in this treatise.

2. Section: Not a Tormentor But a Teacher — Why King Yama Deserves the Name "Dharma King"

The treatise opens its brief discussion of hell-beings' own basis with a striking question: why is King Yama called the Dharma King — is it because he harms beings, or because he benefits them? If it were because he harms them, the treatise objects, that title would make no sense at all; if instead it is because he benefits them, an explanation of exactly how he does so is called for. The answer is unambiguous: it is through benefit, not harm, that King Yama earns this title. When beings are first dragged before him, he causes an image resembling whatever being they once harmed to appear before them, precisely in order to jog their own memory, and tells them plainly that they are simply receiving the fruit of actions they themselves once committed. Through this very reminder, such beings come to understand for themselves that this suffering is the direct result of their own past deeds, ripening now through their own accumulated karmic force — and, understanding this, they no longer regard King Yama's own attendants, who are not truly independent sentient beings at all but simply a karmic manifestation conjured by the collective force of the tormented beings' own past actions, with any thought of retaliation, any anger, or any lingering resentment whatsoever. And it is precisely because of this shift — ceasing to generate fresh hatred toward what are, in truth, illusory apparitions rather than real persons — that such beings stop accumulating any further karma capable of prolonging their stay in hell, so that once their existing karma has finally run its course, they are genuinely released from that realm for good.

Chinese Source

問：何故焰摩名為法王？為能損害諸眾生故？為能饒益諸眾生故？若由損害眾生，名為法王，不應道理。若由饒益眾生，今應當說云何饒益？答：由能饒益，不由損害。何以故？若諸眾生執到王所，令憶念故，遂為現彼相似之身。告言：汝等自所作業，當受其果。由是因緣，彼諸眾生各自了知，自所作業，還自受果。便於焰摩使者，眾生業力增上所生，猶如變化，非眾生所，無反害心、無瞋恚心，不懷怨恨。乃由此故，感那落迦新業更不積集，故業盡已，脫那落迦趣。是故焰摩由能饒益諸眾生故，名為法王。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: why is Yama called the Dharma King — because he harms beings, or because he benefits them? If because he harms them, this title makes no sense; if because he benefits them, how does he benefit them? Answer: through benefit, not through harm. Why? When beings are dragged before him, in order to help them remember, he causes an image resembling the being they harmed to appear, telling them: you are receiving the fruit of what you yourselves have done. Through this cause, such beings each come to understand that they are receiving the fruit of their own actions, and toward Yama's attendants — a manifestation produced by the increasing force of beings' own karma, like a magical transformation, not truly sentient beings — they give rise to no thought of retaliation, no hatred, and no lingering resentment. Precisely because of this, they cease accumulating any new karma capable of drawing further hellish rebirth, and once their existing karma is exhausted, they are released from the hell realms. It is therefore because he benefits beings that Yama is called the Dharma King.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this reframing of King Yama as one of this fascicle's own most striking opening images: a figure ordinarily imagined purely as a fearsome judge of the dead is here revealed instead as something closer to a compassionate teacher, deliberately engineering a moment of genuine self-recognition precisely so that suffering beings can stop making their own situation worse. He highlights the psychological precision of the mechanism described — recognizing one's own suffering as the fruit of one's own past action defuses the very anger that would otherwise generate fresh karma and prolong that suffering indefinitely — as a teaching with real application well beyond the hell realms themselves, the same principle underlying any suffering compounded by resentment rather than genuine acceptance. He notes the scholarly debate, cited at length in the surrounding commentary, over whether hell's own attendants are genuinely sentient beings or purely karmic apparitions, as this tradition's own careful insistence that not even hell's most fearsome figures are exempted from its rigorous analysis of what is, and is not, ultimately real.

3. Section: Three Kinds of Forgetting, and Why the Ocean Tastes of Salt

The treatise names three kinds of being who, upon arriving in hell, do not spontaneously remember their own past lives, and for whom King Yama's own intervention therefore becomes genuinely necessary. The first is extreme ignorance: someone born in a remote borderland utterly lacking any exposure to genuine teaching, unable to reflect on or investigate anything at all, simply following whatever wrongdoing happens to be modeled around them. The second is extreme indulgence: someone thoroughly given over to sensory pleasure, so powerfully absorbed in craving that no reflection or investigation ever interrupts that same indulgence, following the wrongdoing of like-minded companions without question. And the third is extreme wrong view: someone who has adopted a comprehensive, confidently held set of views actively denying cause and effect altogether, again incapable of genuine reflection, and so likewise following others into wrongdoing. Since none of these three types can naturally recall their own past karma unaided, King Yama specifically helps them remember. The treatise then turns briefly to animals' own basis, opening with a question many students have likely wondered about: why is the ocean's water salty? Two causes are named. First, the very saltiness of the water itself keeps human beings from wading or dwelling within it, thereby protecting the countless minute creatures living there from being caught and killed — this saltiness itself being the fruit of those sea-dwelling beings' own collective merit. Second, the ocean also contains many kinds of genuine treasure, yet its salt water makes reaching that treasure exceptionally difficult for land-dwelling beings, this same difficulty being the fruit of land-dwellers' own partial demerit. The lecture supplements this with three further explanations offered in the Dīrgha Āgama: that clouds periodically rain down over the entire universe, washing every impurity from the heavenly palaces on downward until it finally collects in the ocean as a single briny mixture; that an ancient sage once cursed the ocean's water to remain permanently salty and undrinkable; and that the ocean is also home to enormous creatures, some measured in the hundreds of leagues, whose own bodily waste contributes to the sea's own bitter, salty taste.

Chinese Source

略有三種補特伽羅，生那落迦不憶宿命。一、極愚癡。謂生邊地，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。二、極放逸。謂受欲者，於諸欲中增上耽著，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。三、極邪見。謂成就一切誹謗邪見，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。由彼不能自然憶念，故令憶念。復次，二因緣故，大海水鹹。一、生彼眾生福增上故；二、陸地眾生一分非福增上故。所以者何？由水鹹故，非人所涉，生彼無量微細眾生不被採害。又大海中，種種珍寶差別可得，由水鹹故，陸地眾生一分難得。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, three kinds of beings who, born in the hells, do not remember their past lives. First, extreme ignorance — born in a borderland, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Second, extreme indulgence — one given to sensory pleasure, powerfully absorbed in craving, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Third, extreme wrong view — one who has adopted comprehensive views denying cause and effect, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Since they cannot naturally remember, he causes them to remember. Next, for two reasons the ocean's water is salty: first, because of the increasing merit of beings born there; second, because of a portion of demerit belonging to land-dwelling beings. Why? Because the water's saltiness keeps it from being waded through by humans, so the countless minute beings born there are not caught and killed. Also, the ocean contains all manner of distinct treasures, yet because the water is salty, a portion of land-dwelling beings find them difficult to obtain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three kinds of forgetting as a subtle but important qualification to the earlier King Yama story: even his own compassionate intervention has its limits, working specifically for those whose forgetting stems from ignorance, indulgence, or wrong view, rather than suggesting every hell-being automatically receives this same reminder regardless of their own underlying disposition. He treats the ocean's salinity explanation as a lovely example of this treatise's own consistent instinct to find karmic explanation even in the most mundane natural facts, an everyday feature of the physical world reframed as the visible fruit of countless beings' own accumulated merit and demerit working together. He notes the citation of alternate explanations from the Dīrgha Āgama as this tradition's own comfortable pluralism on cosmological detail — several accounts can coexist without one needing to be declared the sole truth, the underlying karmic principle mattering more than the precise mechanism.

4. Section: Sharp Fetters, Dull Fetters — Opening the Vast Study of Affliction

With the brief discussion of hell-beings and animals now complete, the treatise turns to its truly major undertaking for this fascicle and the next: deciding the defilement of affliction, established through five distinct aspects — its intrinsic nature, the specific varieties within that nature, the distinction between its defiled and pure forms, the distinction between what confuses it and what severs it, and the distinction among the various antidotes capable of countering it. Opening with intrinsic nature, the treatise names two basic kinds of affliction. View-natured afflictions are the five sharper fetters — the view of a self within the aggregates, the extreme view of permanence or annihilation, wrong view itself, attachment to one's own view as supreme, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances — each a genuinely mistaken conviction actively adopted about the nature of reality, sharp in the sense of being confidently, deliberately held. Non-view-natured afflictions are instead the five duller fetters — greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt — arising not from any actively adopted wrong conviction but from a more basic, sluggish confusion about ordinary experience itself, doubt included among these five specifically because its own hesitating, undecided quality lacks the same sharp, confident edge that defines the view-natured afflictions.

Chinese Source

復次，煩惱雜染決擇，我今當說。如先所說煩惱雜染義當知。此煩惱由五種相建立差別。何等為五？一、自性故；二、自性差別故；三、染淨差別故；四、迷斷差別故；五、對治差別故。云何自性？略有二種。一、見性煩惱，二、非見性煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the deciding discernment of the defilement of affliction shall now be explained. Its meaning should be understood as already explained earlier. This affliction is established through five distinguishing aspects. What are the five? First, intrinsic nature; second, its specific varieties; third, the distinction between defiled and pure; fourth, the distinction between confusion and severance; fifth, the distinction among antidotes. What is its intrinsic nature? In brief, two kinds: view-natured affliction, and non-view-natured affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the view-natured versus non-view-natured distinction as a genuinely useful lens for recognizing one's own afflictions in daily life: the five sharper fetters are actively held convictions someone would readily defend if challenged, while the five duller fetters are more like background static, rarely examined or even noticed, yet still shaping behavior continuously beneath conscious awareness. He notes the classification of doubt among the duller fetters, despite technically also concerning the four truths, as a fine but important distinction — doubt's own hesitating, unresolved quality makes it categorically different from a confidently, wrongly held view, even though both ultimately obstruct the same clear seeing of reality. He flags that this opening definition is only the very beginning of an extensive, multi-fascicle treatment of affliction to follow, promising considerably more depth on both categories in the sections ahead.

5. Section: Ten Root Afflictions, Starting with the Self-View Even Animals Are Born With

The treatise names all ten specific varieties of root affliction: five belonging to the view-natured category, and five belonging to the non-view-natured category, together making up the entire scope of what is meant by affliction's own specific varieties. The five view-natured afflictions are self-view, extreme view, wrong view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances; the five non-view-natured afflictions are greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt. Taking up self-view first, the treatise defines it precisely: clinging, through the mind's own superimposing activity, to the five aggregates of clinging as constituting a genuine self, or as belonging to such a self — this very superimposed clinging is what self-view means. Self-view itself divides into two kinds. The innate kind is present in every ordinary, unenlightened being without exception, arising spontaneously and effortlessly even in animals — every cat, dog, pig, or sheep instinctively clings to its own body and mind as "me" and "mine" without ever having been taught to do so. The intellectually-adopted kind, by contrast, belongs specifically to non-Buddhist philosophers who, through deliberate reasoning, examination, and reflection, actively construct and adopt some particular theory of self — whether a self existing entirely apart from the aggregates, or a self identical with the aggregates themselves — arriving at self-view not spontaneously but through their own conscious philosophical reasoning.

Chinese Source

略有十種。見性煩惱五種差別，非見性者亦有五種。總此十種，名為煩惱自性差別。見性五者，謂薩迦耶見、邊執見、邪見、見取、戒禁取。非見性五者，謂貪、恚、慢、無明、疑。薩迦耶見者，於五取蘊，心執增益，見我、我所，名薩迦耶見。此復二種。一者、俱生，二、分別起。俱生者，一切愚夫異生乃至禽獸并皆現行。分別起者，諸外道等計度而起。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, ten kinds: five view-natured afflictions, and five non-view-natured. Together these ten are called affliction's own specific varieties. The five view-natured are self-view, extreme view, wrong view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances. The five non-view-natured are greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt. Self-view is clinging, through the mind's superimposing activity, to the five aggregates of clinging, seeing them as self or as belonging to self — this is called self-view. It is further of two kinds: innate, and intellectually adopted. The innate kind manifests in every foolish, ordinary being, down to animals and birds. The intellectually adopted kind arises through various non-Buddhist philosophers' own deliberate reasoning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the innate/intellectually-adopted distinction for self-view as genuinely clarifying for any student wondering why this teaching bothers refuting philosophical theories of self at all, when the far more basic, wordless clinging every creature is simply born with seems like the real target: both forms need addressing, the philosophical version simply adding a further, actively constructed layer of confusion on top of the same underlying, instinctive clinging every unenlightened being already carries from birth. He highlights the animal examples — cats, dogs, pigs, sheep — as this treatise's own vivid way of making an abstract technical definition immediately recognizable, self-clinging being something visible in the most ordinary behavior of any creature, not merely an obscure philosophical puzzle. He flags that the treatise is about to work through the remaining nine afflictions with this same careful, two-part precision, extreme view and wrong view following immediately next.

6. Section: Sixty-Two Wrong Views, Seven Ways to Believe the Self Simply Vanishes

The treatise turns to extreme view, defined as clinging, under self-view's own increasing power, to the belief that this same self is either eternally permanent or utterly annihilated at death — the permanence view and the annihilation view together constituting extreme view. The permanence view is said to be subsumed within the famous catalog of sixty-two wrong views found scattered across non-Buddhist schools, itself organized around speculation about the distant past and the distant future — theories of universal or partial eternity concerning what came before, and theories concerning consciousness, non-consciousness, or neither after death concerning what comes after. The annihilation view, meanwhile, is illustrated through seven distinct theories some ascetics and brahmins held about the self's own utter cessation at death: some held that a self composed of coarse matter and the four great elements simply ceases entirely at death; others held this specifically of a self reborn in the desire-realm heavens, or the form-realm heavens, ceasing there instead; and still others extended this same claim to a self reborn in each of the four formless attainments in turn, each group regarding its own particular realm as the very summit of existence, beyond which nothing further could possibly continue. The treatise notes that extreme view of both kinds is, strictly speaking, only ever intellectually adopted rather than innate, with one notable exception: a person who has, across many past lives, so thoroughly and repeatedly cultivated this same permanence or annihilation view that it now arises in the present life spontaneously, almost as though innate, through sheer accumulated habituation alone. The treatise then turns briefly to wrong view itself, defining it with striking breadth as any and every distorted understanding whatsoever — any view that turns away from and contradicts the true nature of whatever is actually known, regardless of its specific content, falls under this single, comprehensive category.

Chinese Source

邊執見者，於五取蘊，薩迦耶見增上力故，心執增益，見我斷常，名邊執見。常見所攝邊執見者，謂六十二諸見趣中，計度前際諸遍常論、一分常論，及計後際諸有想論、無想論、非想非非想論。斷見所攝邊執見者，謂諸沙門、若婆羅門七事斷論。此邊執見唯分別起，無有俱生。唯除即此先世已來，串習隨逐邊執見等。邪見者，一切倒見，於所知事顛倒而轉，皆名邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Extreme view is clinging, through self-view's own increasing power, to the belief that the self is either permanent or annihilated — this is extreme view. Extreme view subsumed under the permanence view refers to, among the sixty-two views, speculation about the past through theories of universal or partial eternity, and speculation about the future through theories of having perception, lacking perception, or neither. Extreme view subsumed under the annihilation view refers to seven theories of annihilation held by various ascetics and brahmins. This extreme view is only ever intellectually adopted, never innate — except where it has, from a former life onward, been so habitually cultivated that it now recurs as though innate. Wrong view is every distorted understanding, turning away from the true nature of whatever is known — all such views are called wrong view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sixty-two views catalog as a genuinely staggering demonstration of how thoroughly ancient Indian philosophy had already mapped out every conceivable position on the self's own past and future status, long before this treatise's own careful refutation of the whole enterprise as fundamentally misguided from the start. He highlights the seven annihilation theories' own escalating structure — from a coarse physical self ceasing at ordinary death, up through increasingly refined meditative attainments each claiming to be existence's own final summit — as revealing something genuinely human in these ancient errors: even sophisticated meditators mistook their own deepest attainable state for absolute reality's own outer boundary, precisely the kind of premature closure this entire tradition warns against throughout. He notes wrong view's own remarkably broad definition — any distorted understanding whatsoever — as functioning almost as a catch-all category, with the more specific afflictions like extreme view themselves technically counting as particular instances falling within this same broader umbrella.

7. Section: The Meditator Who Mistook Bliss for Nirvāṇa, and Sixty-Two Views Sorted Three Ways

The treatise divides wrong view into two kinds: superimposing wrong view, which asserts that something exists when it genuinely does not — self-view, extreme view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts are all, in this broader sense, themselves varieties of superimposing wrong view — and negating wrong view, which denies that something exists when it genuinely does, unpacked into four specific denials. Denying cause describes the claim that giving yields no result, that honoring the worthy brings no benefit, and that ritual offering accomplishes nothing. Denying function describes the claim that neither wholesome nor unwholesome conduct actually does anything at all. Denying result describes the claim that neither wholesome nor unwholesome conduct produces any corresponding karmic fruit, whether immediate or later-ripening. And denying genuine facts describes the claim that there is no such thing as father or mother worthy of honor, no beings born spontaneously through karmic transformation, and, most strikingly, no genuine arhat anywhere in the world who has truly and permanently exhausted every affliction — a claim the lecture traces to a specific, sobering error: a meditator who attained the fourth absorption, resting there in its own deep equanimity and bliss, mistook that very stillness for nirvāṇa itself and developed the overestimation of believing himself an arhat, only to be shocked, at the moment of his own death, by unmistakable signs of an approaching rebirth — and rather than recognizing his own mistaken self-assessment, concluded instead that no genuine arhat could exist anywhere at all, since even his own apparently complete attainment had failed him. Altogether, the treatise notes, the entire catalog of sixty-two views, all rooted in self-view, sorts into exactly three categories: forty belonging to the permanence-view branch of extreme view, seven belonging to the annihilation-view branch, and fifteen counted simply as wrong views proper.

Chinese Source

當知此見略有二種。一者、增益，二者、損減。薩迦耶見、邊執見、見取、戒禁取，此四見等，一切皆名增益邪見。謗因、謗用、謗果、壞實事等，心執增益所有諸見，一切皆名損減邪見。無施、無愛，亦無祠祀，是名謗因。無有妙行，亦無惡行，是名謗用。無有妙行、惡行諸業果及異熟，是名謗果。無父無母、無化生有情，亦無世間真阿羅漢諸漏永盡，乃至廣說，如是一切名壞實事。當知如是薩迦耶見以為根本六十二見，三見所攝，謂常見所攝諸邊執見、斷見所攝諸邊執見，及諸邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

This view is, in brief, of two kinds: superimposing, and negating. Self-view, extreme view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts are all called superimposing wrong view. Denying cause, denying function, denying result, and destroying genuine facts are all called negating wrong view. Denying that giving, honoring, or offering accomplish anything is called denying cause. Denying that wholesome or unwholesome conduct does anything is called denying function. Denying that such conduct produces any karmic fruit is called denying result. Denying father, mother, spontaneously born beings, or any genuine arhat in the world who has truly exhausted every affliction, and so on, is called destroying genuine facts. It should be understood that these sixty-two views, rooted in self-view, are subsumed under three categories: extreme view subsumed under the permanence view, extreme view subsumed under the annihilation view, and wrong views themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the meditator's own story as a genuinely cautionary tale worth remembering well beyond this specific technical context: his error was not a lack of genuine attainment, but a failure to correctly recognize the limits of what he had actually attained, and his response to being proven wrong was not humility but an even larger error, denying the very possibility of the goal he had merely mistaken his own partial progress for. He highlights the superimposing/negating distinction as this entire section's own organizing principle, useful well beyond wrong view specifically: virtually every kind of mistaken belief either adds something to reality that isn't there or subtracts something that is, these two directions covering essentially the whole space of possible error. With the sixty-two views now fully sorted into three tidy categories, he notes, the treatise turns to the remaining view-natured afflictions still to be examined.

8. Section: Clinging to a View as the Only Truth, and the Deeper Roots of Craving

The treatise turns to view-attachment, defined as clinging, with considerable forcefulness, to any single view among the sixty-two — whichever one happens to be adopted — as the very finest, the most excellent, the most supreme, the most sublime, declaring outright that only this view is genuinely true and every other view is mere falsehood, believing that this one view alone leads to purity, liberation, and genuine release from suffering. The treatise turns next to attachment to wrong precepts and observances, defined similarly: clinging to whatever ascetic discipline or ritual observance happens to accord with, support, or follow from one's own already-adopted view, mistakenly regarding that particular discipline as the very finest, most excellent, most supreme, most sublime of all possible practices, declaring with the same forcefulness that only this discipline is genuinely true and every other discipline mere falsehood, believing that this discipline alone leads to purity, liberation, and genuine release. With all five view-natured afflictions now examined, the treatise turns to the five non-view-natured afflictions, beginning with greed, defined simply as the mental factor whose very nature is to indulge in and cling to whatever is found desirable. Greed itself divides into four kinds according to its object: clinging to views themselves, and clinging to the desirable objects proper to the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless realm in turn — craving, in other words, is never confined merely to sensory pleasure, but extends even to intellectual conviction and to the most refined meditative attainments alike.

Chinese Source

見取者，於六十二諸見趣等，一一別計為最、為上、為勝、為妙，威勢取執，隨起言說：唯此諦實，餘皆虛妄。由此見故，能得清淨、解脫、出離，是名見取。戒禁取者，謂所受持隨順見取、見取眷屬、見取隨法若戒若禁，於所受持諸戒禁中，妄計為最、為上、為勝、為妙，威勢執取，隨起言說：唯此諦實，餘皆虛妄。由此戒禁，能得清淨、解脫、出離，是名戒禁取。貪者，謂能耽著，心所為性。此復四種。謂著諸見、欲、色、無色。

Ru-Yi Translation

View-attachment is clinging, one by one, to any of the sixty-two views as the finest, most excellent, most supreme, most sublime, declaring: only this is true, all else is false. Through this view, purity, liberation, and release are believed to be attained — this is view-attachment. Attachment to wrong precepts and observances is clinging to whatever discipline accords with, belongs to, or follows from view-attachment, mistakenly regarding it as the finest, most excellent, most supreme, most sublime, declaring: only this is true, all else is false. Through this discipline, purity, liberation, and release are believed to be attained — this is attachment to wrong precepts. Greed is the mental factor whose nature is to indulge and cling. It is further of four kinds: clinging to views, and to the desire, form, and formless realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats view-attachment and precept-attachment as together revealing something important about how conviction itself can become a trap, independent of whatever specific content is being believed: the very insistence that "only this is true" is itself already a kind of clinging, regardless of whether the underlying view happens to be closer to or further from the truth. He highlights greed's own fourfold classification as a useful corrective to any assumption that craving is simply about sensory pleasure — clinging to an intellectual position, or to the refined bliss of formless meditative absorption, is shown here to be every bit as much an instance of greed as ordinary desire for food or comfort. He notes that the treatise is about to work through the remaining four non-view-natured afflictions with this same careful precision, hatred taken up next.

9. Section: Four Faces of Hatred, and the Seven Kinds of Pride Behind Every Comparison

The treatise turns to hatred, defined as the mental factor whose very nature is to wish harm upon whatever gives rise to displeasure. Hatred divides into four kinds according to its object: hatred arising when another's own view causes one harm, a familiar source of conflict whenever disagreement over ideas escalates into genuine hostility; hatred arising when another being directly causes one harm, the most ordinary and immediate form hatred takes; hatred arising when another fails to benefit, or actively harms, someone one loves, since harm to what belongs to oneself provokes self-attachment's own defensive reaction just as surely as direct personal harm does; and hatred arising simply because another shows favor toward one's own enemy, since an ordinary mind's own sharp division between loved and hated makes even a third party's kindness toward a disliked person feel like a kind of betrayal. The treatise then turns to conceit, defined as the mental factor whose nature is to make the mind swell with a sense of its own elevation, always arising on the basis of some underlying self-view and some comparison, whether real or imagined, against other beings. Conceit divides into four kinds according to its object: conceit arising from one's own views, clinging to one's own opinion as superior and final; conceit arising in relation to other beings, comparing oneself against those judged inferior, equal, or superior; conceit arising from the pleasures one currently enjoys, taking pride in especially fine or excellent possessions and circumstances; and conceit arising in relation to some desirable future rebirth, the mind swelling with anticipated satisfaction even before that future result has actually arrived.

Chinese Source

恚者，謂能損害，心所為性。此復四種。謂於損己他見、他有情所，及於所愛不饒益所、於所不愛作饒益所所有瞋恚。慢者，謂令心舉，心所為性。此復四種。謂於諸見、於諸有情、於受用欲、於諸後有處起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hatred is the mental factor whose nature is to cause harm. It is further of four kinds: hatred toward another's view that has harmed oneself, toward another being who has harmed oneself, toward failure to benefit what one loves, and toward benefit shown to what one does not love. Conceit is the mental factor whose nature is to make the mind swell. It is further of four kinds, arising in relation to views, to other beings, to enjoyed pleasures, and to future existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats hatred's fourth kind — resenting kindness shown to one's own enemy — as a genuinely sharp psychological observation, capturing a pettiness many people recognize in themselves only reluctantly: it is not enough to dislike someone, an ordinary mind often wants everyone else to dislike them too, and feels quietly betrayed when they do not. He highlights conceit's own dependence on comparison, whether against other beings, against one's own past circumstances, or against an anticipated future, as revealing just how relational this particular affliction always is — pride rarely arises in a vacuum, almost always measuring the self against some real or imagined other. He flags that conceit's own remaining classifications, distinguishing accurate from genuinely mistaken forms of this same swelling pride, are about to follow in considerably more technical detail.

10. Section: Seven Kinds of Pride, and the Difference Between Confidence and Delusion

The treatise offers a second classification of conceit, dividing it into two broader kinds: unconfused conceit, and confused conceit. Unconfused conceit is genuine pride that nonetheless accords with the actual facts — feeling superior to someone genuinely inferior in wealth, family standing, wisdom, or following, or feeling equal to someone genuinely comparable in these same respects, the underlying comparison itself being accurate even though the swelling of pride remains, technically, an affliction. Confused conceit, by contrast, is pride that positively contradicts the actual facts, and covers the remaining six of the traditional seven kinds of pride recognized in this tradition: feeling superior to, or merely equal to, someone who is actually superior to oneself, both plainly mistaken; feeling only slightly inferior to someone who genuinely and vastly surpasses oneself, likewise a distortion of the real gap involved; the pride of clinging to a self within the five aggregates where no such genuine self can be found at all; the overestimation of believing oneself to have attained some meditative state or noble fruit not actually attained; and the wrongheaded pride of believing some genuinely worthless undertaking to possess real merit, such as constructing a shrine to some minor spirit and imagining this to be a genuinely meritorious deed. The treatise then examines this same confused/unconfused distinction through two further lenses. Concerning enjoyed resources: taking pride in wealth and happiness while actually making use of only coarse, inferior possessions is confused conceit, mistaking one's actual circumstances; taking that same pride while genuinely enjoying excellent, superior possessions is unconfused conceit, an accurate assessment even if still technically prideful. Concerning anticipated future existence: taking pride in an expected excellent rebirth while having actually engaged in wrongful conduct is confused conceit, since wrongful conduct could never actually produce such a result; taking that same pride while having genuinely practiced uprightly is unconfused conceit, the expectation this time actually well-founded.

Chinese Source

又此慢略有二種。一、惑亂慢，二、不惑亂慢。不惑亂慢者，謂於下劣計己為勝、於等計等而生憍慢。惑亂慢者，謂餘六慢。又由受用鄙劣資具，自謂富樂，名惑亂慢。若由受用勝妙資具，自謂富樂，名不惑亂慢。又由邪行，謂後有勝，名惑亂慢。若由正行，謂後有勝，名不惑亂慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

This conceit is, in brief, of two kinds: confused conceit, and unconfused conceit. Unconfused conceit is feeling superior to one genuinely inferior, or equal to one genuinely equal. Confused conceit is the remaining six kinds. Also, taking pride in wealth and happiness while using only inferior resources is confused conceit; while using genuinely excellent resources, it is unconfused conceit. Taking pride in a superior future existence through wrongful conduct is confused conceit; through upright conduct, it is unconfused conceit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the confused/unconfused distinction as a genuinely important qualification within this whole discussion of conceit: even accurate self-assessment, when it swells into pride, still counts as an affliction requiring eventual correction, this treatise never suggesting that "unconfused" conceit is therefore harmless simply because its underlying comparison happens to be factually correct. He highlights the shrine-building example — genuine effort spent on something of no real spiritual value, mistaken for genuine merit — as a small but pointed warning against confusing sincere activity with actually worthwhile activity, sincerity alone never being sufficient to guarantee genuine benefit. He notes that with hatred and conceit now both fully examined, the treatise turns next to ignorance, the affliction this tradition consistently treats as standing at the very root of all the others.

11. Section: The Ignorance Even an Arhat Still Carries

The treatise turns to ignorance, defined as the mental factor whose nature is to obscure and obstruct genuine awakening to the true nature of whatever can be known. Ignorance divides first into four kinds. Uncomprehending ignorance describes simply never having understood selflessness at all, having never observed or reflected on it in the first place. Negligent ignorance describes a lapse in someone who has genuinely understood before, losing mindfulness and falling into distraction despite already possessing the relevant insight. Defiled ignorance describes the confusion bound up with the four fundamental distortions — mistaking the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, the selfless for a genuine self, and the impure for pure — the ignorance underlying a self mistakenly clung to where none exists. And undefiled ignorance describes a subtler confusion that persists even once that self-clinging distortion has been fully overcome — a lingering failure to fully realize the selflessness of phenomena themselves, present even in fully accomplished arhats and solitary realizers, who have entirely eliminated the first three kinds yet still carry this fourth, most refined kind of ignorance. Ignorance divides a second way as well, into ignorance associated with other afflictions, since no affliction whatsoever, whether greed or any other, can actually arise without ignorance's own underlying confusion accompanying it, and independently arising ignorance, present even without any accompanying root affliction, simply through unwise attention toward the four truths in a duller-natured person, an inability to properly discern their meaning that leaves the mind clouded and obscured on its own. This independently arising ignorance itself divides further, in this tradition's own careful analysis, into a form arising alongside the greater and middling secondary afflictions where ignorance's own character remains the dominant, visible feature, a form arising alongside lesser secondary afflictions where those afflictions' own character dominates instead, and, most distinctively, a constantly active form associated specifically with the seventh consciousness, present without interruption in every unenlightened being since beginningless time, arising together with self-view, self-love, and self-conceit, and obstructing genuine wisdom at the deepest, most continuous level of all.

Chinese Source

無明者，謂於所知真實覺悟能覆、能障，心所為性。此略四種。一、無解愚，二、放逸愚，三、染汙愚，四、不染汙愚。若於不見聞覺知所知義中所有無智，名無解愚。若於見聞覺知所知義中散亂失念所有無智，名放逸愚。於顛倒心所有無智，名染汙愚。不顛倒心所有無智，名不染汙愚。又此無明總有二種。一、煩惱相應無明，二、獨行無明。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance is the mental factor whose nature is to obscure and obstruct genuine awakening to what is known. It is, in brief, of four kinds: uncomprehending ignorance, negligent ignorance, defiled ignorance, and undefiled ignorance. Not understanding what has never been seen, heard, sensed, or cognized is called uncomprehending ignorance. Losing mindfulness amid distraction, regarding what has been seen, heard, sensed, or cognized, is called negligent ignorance. Confusion bound up with a distorted mind is called defiled ignorance. Confusion without such distortion is called undefiled ignorance. This ignorance is also, altogether, of two kinds: ignorance associated with affliction, and independently arising ignorance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats undefiled ignorance's own persistence even in fully accomplished arhats as one of this tradition's own most humbling technical teachings: liberation from the cycle of birth and death does not automatically mean complete omniscience, a subtler confusion about the selflessness of phenomena themselves remaining even after every affliction bound up with self-clinging has been entirely eliminated. He highlights the seventh-consciousness-associated constantly active ignorance as a distinctively Yogācāra contribution to this whole discussion, identifying a level of confusion so continuous and fundamental that an ordinary being never experiences even a single moment free of it, arising inseparably with self-view, self-love, and self-conceit from beginningless time onward. He notes that with ignorance now fully classified, only doubt remains among the ten root afflictions still to be examined.

12. Section: Five Kinds of Doubt, and Closing the Catalog of Ten Root Afflictions

The treatise turns to its final root affliction, doubt, defined as the mental factor whose nature is simply hesitating, undecided, between two possibilities. Doubt is established through five distinct aspects. Doubt about other lives questions whether past and future existence genuinely occur at all. Doubt about function questions whether an intermediate-state being genuinely exists between death and rebirth, and whether parents genuinely play the specific role attributed to them in a new being's own conception. Doubt about cause and effect questions whether wholesome and unwholesome conduct truly differ in their underlying cause, and whether such conduct truly yields its corresponding pleasant or painful result. Doubt about the truths questions whether the four noble truths of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path are genuinely real. And doubt about the Three Jewels questions whether the Buddha, the teaching, and the community actually possess the genuine qualities attributed to them. With this, the entire catalog of ten root afflictions — the five view-natured and five non-view-natured kinds first named at the very opening of this discussion — now stands fully classified in its every specific variety. The treatise closes this whole stretch with a further clarifying observation: every one of these ten afflictions can take as its object either ordinary phenomena directly, such as the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields, or other afflictions themselves, one affliction capable of triggering or reinforcing another in an ongoing, self-perpetuating chain.

Chinese Source

疑者，猶豫二分不決定，心所為性。當知此疑，略由五相差別建立。謂於他世作用、因、果、諸諦、寶中，心懷猶豫。如是所說十種煩惱，亦緣事轉、亦緣煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Doubt is the mental factor whose nature is hesitating, undecided, between two possibilities. It should be understood that this doubt is, in brief, established through five distinguishing aspects: hesitation concerning other lives, function, cause and effect, the truths, and the Three Jewels. These ten afflictions just described take as their object both ordinary phenomena and other afflictions themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats doubt's own fivefold structure as tracing a natural progression from the most basic uncertainty — does anything at all continue beyond this present life — through increasingly specific uncertainty about karma's own mechanics, culminating in doubt about the very framework, the four truths, and the very community of practice, the Three Jewels, that this entire tradition depends on. He highlights the closing observation, that every affliction can take either ordinary phenomena or other afflictions as its object, as an important structural point worth carrying forward: afflictions do not simply arise in isolation reacting to the world, but actively feed one another in a self-sustaining tangle, exactly why uprooting even one root affliction thoroughly can have such disproportionate benefit for the whole afflicted system at once. With the entire catalog of specific varieties now closed, he notes, the treatise turns next to its third establishing aspect: the distinction between defilement and purity.

13. Section: Why Lower Realms Can Think About Higher Ones, But Not the Reverse

The treatise closes its examination of what each affliction takes as its object: all ten root afflictions can mutually reference every affliction belonging to their own realm, and can likewise take as their object whatever outflow-bound phenomena belong to that same realm. Afflictions belonging to a lower realm can additionally take a higher realm's own afflictions and phenomena as their object, yet the reverse does not hold — a higher realm's own afflictions cannot directly reference a lower realm's afflictions and phenomena, since beings in higher realms, already possessing their own realm's more excellent qualities, naturally regard themselves as superior and give the lower realms no further thought. The treatise then turns to a new distinction: the moral quality of the ten root afflictions, illustrated through self-view as its representative example. Innate self-view is purely morally neutral, since it operates constantly and gently in the background of ordinary experience without ever rising to the level of seriously harming oneself or others. Intellectually-adopted self-view, by contrast, is genuinely unwholesome when it occurs in the desire realm, its firm, deliberately reasoned grasping making it considerably more forceful than the innate kind. When that same intellectually-adopted self-view occurs instead in the form or formless realms, it becomes morally neutral once again, restrained there by the power of calm-abiding meditation and surrounded by predominantly wholesome qualities — yet it remains, even so, a concealed kind of neutrality, since its underlying essence is still a defiled wisdom obstructing the noble path, rather than a genuinely innocent neutrality free of any obstruction at all. The very same reasoning, the treatise notes, extends by analogy to each of the remaining nine root afflictions in turn.

Chinese Source

下地煩惱能緣上地煩惱及事，非上地惑能緣下地煩惱及事。復次，俱生薩迦耶見唯無記性，數現行故，非極損惱自他處故。若分別起薩迦耶見，由堅執故，與前相違。在欲界者，唯不善性。若在上地，奢摩他力所制持故，多白淨法所攝受故，成無記性。由染汙故，體是隱沒。所餘煩惱，由此道理，隨應當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Afflictions belonging to a lower realm can take a higher realm's afflictions and phenomena as their object; a higher realm's afflictions cannot take a lower realm's afflictions and phenomena as their object. Next, innate self-view is purely neutral, since it operates constantly yet does not severely harm oneself or others. Intellectually-adopted self-view, being firmly grasped, differs from this — in the desire realm it is purely unwholesome. In the higher realms, restrained by the power of calm-abiding and surrounded by predominantly wholesome qualities, it becomes neutral, yet remains, because of its defiled nature, a concealed kind of neutrality. The remaining afflictions should be understood by this same principle, according to what applies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the asymmetry in what different realms can reference — lower realms reaching upward, higher realms never reaching back down — as a subtle but telling detail about the psychology of spiritual attainment itself: genuine progress does not require constant awareness of what has been left behind, a being genuinely settled in a higher realm's own excellence simply has no occasion to dwell on lower states at all. He highlights "concealed neutrality" as this section's own important nuance: a technically neutral mental state is not automatically a harmless one, since concealment specifically names a neutrality that still obstructs the noble path even while causing no obvious immediate harm, a distinction worth remembering whenever meditative calm is mistaken for genuine purification.

14. Section: Three Conditions That Actually Send an Affliction to a Miserable Rebirth

The treatise turns to a fuller examination of unwholesome afflictions, explaining first why desire-realm afflictions are so predominantly unwholesome: they serve as the very foundation upon which unwholesome conduct itself takes root and stands, making unwholesomeness their own default character. The treatise then names three specific conditions that, together, determine whether an unwholesome affliction will actually lead to a miserable rebirth — other afflictions lacking all three conditions do not reliably produce this same result. The first condition is extensive, earnest, and uninterrupted cultivation of the affliction, mistaking it for something genuinely beneficial, never recognizing its fault or its harm, and indulging it without any restraint whatsoever. The second condition is actually relying on that same affliction as a basis for committing and further compounding unwholesome deeds through body, speech, and mind. And the third condition is using that affliction to actively sever another being's own wholesome roots and instill unwholesome ones in their place — precisely the pattern seen, the lecture notes, in extremists who not only hold the conviction that killing leads to heavenly reward, but actively teach that same conviction to others, drawing them into the very same wrongdoing. The treatise clarifies that this threefold analysis concerns only affliction generated in this present life, excluding karma from a past life whose result is simply delayed until some later rebirth, a different matter entirely from what actually determines an affliction's own immediate trajectory. The treatise then turns to a further distinction: which of the ten root afflictions correspond to which of the six consciousnesses. Seven of the ten belong exclusively to the mind-consciousness alone, while greed, hatred, and ignorance also extend to the five sense-consciousnesses as well, these three alone capable of accompanying direct sensory experience itself, not only conceptual reflection.

Chinese Source

欲界煩惱為諸惡行安足處故，多不善性。又不善者，具三因緣能往惡趣，餘則不定。何等為三？謂極多修習，殷重、無間計為功德，不見其失、不見其患，縱情而起，是初因緣。用此煩惱以為依處，由身語意，於諸惡行作及增長，是第二因緣。由此煩惱斷他善品，授不善品，是第三因緣。除先所作能往惡趣順後受業。又十煩惱，七唯意地，貪、恚、無明亦通五識。

Ru-Yi Translation

Desire-realm afflictions, serving as the foundation for unwholesome conduct, are mostly unwholesome. Unwholesome afflictions possessing three conditions definitely lead to a miserable rebirth; other afflictions do not necessarily do so. What are the three? Extensive, earnest, uninterrupted cultivation, mistaken for merit, its fault and harm unseen, indulged without restraint — this is the first condition. Relying on this affliction as a basis for committing and increasing unwholesome deeds through body, speech, and mind — this is the second. Using this affliction to sever another's wholesome roots and instill unwholesome ones — this is the third. This excludes karma from a past life whose result is delayed to a later rebirth. Also, of the ten afflictions, seven belong only to the mind ground; greed, hatred, and ignorance also extend to the five sense-consciousnesses.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three conditions for a miserable rebirth as offering genuine, practical diagnostic value beyond pure classification: an affliction merely arisen once, quickly recognized as harmful and released, is categorically different from one repeatedly cultivated, actively acted upon, and spread to others, even though both might technically be called the same affliction in the abstract. He highlights the explicit reference to those who teach that killing leads to heavenly reward as a sober, contemporary-feeling illustration of the third condition's own severity — actively corrupting another person's own wholesome roots being treated as a distinct and serious escalation beyond merely harboring or acting on one's own affliction. He notes the correspondence with the six consciousnesses as a fittingly technical close to this stretch, greed, hatred, and ignorance's own extension into the five senses explaining why these three, uniquely among the ten, can arise the very instant a desirable or aversive object is first perceived, without needing conceptual reflection to intervene first.

15. Section: Which Feelings Accompany Which Affliction, and What Each One Actually Latches Onto

The treatise details, for the desire realm, exactly which feelings accompany which afflictions, understood as a broad rather than absolute correspondence, with subtler exceptions to be addressed later. Four of the five views, together with conceit, are accompanied by joy and equanimity. Greed is accompanied by pleasure, joy, and equanimity. Hatred is accompanied by pain, sorrow, and equanimity. Wrong view specifically is accompanied by joy, sorrow, and equanimity. Doubt is accompanied by sorrow and equanimity. And ignorance alone is accompanied by all five feelings without exception. In the higher realms, this same correspondence simply follows whichever feelings that realm itself actually possesses. The treatise then turns to what each affliction actually takes as its object, distinguishing those that lack any genuine substance from those that possess it. Afflictions severed through the path of vision are said to lack substance, since they arise predominantly through conceptual misunderstanding of emptiness itself, their object never being anything genuinely, conventionally real in the first place — self-view, extreme view, and attachment to wrong precepts all cling to something that was never conventionally there to begin with. The remaining afflictions, severed instead through the path of cultivation, are said by contrast to possess genuine substance, their object being a real, ordinary phenomenon rather than a purely conceptual error. The treatise then distinguishes a further pair: greed and conceit take only a partial object, arising specifically in relation to whatever outflow-bound thing happens to be found desirable, while hatred likewise takes only a partial object, arising specifically in relation to whatever is found undesirable — these three alone confined to a specific, limited range. The remaining seven afflictions, by contrast, are called pervasive, ranging freely across every possible object, internal or external, desirable, undesirable, or neither, taking the entire field of outflow-bound experience as their potential scope.

Chinese Source

又於欲界，四見及慢，喜、捨相應；貪，樂、喜、捨相應；恚，苦、憂、捨相應；邪見，喜、憂、捨相應；疑，憂、捨相應；無明，一切五根相應。又十煩惱，見所斷者，名曰無事，彼所緣事非成實故。又貪與慢緣有漏一分可意事生，恚緣一分非可意生，是故此三煩惱一分所生，名取一分。所餘煩惱，通緣內外若愛非愛，及俱相違有漏事生，是故說彼名曰遍行，取一切事。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the desire realm, four views and conceit are accompanied by joy and equanimity; greed by pleasure, joy, and equanimity; hatred by pain, sorrow, and equanimity; wrong view by joy, sorrow, and equanimity; doubt by sorrow and equanimity; ignorance by all five. Afflictions severed by the path of vision lack genuine substance, their object never being conventionally real. Greed and conceit arise in relation to a partial, desirable outflow-bound object; hatred arises in relation to a partial, undesirable one — these three are said to take a partial object. The remaining afflictions, ranging across internal and external, desirable, undesirable, and neither, are called pervasive, taking every object as their scope.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats ignorance's own unique correspondence with all five feelings as a fitting confirmation of everything already said about it — standing at the root of every other affliction, it makes sense that ignorance alone accompanies every possible felt quality of experience, never confined the way greed or hatred are to a narrower emotional register. He highlights the has-substance/lacks-substance distinction as directly paralleling the earlier explanation of extreme view and self-view as purely intellectually constructed errors, this same logic now generalized across the entire path-of-vision category of affliction. He notes the partial/pervasive distinction as a useful reminder that not every affliction operates the same way: greed and hatred are, relatively speaking, narrowly targeted, while something like doubt or ignorance can, in principle, attach itself to absolutely anything at all.

16. Section: A New Topic Opens — What Are the Secondary Afflictions?

With the ten root afflictions now examined from every angle, the treatise turns to an entirely new topic within this same broader study: the secondary afflictions, defined as whatever accords with these same ten root afflictions, arises together with them, or belongs to their same general category. The treatise establishes this category through four distinguishing aspects. Some secondary afflictions pervade every unwholesome state of mind without exception, present whenever any unwholesome mental state occurs at all. Some pervade every defiled state of mind more broadly, including neutral-but-obstructed states as well as outright unwholesome ones. Some arise only in connection with a specific, particular unwholesome state of mind, rather than accompanying unwholesome states universally. And some arise across wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral states of mind alike, yet not in every possible place or at every possible time, appearing only under more limited, specific circumstances even within this broader range.

Chinese Source

若有隨順如是煩惱、煩惱俱行、煩惱品類，名隨煩惱。云何名隨煩惱？略由四相差別建立。一、通一切不善心起；二、通一切染汙心起；三、於各別不善心起；四、善、不善、無記心起，非一切處、非一切時。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever accords with these afflictions, arises together with them, or belongs to their category, is called secondary affliction. What is secondary affliction? It is established, in brief, through four distinguishing aspects: pervading every unwholesome mind; pervading every defiled mind; arising with a specific unwholesome mind; and arising with wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral minds, though not in every place or at every time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold framework as the treatise's own organizing key to the entire, considerably longer list of secondary afflictions about to follow — the greater, middling, and lesser secondary afflictions this tradition catalogs elsewhere, each falling into one of exactly these four categories depending on how broadly or narrowly it actually operates. He notes that secondary afflictions are best understood as satellites of the ten root afflictions already examined at such length, deriving their own defiled character from association with those roots rather than possessing some entirely independent source of their own, exactly as the definition's own opening phrase — according with, co-arising with, or belonging to the same category as the root afflictions — makes explicit.

17. Section: Shamelessness, Ten Great Afflictions, and Ten That Never Arise Together

The treatise names the secondary afflictions belonging to each of its four categories. Pervading every unwholesome mind without exception are just two: shamelessness, the absence of any inner sense of conscience, and lack of regard for others, the absence of any concern for how one's own conduct reflects on or affects other people — these two accompanying every unwholesome thought, since without them, greed, hatred, and the rest could never actually arise unchecked in the first place. Pervading every defiled mind more broadly, spanning all three realms without exception, are ten further secondary afflictions: heedlessness, restlessness, dullness, lack of faith, laziness, wrongly directed desire, wrongly held conviction, wrongly oriented mindfulness, distraction, and incorrect discernment — each of these ten present whenever any defiled state of mind occurs at all, whether outright unwholesome or merely neutral-but-obstructed. Arising instead only individually, one at a time, with a specific unwholesome mind, and never accompanied by any of the others at that same moment, are ten more: wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation, envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, arrogance, and the wish to harm — nine of these ten confined entirely to the desire realm, with only deceit and flattery extending as far as the first meditative absorption, since both are so closely tied to the pursuit of reputation and material gain that they persist a little further than most, and arrogance alone extending across all three realms, present even in beings intoxicated by their own refined attainments, though in the higher realms restrained by meditative power into a merely neutral-but-obstructed form.

Shamelessness and lack of regard for others pervade every unwholesome mind. Heedlessness, restlessness, dullness, lack of faith, laziness, wrong desire, wrong conviction, wrong mindfulness, distraction, and incorrect discernment — these ten pervade every defiled mind, confined to no single realm. Wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation, envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, arrogance, and the wish to harm — these ten arise individually with a specific unwholesome mind; when one arises, none of the others can arise alongside it. Arrogance extends across all three realms; together with the previous two, in the higher realms it becomes purely neutral.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats shamelessness and lack of regard for others as this entire catalog's own foundational pair, worth remembering as the quiet precondition beneath every other unwholesome state: without some baseline indifference to conscience and to others' judgment, the sharper afflictions like wrath or envy would have nowhere to actually take root and act themselves out. He highlights the "never arise together" rule for the ten individually-arising afflictions as a genuinely useful piece of introspective psychology: a single unwholesome moment tends to be dominated by one particular flavor of affliction at a time, wrath or envy or stinginess taking its turn rather than all arising simultaneously in some chaotic simultaneous swarm. He notes deceit, flattery, and arrogance's own unusual persistence into the higher meditative realms as a sobering reminder that even considerable meditative attainment does not automatically eliminate every subtler form of self-regard.

18. Section: A Monk's Cure for a Dying Man — Three Kinds of Delusive Thought

The treatise names its fourth and final category of secondary affliction: four factors that arise across wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral states of mind alike, yet neither universally nor at every moment — examination, reflection, regret, and drowsiness. Prolonged, exhausting examination and reflection eventually tire the body, dull mindfulness, and strain the mind itself, which is precisely why they count as afflictions despite sometimes accompanying wholesome states, and why their own presence extends only as far as the first meditative absorption, beyond which such coarse mental activity no longer occurs. Regret and drowsiness, by contrast, belong exclusively to the desire realm, since beings in the higher realms, free of any occasion for regretting past conduct and sustained instead by meditative absorption rather than ordinary sleep, never experience either. The lecture illustrates prolonged examination and reflection's own genuinely harmful potential with a story of a man bedridden for years by a wasting illness no medicine could cure, visited at last by a wandering monk who diagnosed his condition as rooted in affliction, and affliction as rooted in delusive thought of three specific kinds — dwelling obsessively on decades of past glory, grudges, and idle memory; anxiously hesitating over present matters that could simply be met as they arise; and endlessly scheming after some hoped-for future of wealth, honor, and flourishing descendants that may never actually come to pass. The monk taught him that such thoughts, ceaselessly arising and passing, are themselves nothing but illusion, and that recognizing them as illusion the instant they arise is itself enough to dissolve them — not fearing a thought's arising, only fearing failing to notice it in time, since a noticed thought is itself already medicine, while an unnoticed one left to continue is itself already sickness. The man withdrew to a single quiet room, cleared his mind of every entanglement, and sat in gathered stillness for over a month, at the end of which his illness had genuinely begun to lift. The treatise closes this entire study of the secondary afflictions by confirming that every variety named anywhere else in scripture — grief, lamentation, sorrow, distress, and every other form of disturbance described in the sūtras and this treatise's own broader compendium — reduces without exception to these same four established categories, nothing left over that these four do not already account for.

Examination, reflection, regret, and drowsiness — these four arise across wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral minds, though not universally nor at every time. Prolonged examination and reflection tire the body, cause loss of mindfulness, and strain the mind, which is why they are called secondary afflictions; these two extend as far as the first absorption. Regret and drowsiness belong only to the desire realm. All the varieties of secondary affliction the Blessed One spoke of elsewhere, extending to grief, lamentation, sorrow, and distress, and all that is analyzed at length in the Compendium of Explanations, are without exception subsumed under these same four categories, according to what applies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the bedridden patient's story as one of this fascicle's own most genuinely therapeutic teachings, offering a piece of practical wisdom any listener could apply directly to their own restless mind: the specific insight that a recognized thought is already medicine, while an unrecognized one left to run its course is already sickness, reframing meditation's own basic task not as suppressing thought but simply as noticing it promptly. He highlights the three kinds of delusive thinking — obsessive rehearsal of the past, anxious hesitation over the present, endless scheming toward an uncertain future — as a remarkably precise, still entirely recognizable map of ordinary mental restlessness, exactly the pattern any modern meditator discovers the first time they actually try to sit quietly and observe their own mind. He notes the closing confirmation — every scriptural term for affliction reducing to these same four categories — as bringing this entire, extensive study of the secondary afflictions to a tidy and satisfying close.

19. Section: Twelve Situations Where Affliction Takes Root, and the Monk Who Hid His Own Excrement

The treatise now maps the secondary afflictions onto twelve specific situations in which they characteristically arise: clinging to bad conduct; quarreling and contention; breaking ethical discipline; following a bad teacher's own unwholesome ways; wrong livelihood; indulging in sensory desire; reflecting carefully on a teaching just heard; stilling the mind in meditative practice on that same teaching's meaning; sharing wealth or teaching with others; living incompatibly among fellow practitioners; dwelling apart in solitary lodging; and the general gathering-place of every kind of suffering. Greed, hatred, and delusion — the three unwholesome roots underlying every unwholesome deed — arise from the very first situation, clinging to bad conduct itself, since it is precisely through self-attachment and attachment to phenomena that desirable objects provoke greed, undesirable objects provoke hatred, and neutral objects provoke delusion in turn. The eight afflictions of wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation, envy, stinginess, deceit, and flattery all arise from the second situation, quarreling and contention: wrath when hostility first flares visibly across the face, resentment when that same hostility knots itself into a lingering grudge nursed silently rather than shown, vexation when old resentment ignites into sudden harsh, coarse outbursts upon meeting the same old friction again, and concealment when fear of losing one's own reputation or gain drives someone to hide a wrongdoing they have actually committed. The lecture illustrates concealment's own subtlety with a story of a Tibetan monk in solitary retreat, who, expecting a lay visitor, at first swept and decorated his shrine room beautifully to make a good impression — only to catch himself mid-task, realizing this impulse was itself a kind of flattery, concealing his own ordinary, unkempt daily habits behind a performance staged for someone else's benefit, and so deliberately placed his own excrement back in plain view instead, for which his own teacher, learning what had happened, praised him as a genuinely honest practitioner.

Greed, hatred, and delusion arise from the first situation; wrath through flattery arise from the second.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this entire twelve-situation mapping as this fascicle's own most practically useful contribution to the whole study of affliction, moving decisively from abstract classification toward genuinely recognizable, everyday circumstances where a practitioner can actually catch a specific affliction arising in real time. He treats the Tibetan monk's own story as an unusually vivid illustration of concealment's own subtlety: the impulse to look good for a visitor is so ordinary that most people would never even notice it as a form of flattery, let alone catch and correct it in the very moment of doing so, making this monk's own reversal a genuinely striking example of honest self-scrutiny in action. He notes that the remaining ten situations, each generating their own specific cluster of afflictions, promise an equally concrete and recognizable account of where affliction actually takes root in ordinary practice.

20. Section: A Son's Debt Repaid as a Pig — Wrong Livelihood and Its Consequences

The treatise continues its mapping: shamelessness and lack of regard for others both arise specifically from breaking ethical discipline, since it is precisely the absence of any inner or outer sense of accountability that permits a precept to be broken in the first place. Deceit, flattery, arrogance, and the wish to harm all arise from following a bad teacher's own unwholesome ways, close association with unwholesome company naturally drawing a person into these same unwholesome patterns. And a further cluster of afflictions — feigned pretense, outright deception, and regret among them — arises from wrong livelihood itself, illustrated through five classic ways ancient monastics were warned against improperly seeking material support: falsely displaying some extraordinary spiritual attainment to inspire donors' reverence; boasting of one's own abilities to win others' confidence; practicing fortune-telling or divination for material gain; speaking loudly and affecting an imposing manner to inspire awe; and strategically mentioning gifts received from one donor to prompt greater generosity from another. The lecture illustrates the seriousness of misusing even a family member's own wealth with a striking story from the Sūtra of Cause and Effect: a boy repeatedly stole money from his own father, who reasoned that since the money would eventually belong to his son anyway, no real harm was being done — yet the boy grew increasingly corrupted by this pattern and died young, only to be reborn as a pig in that very same household. When the father moved to slaughter the pig for food, it appeared to him in a dream, begging to be spared and revealing that it was, in fact, his own son, reborn specifically to repay through its own body the exact debt those stolen sums represented — a visiting practitioner, consulted afterward, confirmed the pig's own account, teaching the father that even a parent's wealth, however freely it might eventually have been given, still incurs a genuine debt when simply taken without permission.

Ru-Yi Translation

Shamelessness and lack of regard for others arise from the third situation; deceit through the wish to harm arise from the fourth; feigned pretense through regret arise from the fifth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pig-reincarnation story as this section's own memorable and genuinely sobering illustration of a principle easy to state abstractly but harder to feel viscerally: that even wealth belonging to one's own family carries a genuine debt when taken without permission, however casually a parent might excuse the taking in the moment. He highlights the five wrong ways of seeking livelihood as remarkably specific and still entirely recognizable warnings against exploiting others' generosity or credulity for material gain, whether through false spiritual display, self-promotion, fortune-telling, intimidation, or strategic hinting — a checklist any sincere practitioner, ancient or modern, could still use to examine their own conduct honestly. He notes that the remaining situations — indulgence in desire, mis-heard teachings, meditative obstacles, shared resources, incompatible cohabitation, solitary retreat, and suffering itself — each generate their own further, equally specific clusters of affliction still to come.
