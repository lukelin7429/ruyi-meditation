Fascicle 58 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十八 · Deciding the Three Stages of Examination and Reflection Begins

Why King Yama Is Called the Dharma King

Having fully determined all six kinds of skillful understanding across seven fascicles, the treatise opens an entirely new topic — deciding discernment for the three stages of examination and reflection — beginning with hell-beings' own basis, and the Buddha's own surprising answer to why King Yama, lord of the underworld, deserves to be called a benefactor rather than a tormentor.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 58 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Topic Opens — Deciding the Three Stages of Examination and Reflection

With the skillful understanding of the aggregates, the elements, the sense-fields, dependent origination, the valid and the invalid, and the faculties all now fully determined across the preceding seven fascicles, the treatise turns to an entirely new topic within this same chapter of deciding discernment: the stage possessing both examination and reflection, the stage free of examination yet still possessing reflection, and the stage free of both — three ways of classifying the entire universe of beings according to the coarseness or refinement of their own mental activity, the first spanning the desire realm together with the first meditative absorption, the second spanning the intermediate meditative state between the first and second absorptions, associated with the great Brahmā realm, and the third spanning everything from the second absorption upward, including the third and fourth absorptions and the four formless attainments beyond them. Before formally opening this new topic, the treatise first addresses two matters left incompletely covered by an earlier discussion of these same three realms — the specific basis belonging to beings born in the hells, and the specific basis belonging to animals — devoting only a brief stretch of text to these two matters before turning to a far more extensive treatment of three kinds of defilement that will occupy most of this fascicle and the three that follow: the defilement of affliction, the defilement of karma, and the defilement of rebirth itself, examined here with a thoroughness this treatise has not yet applied to these particular topics anywhere else.

Chinese Source

如是已說五識身相應地、意地決擇。有尋有伺等三地決擇，我今當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the deciding discernment of the stage associated with the five sense-consciousness-bodies and the stage of mind has been explained. The deciding discernment of the stages of examination and reflection shall now be explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this fascicle opens a genuinely new phase of the treatise's own chapter of deciding discernment, moving from the six kinds of skillful understanding just completed toward a different organizing framework entirely — sorting the whole of existence by the coarseness or subtlety of examination and reflection, the very same threefold classification this treatise used many fascicles earlier in its own foundational account of the three realms. He highlights that this fascicle's own most substantial contribution will not be its brief opening remarks on hell-beings and animals, but the extensive, systematic treatment of the three defilements — affliction, karma, and rebirth — that begins here and continues across the next three fascicles, promising a level of detail on these three topics beyond anything offered elsewhere in this treatise.

2. Section: Not a Tormentor But a Teacher — Why King Yama Deserves the Name "Dharma King"

The treatise opens its brief discussion of hell-beings' own basis with a striking question: why is King Yama called the Dharma King — is it because he harms beings, or because he benefits them? If it were because he harms them, the treatise objects, that title would make no sense at all; if instead it is because he benefits them, an explanation of exactly how he does so is called for. The answer is unambiguous: it is through benefit, not harm, that King Yama earns this title. When beings are first dragged before him, he causes an image resembling whatever being they once harmed to appear before them, precisely in order to jog their own memory, and tells them plainly that they are simply receiving the fruit of actions they themselves once committed. Through this very reminder, such beings come to understand for themselves that this suffering is the direct result of their own past deeds, ripening now through their own accumulated karmic force — and, understanding this, they no longer regard King Yama's own attendants, who are not truly independent sentient beings at all but simply a karmic manifestation conjured by the collective force of the tormented beings' own past actions, with any thought of retaliation, any anger, or any lingering resentment whatsoever. And it is precisely because of this shift — ceasing to generate fresh hatred toward what are, in truth, illusory apparitions rather than real persons — that such beings stop accumulating any further karma capable of prolonging their stay in hell, so that once their existing karma has finally run its course, they are genuinely released from that realm for good.

Chinese Source

問：何故焰摩名為法王？為能損害諸眾生故？為能饒益諸眾生故？若由損害眾生，名為法王，不應道理。若由饒益眾生，今應當說云何饒益？答：由能饒益，不由損害。何以故？若諸眾生執到王所，令憶念故，遂為現彼相似之身。告言：汝等自所作業，當受其果。由是因緣，彼諸眾生各自了知，自所作業，還自受果。便於焰摩使者，眾生業力增上所生，猶如變化，非眾生所，無反害心、無瞋恚心，不懷怨恨。乃由此故，感那落迦新業更不積集，故業盡已，脫那落迦趣。是故焰摩由能饒益諸眾生故，名為法王。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: why is Yama called the Dharma King — because he harms beings, or because he benefits them? If because he harms them, this title makes no sense; if because he benefits them, how does he benefit them? Answer: through benefit, not through harm. Why? When beings are dragged before him, in order to help them remember, he causes an image resembling the being they harmed to appear, telling them: you are receiving the fruit of what you yourselves have done. Through this cause, such beings each come to understand that they are receiving the fruit of their own actions, and toward Yama's attendants — a manifestation produced by the increasing force of beings' own karma, like a magical transformation, not truly sentient beings — they give rise to no thought of retaliation, no hatred, and no lingering resentment. Precisely because of this, they cease accumulating any new karma capable of drawing further hellish rebirth, and once their existing karma is exhausted, they are released from the hell realms. It is therefore because he benefits beings that Yama is called the Dharma King.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this reframing of King Yama as one of this fascicle's own most striking opening images: a figure ordinarily imagined purely as a fearsome judge of the dead is here revealed instead as something closer to a compassionate teacher, deliberately engineering a moment of genuine self-recognition precisely so that suffering beings can stop making their own situation worse. He highlights the psychological precision of the mechanism described — recognizing one's own suffering as the fruit of one's own past action defuses the very anger that would otherwise generate fresh karma and prolong that suffering indefinitely — as a teaching with real application well beyond the hell realms themselves, the same principle underlying any suffering compounded by resentment rather than genuine acceptance. He notes the scholarly debate, cited at length in the surrounding commentary, over whether hell's own attendants are genuinely sentient beings or purely karmic apparitions, as this tradition's own careful insistence that not even hell's most fearsome figures are exempted from its rigorous analysis of what is, and is not, ultimately real.

3. Section: Three Kinds of Forgetting, and Why the Ocean Tastes of Salt

The treatise names three kinds of being who, upon arriving in hell, do not spontaneously remember their own past lives, and for whom King Yama's own intervention therefore becomes genuinely necessary. The first is extreme ignorance: someone born in a remote borderland utterly lacking any exposure to genuine teaching, unable to reflect on or investigate anything at all, simply following whatever wrongdoing happens to be modeled around them. The second is extreme indulgence: someone thoroughly given over to sensory pleasure, so powerfully absorbed in craving that no reflection or investigation ever interrupts that same indulgence, following the wrongdoing of like-minded companions without question. And the third is extreme wrong view: someone who has adopted a comprehensive, confidently held set of views actively denying cause and effect altogether, again incapable of genuine reflection, and so likewise following others into wrongdoing. Since none of these three types can naturally recall their own past karma unaided, King Yama specifically helps them remember. The treatise then turns briefly to animals' own basis, opening with a question many students have likely wondered about: why is the ocean's water salty? Two causes are named. First, the very saltiness of the water itself keeps human beings from wading or dwelling within it, thereby protecting the countless minute creatures living there from being caught and killed — this saltiness itself being the fruit of those sea-dwelling beings' own collective merit. Second, the ocean also contains many kinds of genuine treasure, yet its salt water makes reaching that treasure exceptionally difficult for land-dwelling beings, this same difficulty being the fruit of land-dwellers' own partial demerit. The lecture supplements this with three further explanations offered in the Dīrgha Āgama: that clouds periodically rain down over the entire universe, washing every impurity from the heavenly palaces on downward until it finally collects in the ocean as a single briny mixture; that an ancient sage once cursed the ocean's water to remain permanently salty and undrinkable; and that the ocean is also home to enormous creatures, some measured in the hundreds of leagues, whose own bodily waste contributes to the sea's own bitter, salty taste.

Chinese Source

略有三種補特伽羅，生那落迦不憶宿命。一、極愚癡。謂生邊地，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。二、極放逸。謂受欲者，於諸欲中增上耽著，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。三、極邪見。謂成就一切誹謗邪見，不解觀察，隨諸惡轉。由彼不能自然憶念，故令憶念。復次，二因緣故，大海水鹹。一、生彼眾生福增上故；二、陸地眾生一分非福增上故。所以者何？由水鹹故，非人所涉，生彼無量微細眾生不被採害。又大海中，種種珍寶差別可得，由水鹹故，陸地眾生一分難得。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, three kinds of beings who, born in the hells, do not remember their past lives. First, extreme ignorance — born in a borderland, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Second, extreme indulgence — one given to sensory pleasure, powerfully absorbed in craving, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Third, extreme wrong view — one who has adopted comprehensive views denying cause and effect, unable to reflect and investigate, following wrongdoing. Since they cannot naturally remember, he causes them to remember. Next, for two reasons the ocean's water is salty: first, because of the increasing merit of beings born there; second, because of a portion of demerit belonging to land-dwelling beings. Why? Because the water's saltiness keeps it from being waded through by humans, so the countless minute beings born there are not caught and killed. Also, the ocean contains all manner of distinct treasures, yet because the water is salty, a portion of land-dwelling beings find them difficult to obtain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three kinds of forgetting as a subtle but important qualification to the earlier King Yama story: even his own compassionate intervention has its limits, working specifically for those whose forgetting stems from ignorance, indulgence, or wrong view, rather than suggesting every hell-being automatically receives this same reminder regardless of their own underlying disposition. He treats the ocean's salinity explanation as a lovely example of this treatise's own consistent instinct to find karmic explanation even in the most mundane natural facts, an everyday feature of the physical world reframed as the visible fruit of countless beings' own accumulated merit and demerit working together. He notes the citation of alternate explanations from the Dīrgha Āgama as this tradition's own comfortable pluralism on cosmological detail — several accounts can coexist without one needing to be declared the sole truth, the underlying karmic principle mattering more than the precise mechanism.

4. Section: Sharp Fetters, Dull Fetters — Opening the Vast Study of Affliction

With the brief discussion of hell-beings and animals now complete, the treatise turns to its truly major undertaking for this fascicle and the next: deciding the defilement of affliction, established through five distinct aspects — its intrinsic nature, the specific varieties within that nature, the distinction between its defiled and pure forms, the distinction between what confuses it and what severs it, and the distinction among the various antidotes capable of countering it. Opening with intrinsic nature, the treatise names two basic kinds of affliction. View-natured afflictions are the five sharper fetters — the view of a self within the aggregates, the extreme view of permanence or annihilation, wrong view itself, attachment to one's own view as supreme, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances — each a genuinely mistaken conviction actively adopted about the nature of reality, sharp in the sense of being confidently, deliberately held. Non-view-natured afflictions are instead the five duller fetters — greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt — arising not from any actively adopted wrong conviction but from a more basic, sluggish confusion about ordinary experience itself, doubt included among these five specifically because its own hesitating, undecided quality lacks the same sharp, confident edge that defines the view-natured afflictions.

Chinese Source

復次，煩惱雜染決擇，我今當說。如先所說煩惱雜染義當知。此煩惱由五種相建立差別。何等為五？一、自性故；二、自性差別故；三、染淨差別故；四、迷斷差別故；五、對治差別故。云何自性？略有二種。一、見性煩惱，二、非見性煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, the deciding discernment of the defilement of affliction shall now be explained. Its meaning should be understood as already explained earlier. This affliction is established through five distinguishing aspects. What are the five? First, intrinsic nature; second, its specific varieties; third, the distinction between defiled and pure; fourth, the distinction between confusion and severance; fifth, the distinction among antidotes. What is its intrinsic nature? In brief, two kinds: view-natured affliction, and non-view-natured affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the view-natured versus non-view-natured distinction as a genuinely useful lens for recognizing one's own afflictions in daily life: the five sharper fetters are actively held convictions someone would readily defend if challenged, while the five duller fetters are more like background static, rarely examined or even noticed, yet still shaping behavior continuously beneath conscious awareness. He notes the classification of doubt among the duller fetters, despite technically also concerning the four truths, as a fine but important distinction — doubt's own hesitating, unresolved quality makes it categorically different from a confidently, wrongly held view, even though both ultimately obstruct the same clear seeing of reality. He flags that this opening definition is only the very beginning of an extensive, multi-fascicle treatment of affliction to follow, promising considerably more depth on both categories in the sections ahead.

5. Section: Ten Root Afflictions, Starting with the Self-View Even Animals Are Born With

The treatise names all ten specific varieties of root affliction: five belonging to the view-natured category, and five belonging to the non-view-natured category, together making up the entire scope of what is meant by affliction's own specific varieties. The five view-natured afflictions are self-view, extreme view, wrong view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances; the five non-view-natured afflictions are greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt. Taking up self-view first, the treatise defines it precisely: clinging, through the mind's own superimposing activity, to the five aggregates of clinging as constituting a genuine self, or as belonging to such a self — this very superimposed clinging is what self-view means. Self-view itself divides into two kinds. The innate kind is present in every ordinary, unenlightened being without exception, arising spontaneously and effortlessly even in animals — every cat, dog, pig, or sheep instinctively clings to its own body and mind as "me" and "mine" without ever having been taught to do so. The intellectually-adopted kind, by contrast, belongs specifically to non-Buddhist philosophers who, through deliberate reasoning, examination, and reflection, actively construct and adopt some particular theory of self — whether a self existing entirely apart from the aggregates, or a self identical with the aggregates themselves — arriving at self-view not spontaneously but through their own conscious philosophical reasoning.

Chinese Source

略有十種。見性煩惱五種差別，非見性者亦有五種。總此十種，名為煩惱自性差別。見性五者，謂薩迦耶見、邊執見、邪見、見取、戒禁取。非見性五者，謂貪、恚、慢、無明、疑。薩迦耶見者，於五取蘊，心執增益，見我、我所，名薩迦耶見。此復二種。一者、俱生，二、分別起。俱生者，一切愚夫異生乃至禽獸并皆現行。分別起者，諸外道等計度而起。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, ten kinds: five view-natured afflictions, and five non-view-natured. Together these ten are called affliction's own specific varieties. The five view-natured are self-view, extreme view, wrong view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances. The five non-view-natured are greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt. Self-view is clinging, through the mind's superimposing activity, to the five aggregates of clinging, seeing them as self or as belonging to self — this is called self-view. It is further of two kinds: innate, and intellectually adopted. The innate kind manifests in every foolish, ordinary being, down to animals and birds. The intellectually adopted kind arises through various non-Buddhist philosophers' own deliberate reasoning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the innate/intellectually-adopted distinction for self-view as genuinely clarifying for any student wondering why this teaching bothers refuting philosophical theories of self at all, when the far more basic, wordless clinging every creature is simply born with seems like the real target: both forms need addressing, the philosophical version simply adding a further, actively constructed layer of confusion on top of the same underlying, instinctive clinging every unenlightened being already carries from birth. He highlights the animal examples — cats, dogs, pigs, sheep — as this treatise's own vivid way of making an abstract technical definition immediately recognizable, self-clinging being something visible in the most ordinary behavior of any creature, not merely an obscure philosophical puzzle. He flags that the treatise is about to work through the remaining nine afflictions with this same careful, two-part precision, extreme view and wrong view following immediately next.

6. Section: Sixty-Two Wrong Views, Seven Ways to Believe the Self Simply Vanishes

The treatise turns to extreme view, defined as clinging, under self-view's own increasing power, to the belief that this same self is either eternally permanent or utterly annihilated at death — the permanence view and the annihilation view together constituting extreme view. The permanence view is said to be subsumed within the famous catalog of sixty-two wrong views found scattered across non-Buddhist schools, itself organized around speculation about the distant past and the distant future — theories of universal or partial eternity concerning what came before, and theories concerning consciousness, non-consciousness, or neither after death concerning what comes after. The annihilation view, meanwhile, is illustrated through seven distinct theories some ascetics and brahmins held about the self's own utter cessation at death: some held that a self composed of coarse matter and the four great elements simply ceases entirely at death; others held this specifically of a self reborn in the desire-realm heavens, or the form-realm heavens, ceasing there instead; and still others extended this same claim to a self reborn in each of the four formless attainments in turn, each group regarding its own particular realm as the very summit of existence, beyond which nothing further could possibly continue. The treatise notes that extreme view of both kinds is, strictly speaking, only ever intellectually adopted rather than innate, with one notable exception: a person who has, across many past lives, so thoroughly and repeatedly cultivated this same permanence or annihilation view that it now arises in the present life spontaneously, almost as though innate, through sheer accumulated habituation alone. The treatise then turns briefly to wrong view itself, defining it with striking breadth as any and every distorted understanding whatsoever — any view that turns away from and contradicts the true nature of whatever is actually known, regardless of its specific content, falls under this single, comprehensive category.

邊執見者，於五取蘊，薩迦耶見增上力故，心執增益，見我斷常，名邊執見。常見所攝邊執見者，謂六十二諸見趣中，計度前際諸遍常論、一分常論，及計後際諸有想論、無想論、非想非非想論。斷見所攝邊執見者，謂諸沙門、若婆羅門七事斷論。此邊執見唯分別起，無有俱生。唯除即此先世已來，串習隨逐邊執見等。邪見者，一切倒見，於所知事顛倒而轉，皆名邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Extreme view is clinging, through self-view's own increasing power, to the belief that the self is either permanent or annihilated — this is extreme view. Extreme view subsumed under the permanence view refers to, among the sixty-two views, speculation about the past through theories of universal or partial eternity, and speculation about the future through theories of having perception, lacking perception, or neither. Extreme view subsumed under the annihilation view refers to seven theories of annihilation held by various ascetics and brahmins. This extreme view is only ever intellectually adopted, never innate — except where it has, from a former life onward, been so habitually cultivated that it now recurs as though innate. Wrong view is every distorted understanding, turning away from the true nature of whatever is known — all such views are called wrong view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sixty-two views catalog as a genuinely staggering demonstration of how thoroughly ancient Indian philosophy had already mapped out every conceivable position on the self's own past and future status, long before this treatise's own careful refutation of the whole enterprise as fundamentally misguided from the start. He highlights the seven annihilation theories' own escalating structure — from a coarse physical self ceasing at ordinary death, up through increasingly refined meditative attainments each claiming to be existence's own final summit — as revealing something genuinely human in these ancient errors: even sophisticated meditators mistook their own deepest attainable state for absolute reality's own outer boundary, precisely the kind of premature closure this entire tradition warns against throughout. He notes wrong view's own remarkably broad definition — any distorted understanding whatsoever — as functioning almost as a catch-all category, with the more specific afflictions like extreme view themselves technically counting as particular instances falling within this same broader umbrella.

7. Section: The Meditator Who Mistook Bliss for Nirvāṇa, and Sixty-Two Views Sorted Three Ways

The treatise divides wrong view into two kinds: superimposing wrong view, which asserts that something exists when it genuinely does not — self-view, extreme view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts are all, in this broader sense, themselves varieties of superimposing wrong view — and negating wrong view, which denies that something exists when it genuinely does, unpacked into four specific denials. Denying cause describes the claim that giving yields no result, that honoring the worthy brings no benefit, and that ritual offering accomplishes nothing. Denying function describes the claim that neither wholesome nor unwholesome conduct actually does anything at all. Denying result describes the claim that neither wholesome nor unwholesome conduct produces any corresponding karmic fruit, whether immediate or later-ripening. And denying genuine facts describes the claim that there is no such thing as father or mother worthy of honor, no beings born spontaneously through karmic transformation, and, most strikingly, no genuine arhat anywhere in the world who has truly and permanently exhausted every affliction — a claim the lecture traces to a specific, sobering error: a meditator who attained the fourth absorption, resting there in its own deep equanimity and bliss, mistook that very stillness for nirvāṇa itself and developed the overestimation of believing himself an arhat, only to be shocked, at the moment of his own death, by unmistakable signs of an approaching rebirth — and rather than recognizing his own mistaken self-assessment, concluded instead that no genuine arhat could exist anywhere at all, since even his own apparently complete attainment had failed him. Altogether, the treatise notes, the entire catalog of sixty-two views, all rooted in self-view, sorts into exactly three categories: forty belonging to the permanence-view branch of extreme view, seven belonging to the annihilation-view branch, and fifteen counted simply as wrong views proper.

Chinese Source

當知此見略有二種。一者、增益，二者、損減。薩迦耶見、邊執見、見取、戒禁取，此四見等，一切皆名增益邪見。謗因、謗用、謗果、壞實事等，心執增益所有諸見，一切皆名損減邪見。無施、無愛，亦無祠祀，是名謗因。無有妙行，亦無惡行，是名謗用。無有妙行、惡行諸業果及異熟，是名謗果。無父無母、無化生有情，亦無世間真阿羅漢諸漏永盡，乃至廣說，如是一切名壞實事。當知如是薩迦耶見以為根本六十二見，三見所攝，謂常見所攝諸邊執見、斷見所攝諸邊執見，及諸邪見。

Ru-Yi Translation

This view is, in brief, of two kinds: superimposing, and negating. Self-view, extreme view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts are all called superimposing wrong view. Denying cause, denying function, denying result, and destroying genuine facts are all called negating wrong view. Denying that giving, honoring, or offering accomplish anything is called denying cause. Denying that wholesome or unwholesome conduct does anything is called denying function. Denying that such conduct produces any karmic fruit is called denying result. Denying father, mother, spontaneously born beings, or any genuine arhat in the world who has truly exhausted every affliction, and so on, is called destroying genuine facts. It should be understood that these sixty-two views, rooted in self-view, are subsumed under three categories: extreme view subsumed under the permanence view, extreme view subsumed under the annihilation view, and wrong views themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the meditator's own story as a genuinely cautionary tale worth remembering well beyond this specific technical context: his error was not a lack of genuine attainment, but a failure to correctly recognize the limits of what he had actually attained, and his response to being proven wrong was not humility but an even larger error, denying the very possibility of the goal he had merely mistaken his own partial progress for. He highlights the superimposing/negating distinction as this entire section's own organizing principle, useful well beyond wrong view specifically: virtually every kind of mistaken belief either adds something to reality that isn't there or subtracts something that is, these two directions covering essentially the whole space of possible error. With the sixty-two views now fully sorted into three tidy categories, he notes, the treatise turns to the remaining view-natured afflictions still to be examined.

8. Section: Clinging to a View as the Only Truth, and the Deeper Roots of Craving

The treatise turns to view-attachment, defined as clinging, with considerable forcefulness, to any single view among the sixty-two — whichever one happens to be adopted — as the very finest, the most excellent, the most supreme, the most sublime, declaring outright that only this view is genuinely true and every other view is mere falsehood, believing that this one view alone leads to purity, liberation, and genuine release from suffering. The treatise turns next to attachment to wrong precepts and observances, defined similarly: clinging to whatever ascetic discipline or ritual observance happens to accord with, support, or follow from one's own already-adopted view, mistakenly regarding that particular discipline as the very finest, most excellent, most supreme, most sublime of all possible practices, declaring with the same forcefulness that only this discipline is genuinely true and every other discipline mere falsehood, believing that this discipline alone leads to purity, liberation, and genuine release. With all five view-natured afflictions now examined, the treatise turns to the five non-view-natured afflictions, beginning with greed, defined simply as the mental factor whose very nature is to indulge in and cling to whatever is found desirable. Greed itself divides into four kinds according to its object: clinging to views themselves, and clinging to the desirable objects proper to the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless realm in turn — craving, in other words, is never confined merely to sensory pleasure, but extends even to intellectual conviction and to the most refined meditative attainments alike.

見取者，於六十二諸見趣等，一一別計為最、為上、為勝、為妙，威勢取執，隨起言說：唯此諦實，餘皆虛妄。由此見故，能得清淨、解脫、出離，是名見取。戒禁取者，謂所受持隨順見取、見取眷屬、見取隨法若戒若禁，於所受持諸戒禁中，妄計為最、為上、為勝、為妙，威勢執取，隨起言說：唯此諦實，餘皆虛妄。由此戒禁，能得清淨、解脫、出離，是名戒禁取。貪者，謂能耽著，心所為性。此復四種。謂著諸見、欲、色、無色。

Ru-Yi Translation

View-attachment is clinging, one by one, to any of the sixty-two views as the finest, most excellent, most supreme, most sublime, declaring: only this is true, all else is false. Through this view, purity, liberation, and release are believed to be attained — this is view-attachment. Attachment to wrong precepts and observances is clinging to whatever discipline accords with, belongs to, or follows from view-attachment, mistakenly regarding it as the finest, most excellent, most supreme, most sublime, declaring: only this is true, all else is false. Through this discipline, purity, liberation, and release are believed to be attained — this is attachment to wrong precepts. Greed is the mental factor whose nature is to indulge and cling. It is further of four kinds: clinging to views, and to the desire, form, and formless realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats view-attachment and precept-attachment as together revealing something important about how conviction itself can become a trap, independent of whatever specific content is being believed: the very insistence that "only this is true" is itself already a kind of clinging, regardless of whether the underlying view happens to be closer to or further from the truth. He highlights greed's own fourfold classification as a useful corrective to any assumption that craving is simply about sensory pleasure — clinging to an intellectual position, or to the refined bliss of formless meditative absorption, is shown here to be every bit as much an instance of greed as ordinary desire for food or comfort. He notes that the treatise is about to work through the remaining four non-view-natured afflictions with this same careful precision, hatred taken up next.

9. Section: Four Faces of Hatred, and the Seven Kinds of Pride Behind Every Comparison

The treatise turns to hatred, defined as the mental factor whose very nature is to wish harm upon whatever gives rise to displeasure. Hatred divides into four kinds according to its object: hatred arising when another's own view causes one harm, a familiar source of conflict whenever disagreement over ideas escalates into genuine hostility; hatred arising when another being directly causes one harm, the most ordinary and immediate form hatred takes; hatred arising when another fails to benefit, or actively harms, someone one loves, since harm to what belongs to oneself provokes self-attachment's own defensive reaction just as surely as direct personal harm does; and hatred arising simply because another shows favor toward one's own enemy, since an ordinary mind's own sharp division between loved and hated makes even a third party's kindness toward a disliked person feel like a kind of betrayal. The treatise then turns to conceit, defined as the mental factor whose nature is to make the mind swell with a sense of its own elevation, always arising on the basis of some underlying self-view and some comparison, whether real or imagined, against other beings. Conceit divides into four kinds according to its object: conceit arising from one's own views, clinging to one's own opinion as superior and final; conceit arising in relation to other beings, comparing oneself against those judged inferior, equal, or superior; conceit arising from the pleasures one currently enjoys, taking pride in especially fine or excellent possessions and circumstances; and conceit arising in relation to some desirable future rebirth, the mind swelling with anticipated satisfaction even before that future result has actually arrived.

Chinese Source

恚者，謂能損害，心所為性。此復四種。謂於損己他見、他有情所，及於所愛不饒益所、於所不愛作饒益所所有瞋恚。慢者，謂令心舉，心所為性。此復四種。謂於諸見、於諸有情、於受用欲、於諸後有處起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hatred is the mental factor whose nature is to cause harm. It is further of four kinds: hatred toward another's view that has harmed oneself, toward another being who has harmed oneself, toward failure to benefit what one loves, and toward benefit shown to what one does not love. Conceit is the mental factor whose nature is to make the mind swell. It is further of four kinds, arising in relation to views, to other beings, to enjoyed pleasures, and to future existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats hatred's fourth kind — resenting kindness shown to one's own enemy — as a genuinely sharp psychological observation, capturing a pettiness many people recognize in themselves only reluctantly: it is not enough to dislike someone, an ordinary mind often wants everyone else to dislike them too, and feels quietly betrayed when they do not. He highlights conceit's own dependence on comparison, whether against other beings, against one's own past circumstances, or against an anticipated future, as revealing just how relational this particular affliction always is — pride rarely arises in a vacuum, almost always measuring the self against some real or imagined other. He flags that conceit's own remaining classifications, distinguishing accurate from genuinely mistaken forms of this same swelling pride, are about to follow in considerably more technical detail.

10. Section: Seven Kinds of Pride, and the Difference Between Confidence and Delusion

The treatise offers a second classification of conceit, dividing it into two broader kinds: unconfused conceit, and confused conceit. Unconfused conceit is genuine pride that nonetheless accords with the actual facts — feeling superior to someone genuinely inferior in wealth, family standing, wisdom, or following, or feeling equal to someone genuinely comparable in these same respects, the underlying comparison itself being accurate even though the swelling of pride remains, technically, an affliction. Confused conceit, by contrast, is pride that positively contradicts the actual facts, and covers the remaining six of the traditional seven kinds of pride recognized in this tradition: feeling superior to, or merely equal to, someone who is actually superior to oneself, both plainly mistaken; feeling only slightly inferior to someone who genuinely and vastly surpasses oneself, likewise a distortion of the real gap involved; the pride of clinging to a self within the five aggregates where no such genuine self can be found at all; the overestimation of believing oneself to have attained some meditative state or noble fruit not actually attained; and the wrongheaded pride of believing some genuinely worthless undertaking to possess real merit, such as constructing a shrine to some minor spirit and imagining this to be a genuinely meritorious deed. The treatise then examines this same confused/unconfused distinction through two further lenses. Concerning enjoyed resources: taking pride in wealth and happiness while actually making use of only coarse, inferior possessions is confused conceit, mistaking one's actual circumstances; taking that same pride while genuinely enjoying excellent, superior possessions is unconfused conceit, an accurate assessment even if still technically prideful. Concerning anticipated future existence: taking pride in an expected excellent rebirth while having actually engaged in wrongful conduct is confused conceit, since wrongful conduct could never actually produce such a result; taking that same pride while having genuinely practiced uprightly is unconfused conceit, the expectation this time actually well-founded.

Chinese Source

又此慢略有二種。一、惑亂慢，二、不惑亂慢。不惑亂慢者，謂於下劣計己為勝、於等計等而生憍慢。惑亂慢者，謂餘六慢。又由受用鄙劣資具，自謂富樂，名惑亂慢。若由受用勝妙資具，自謂富樂，名不惑亂慢。又由邪行，謂後有勝，名惑亂慢。若由正行，謂後有勝，名不惑亂慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

This conceit is, in brief, of two kinds: confused conceit, and unconfused conceit. Unconfused conceit is feeling superior to one genuinely inferior, or equal to one genuinely equal. Confused conceit is the remaining six kinds. Also, taking pride in wealth and happiness while using only inferior resources is confused conceit; while using genuinely excellent resources, it is unconfused conceit. Taking pride in a superior future existence through wrongful conduct is confused conceit; through upright conduct, it is unconfused conceit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the confused/unconfused distinction as a genuinely important qualification within this whole discussion of conceit: even accurate self-assessment, when it swells into pride, still counts as an affliction requiring eventual correction, this treatise never suggesting that "unconfused" conceit is therefore harmless simply because its underlying comparison happens to be factually correct. He highlights the shrine-building example — genuine effort spent on something of no real spiritual value, mistaken for genuine merit — as a small but pointed warning against confusing sincere activity with actually worthwhile activity, sincerity alone never being sufficient to guarantee genuine benefit. He notes that with hatred and conceit now both fully examined, the treatise turns next to ignorance, the affliction this tradition consistently treats as standing at the very root of all the others.

11. Section: The Ignorance Even an Arhat Still Carries

The treatise turns to ignorance, defined as the mental factor whose nature is to obscure and obstruct genuine awakening to the true nature of whatever can be known. Ignorance divides first into four kinds. Uncomprehending ignorance describes simply never having understood selflessness at all, having never observed or reflected on it in the first place. Negligent ignorance describes a lapse in someone who has genuinely understood before, losing mindfulness and falling into distraction despite already possessing the relevant insight. Defiled ignorance describes the confusion bound up with the four fundamental distortions — mistaking the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, the selfless for a genuine self, and the impure for pure — the ignorance underlying a self mistakenly clung to where none exists. And undefiled ignorance describes a subtler confusion that persists even once that self-clinging distortion has been fully overcome — a lingering failure to fully realize the selflessness of phenomena themselves, present even in fully accomplished arhats and solitary realizers, who have entirely eliminated the first three kinds yet still carry this fourth, most refined kind of ignorance. Ignorance divides a second way as well, into ignorance associated with other afflictions, since no affliction whatsoever, whether greed or any other, can actually arise without ignorance's own underlying confusion accompanying it, and independently arising ignorance, present even without any accompanying root affliction, simply through unwise attention toward the four truths in a duller-natured person, an inability to properly discern their meaning that leaves the mind clouded and obscured on its own. This independently arising ignorance itself divides further, in this tradition's own careful analysis, into a form arising alongside the greater and middling secondary afflictions where ignorance's own character remains the dominant, visible feature, a form arising alongside lesser secondary afflictions where those afflictions' own character dominates instead, and, most distinctively, a constantly active form associated specifically with the seventh consciousness, present without interruption in every unenlightened being since beginningless time, arising together with self-view, self-love, and self-conceit, and obstructing genuine wisdom at the deepest, most continuous level of all.

Chinese Source

無明者，謂於所知真實覺悟能覆、能障，心所為性。此略四種。一、無解愚，二、放逸愚，三、染汙愚，四、不染汙愚。若於不見聞覺知所知義中所有無智，名無解愚。若於見聞覺知所知義中散亂失念所有無智，名放逸愚。於顛倒心所有無智，名染汙愚。不顛倒心所有無智，名不染汙愚。又此無明總有二種。一、煩惱相應無明，二、獨行無明。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ignorance is the mental factor whose nature is to obscure and obstruct genuine awakening to what is known. It is, in brief, of four kinds: uncomprehending ignorance, negligent ignorance, defiled ignorance, and undefiled ignorance. Not understanding what has never been seen, heard, sensed, or cognized is called uncomprehending ignorance. Losing mindfulness amid distraction, regarding what has been seen, heard, sensed, or cognized, is called negligent ignorance. Confusion bound up with a distorted mind is called defiled ignorance. Confusion without such distortion is called undefiled ignorance. This ignorance is also, altogether, of two kinds: ignorance associated with affliction, and independently arising ignorance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats undefiled ignorance's own persistence even in fully accomplished arhats as one of this tradition's own most humbling technical teachings: liberation from the cycle of birth and death does not automatically mean complete omniscience, a subtler confusion about the selflessness of phenomena themselves remaining even after every affliction bound up with self-clinging has been entirely eliminated. He highlights the seventh-consciousness-associated constantly active ignorance as a distinctively Yogācāra contribution to this whole discussion, identifying a level of confusion so continuous and fundamental that an ordinary being never experiences even a single moment free of it, arising inseparably with self-view, self-love, and self-conceit from beginningless time onward. He notes that with ignorance now fully classified, only doubt remains among the ten root afflictions still to be examined.

12. Section: Five Kinds of Doubt, and Closing the Catalog of Ten Root Afflictions

The treatise turns to its final root affliction, doubt, defined as the mental factor whose nature is simply hesitating, undecided, between two possibilities. Doubt is established through five distinct aspects. Doubt about other lives questions whether past and future existence genuinely occur at all. Doubt about function questions whether an intermediate-state being genuinely exists between death and rebirth, and whether parents genuinely play the specific role attributed to them in a new being's own conception. Doubt about cause and effect questions whether wholesome and unwholesome conduct truly differ in their underlying cause, and whether such conduct truly yields its corresponding pleasant or painful result. Doubt about the truths questions whether the four noble truths of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path are genuinely real. And doubt about the Three Jewels questions whether the Buddha, the teaching, and the community actually possess the genuine qualities attributed to them. With this, the entire catalog of ten root afflictions — the five view-natured and five non-view-natured kinds first named at the very opening of this discussion — now stands fully classified in its every specific variety. The treatise closes this whole stretch with a further clarifying observation: every one of these ten afflictions can take as its object either ordinary phenomena directly, such as the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields, or other afflictions themselves, one affliction capable of triggering or reinforcing another in an ongoing, self-perpetuating chain.

Chinese Source

疑者，猶豫二分不決定，心所為性。當知此疑，略由五相差別建立。謂於他世作用、因、果、諸諦、寶中，心懷猶豫。如是所說十種煩惱，亦緣事轉、亦緣煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Doubt is the mental factor whose nature is hesitating, undecided, between two possibilities. It should be understood that this doubt is, in brief, established through five distinguishing aspects: hesitation concerning other lives, function, cause and effect, the truths, and the Three Jewels. These ten afflictions just described take as their object both ordinary phenomena and other afflictions themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats doubt's own fivefold structure as tracing a natural progression from the most basic uncertainty — does anything at all continue beyond this present life — through increasingly specific uncertainty about karma's own mechanics, culminating in doubt about the very framework, the four truths, and the very community of practice, the Three Jewels, that this entire tradition depends on. He highlights the closing observation, that every affliction can take either ordinary phenomena or other afflictions as its object, as an important structural point worth carrying forward: afflictions do not simply arise in isolation reacting to the world, but actively feed one another in a self-sustaining tangle, exactly why uprooting even one root affliction thoroughly can have such disproportionate benefit for the whole afflicted system at once. With the entire catalog of specific varieties now closed, he notes, the treatise turns next to its third establishing aspect: the distinction between defilement and purity.

13. Section: Why Lower Realms Can Think About Higher Ones, But Not the Reverse

The treatise closes its examination of what each affliction takes as its object: all ten root afflictions can mutually reference every affliction belonging to their own realm, and can likewise take as their object whatever outflow-bound phenomena belong to that same realm. Afflictions belonging to a lower realm can additionally take a higher realm's own afflictions and phenomena as their object, yet the reverse does not hold — a higher realm's own afflictions cannot directly reference a lower realm's afflictions and phenomena, since beings in higher realms, already possessing their own realm's more excellent qualities, naturally regard themselves as superior and give the lower realms no further thought. The treatise then turns to a new distinction: the moral quality of the ten root afflictions, illustrated through self-view as its representative example. Innate self-view is purely morally neutral, since it operates constantly and gently in the background of ordinary experience without ever rising to the level of seriously harming oneself or others. Intellectually-adopted self-view, by contrast, is genuinely unwholesome when it occurs in the desire realm, its firm, deliberately reasoned grasping making it considerably more forceful than the innate kind. When that same intellectually-adopted self-view occurs instead in the form or formless realms, it becomes morally neutral once again, restrained there by the power of calm-abiding meditation and surrounded by predominantly wholesome qualities — yet it remains, even so, a concealed kind of neutrality, since its underlying essence is still a defiled wisdom obstructing the noble path, rather than a genuinely innocent neutrality free of any obstruction at all. The very same reasoning, the treatise notes, extends by analogy to each of the remaining nine root afflictions in turn.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Afflictions belonging to a lower realm can take a higher realm's afflictions and phenomena as their object; a higher realm's afflictions cannot take a lower realm's afflictions and phenomena as their object. Next, innate self-view is purely neutral, since it operates constantly yet does not severely harm oneself or others. Intellectually-adopted self-view, being firmly grasped, differs from this — in the desire realm it is purely unwholesome. In the higher realms, restrained by the power of calm-abiding and surrounded by predominantly wholesome qualities, it becomes neutral, yet remains, because of its defiled nature, a concealed kind of neutrality. The remaining afflictions should be understood by this same principle, according to what applies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the asymmetry in what different realms can reference — lower realms reaching upward, higher realms never reaching back down — as a subtle but telling detail about the psychology of spiritual attainment itself: genuine progress does not require constant awareness of what has been left behind, a being genuinely settled in a higher realm's own excellence simply has no occasion to dwell on lower states at all. He highlights "concealed neutrality" as this section's own important nuance: a technically neutral mental state is not automatically a harmless one, since concealment specifically names a neutrality that still obstructs the noble path even while causing no obvious immediate harm, a distinction worth remembering whenever meditative calm is mistaken for genuine purification.

14. Section: Three Conditions That Actually Send an Affliction to a Miserable Rebirth

The treatise turns to a fuller examination of unwholesome afflictions, explaining first why desire-realm afflictions are so predominantly unwholesome: they serve as the very foundation upon which unwholesome conduct itself takes root and stands, making unwholesomeness their own default character. The treatise then names three specific conditions that, together, determine whether an unwholesome affliction will actually lead to a miserable rebirth — other afflictions lacking all three conditions do not reliably produce this same result. The first condition is extensive, earnest, and uninterrupted cultivation of the affliction, mistaking it for something genuinely beneficial, never recognizing its fault or its harm, and indulging it without any restraint whatsoever. The second condition is actually relying on that same affliction as a basis for committing and further compounding unwholesome deeds through body, speech, and mind. And the third condition is using that affliction to actively sever another being's own wholesome roots and instill unwholesome ones in their place — precisely the pattern seen, the lecture notes, in extremists who not only hold the conviction that killing leads to heavenly reward, but actively teach that same conviction to others, drawing them into the very same wrongdoing. The treatise clarifies that this threefold analysis concerns only affliction generated in this present life, excluding karma from a past life whose result is simply delayed until some later rebirth, a different matter entirely from what actually determines an affliction's own immediate trajectory. The treatise then turns to a further distinction: which of the ten root afflictions correspond to which of the six consciousnesses. Seven of the ten belong exclusively to the mind-consciousness alone, while greed, hatred, and ignorance also extend to the five sense-consciousnesses as well, these three alone capable of accompanying direct sensory experience itself, not only conceptual reflection.

Chinese Source

Unwholesome afflictions possessing three conditions definitely lead to a miserable rebirth; other afflictions do not necessarily do so. What are the three? Extensive, earnest, uninterrupted cultivation, mistaken for merit, its fault and harm unseen, indulged without restraint — this is the first condition. Relying on this affliction as a basis for committing and increasing unwholesome deeds through body, speech, and mind — this is the second. Using this affliction to sever another's wholesome roots and instill unwholesome ones — this is the third. This excludes karma from a past life whose result is delayed to a later rebirth. Also, of the ten afflictions, seven belong only to the mind ground; greed, hatred, and ignorance also extend to the five sense-consciousnesses.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three conditions for a miserable rebirth as offering genuine, practical diagnostic value beyond pure classification: an affliction merely arisen once, quickly recognized as harmful and released, is categorically different from one repeatedly cultivated, actively acted upon, and spread to others, even though both might technically be called the same affliction in the abstract. He highlights the explicit reference to those who teach that killing leads to heavenly reward as a sober, contemporary-feeling illustration of the third condition's own severity — actively corrupting another person's own wholesome roots being treated as a distinct and serious escalation beyond merely harboring or acting on one's own affliction. He notes the correspondence with the six consciousnesses as a fittingly technical close to this stretch, greed, hatred, and ignorance's own extension into the five senses explaining why these three, uniquely among the ten, can arise the very instant a desirable or aversive object is first perceived, without needing conceptual reflection to intervene first.

15. Section: Which Feelings Accompany Which Affliction, and What Each One Actually Latches Onto

The treatise details, for the desire realm, exactly which feelings accompany which afflictions, understood as a broad rather than absolute correspondence, with subtler exceptions to be addressed later. Four of the five views, together with conceit, are accompanied by joy and equanimity. Greed is accompanied by pleasure, joy, and equanimity. Hatred is accompanied by pain, sorrow, and equanimity. Wrong view specifically is accompanied by joy, sorrow, and equanimity. Doubt is accompanied by sorrow and equanimity. And ignorance alone is accompanied by all five feelings without exception. In the higher realms, this same correspondence simply follows whichever feelings that realm itself actually possesses. The treatise then turns to what each affliction actually takes as its object, distinguishing those that lack any genuine substance from those that possess it. Afflictions severed through the path of vision are said to lack substance, since they arise predominantly through conceptual misunderstanding of emptiness itself, their object never being anything genuinely, conventionally real in the first place — self-view, extreme view, and attachment to wrong precepts all cling to something that was never conventionally there to begin with. The remaining afflictions, severed instead through the path of cultivation, are said by contrast to possess genuine substance, their object being a real, ordinary phenomenon rather than a purely conceptual error. The treatise then distinguishes a further pair: greed and conceit take only a partial object, arising specifically in relation to whatever outflow-bound thing happens to be found desirable, while hatred likewise takes only a partial object, arising specifically in relation to whatever is found undesirable — these three alone confined to a specific, limited range. The remaining seven afflictions, by contrast, are called pervasive, ranging freely across every possible object, internal or external, desirable, undesirable, or neither, taking the entire field of outflow-bound experience as their potential scope.

Chinese Source

Afflictions severed by the path of vision lack genuine substance, their object never being conventionally real. Greed and conceit arise in relation to a partial, desirable outflow-bound object; hatred arises in relation to a partial, undesirable one — these three are said to take a partial object. The remaining afflictions, ranging across internal and external, desirable, undesirable, and neither, are called pervasive, taking every object as their scope.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats ignorance's own unique correspondence with all five feelings as a fitting confirmation of everything already said about it — standing at the root of every other affliction, it makes sense that ignorance alone accompanies every possible felt quality of experience, never confined the way greed or hatred are to a narrower emotional register. He highlights the has-substance/lacks-substance distinction as directly paralleling the earlier explanation of extreme view and self-view as purely intellectually constructed errors, this same logic now generalized across the entire path-of-vision category of affliction. He notes the partial/pervasive distinction as a useful reminder that not every affliction operates the same way: greed and hatred are, relatively speaking, narrowly targeted, while something like doubt or ignorance can, in principle, attach itself to absolutely anything at all.

16. Section: A New Topic Opens — What Are the Secondary Afflictions?

With the ten root afflictions now examined from every angle, the treatise turns to an entirely new topic within this same broader study: the secondary afflictions, defined as whatever accords with these same ten root afflictions, arises together with them, or belongs to their same general category. The treatise establishes this category through four distinguishing aspects. Some secondary afflictions pervade every unwholesome state of mind without exception, present whenever any unwholesome mental state occurs at all. Some pervade every defiled state of mind more broadly, including neutral-but-obstructed states as well as outright unwholesome ones. Some arise only in connection with a specific, particular unwholesome state of mind, rather than accompanying unwholesome states universally. And some arise across wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral states of mind alike, yet not in every possible place or at every possible time, appearing only under more limited, specific circumstances even within this broader range.

Chinese Source

若有隨順如是煩惱、煩惱俱行、煩惱品類，名隨煩惱。云何名隨煩惱？略由四相差別建立。一、通一切不善心起；二、通一切染汙心起；三、於各別不善心起；四、善、不善、無記心起，非一切處、非一切時。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever accords with these afflictions, arises together with them, or belongs to their category, is called secondary affliction. What is secondary affliction? It is established, in brief, through four distinguishing aspects: pervading every unwholesome mind; pervading every defiled mind; arising with a specific unwholesome mind; and arising with wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral minds, though not in every place or at every time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold framework as the treatise's own organizing key to the entire, considerably longer list of secondary afflictions about to follow — the greater, middling, and lesser secondary afflictions this tradition catalogs elsewhere, each falling into one of exactly these four categories depending on how broadly or narrowly it actually operates. He notes that secondary afflictions are best understood as satellites of the ten root afflictions already examined at such length, deriving their own defiled character from association with those roots rather than possessing some entirely independent source of their own, exactly as the definition's own opening phrase — according with, co-arising with, or belonging to the same category as the root afflictions — makes explicit.

17. Section: Shamelessness, Ten Great Afflictions, and Ten That Never Arise Together

The treatise names the secondary afflictions belonging to each of its four categories. Pervading every unwholesome mind without exception are just two: shamelessness, the absence of any inner sense of conscience, and lack of regard for others, the absence of any concern for how one's own conduct reflects on or affects other people — these two accompanying every unwholesome thought, since without them, greed, hatred, and the rest could never actually arise unchecked in the first place. Pervading every defiled mind more broadly, spanning all three realms without exception, are ten further secondary afflictions: heedlessness, restlessness, dullness, lack of faith, laziness, wrongly directed desire, wrongly held conviction, wrongly oriented mindfulness, distraction, and incorrect discernment — each of these ten present whenever any defiled state of mind occurs at all, whether outright unwholesome or merely neutral-but-obstructed. Arising instead only individually, one at a time, with a specific unwholesome mind, and never accompanied by any of the others at that same moment, are ten more: wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation, envy, stinginess, deceit, flattery, arrogance, and the wish to harm — nine of these ten confined entirely to the desire realm, with only deceit and flattery extending as far as the first meditative absorption, since both are so closely tied to the pursuit of reputation and material gain that they persist a little further than most, and arrogance alone extending across all three realms, present even in beings intoxicated by their own refined attainments, though in the higher realms restrained by meditative power into a merely neutral-but-obstructed form.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats shamelessness and lack of regard for others as this entire catalog's own foundational pair, worth remembering as the quiet precondition beneath every other unwholesome state: without some baseline indifference to conscience and to others' judgment, the sharper afflictions like wrath or envy would have nowhere to actually take root and act themselves out. He highlights the "never arise together" rule for the ten individually-arising afflictions as a genuinely useful piece of introspective psychology: a single unwholesome moment tends to be dominated by one particular flavor of affliction at a time, wrath or envy or stinginess taking its turn rather than all arising simultaneously in some chaotic simultaneous swarm. He notes deceit, flattery, and arrogance's own unusual persistence into the higher meditative realms as a sobering reminder that even considerable meditative attainment does not automatically eliminate every subtler form of self-regard.

18. Section: A Monk's Cure for a Dying Man — Three Kinds of Delusive Thought

The treatise names its fourth and final category of secondary affliction: four factors that arise across wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral states of mind alike, yet neither universally nor at every moment — examination, reflection, regret, and drowsiness. Prolonged, exhausting examination and reflection eventually tire the body, dull mindfulness, and strain the mind itself, which is precisely why they count as afflictions despite sometimes accompanying wholesome states, and why their own presence extends only as far as the first meditative absorption, beyond which such coarse mental activity no longer occurs. Regret and drowsiness, by contrast, belong exclusively to the desire realm, since beings in the higher realms, free of any occasion for regretting past conduct and sustained instead by meditative absorption rather than ordinary sleep, never experience either. The lecture illustrates prolonged examination and reflection's own genuinely harmful potential with a story of a man bedridden for years by a wasting illness no medicine could cure, visited at last by a wandering monk who diagnosed his condition as rooted in affliction, and affliction as rooted in delusive thought of three specific kinds — dwelling obsessively on decades of past glory, grudges, and idle memory; anxiously hesitating over present matters that could simply be met as they arise; and endlessly scheming after some hoped-for future of wealth, honor, and flourishing descendants that may never actually come to pass. The monk taught him that such thoughts, ceaselessly arising and passing, are themselves nothing but illusion, and that recognizing them as illusion the instant they arise is itself enough to dissolve them — not fearing a thought's arising, only fearing failing to notice it in time, since a noticed thought is itself already medicine, while an unnoticed one left to continue is itself already sickness. The man withdrew to a single quiet room, cleared his mind of every entanglement, and sat in gathered stillness for over a month, at the end of which his illness had genuinely begun to lift. The treatise closes this entire study of the secondary afflictions by confirming that every variety named anywhere else in scripture — grief, lamentation, sorrow, distress, and every other form of disturbance described in the sūtras and this treatise's own broader compendium — reduces without exception to these same four established categories, nothing left over that these four do not already account for.

Regret and drowsiness belong only to the desire realm. All the varieties of secondary affliction the Blessed One spoke of elsewhere, extending to grief, lamentation, sorrow, and distress, and all that is analyzed at length in the Compendium of Explanations, are without exception subsumed under these same four categories, according to what applies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the bedridden patient's story as one of this fascicle's own most genuinely therapeutic teachings, offering a piece of practical wisdom any listener could apply directly to their own restless mind: the specific insight that a recognized thought is already medicine, while an unrecognized one left to run its course is already sickness, reframing meditation's own basic task not as suppressing thought but simply as noticing it promptly. He highlights the three kinds of delusive thinking — obsessive rehearsal of the past, anxious hesitation over the present, endless scheming toward an uncertain future — as a remarkably precise, still entirely recognizable map of ordinary mental restlessness, exactly the pattern any modern meditator discovers the first time they actually try to sit quietly and observe their own mind. He notes the closing confirmation — every scriptural term for affliction reducing to these same four categories — as bringing this entire, extensive study of the secondary afflictions to a tidy and satisfying close.

19. Section: Twelve Situations Where Affliction Takes Root, and the Monk Who Hid His Own Excrement

The treatise now maps the secondary afflictions onto twelve specific situations in which they characteristically arise: clinging to bad conduct; quarreling and contention; breaking ethical discipline; following a bad teacher's own unwholesome ways; wrong livelihood; indulging in sensory desire; reflecting carefully on a teaching just heard; stilling the mind in meditative practice on that same teaching's meaning; sharing wealth or teaching with others; living incompatibly among fellow practitioners; dwelling apart in solitary lodging; and the general gathering-place of every kind of suffering. Greed, hatred, and delusion — the three unwholesome roots underlying every unwholesome deed — arise from the very first situation, clinging to bad conduct itself, since it is precisely through self-attachment and attachment to phenomena that desirable objects provoke greed, undesirable objects provoke hatred, and neutral objects provoke delusion in turn. The eight afflictions of wrath, resentment, concealment, vexation, envy, stinginess, deceit, and flattery all arise from the second situation, quarreling and contention: wrath when hostility first flares visibly across the face, resentment when that same hostility knots itself into a lingering grudge nursed silently rather than shown, vexation when old resentment ignites into sudden harsh, coarse outbursts upon meeting the same old friction again, and concealment when fear of losing one's own reputation or gain drives someone to hide a wrongdoing they have actually committed. The lecture illustrates concealment's own subtlety with a story of a Tibetan monk in solitary retreat, who, expecting a lay visitor, at first swept and decorated his shrine room beautifully to make a good impression — only to catch himself mid-task, realizing this impulse was itself a kind of flattery, concealing his own ordinary, unkempt daily habits behind a performance staged for someone else's benefit, and so deliberately placed his own excrement back in plain view instead, for which his own teacher, learning what had happened, praised him as a genuinely honest practitioner.

Ru-Yi Translation

Clinging to bad conduct; quarreling and contention; breaking ethical discipline; following the unwholesome ways of a bad teacher; wrong livelihood; indulging in desire; reflecting carefully on a teaching just heard; stilling the mind through meditative practice on its meaning; mutually sharing wealth and teaching; living incompatibly among fellow practitioners; dwelling apart in solitary lodging; and the gathering-place of every suffering. Greed, hatred, and delusion arise from the first situation; wrath through flattery arise from the second.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this entire twelve-situation mapping as this fascicle's own most practically useful contribution to the whole study of affliction, moving decisively from abstract classification toward genuinely recognizable, everyday circumstances where a practitioner can actually catch a specific affliction arising in real time. He treats the Tibetan monk's own story as an unusually vivid illustration of concealment's own subtlety: the impulse to look good for a visitor is so ordinary that most people would never even notice it as a form of flattery, let alone catch and correct it in the very moment of doing so, making this monk's own reversal a genuinely striking example of honest self-scrutiny in action. He notes that the remaining ten situations, each generating their own specific cluster of afflictions, promise an equally concrete and recognizable account of where affliction actually takes root in ordinary practice.

20. Section: A Son's Debt Repaid as a Pig — Wrong Livelihood and Its Consequences

The treatise continues its mapping: shamelessness and lack of regard for others both arise specifically from breaking ethical discipline, since it is precisely the absence of any inner or outer sense of accountability that permits a precept to be broken in the first place. Deceit, flattery, arrogance, and the wish to harm all arise from following a bad teacher's own unwholesome ways, close association with unwholesome company naturally drawing a person into these same unwholesome patterns. And a further cluster of afflictions — feigned pretense, outright deception, and regret among them — arises from wrong livelihood itself, illustrated through five classic ways ancient monastics were warned against improperly seeking material support: falsely displaying some extraordinary spiritual attainment to inspire donors' reverence; boasting of one's own abilities to win others' confidence; practicing fortune-telling or divination for material gain; speaking loudly and affecting an imposing manner to inspire awe; and strategically mentioning gifts received from one donor to prompt greater generosity from another. The lecture illustrates the seriousness of misusing even a family member's own wealth with a striking story from the Sūtra of Cause and Effect: a boy repeatedly stole money from his own father, who reasoned that since the money would eventually belong to his son anyway, no real harm was being done — yet the boy grew increasingly corrupted by this pattern and died young, only to be reborn as a pig in that very same household. When the father moved to slaughter the pig for food, it appeared to him in a dream, begging to be spared and revealing that it was, in fact, his own son, reborn specifically to repay through its own body the exact debt those stolen sums represented — a visiting practitioner, consulted afterward, confirmed the pig's own account, teaching the father that even a parent's wealth, however freely it might eventually have been given, still incurs a genuine debt when simply taken without permission.

Shamelessness and lack of regard for others arise from the third situation; deceit through the wish to harm arise from the fourth; feigned pretense through regret arise from the fifth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pig-reincarnation story as this section's own memorable and genuinely sobering illustration of a principle easy to state abstractly but harder to feel viscerally: that even wealth belonging to one's own family carries a genuine debt when taken without permission, however casually a parent might excuse the taking in the moment. He highlights the five wrong ways of seeking livelihood as remarkably specific and still entirely recognizable warnings against exploiting others' generosity or credulity for material gain, whether through false spiritual display, self-promotion, fortune-telling, intimidation, or strategic hinting — a checklist any sincere practitioner, ancient or modern, could still use to examine their own conduct honestly. He notes that the remaining situations — indulgence in desire, mis-heard teachings, meditative obstacles, shared resources, incompatible cohabitation, solitary retreat, and suffering itself — each generate their own further, equally specific clusters of affliction still to come.

21. Section: From Sensory Indulgence to the Five Hindrances Blocking Meditation

The treatise continues its twelve-situation mapping through five further loci. Indulgence in sensory desire generates an entire cluster of afflictions: an inability to bear insult without retaliating in kind, deep attachment to one's own already-enjoyed pleasures, an even broader craving extending to whatever pleasures belong to others as well, unlawful craving directed toward wrongdoing itself, wrongful clinging to possessions that are not genuinely one's own, such as treating a parent's wealth as though it were already one's own without their actual consent, and the corrupted greed of denying a debt owed to someone who has entrusted something into one's own care. Reflecting on a teaching just heard, if that reflection goes astray, can itself generate self-view, the view of continued existence after death, and the view of utter annihilation at death — precisely the danger of hearing genuine teaching yet failing to reflect on it correctly, whether through exposure to a non-Buddhist doctrine or through careless misreading of the Buddha's own teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness. Stilling the mind in meditative practice generates the five hindrances that obstruct calm-abiding directly — sensory desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and regret, and doubt — together with a heavy, sluggish inattentiveness, an unreasonable, joyless despondency, physical restlessness that makes sitting still nearly impossible, and immoderate eating that itself disturbs the body and mind's own capacity for stillness. Sharing wealth or teaching with others generates a lingering, clinging attachment to what is being shared, likened to a heavy drowsiness that obscures the very mind meant to remain clear and generous. And living incompatibly among fellow practitioners generates a lack of straightforwardness toward one's own teachers and companions, harsh and unyielding conduct in body and speech, and a basic incompatibility in discipline, view, and livelihood that keeps genuine communal harmony from ever actually forming.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifth hindrance's own accompanying afflictions — sluggish inattentiveness, joyless despondency, physical restlessness, and immoderate eating — as this section's own genuinely useful checklist for any meditator struggling to actually sit still, each obstacle recognizable enough that most practitioners will find at least one uncomfortably familiar. He highlights the seventh situation's own warning as a subtle but important one: even genuinely hearing correct teaching offers no automatic protection against wrong conclusions if the subsequent reflection itself goes astray, self-view and its extreme variants capable of arising even from garbled or careless engagement with authentic Buddhist material, not only from exposure to outright non-Buddhist doctrine. He notes that the treatise is about to turn to its most extensively developed situation of all — solitary retreat, and the eight kinds of harmful discursive thought that arise there.

22. Section: The Eight Thoughts That Ambush a Meditator in Solitude

The treatise turns to its eleventh situation, dwelling apart in solitary lodging, generating eight distinct kinds of harmful discursive thought that repeatedly ambush a meditator precisely when external distraction has been removed and only the mind's own restlessness remains to contend with. Thought of desire arises when the mind wanders back toward remembered sensory pleasure, dwelling there and drawing corresponding satisfaction from the very reflection itself. Thought of hatred arises when old resentment toward another being resurfaces, the mind rehearsing harm it wishes it could inflict. Thought of harm arises similarly, dwelling specifically on causing another being actual distress or injury — precisely the impulse this tradition's own teaching that every being has, across countless lifetimes, been one's own parent warns against indulging. Thought of family arises when the mind drifts toward relatives left behind, worrying over their wellbeing or delighting in shared memories, a thought this tradition considers genuinely counterproductive for a renunciant who has already deliberately left family ties behind to pursue the path. Thought of homeland arises when the mind wanders toward some distant, more pleasant place, imagining greater ease or beauty there than present circumstances offer — a restlessness that, the lecture notes, itself undermines concentration regardless of whether the distant place in question is genuinely as pleasant as imagined. Thought of immortality, or delusion about death, arises when a meditator, still young or in reasonably good health, quietly assumes there will be ample time later to practice seriously, resolving to first finish other pursuits, accumulate followers, or complete various worldly projects before truly committing to the path — a self-deception this tradition considers especially dangerous, since death's own timing is never actually predictable, and dying with this same procrastination still unresolved leaves a person, in the lecture's own blunt phrase, no better off than an animal that simply expired one day without ever having accomplished anything of genuine worth.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the thought of immortality as this entire eightfold list's own most quietly dangerous member, precisely because it never announces itself as an affliction at all, dressing itself up instead as simple reasonableness — of course there is time later, why rush. He notes the tradition's own remedy cited alongside it: reflecting deliberately and often on death's genuine unpredictability, treating each day's practice with the same urgency as someone whose hair has caught fire, rather than assuming tomorrow is guaranteed. He highlights thought of family and thought of homeland together as revealing something genuinely human about even sincere renunciation: leaving attachment behind is rarely a single completed act, but something the mind keeps quietly trying to undo in solitude, which is exactly why this tradition names these wandering thoughts explicitly rather than assuming a committed practitioner would simply never experience them. He flags that two further kinds of harmful thought — contempt born of comparing oneself to others, and preoccupation with one's own family standing — are about to be unpacked in their own right.

23. Section: Closing the Twelve Loci — Contempt, False Charity, and the Fire Beneath All Suffering

The treatise names the final two of the eight harmful thoughts arising in solitary retreat. Thought bound up with contempt arises when a meditator's mind drifts toward comparing itself favorably against others — their lineage, appearance, wealth, skill, ethical discipline, sharpness of faculty, meditative attainment, or wisdom all judged inferior to one's own — a comparison this tradition considers thoroughly unfounded, since all such distinctions are impermanent, since every body alike is filled with the same impure substances beneath the skin, since old age, sickness, and death spare no one regardless of present standing, and since this very sense of superiority is itself simply another expression of the same underlying ignorance a wise person would never indulge. Thought bound up with family status arises when a meditator's mind wanders toward wishing greater prosperity specifically upon lay supporters, imagining how their increased wealth might translate into greater generosity toward oneself or one's community — a thought that might seem, at first glance, to resemble genuine compassion, yet is rejected here on the grounds that merely wishing worldly benefit upon someone accomplishes nothing real for them at all, while the wishing itself only disturbs a meditator's own settled concentration to no productive end. The treatise then closes its entire twelve-situation mapping with the final locus: the general gathering-place of every kind of suffering, generating grief, lamentation, physical pain, mental sorrow, and overall distress — arising, as scripture describes, whenever someone loses a parent, sibling, teacher, or friend to death, loses their entire extended family, or loses all their wealth and status at once, the heart seized by a sharp, violent anguish that burns like fire and finds voice in bitter, mournful complaint. The lecture supplements this with a striking citation describing the six sense faculties themselves as inherently "ablaze" — ablaze with the fires of greed, hatred, and delusion, and ablaze as well with birth, old age, sickness, death, grief, lament, sorrow, and distress — precisely the fire only genuine realization of selflessness can finally extinguish.

愁、歎等，依第十二處轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Grief, lamentation, and the rest arise from the twelfth situation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the contempt-thought's own refutation as this entire eightfold list's own most universally applicable remedy: whatever specific comparison a mind might reach for — lineage, looks, wealth, skill — the same underlying facts of impermanence, bodily impurity, and inevitable decline apply equally to everyone, making any felt superiority simply a failure to look closely enough. He highlights the family-status thought's own rejection as a subtle but important correction to a natural but mistaken instinct: sincerely wishing someone well is not the same as actually helping them, and a meditator's own settled mind is itself too valuable a resource to spend on wishes that change nothing in the world. He treats the closing image of the six senses themselves being "ablaze" as this entire twelve-situation study's own fitting summary — every one of these twelve loci, in the end, simply describing different places where that same underlying fire finds fuel to keep burning.

24. Section: Why Two Wrong Views Never Arise Together

The treatise turns to a new distinction: which afflictions can, and cannot, actually co-occur within a single moment of mind. The five views — self-view, extreme view, wrong view, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts — never correspond with one another, since all five share the very same underlying essential nature, the mental factor of wisdom itself, and a single moment of mind can only ever engage one instance of wisdom at a time, precisely as it can only think one thought at a time; one view's own essential nature therefore simply cannot coexist with another view's essential nature within that same moment. Greed, hatred, conceit, and doubt likewise mutually exclude one another in pairs, though for a different reason: greed and hatred take opposite kinds of object, desirable and undesirable respectively, and so cannot arise toward the very same object simultaneously; conceit and doubt likewise take opposite kinds of object, a fixed, determinate comparison against an undecided, indeterminate uncertainty, and so cannot coexist either. Greed and conceit stand opposed in their own distinct way as well — greed's own craving tends to lower the mind into a felt sense of need and inferiority, while conceit's own pride elevates the mind into a felt sense of superiority, two directions that cannot both hold at once.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the underlying logic here as genuinely elegant once seen clearly: it is not that these afflictions are somehow morally incompatible, but that a single moment of mind simply cannot hold two instances of the same mental factor, or two mutually opposed orientations, at once — a structural rather than ethical constraint. He notes the commentary's own honest acknowledgment that this treatise elsewhere describes greed as capable of coexisting with conceit or wrong view after all, a genuine tension the tradition resolves by distinguishing coarser, more intense forms of these afflictions, which truly do exclude each other, from subtler, milder forms, which can indeed arise together in sequence or in combination — a reminder that even this treatise's own careful classifications sometimes require this kind of further nuance to fully reconcile.

25. Section: Rowing Upstream — Why Heedlessness Belongs to Every Family of Affliction

The treatise turns to a further classification: which family, or outflow-lineage, each secondary affliction actually belongs to among the ten root afflictions. Heedlessness belongs to every single family without exception, present whenever craving arises, and equally present even when doubt itself arises — wherever any affliction whatsoever is found, heedlessness is invariably found alongside it, since a mind not actively engaged in restraining and countering affliction through diligent practice will inevitably let whatever affliction happens to be present run its own course unchecked. The lecture illustrates this pervasive danger with a well-known proverb: learning is like rowing a boat against the current — fail to advance, and one is inevitably swept backward — a warning this tradition applies with even greater force to the far weightier task of severing affliction and escaping the cycle of birth and death altogether, since heedlessness left unaddressed only strengthens whatever affliction it accompanies, deepening the very cycle a sincere practitioner is trying to escape. Turning to specific families, attachment, stinginess, arrogance, and restlessness are all identified as belonging to greed's own family, each one simply a further expression of the same underlying craving in a different guise — attachment clinging directly to some desired object, stinginess refusing to release what is already possessed, arrogance taking pride in what is possessed, and restlessness arising from the mind's own agitated recollection of past pleasure. Wrath, resentment, vexation, envy, and the wish to harm are all identified as belonging to hatred's own family, each one simply hatred itself expressed through a different specific pattern of aversion. And deceit and flattery are identified as belonging to wrong view's own family, both being fundamentally rooted in greed and delusion together, yet consistently accompanied by some degree of distorted, self-serving view about how the world actually works.

Deceit and flattery are the family and outflow of wrong view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats heedlessness's own universal presence as this classification's own most important structural insight: every other secondary affliction has some specific root affliction it traces back to, but heedlessness alone accompanies literally all of them, functioning less as one affliction among many and more as the enabling condition that lets every other affliction actually run unchecked. He highlights the rowing-upstream proverb as this section's own memorable, practical reminder that spiritual practice admits no neutral standing-still — without active, ongoing effort against affliction, the current of habitual pattern simply carries a person backward regardless of how much progress may have already been made. He notes that this family-based classification, tracing each secondary affliction back to its own root, brings the entire study of both root and secondary afflictions to a natural, well-organized close, with three further establishing-aspects for affliction's own defilement still to come.

26. Section: Closing the Specific Varieties, and What Active Affliction Actually Is

The treatise names concealment as belonging to flattery's own outflow, and hence, by extension, to wrong view's own family as well, since it too involves hiding one's own wrongdoing out of fear for reputation or gain. All remaining secondary afflictions not yet assigned to a specific family are said to belong to delusion's own family instead, delusion serving as the catch-all root beneath whatever secondary affliction does not trace more specifically to greed, hatred, or wrong view. The treatise makes one notable exception: examination and reflection alone are excluded from this entire family-based classification, since both are, in their own essential nature, simply the mental factor of wisdom operating through the sixth consciousness's own conceptual, verbal thinking — coarse investigation hurriedly seeking an answer is called examination, while calm, unhurried, careful investigation of that same object is called reflection, both genuinely wisdom-based rather than affliction-based, even though they happen to share a family resemblance with the erroneous views examined earlier in this same discussion. With this, the treatise formally confirms that its entire account of affliction's own specific varieties — spanning both the ten root afflictions and the full catalog of secondary afflictions just completed — now stands fully established. The treatise turns to its third major aspect: the distinction between defilement and purity. Beings are defiled by affliction through two conditions: active manifestation, meaning affliction currently arisen and actively operating, and latent tendency, meaning affliction's own seed not yet severed or damaged, also called coarse heaviness, since this dormant seed alone is enough to render a being incapable of genuinely practicing the noble path. A further angle distinguishes the same two conditions differently: an unaware state, such as infancy or an animal's own undeveloped condition, where language and reasoning have not yet matured, corresponds to latent tendency, while an aware state, where the faculties have fully matured and genuine language and reasoning are possible, corresponds to active manifestation.

27. Section: What Each Realm Still Carries, Suppressed or Otherwise

The treatise examines which realm's afflictions a being actually possesses as latent seed. A being with every fetter fully intact, born in the desire realm, possesses the latent tendencies belonging to all three realms at once — desire, form, and formless alike. A being born in the form realm possesses the desire realm's own latent tendencies only in a suppressed form, held in check by the power of calm-abiding meditation without being severed, while the form and formless realms' own latent tendencies remain entirely unsuppressed, still fully capable of active manifestation. A being born in the formless realm possesses both the desire and form realms' own latent tendencies in this same suppressed form, while the formless realm's own latent tendencies alone remain unsuppressed. The very same reasoning, the treatise notes, extends by analogy from the three broad realms to each of the nine specific meditative stages within them. Turning to active manifestation itself, the treatise explains that afflictions belonging to one's own current realm actively manifest for anyone who has not yet become free of desire for that very realm, and cease manifesting entirely once that desire has genuinely been overcome — a straightforward principle equally applicable whether the realm in question is the desire realm, the form realm, or the formless realm.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the crucial distinction between suppression and severing as this entire section's own central point: a form-realm being's own desire-realm afflictions are genuinely absent from active manifestation, yet still fully present as an unsevered seed, ready to reassert themselves the moment that same being were ever to fall back into the desire realm — meditative attainment alone, however genuine, never substitutes for actually uprooting an affliction at its source. He notes this same principle as directly explaining a phenomenon this tradition takes very seriously: a highly accomplished meditator, having genuinely suppressed coarse affliction for an entire lifetime through deep absorption, can still experience a jarring return of that same affliction upon later losing access to that concentration, since suppression alone, however impressive its outward effects, was never the same achievement as severing the underlying seed for good.

28. Section: Why Only Present Affliction Truly Counts as Bondage

The treatise closes its discussion of active manifestation with a final note: a being in a lower realm can still actively manifest a higher realm's own afflictions, yet a being in a higher realm cannot actively manifest a lower realm's afflictions — except at the very moment of death, when that being is actually about to be reborn into that lower realm once more, at which point the corresponding affliction can indeed briefly reassert itself in anticipation of the coming rebirth. The treatise then turns to why affliction is properly called bondage in the first place, asking specifically about someone with every fetter still fully intact: are the afflictions binding such a being bound to the past, the future, or the present? The answer is that past affliction, its own karmic consequence already fully spent and its own essential nature already dissolved, is no longer properly called bondage at all — it was bondage once, but that bondage belongs entirely to a moment now gone. Only present affliction is genuinely called bondage, whether as an actively manifesting affliction currently arisen, or as that same affliction's own seed, uninterruptedly continuing to accompany one's own underlying consciousness without yet having been abandoned. Future affliction, not yet having arisen at all, is likewise not yet properly called bondage, since it will only become bondage once it actually occurs. This same reasoning, the treatise notes, extends without further elaboration to every other affliction and secondary affliction as well, and extends equally to those without every fetter intact — the various grades of noble trainee, from stream-entry through the approach to arhatship — for whom whatever affliction has already been genuinely severed is simply no longer bondage at all, while whatever remains unsevered continues to count as bondage in exactly the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this precise temporal restriction of genuine bondage to the present alone as a genuinely liberating way of framing the entire discussion: neither a past affliction, already spent, nor a future affliction, not yet arisen, can actually bind anyone right now, leaving only the present moment's own affliction as the sole thing ever truly requiring attention — a reframe with real practical value for anyone tempted to dwell on past failures or anticipate future struggles rather than simply working with whatever affliction is genuinely present. He highlights the extension to noble trainees as this section's own encouraging note: bondage shrinks progressively and permanently as genuine severing actually occurs, each stage of the path leaving strictly less bondage behind than the one before it, nothing regained once truly let go.

29. Section: Three Powers for Taming Affliction Before It Can Be Fully Severed

The treatise turns to the purifying side of this same distinction, asking how a practitioner actually suppresses affliction's own active manifestation. The answer names three powers of antidote. The first is genuinely understanding affliction's own essential nature and its countless faults — recognizing that affliction first disturbs one's own mind, then distorts one's perception of whatever object it encounters, further entrenching its own seed and perpetuating its own equal-outflow continuation; that it harms oneself, harms others, and harms both together; that it generates fault in this very life and in lives to come; that it brings the suffering of old age, sickness, and death in its wake; that it keeps one far from nirvāṇa's own genuine bliss; that it causes wholesome qualities already gained to be lost, and material resources already gathered to be squandered; that it strips away confidence and dignity in the company of others; that it spreads a damaged reputation in every direction, drawing the criticism of the wise; and that it brings deep regret at the moment of death and a miserable rebirth to follow — countless faults, once genuinely seen clearly, naturally giving rise to the wish to be free of them. The second power is reflecting on whatever specific object best counters one's own particular affliction, following this tradition's own five classic contemplative subjects: contemplating the body's own impurity to counter strong desire, contemplating loving-kindness to counter a quick temper, contemplating the twelve links of dependent origination to counter delusion about cause and effect, analyzing the body's own six elemental constituents to counter the pride born of self-clinging, and counting the breath to counter a scattered, distracted mind. The third power is nourishing the mind's own continuity with superior wholesome qualities — sustained practice of generosity, ethical discipline, patience, and the rest, carried forward moment after moment without interruption. The treatise closes by clarifying that this entire threefold approach is only the preliminary path preceding genuine, undefiled right view — the first two powers belonging to the stage of accumulating provisions, and the third to the stage of preparatory effort, suppressing affliction's own active manifestation for now, but not yet severing its underlying seed for good.

He highlights the closing clarification — that this entire threefold approach remains merely preliminary, suppression rather than severing — as an important corrective against mistaking calm for liberation: a practitioner who has genuinely mastered all three powers has accomplished something real and valuable, yet has not yet reached the deeper transformation only undefiled wisdom itself can ultimately provide.

30. Section: Why Cutting the Root Alone Counts as Genuine Severing

The treatise turns from suppressing affliction to genuinely severing it, asking a precise question: when a practitioner actually severs affliction, is it the active manifestation that gets relinquished, or the underlying latent tendency, and by severing which of these does the term "severing" genuinely apply? The answer is that only the latent tendency, the seed itself, is actually relinquished at this point, since the active manifestation has already been relinquished earlier, through the very suppression already accomplished by the three powers of antidote — severing the seed alone is therefore what "severing" properly means. The reasoning is straightforward: even once active manifestation has been suppressed, so long as its own underlying seed remains unsevered, that same affliction can and will repeatedly arise again whenever suitable conditions appear, exactly as cutting grass at the surface alone, leaving its root intact, guarantees nothing but a faster regrowth once rain falls again — precisely the old teaching that the moment one steps out the door, grass is everywhere, meaning that so long as the seed of affliction remains, every single engagement of the six faculties with their six objects risks that same grass sprouting anew. Only once the seed itself has genuinely been severed do both the seed and its corresponding manifestation become permanently incapable of ever arising again, this finality being exactly what distinguishes genuine severing from mere temporary suppression.

He highlights the old teaching that grass awaits the moment one simply steps outside the door as a vivid reminder that ordinary daily engagement with the world constantly tests whether suppression alone, however impressive, has ever actually been converted into genuine severing.

31. Section: A Balance Scale's Perfect Simultaneity — How "Severing All Three Times" Actually Works

The treatise asks a further, genuinely subtle question: does severing affect the past, the future, or the present affliction-seed? The answer is that, strictly speaking, none of the three times is actually what gets severed, even though this same process is conventionally described as severing all three. The reasoning unfolds carefully. A past moment of mind bearing an affliction-seed has already ceased entirely on its own, its own essential nature already dissolved — there is nothing left there to sever. A future moment of mind bearing an affliction-seed has not yet arisen at all, its own essential nature not yet existing — there is likewise nothing yet there to sever. And a present moment of mind bearing an affliction-seed, precisely because every conditioned moment is inherently momentary, does not actually persist even long enough to be acted upon, while its own nature, being fundamentally incompatible with the antidote-mind that would need to coexist alongside it in order to sever it, simply cannot be severed by that very antidote in the same instant either. And yet, the treatise explains, through the combined conditions of hearing genuine teaching from another and correctly, inwardly reflecting on it, a mind genuinely corresponding to right view does arise — and the very moment that antidote-mind arises is precisely the very moment the mind bearing the corresponding affliction-seed ceases, the two occurring with perfect simultaneity, exactly like the two ends of a balance scale, one side rising the very instant the other falls. From that first moment of genuine right view onward, that same mind-continuum's every subsequent moment, whether counted as past, present, or future relative to any later point, remains free of that particular affliction-seed for good — and it is precisely this permanent freedom extending across every moment from that point forward that justifies conventionally describing the whole process as severing the affliction across all three times together, even though, examined with strict precision, no literal past, present, or future seed was ever directly destroyed in the way the phrase might casually suggest. With this, the treatise closes its entire account of the defiled-and-pure distinction, and turns to its fourth major aspect: the distinction between confusion and severance.

32. Section: Fifteen Latent Tendencies Across Three Realms and Four Truths

Taking up its fourth major aspect for affliction defilement, the confusion-and-severance distinction, the treatise briefly answers its own opening question: in summary, there are fifteen kinds of latent affliction to be severed. Those bound to the desire realm, whether severed by seeing the four truths of suffering, its origination, its cessation, and the path, or severed instead by ongoing cultivation, number five in total; the form realm and formless realm, following the same pattern, each likewise contribute five, for fifteen altogether across the three realms. Within the desire realm specifically, delusion about the truth of suffering alone accounts for ten root afflictions, since the five clinging-aggregates themselves are what suffering means, and every one of the ten roots can attach to confusion about them; delusion about the truths of origination, cessation, and the path, by contrast, each accounts for only eight, self-view and extreme-view being excluded, since those two views take the five aggregates themselves as their object and therefore cannot attach to confusion about origination, cessation, or the path as such. The upper two realms, having attained meditative absorption, are free of hatred altogether, so their own delusion about suffering involves nine root afflictions rather than ten, and their delusion about the remaining three truths involves seven each rather than eight, otherwise following the very same pattern as the desire realm.

33. Section: Self-View's Twenty Modes, and the Heretics Who Denied Suffering Itself

The treatise now turns to its extensive analysis, asking directly why delusion about the truth of suffering specifically involves ten latent afflictions, and answering root by root. The five clinging-aggregates, in brief, are simply what is meant by suffering, and it is with respect to these five aggregates that the ignorant, unlearned ordinary being generates twenty distinct modes of self-view: computing that form, feeling, perception, mental formations, or consciousness itself is the self accounts for five of these modes and constitutes self-view proper, while the remaining fifteen modes, computing that the self possesses each aggregate, that each aggregate belongs to the self, or that the self dwells within each aggregate, constitute self-belonging-view — the fifteen arising, respectively, from an assumed self's cooperative association with what belongs to it, from that self's presumed authority over the aggregate's every activity, and from that self's presumed inseparable residence within the aggregate. Taking this same self-view as its own foundation, extreme-view then grasps these very aggregates as either annihilated at death or eternally persisting, and so likewise counts as delusion about suffering. Wrong view, in one of its own forms, denies giving, and denies the very existence of virtuous or unwholesome karmic action and its ripened fruit altogether; in another form, it denies the existence of parents or of spontaneously-born beings, a denial the treatise carefully splits between two truths at once, since parents and spontaneously-born beings, considered as bodies of present result, are themselves instances of suffering, while considered as the very cause from which future rebirth arises, they equally implicate the truth of origination. And in its most extreme form, wrong view denies the truth of suffering directly: certain heretics, unwilling to accept that the five clinging-aggregates are genuinely suffering, claim outright that the ascetic of the Gautama clan merely fabricated this truth of suffering for the sake of his own disciples, and that no such truth exists at all — a view the treatise names as itself a form of delusion about suffering, since it denies the very reality that every other root affliction here has been shown to misconstrue.

34. Section: Eternalist Gods, the Edge of the World, and Eel-Wriggling Evasion

The treatise continues cataloguing wrong views that count as delusion about suffering. Some falsely compute that a lord of self-mastery, a sovereign of the world, Indra, Brahmā, or some other such being is permanent, eternal, and utterly without change, failing to recognize that even a god's own five clinging-aggregates remain impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — this too is delusion about suffering. Others hold views computing the world or its beings as having an edge or as having no edge at all, a computation likewise counted as delusion about suffering. And a portion of what is called eel-wriggling evasion, the tactic of certain ascetics and brahmins who, seeking rebirth in the heaven of immortality yet never having genuinely learned the path there, evasively equivocate whenever questioned rather than admit their own ignorance, for fear of appearing wrong, also counts as delusion about suffering, since it too fails to recognize that even the aggregates of that imagined immortal heaven remain impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self. 35. Section: Closing the Ten Roots of Delusion About Suffering

The treatise completes its tenfold catalogue. Doubt about suffering belongs to those heretics who neither firmly accept the views just catalogued nor outright reject the suffering-truth the Tathāgata established, but instead simply waver, uncertain whether the five aggregates are truly suffering at all — a hesitation, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, by no means confined to ancient heretics, since anyone whose life feels comfortable and complete may just as readily wonder how such a life could possibly be called suffering, knowing suffering as a genuine truth being properly the wisdom of the noble ones rather than an ordinary person's own untrained judgment. Greed about suffering is treasuring and clinging firmly to one's own views about the aggregates as something precious; hatred about suffering is the wish to oppose or harm another whose view of the aggregates differs from one's own; conceit about suffering is the elevated, arrogant mind that relies on one's own view as supreme; and ignorance about suffering is the lack of wisdom that either accompanies all these views, doubt, greed, hatred, and conceit together, or, in its own unassociated form, simply fails on its own to recognize the aggregates as suffering. With this, the treatise formally closes its account: all ten of these afflictions are delusion about the truth of suffering, and every one is severed at the path of seeing. It then turns to ask, in turn, why delusion about the truth of origination itself involves eight latent afflictions, opening this second inquiry with the wrong views of those ascetics and brahmins who deny any cause behind pleasant and painful results at all, and of those who instead falsely compute a lord of self-mastery or similar being as the very producer, transformer, and maker of all things — an evil-cause theory that likewise counts as delusion about origination.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the formal closing statement here, that all ten afflictions attached to delusion about suffering are severed specifically at the path of seeing, as a quietly important structural marker: everything catalogued across this long stretch belongs to the very first breakthrough of insight, the initial seeing of the four truths, rather than to the gradual work of cultivation that follows afterward and unfolds over a much longer stretch of practice. He notes the parallel opening of delusion about origination, beginning immediately with denial of causation altogether and with the evil-cause theory of a creator god, as a deliberate structural echo of how delusion about suffering itself opened, the treatise consistently working outward from the crudest possible misunderstanding, before working inward toward the successively subtler distortions still to come.

36. Section: Closing Delusion About Origination

The treatise continues its catalogue of delusion about the truth of origination: certain heretics slander the origination-truth outright, claiming that whatever the ascetic of the Gautama clan taught his disciples about origination simply does not exist, all such views together being named delusion about origination. From these wrong views arise, exactly as before, view-attachment grasping them as foremost and able to purify, liberate, and release; precept-attachment grasping whatever disciplines follow from them as likewise able to purify, liberate, and release; and, the treatise notes, the remaining doubt, greed, hatred, conceit, and ignorance should all be understood exactly as already explained for delusion about suffering, the only difference being that here they take as their object the very cause of the aggregates, karmic action and affliction, rather than the aggregates themselves. With this, all eight afflictions of delusion about origination are named, likewise severed at the path of seeing when origination itself is directly seen. The treatise then turns to its third inquiry, why delusion about the truth of cessation similarly involves eight latent afflictions, opening with those ascetics and brahmins who compute the world or its beings as having an edge or no edge, and with a further portion of eel-wriggling evasion, now applied specifically to questions about nirvāṇa rather than about the aggregates or their cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the treatise's own repeated shorthand, "as before," across view-attachment, precept-attachment, doubt, greed, hatred, conceit, and ignorance for each of the four truths in turn is not laziness but precision: the underlying emotional and cognitive structure of clinging to a view, resenting its critics, feeling superior about it, and remaining unable to see clearly is identical in every case, and the only thing that actually changes from one truth to the next is the specific object being misconstrued, the aggregates themselves, their cause, their cessation, or the path to that cessation. He treats this as a genuinely useful diagnostic for practitioners today: the same eight or ten afflictions can be traced back to whichever of the four truths a person's own confusion happens to be rooted in, simply by asking what, exactly, the mistaken view is actually about.

37. Section: Bliss Mistaken for Nirvana, and the Arhat's Two Virtues Denied

The treatise continues its account of delusion about cessation. Some ascetics and brahmins teach that nirvāṇa is attainable in this very life, without needing to wait for death, one group holding that unrestrained indulgence in the five sensory pleasures of gods and humans already constitutes nirvāṇa, and four further groups holding that the bliss generated by each of the four levels of meditative absorption is itself nirvāṇa — every one of these being ignorance of what cessation actually means, and so delusion about the truth of cessation. Others hold a wrong view denying that any true arhat exists in the world at all. An arhat, the treatise explains, is distinguished by two virtues: the virtue of severance, permanently ending the manifestation and the latent seed of greed, hatred, and delusion alike; and the virtue of wisdom, comprising the exhaustion-knowledge that afflictions will never again arise and the no-further-birth-knowledge that, causes being exhausted, no further rebirth in the three realms will ever again occur. Since heretics, lacking wisdom themselves, cannot even conceive of this second virtue, the treatise notes that what is actually being denied here, and what properly counts as delusion about cessation, is specifically the wrong view denying the arhat's virtue of severance. Beyond this, further wrong views directly slander the cessation-truth itself as nonexistent, exactly as earlier heretics slandered suffering and origination, and still others falsely compute various mistaken forms of liberation, such as believing that rebirth in heaven itself constitutes liberation — all of these together being named the wrong views that arise from delusion about the truth of cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five varieties of "nirvana attained in this very life" as this section's own most practically urgent warning, precisely because they do not sound like heresy at all: mistaking sensory indulgence, or even the genuine bliss of deep meditative absorption, for the final goal is an error a sincere meditator can make from the inside, having actually tasted something real and simply misnaming it, rather than an error only committed by outsiders with obviously wrong philosophies. He highlights the careful distinction between the arhat's two virtues as this passage's own quiet correction of a common oversimplification: liberation is not simply the cessation of suffering by itself, but the union of a negative achievement, the permanent severing of the three poisons at their root, with a positive one, the wisdom that knows with certainty that they will never return — and it is specifically the denial of the first of these two, he notes, that the treatise identifies as the technical content of this particular wrong view.

38. Section: Fear of Extinction, and Closing All the See-Severed Afflictions

The treatise notes one genuine difference within delusion about cessation: while its remaining greed, conceit, ignorance, and doubt should be understood exactly as before, its hatred takes a distinctive form. Hearing the truth of cessation taught, some generate a mind of dread, a mind bent on harm, a mind of resentful anguish, imagining nirvāṇa as the utter extinction of themselves, of their own body, and of everyone and everything they love — this fear-driven hatred, unique to delusion about cessation, completes its own eightfold count, all severed at the very moment cessation is directly seen. The treatise then opens its fourth and final inquiry, why delusion about the path similarly involves eight latent afflictions. Some deny that any true arhat exists in the world, specifically by denying that the unconditioned, undefiled wisdom leading the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment can actually produce one; a further portion of eel-wriggling evasion applies here as well; other heretics slander the very existence of a genuine path, claiming that whatever path of renunciation the ascetic of the Gautama clan taught his disciples is not genuine renunciation at all, and that his teaching of selflessness together with its accompanying precepts is nothing but a wicked, deviant path rather than the true and wondrous one; and these same heretics, in turn, falsely compute that whatever practice or path they themselves follow is the true path, fully capable of exhausting and escaping every suffering. View-attachment then grasps these very views as foremost, precept-attachment grasps whatever disciplines follow from them as foremost, and the remaining greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt should be understood exactly as under delusion about cessation. With all eight now named, the treatise formally closes its entire fourfold account of the afflictions severed at the path of seeing, and turns at last to those severed instead through prolonged cultivation.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fear of extinction described here as a genuinely universal obstacle rather than a quaint ancient misunderstanding, since the same instinctive dread of losing oneself, one's body, and everyone one loves can quietly color how even a sincere modern practitioner hears the word nirvana, long before any philosophical argument about selflessness has actually been absorbed. 39. Section: Why Some Afflictions Take a Lifetime — The Deeper-Rooted Four

Having closed the afflictions severed at the moment of seeing, the treatise turns to those severed instead only through prolonged cultivation, asking directly what these are. The answer names just four: hatred in the desire realm, together with greed, conceit, and ignorance across all three realms — greed, conceit, and ignorance being common to every realm, hatred belonging to the desire realm alone since the upper two realms, having attained meditative absorption, are free of it. These four are named as cultivation-severed precisely because they run too deep to be uprooted by the initial breakthrough of seeing the four truths directly; only the sustained sequence of tirelessly practicing the four foundations of mindfulness and the remaining factors of the path over a long stretch of time can finally exhaust them. The treatise further specifies that each of these afflictions, within every one of the nine stages spanning the three realms, itself comes in three grades, lower, middle, and upper, and that the very antidote capable of severing them likewise comes in three corresponding grades — yet, counter to what might be expected, the correspondence runs in reverse: it is the lower grade of antidote that severs the upper grade of affliction, the middle grade of antidote that severs the middle grade of affliction, and only the upper grade of antidote that finally severs the lower, most stubborn grade of affliction remaining.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this reversed correspondence, weak antidote against strong affliction first, strong antidote against faint affliction last, as one of the treatise's own quietly counterintuitive but psychologically accurate observations: the grossest, most obvious manifestations of greed, hatred's desire-realm form, conceit, and ignorance are, paradoxically, the easiest to weaken, since their very grossness makes them visible and thus workable, while what remains after the coarse layers have been peeled away is not nothing but rather an ever-subtler residue that keeps demanding an ever-more-refined tool to finally remove — which is exactly why, he notes, genuine cultivation of the path cannot be rushed through a single burst of effort, however sincere, but must instead unfold across a long, graduated sequence matched to how affliction itself actually thins out.

40. Section: Closing Confusion-Severance, and the Five Objects Affliction Actually References

The treatise closes its account of the cultivation-severed afflictions by noting their distinctive relationship to their own object: unlike the see-severed afflictions, which arise from discrimination, these four operate spontaneously with respect to whatever arises through affliction's own conditioning, remaining stubbornly fixed across an entire lifetime and correspondingly difficult to free oneself from, precisely because the very aggregates they are confused about are never absent even for a moment. With this, the treatise formally closes its entire account of the confusion-and-severance distinction, the fourth of affliction defilement's five establishing aspects, and turns to a further clarification: in brief, the afflictions severed at the path of seeing and those severed by cultivation reference five kinds of objects between them. Afflictions referencing suffering and origination reference an object arisen through wrong discrimination, a conceptual construction of the sixth consciousness rather than something directly and functionally cognized, much as an image of fire reflected in a mirror has no actual burning power. View-attachment, greed, and the rest, apart from doubt, reference the view itself as their object. Precept-attachment references the precepts and vows themselves. Afflictions referencing cessation and the path, or referencing a realm other than one's own, reference an object arisen through one's own discrimination expressed in name and concept, since an ordinary being untouched by noble wisdom cannot actually cognize the pure truths of cessation and the path directly, nor directly cognize a higher realm's own objects, but can only construct an approximate image of them through familiar language and doctrine — not, the treatise is careful to note, that there is truly no object at all, only that the object referenced is a conceptual one rather than the genuine, functioning thing itself. And the afflictions severed by cultivation alone reference the spontaneous, persistently continuous object that is one's own five aggregates, ever-present and therefore ever-available as an occasion for affliction to arise.

41. Section: The Fifth and Final Aspect — Four Kinds of Antidote, Beginning with the Thirteen Requisites

The treatise now takes up the fifth and final of affliction defilement's five establishing aspects: the antidote distinction. In brief, it names four kinds of antidote. The continuity-maturation antidote belongs to the path of accumulation, where a practitioner steadily gathers the two provisions of merit and wisdom across a continuous stretch of life until the very capacity to counter affliction gradually ripens within them. The near-severing antidote belongs to the joined-practice stage that follows, once access concentration has been attained and insight into the true nature of things is cultivated within it, corresponding to the four stages of heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma, so named because the practitioner's own effort has, by this point, grown genuinely warm, like a fire just beginning to catch. The partial-severing antidote belongs to the path of seeing, where the discrimination-arisen afflictions are severed, though the innate afflictions bound up with one's own karmic result remain untouched. And the complete-severing antidote belongs to the path of cultivation, where these remaining innate afflictions are at last entirely exhausted. Turning to the first of these in detail, the treatise points back to its own extensive treatment, in the Stage of the Hearers, of the thirteen requisites of the path of accumulation, self-completeness, the completeness of one's teacher, wholesome aspiration, moral discipline, restraint of the faculties, moderation in eating, wakeful diligence through the watches of the night, mindful awareness in every activity, good companionship, hearing the correct teaching, reflecting on it, freedom from obstruction, and the ascetic's own adornments, hearing and reflecting being counted together as a single requisite to reach the number thirteen — together constituting what is called the continuity-maturation antidote.

As already fully explained in the Stage of the Hearers, the thirteen requisites of the path of accumulation are called the continuity-maturation antidote.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the arrival at this fifth and final establishing aspect as a genuinely significant structural moment: with self-nature, specific varieties, the defiled-pure distinction, and the confusion-severance distinction all now behind it, the treatise has one remaining task, showing concretely how affliction is actually overcome in practice, and it is fitting, he notes, that this final aspect should begin not with dramatic breakthrough but with the patient, unglamorous thirteen requisites of ordinary daily discipline, moderation in eating, wakefulness, good companionship, careful listening — the treatise's own reminder that every later breakthrough rests on a foundation built this plainly and this gradually. He highlights the fire image behind "near-severing," heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma, as an apt description of the felt experience of intensive practice on the very threshold of insight, effort that has clearly caught and is visibly building toward something, though the flame of genuine seeing has not yet broken through.

42. Section: Kindling Fire — The Four Antidotes Now Named in Full

The treatise details its remaining three antidotes. The near-severing antidote is named for the four stages of heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma, already fully explained in the Stage of the Hearers, the very threshold of joined practice standing just before the path of seeing itself. The Stage of the Hearers illustrates these four stages through the kindling of a cooking fire: dry wood is laid down, a fire-drill applied above it, and warmth first arises in the wood below; that warmth then increases and rises as heat; the heat redoubles and smoke begins to issue forth; a flameless fire then suddenly flows out; and finally, without interruption, a full, strong flame bursts forth and is at last capable of doing everything fire can do — the warmth, the rising heat, the smoke, and the flameless fire corresponding respectively to the roots of goodness belonging to heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma, and the final blazing flame corresponding to the path of seeing itself, arising in the very next instant. A practitioner who reaches the stage of heat, even should they later regress and commit unwholesome deeds, will nevertheless eventually attain the noble path and enter nirvāṇa; one who reaches summit, though still capable of wavering, will never sever their roots of goodness even if they fall as far as the hells; one who reaches forbearance has fixed their conviction in the four truths beyond further wavering and will never again fall into the three evil destinies; and one who reaches supreme worldly dharma stands at the very highest point attainable within worldly, outflow-bearing states, entering the path of seeing and becoming a noble one in the very next single instant. The path of seeing itself is then named the partial-severing antidote, since it severs only the discrimination-arisen afflictions belonging to the three realms, the innate afflictions bound up with karmic result still remaining; and the path of cultivation is named the complete-severing antidote, its own goal being nothing less than the complete exhaustion of every remaining affliction. With all four antidotes now named, the treatise turns next to an extensive analysis of exactly how the path of seeing and the path of cultivation actually accomplish this severing in a practitioner's own mind.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fire-kindling image as one of the treatise's own most memorable teaching devices precisely because it maps so exactly onto lived spiritual experience: the warmth that can still cool back down, the rising heat that can still falter, the smoke that signals real change is finally underway, and the flameless glow that precedes the sudden, irreversible blaze of genuine insight — each stage recognizable, he notes, to anyone who has sustained serious practice long enough to feel their own effort actually beginning to catch. He notes that although all four antidotes have now been named, completing the basic outline of the antidote distinction, the treatise is not yet finished with this fifth establishing aspect: it turns next to a considerably more detailed examination of what actually happens, moment by moment, in the mind of a practitioner passing through the path of seeing and the path of cultivation, a question the treatise itself signals as still requiring careful, extensive treatment before affliction defilement as a whole can be considered fully established.

43. Section: Nine Minds or One — Two Ways of Speaking About the Same Breakthrough

The treatise now explains how the path of seeing itself is established, through two complementary rationales. The first, the rationale of broadly propagating the noble teaching, still involves conceptual elaboration, since it is how a noble one's own wisdom, gained after direct realization, compassionately expresses what happened in terms disciples can actually learn from and repeat; the second, the rationale of inwardly realizing ultimate truth, is entirely free of conceptual elaboration, being the direct inward experience itself, apart from language altogether. Following the first rationale, the treatise explains that dharma-wisdom, examining what is directly seen within one's own present desire realm, and class-wisdom, examining what is not directly seen in the higher form and formless realms, each generate four minds corresponding to the four truths, making eight minds of penetrating insight in all, unified with a single calm-abiding mind that underlies and sustains them across this entire stretch, for nine minds altogether constituting the completion of the path of seeing on this account. Following the second rationale, only a single mind is spoken of, since what actually occurs is nothing more or less than one continuous mind-moment-class directly corresponding to the realization of suchness itself, its own many constituent instants so similar and continuous that they are simply spoken of together as one. Either way, the treatise notes, a calm-abiding path underlies this process exactly as already explained, since the very moment of direct realization is one in which calming and insight operate in perfect union, focused single-pointedly on suchness itself, free of any grasped or grasping characteristic whatsoever.

He notes the treatise's own quiet insistence that both accounts remain compatible, not contradictory, precisely because the first is openly acknowledged as a pedagogical simplification laid over the second, rather than a separate claim about what is ultimately real.

44. Section: The Skilled Physician Lancing a Wound

The treatise turns to explaining the very nature of this antidote through an extended medical analogy. A skilled physician extracting a poisoned arrow, recognizing that an abscess has fully matured, first lances it with a sharp blade; the pus begins to drain, though not all at once, so the physician must widen the incision further and press repeatedly around it until the pus is fully drained, yet even then the wound is not perfectly clean, its opening still remaining, so that some oily compress or oiled cloth must be applied to help it close, the surrounding flesh gradually contracting and healing only step by step thereafter. This vivid image, the treatise makes clear, is offered precisely to make an otherwise difficult point easy to grasp: that the antidote which severs affliction, however decisive it may seem in the moment it first strikes, does not instantly and perfectly finish its own work in a single stroke, but continues to require further, successive effort before the wound of affliction is genuinely closed and healed. In this same context the treatise recalls the seven kinds of noble practitioner, the faith-follower and the dharma-follower, together counted as one figure practicing the mark-free path precisely while ascending the path of seeing itself, and the conviction-adherent, the one-who-has-arrived-at-vision, the body-witness, the wisdom-liberated, and the both-liberated, these latter five instead already abiding in the mark-free state, their own vision of the noble truths already accomplished and their minds already settled in cessation's own still, undisturbed calm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the physician's lancing analogy as one of the treatise's own most reassuring images for practitioners who expect awakening to be a single clean cut: even the very antidote that genuinely, decisively breaks through affliction still leaves work behind it, further pressing needed to drain what remains, and a dressing needed afterward simply to let the wound close and the surrounding tissue heal — spiritual breakthrough, no less than physical healing, unfolding in real time rather than completing itself instantaneously the moment the decisive cut is made. He highlights the distinction between the faith-follower and dharma-follower still actively practicing the mark-free path, and the further five already abiding within it, as this passage's own careful reminder that even within the noble path there remain real, describable differences of depth and stability, rather than a single undifferentiated state that either has or has not been reached.

45. Section: Reading the Physician's Analogy Point by Point

The treatise now unpacks its own medical analogy in full correspondence. Just as the abscess must first fully mature, so too must the very state of affairs bound up with the afflictions severed at the path of seeing come to readiness; just as the sharp blade lances it, so does the insight-aspect belonging to the path of seeing; just as the surrounding pressing helps drain it fully, so does the calm-abiding-aspect belonging to that same path of seeing; just as the pus itself is what drains out, so are all the latent afflictions severed at the path of seeing; just as the wound, though drained, remains unclean and unhealed, so does the state of affairs bound up with the afflictions still left for the path of cultivation to sever; and just as the oily compress finally closes and heals the wound completely, so does the path of cultivation. The treatise then turns to ordinary, unawakened beings who become free of desire for the desire realm, or free of desire for the form realm, purely through cultivation, having never attained any path of seeing at all: when such a being becomes free of desire for the desire realm, their greed, hatred, and the conceit and ignorance closely bound up with these no longer manifest, and this too may loosely be called severing, yet it is nothing like the path of seeing's own severing of self-view and its companions, since these afflictions still remain, dormant, within that very being's own mindstream, ready to manifest again the moment that being emerges from absorption, precisely because rebirth into a higher realm alone, without genuine noble insight, never actually eliminates the underlying seed at all — a warning, the treatise makes plain, that even deep and genuine meditative attainment can be mistaken for liberation when it is, in truth, only a temporary and reversible calm.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this careful point-by-point unpacking of the physician's own analogy as the treatise's own insistence that no detail in a teaching image is decorative: the maturing of the abscess, the two simultaneous roles of blade and pressing, the pus itself, and the still-unhealed wound each correspond to something exact and technical, precisely so that a practitioner reading the image can locate their own experience within it rather than simply admiring its vividness. He treats the warning about ordinary beings' meditative detachment as this whole section's own most practically urgent message, since it is entirely possible, he notes, to attain a deep, genuine calm indistinguishable in feeling from liberation, remain there for a very long time, even take rebirth in an exalted realm on its strength, and still, having never actually seen the four truths directly, carry every one of the original seeds forward entirely intact.

46. Section: The Perfume Left in an Emptied Bottle — Closing the Five Aspects of Affliction Defilement

The treatise closes by distinguishing two kinds of coarse-heaviness. Outflow-coarse-heaviness is the very unsettledness and incapacity that afflicted mind itself produces within a being's own conscious body, and it is entirely and permanently overcome the moment an arhat's own cultivation-severed afflictions are exhausted. Outflow-possessing coarse-heaviness is something subtler still, the unsettled, suffering-prone, similarly incapacitated quality carried by the very body one already has, itself the ripened and habituated result of past affliction, and while this too grows faint as the corresponding latent tendency is severed, it survives severing itself, in diminished form, as what is properly called the habitual residue of affliction — a residue neither an arhat nor even a solitary awakened one is able to remove completely, only a fully awakened Buddha achieving that final, uncommon accomplishment. As scripture explains, when the untrained ordinary being meets pain or death, sorrow, lamentation, distress, and finally derangement arise one upon another, both bodily feeling and mental feeling compounding together like a person struck first by one poisoned arrow and then immediately by a second; but the trained noble disciple, meeting the very same pain or death, experiences only the bodily feeling alone, without any accompanying mental anguish, like a person struck by a single arrow and no more, precisely because sustained practice of discipline, meditation, and wisdom has already severed greed, hatred, and delusion at the root. With every one of its five establishing aspects, intrinsic nature, specific varieties, defiled-and-pure distinction, confusion-and-severance distinction, and antidote distinction, now fully explained in turn, the treatise here formally closes its entire account: affliction defilement has now been established through these five distinguishing characteristics in full.

47. Section: Correlating Scripture — Why "Desire" Means the Craving, Not the Object

The treatise opens a new stretch of work, correlating its own careful analysis with statements found across the sutras, beginning by asking why the Blessed One's teaching that false discriminative craving alone is what "desire" truly means should exclude the desired objects themselves. Six reasons follow. First, affliction-desire's own nature is inherently defiled, while the objects of desire, in themselves, are not. Second, only affliction-desire can actually give rise to desire for any object at all. Third, affliction-desire generates a whole train of defiled misfortune: whoever has not yet severed or even recognized their own false discriminative craving is first scorched by desire's own burning, then driven by that very burning to pursue objects of desire, suffering bodily and mental exhaustion in the pursuit; should the pursuit fail, they suffer the further pain of wasted effort; should it succeed, they suffer the burden of guarding jealously what they cling to; while actually enjoying it, they suffer the very fire of craving burning within; upon losing it, they suffer grief and sorrow; and thereafter, simply remembering what was lost, they suffer yet again. Fourth, it is this same affliction-desire that gives rise to unwholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind. Fifth, even one who renounces the household life and abandons its outward objects of desire has not thereby abandoned affliction-desire itself, which will arise again the moment suitable conditions for it appear, since only the objects, and not the underlying craving, were actually given up — a household being called dusty and confining, and homelessness called open and spacious as the sky, precisely because the true departure is a departure from affliction rather than from mere possessions. Sixth, it is affliction-desire alone that brings about the suffering of birth, old age, sickness, and death, and rebirth in the evil destinies, within the desire realm. For all these reasons, the treatise concludes, the Blessed One spoke only of affliction-desire as desire in the true sense, never of the desired object itself.

48. Section: Eight Kinds of Discrimination That Kindle Desire

The treatise now asks how many kinds of false discrimination actually give rise to desirous craving, answering that there are eight in brief: inducing discrimination, awakening discrimination, uniting-and-bonding discrimination, marked discrimination, intimate discrimination, joyful discrimination, encroaching discrimination, and utterly intimate discrimination — a sequence the Brahma's Questions Sutra itself renders in verse, naming these same eight as the false discrimination that gives rise to desirous craving, and urging the wise to keep their distance from it. Taking up the first of these in turn, inducing discrimination is what arises once a practitioner's own skillful means, the calming supports and provisions cultivated for freedom from desire, have been abandoned and their own continuity broken, so that attention newly turns toward and settles upon objects of desire once again — the initial spark, in other words, by which a mind that had been genuinely stabilized in renunciation first turns back toward the very objects it had left behind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this eightfold sequence as the treatise's own careful slow-motion replay of exactly how desire actually builds, moment by moment, inside an ordinary mind, from the very first flicker of attention turning back toward an abandoned object, through recognition, association, fixation, closeness, delight, escalating indulgence, and finally total absorption — a sequence he notes is worth learning precisely because it locates, with real precision, the earliest and easiest point at which the whole chain can still be interrupted. He highlights the opening definition of inducing discrimination, the breakdown of one's own prior skillful means, as this whole catalogue's own quiet diagnostic: desire's return is rarely a sudden ambush from without, but far more often the simple consequence of having already, quietly, let go of whatever practice had been keeping attention properly anchored.

49. Section: A Loquat's Whole Life Cycle — Completing the Eightfold Discrimination

The treatise completes its eightfold sequence of discrimination, and Master Cháng Bǎi's own teaching illustrates the whole progression through the simple example of a loquat. Awakening discrimination arises even before the desired object is actually present, when merely thinking of last year's loquat already binds the mind in craving's grip, as when the thought of the coming season's fruit alone stirs a wish to go buy some. Uniting-and-bonding discrimination follows, bound now by that very craving, one actively pursues the object, driving to wherever the loquats are sold. Marked discrimination arises once the object is finally present, grasping at its every attractive feature, its size, its color, its sweetness. Intimate discrimination follows, wishing urgently to take up and actually enjoy it. Joyful discrimination wants ever more of it, a whole basketful rather than a single piece. Encroaching discrimination, having tasted its benefits, wants still more again, redoubling its own demand in both quantity and quality. And utterly intimate discrimination is craving carried to its very extreme, consuming without limit and still not satisfied. The treatise then asks why, among all the afflictions of the desire realm, only greed is singled out as the mark of desire, answering that greed alone underlies both wishing and not-wishing, and that greed alone arises pervasively everywhere; and it further explains why discrimination bound together with greed is spoken of as a single false discriminative craving: the very causes that bring greed itself into active manifestation are the same causes by which its corresponding objects come to be enjoyed, discrimination and craving together forming a single, seamless process. Even a renunciant who has genuinely given up every outward object of desire may still generate this same false discrimination in thought alone — which is precisely why the Blessed One teaches that discrimination itself, quite apart from any actual object, is already desire in its own right, so that such a person may come to recognize it and let it go.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recounts the words of the eminent Chan Master Guǎngqīn on precisely this point: skipping dinner outwardly means nothing if the mind returns to the thought of eating again and again, since each such thought is itself already a full act of eating in every sense that actually matters — a plain-spoken teaching, he notes, that a master with no formal training in these eight technical categories arrived at directly through his own lived observation, and that lands with exactly the same force as this entire elaborate scholastic catalogue. He treats the renunciant who has genuinely abandoned every outward object yet still generates discrimination in thought alone as this whole section's own final and most searching point: the robes and the empty hands prove nothing on their own, since desire, properly understood, was never really about the loquat at all.

50. Section: Why Craving Alone Marks the Truth of Origination

The treatise now asks why, among every affliction capable of generating suffering, only craving is said to mark the truth of origination, and gives two reasons. First, craving is the very basis of both wishing and not-wishing: with respect to one's own body and possessions, currently being enjoyed, craving generates the wish that they continue to be had again in some future life, this being the craving for continued existence; and with respect to what one does not wish, namely the suffering fruit of the three realms, craving, now paired with a view of annihilation, generates instead the wish that no further rebirth ever occur at all, engendering an aversion even toward the very wholesome practices that lead to genuine liberation, this being the craving for non-existence. Second, craving arises pervasively, spreading without exception across pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling alike, across past, present, and future time alike, and across every inner and outer object alike, in a way no other single affliction does. Because of these two kinds of craving, wishing for continued existence on the one hand, and craving non-existence through a mistaken view of annihilation on the other, both alike still rooted in craving itself, the flow of birth and death continues on entirely without interruption.

Question: why is craving alone said to be the mark of the truth of origination? Answer: by two causes and conditions. First, craving is the basis of both wishing and not-wishing. Second, craving arises pervasively. Why is this so? Because that craving, with respect to body and possessions to be wished for, being presently enjoyed, gives rise to wishing. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of wishing-craving and not-wishing-craving as one of the treatise's own sharpest psychological insights: it would be natural to assume that wanting existence and wanting non-existence are opposites, one grasping and one renouncing, yet the treatise identifies both as forms of the very same underlying craving, the second merely craving's mirror image rather than its genuine cure, which is precisely why a wrong view of annihilation, however much it may resemble spiritual renunciation on the surface, still keeps the wheel of birth and death turning exactly as surely as ordinary worldly grasping does. He notes that true liberation, by contrast, requires letting go of the craving underlying both postures at once, wishing to have and wishing not to be, rather than simply trading one for the other.
