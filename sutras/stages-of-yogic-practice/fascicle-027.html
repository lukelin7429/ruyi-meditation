Dependent Origination and the Six Elements

Fascicle 27 continues the second yogic process's third question — objects of meditation — picking up where fascicle 26 left off. Having closed impurity and loving-kindness, the treatise now gives full standalone treatment to the remaining purifying objects: the object of dependent origination, the object of the six elements, and mindfulness of the breath.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 27 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Object of Dependent Origination Opens

Fascicle 27 opens as the second part of the second yogic process's continuous unfolding, resuming directly where fascicle 26 closed: two of five purifying objects (impurity, loving-kindness) are done; three remain (dependent origination, the elements, breath-mindfulness), followed by skillful objects and objects that purify affliction.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之二……云何緣性緣起所緣？謂於三世唯行、唯法、唯事、唯因、唯果，墮正道理。謂觀待道理、作用道理、證成道理、法爾道理。……唯有諸法能引諸法，無有作者及以受者，是名緣性緣起所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the second part of the second yogic process … What is the object of dependent origination? It is contemplating, across the three times, only conditioned formations, only phenomena, only events, only causes, and only effects — falling within the correct principles: the principle of dependence, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of intrinsic nature. … Only phenomena drawing forth further phenomena — there is no agent, and no one who experiences the result. This is called the object of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating this object precisely as the third of the five purifying objects, and immediately flags its unusual density: unlike impurity or loving-kindness, dependent origination is analyzed here through five successive "only" (唯) qualifiers, each stripping away a different layer of the illusion of a controlling self. "Only conditioned formations" (唯行) names the sheer arising-and-perishing of phenomena moment to moment — nothing here that doesn't immediately pass away. "Only phenomena" (唯法) names the fact that what we call "a life" is nothing but language and concept describing a process, with no experiencer standing behind it — the Saṃyukta Āgama's classic formula, "this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises," describes phenomena conditioning phenomena, not a person undergoing anything. "Only events" (唯事) names the five aggregates themselves as nothing but the ripened result of past karma — grasped-at aggregates, but grasped by no one in particular. "Only causes" (唯因) sorts the twelve links into two functional groups: a "drawing" cause (ignorance and volitional formations, planting the seeds of a next rebirth) and a "generating" cause (craving, grasping, and becoming, which water those seeds into actual rebirth) — the drawing cause distant, the generating cause immediate. "Only effects" (唯果) names the two kinds of result that follow: the basic fact of one's own existence (how long one lives, in what body) and the specific experiences — pleasant or unpleasant — one encounters within that existence, both simply the ripening of past karma, however unequal they may look from the outside.

Having established that nothing but this five-fold "only" can be found across past, present, and future, the treatise names four principles that make this contemplation properly reasoned rather than mere assertion: the principle of dependence (each link genuinely depends on the prior one — no ignorance, no volitional formations), the principle of function (each link actually does something — ignorance functions to generate formations, and reversing the chain functions to extinguish suffering), the principle of proof (established by the Buddha's own teaching, by direct realization, and by inference), and the principle of intrinsic nature (this is simply how things are, Buddha or no Buddha — not invented, only discovered). Cháng Bǎi closes this object by naming its clinical use, given next: whoever's dominant affliction is delusion — not understanding that life is without self and without a controlling agent — has that delusion specifically thinned by sustained contemplation of dependent origination this way.

2. The Object of the Six Elements Opens

Chinese Source

云何界差別所緣？謂六界差別。一、地界，二、水界，三、火界，四、風界，五、空界，六、識界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the object of the differentiation of elements? It is the differentiation of the six elements: the earth element, the water element, the fire element, the wind element, the space element, and the consciousness element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth purifying object, per Master Cháng Bǎi, exists specifically to counter pride — seeing the body as nothing but six impersonal constituents leaves nothing left to be proud of. The desire realm and form realm's bodies are composed of all six; the formless realm has no earth, water, fire, or wind at all, only the space and consciousness elements. Concretely: earth is whatever is hard in the body — hair, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinew, bone; water is whatever is moist — saliva, mucus, pus, blood, other bodily fluids, urine; fire is whatever is warm — the body's own heat; wind is whatever moves — the breath and all bodily motion (what's colloquially called "qi" is this wind element, and "blood" the water element — a body in good health is traditionally described as having good "wind and water," the same phrase used for geomantic harmony); space is the necessary gaps and cavities without which no body could function — nostrils, ears, throat, stomach, intestines; and consciousness is the awareness that, if withdrawn, leaves a corpse rather than a living body. Cháng Bǎi notes this sixfold list will now be defined element by element in what follows, beginning with the earth element.

3. The Earth Element: Internal and External

Chinese Source

云何地界？地界有二，一、內，二、外。內地界者，謂此身中內別堅性，堅硬所攝、地地所攝，親附、執受。外地界者，謂外堅性，堅硬所攝，地地所攝，非親附、非執受。……謂髮、毛、爪、齒、塵垢、皮、肉、骸骨、筋、脈、肝、膽、心、肺、脾、腎、肚、胃、大腸、小腸、生藏、熟藏，及糞穢等，名內地界。……謂瓦、木、塊、礫、樹、石、山、巖，如是等類，名外地界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the earth element? The earth element is twofold: internal and external. The internal earth element is whatever hard quality within this body belongs to hardness, belongs to earth, and is bound to and appropriated by consciousness. The external earth element is whatever hard quality outside the body belongs to hardness and to earth, but is neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Hair, body hair, nails, teeth, accumulated grime, skin, flesh, bone, sinew, blood vessels, liver, gallbladder, heart, lungs, spleen, kidneys, stomach lining, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, undigested and digested matter, and excrement — these are the internal earth element. … Tile, wood, clods, gravel, trees, stone, mountains, and cliffs — such things as these are the external earth element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the technical distinction the treatise draws between "bound to" (親附) and "appropriated by" (執受): some hard parts of the body are only loosely attached — trimmed nail or cut hair that grows beyond living tissue feels nothing, so it is bound to the body but not appropriated by consciousness — while the living root still connected to nerve and blood is genuinely appropriated, and cutting it hurts. External earth — a felled tree, a stone, a mountain — shares the identical physical quality of hardness with the body's own bones and teeth; the only difference is that consciousness never claims it as "mine," so no pain accompanies its destruction. The point of the whole exercise, per Cháng Bǎi, is to notice that internal and external earth are made of exactly the same stuff — only appropriation (the sense of "mine") makes one part of it feel like "self" and the rest like mere object.

4. The Water Element: Internal and External

Chinese Source

云何水界？水界有二，一、內，二、外。內水界者，謂此身中內別濕性，濕潤所攝、水水所攝，親附、執受。……謂淚、汗、洟、唾、肪、膏、脂、髓、熱痰、膿血、腦、膜、尿等，名內水界。外水界者，謂外濕性，濕潤所攝、水水所攝，非親附、非執受。……謂井、泉、池、沼、陂、湖、河、海，如是等類，名外水界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the water element? The water element is twofold: internal and external. The internal water element is whatever moist quality within this body belongs to moisture, belongs to water, and is bound to and appropriated by consciousness. … Tears, sweat, mucus, saliva, fat, tallow, marrow, phlegm, pus and blood, brain matter, membrane, and urine — these are the internal water element. The external water element is whatever moist quality outside the body belongs to moisture and to water, but is neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Wells, springs, ponds, marshes, reservoirs, lakes, rivers, and seas — such things as these are the external water element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The same internal/external, bound/appropriated structure repeats exactly for water: every fluid inside the body — tears, sweat, blood, marrow, urine — is chemically no different in kind from the water in a well or a river outside it. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise is building a deliberate rhythm across all six elements, so that by the time it reaches fire, wind, space, and consciousness, the reader has already internalized the pattern: whatever quality the body seems to "own," the identical quality exists freely in the world outside it, unclaimed and unfelt. Nothing in the body is uniquely the body's own material — only the appropriating consciousness makes the difference.

5. The Fire Element: Internal and External

Chinese Source

云何火界？火界有二，一、內，二、外。內火界者，謂此身中內別溫性，溫熱所攝、煖煖所攝，親附、執受。……謂於身中所有溫煖，能令身熱、等熱、遍熱。由是因緣，所食、所飲、所噉、所嘗，易正消變。……外火界者，謂外溫性，溫熱所攝、煖煖所攝，非親附、非執受。……謂於人間依鑽燧等、牛糞末等，以求其火。火既生已，能燒牛糞、或草、或薪……或城、城分、或國、國分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the fire element? The fire element is twofold: internal and external. The internal fire element is whatever warm quality within this body belongs to heat and to warmth, bound to and appropriated by consciousness. … All the warmth within the body, which makes the body hot in one place, hot evenly, or hot throughout. Through this condition, whatever is eaten, drunk, chewed, or tasted is readily digested and transformed. … The external fire element is whatever warm quality outside the body belongs to heat and to warmth, but is neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … In the human world, fire is sought by drilling friction-sticks, by grinding dried dung, and other such means. Once fire has arisen, it can burn dung, grass, firewood … a city or part of a city, a country or part of a country.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Internal fire is the body's own heat — what traditional medicine calls "stomach fire," "liver fire," or "heart fire" — whose main function, per the treatise, is digesting whatever is eaten or drunk; too much of it and the body overheats or sweats, too little and digestion fails. External fire, kindled by friction-sticks, dung, or a burning glass, has an unlimited destructive range — it can consume anything from a handful of grass up through a village, city, or entire country, given the chance. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the same internal/external and bound/appropriated pattern holds exactly as with earth and water: the fire that warms living flesh and the fire that burns a forest are, in their basic nature, identical.

6. The Wind Element Opens — Twelve Kinds of Internal Wind

Chinese Source

云何風界？風界有二，一、內，二、外。內風界者，謂此身中內別風性，風飄所攝，輕性、動性，非親附、非執受。……謂內身中，有上行風、有下行風、有脅臥風、有脊臥風、有腰間風、有髖間風、有小刀風、有大刀風、有針刺風、有畢鉢羅風、有入出息風、有隨支節風。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the wind element? The wind element is twofold: internal and external. The internal wind element is whatever windy quality within this body belongs to motion, being of a light and mobile nature — neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Within the body there is upward-moving wind, downward-moving wind, side-lying wind, spine-lying wind, waist wind, hip wind, small-blade wind, great-blade wind, needle-piercing wind, mottling wind, in-and-out breath wind, and limb-and-joint wind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi flags an important asymmetry here: unlike earth, water, and fire, the internal wind element is explicitly defined as neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness — even inside a living body, wind is too restless and impersonal to be claimed as "mine" the way solid bone or warm blood are. He then walks through the twelve traditional named "winds," most of which name specific bodily conditions rather than ordinary healthy function: upward wind (belching, gas rising), downward wind (flatulence), winds forcing one to lie only on one's side or only flat on the spine, waist and hip winds (what acupuncture calls the "sea of energy"), small-blade and great-blade winds (the sharp, knife-like pain of conditions such as gout), needle-piercing wind (a stabbing sensation), mottling wind (a condition producing dark blotches on the skin), the breath itself (in-and-out wind, the only universally healthy one on the list), and limb-and-joint wind (the ordinary capacity for voluntary movement — writing, walking). Cháng Bǎi observes that this is also where the common word "stroke" (中風, literally "struck by wind") comes from: paralysis, in traditional diagnosis, is exactly this internal wind failing to move freely through the body.

7. External Wind, Space, and Consciousness Close the Six Elements

Chinese Source

外風界者，謂外風性，風飄所攝，輕性、動性，非親附、非執受。……謂在身外，有東來風、有西來風、有南來風、有北來風、有有塵風、有無塵風、有狹小風、有廣大風、有毗濕婆風、有吠藍婆風、有風輪風。有時大風卒起積集，折樹、頹牆、崩山、蕩海……云何空界？謂眼、耳、鼻、口、咽喉等所有孔穴。由此吞咽、於此吞咽，既吞咽已，由此孔穴便下漏泄。云何識界？謂眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意識。又心、意、識三種差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The external wind element is whatever windy quality outside the body belongs to motion, light and mobile in nature, neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Outside the body there is wind from the east, west, south, and north; wind carrying dust and wind free of dust; narrow, localized wind and vast, sweeping wind; the wind of primordial world-formation, the whirlwind, and the wind that encircles the world like a wheel. At times a great wind suddenly gathers and rises, snapping trees, toppling walls, collapsing mountains, and stirring seas … What is the space element? It is all the cavities and openings — the eyes, ears, nose, mouth, throat, and so on. Through these one swallows; having swallowed, waste is passed downward through these same openings. What is the consciousness element? It is the eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness — or, differently divided, mind, mentation, and consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

External wind closes with the same pattern as the other elements — the identical windy quality that moves the breath inside a living body also drives storms, dust clouds, and the vast planetary wind said to encircle the world, unclaimed by anyone. The space element is deliberately minimal: simply the necessary cavities and passages — eyes, ears, nose, mouth, throat, and by extension the whole digestive tract — without which nothing could be swallowed, absorbed, or expelled; a body with no gaps at all could not function. And the consciousness element closes the sixfold list by naming what makes the difference between a living body and a corpse: the six consciousnesses (or, in the finer threefold division Cháng Bǎi notes as the Mahāyāna elaboration, mind/mentation/consciousness — ālaya, the seventh consciousness, and the six active consciousnesses) — the one element among the six that is not itself physical, yet without which none of the other five could be appropriated as "mine" at all.

8. Milestone: The Six Elements Close — Antidote for Pride

Having defined all six elements individually, the treatise closes with their shared clinical function: dismantling the sense of the body as a single, solid, prideworthy whole.

Chinese Source

若諸慢行補特伽羅，於界差別作意思惟，便於身中離一合想，得不淨想。無復高舉，憍慢微薄，於諸慢行，心得清淨。是名慢行補特伽羅由界差別淨行所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a practitioner's dominant affliction is pride, attending to the differentiation of the elements dissolves, within the body, the notion of a single unified mass, giving rise instead to the perception of impurity. No further arrogance arises; conceit thins. This is called the mind of the pride-dominant practitioner being purified through the object of the differentiation of elements.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The whole point of the exercise, per Cháng Bǎi: pride depends entirely on perceiving the body as one solid, unified, special thing — "this is me, and I am impressive." Seeing it instead as six impersonal constituents, each identical in kind to something freely available outside the body and belonging to no one in particular, leaves nothing left to be proud of. "All phenomena are equal, with nothing higher or lower" — everyone's body is assembled from the same six elements in the same way, so what, precisely, is there to feel superior about? This closes the fourth of the five purifying objects. The fifth and final purifying object — mindfulness of the breath, ānāpānasati — opens next, the antidote for a scattered, restless mind.

9. Milestone: Mindfulness of the Breath Opens — the Fifth Purifying Object

The fifth and final purifying object opens: ānāpānasati, mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath, the antidote for a mind dominated by discursive scattering.

Chinese Source

云何阿那波那念所緣？……謂緣入息、出息念，是名阿那波那念。此念所緣入出息等，名阿那波那念所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the object of mindfulness of the breath? … Mindfulness that takes the in-breath and out-breath as its object is called mindfulness of the breath. What this mindfulness takes as its object — the in-breath, the out-breath, and so on — is called the object of mindfulness of the breath.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens with the etymology: ānā means "bringing in" (the in-breath), apāna means "carrying out" (the out-breath) — like a blacksmith's bellows, opening and closing, air drawn in and pushed out. The object itself is simple — the breath, moment to moment — but the treatise's "and so on" (等) signals something larger: from this single point of attention, the treatise says, five distinct modes of cultivation can be developed — counting the breath, and using the breath as a gateway into contemplating the aggregates, dependent origination, and the four noble truths through their sixteen aspects. What looks like the simplest of the five purifying objects, Cháng Bǎi notes, is in fact the one with the broadest reach: sustained breath-mindfulness can eventually open into every other major framework of insight the treatise teaches. Its immediate clinical use is naming a scattered, discursive mind and returning it, breath by breath, to a single point.

10. In-Breath and Out-Breath, and the Subtle Interval Between

Chinese Source

當知此中，入息有二。何等為二？一者、入息，二者、中間入息。出息亦二。何等為二？一者、出息，二者、中間出息。入息者，謂出息無間，內門風轉，始從面門、鼻門乃至臍處。中間入息者，謂入息滅已，乃至出息未生，於其中間在停息處，暫時相似微細風起，是名中間入息。如入息、中間入息，出息、中間出息，當知亦爾。此中差別者，謂入息無間，外門風轉，始從臍處，乃至面門、或至鼻端、或復出外。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that here, the in-breath is twofold. What are the two? The in-breath itself, and the mid-interval in-breath. The out-breath is likewise twofold: the out-breath itself, and the mid-interval out-breath. The in-breath is: immediately following the out-breath, wind turns inward through the inner gate, moving from the face and nose down to the navel. The mid-interval in-breath is: once the in-breath has ceased, and before the out-breath has yet arisen, in that interval, at the point of rest, a faint and subtle wind-like motion briefly arises — this is called the mid-interval in-breath. The out-breath and its mid-interval should be understood the same way, with this difference: immediately following the in-breath, wind turns outward through the outer gate, moving from the navel up to the face, or to the tip of the nose, or beyond, out of the body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise's most technically precise account of the breath cycle anywhere: in-breath travels downward, from the face or nostrils to the navel — the traditional "sea of energy" — and out-breath travels upward, from the navel back out through the nose or mouth. Between each in-breath and out-breath, once the coarse movement has fully ceased and before the next coarse movement has begun, there is a genuinely still point — no motion in or out — where only a faint, subtle residue of wind-like activity remains; this is the "mid-interval breath." Cháng Bǎi notes this interval is far too subtle for a beginner to notice; only once the mind itself has settled into real stillness does the practitioner become able to perceive it directly. Locating this gap accurately is itself an index of how deep one's concentration has become.

11. What Makes Breath Possible: Karma, Body, and Mind Together

Chinese Source

入息、出息有二因緣。何等為二？一、牽引業；二、臍處孔穴，或上身分所有孔穴。……入息、出息有二所依。何等為二？一、身，二、心。所以者何？要依身心，入出息轉，如其所應。……若唯依身而息轉者，入無想定、入滅盡定、生無想天諸有情類，彼息應轉。若唯依心而息轉者，入無色定、生無色界，彼息應轉。若唯依身心而轉，非如其所應者，入第四靜慮，若生於彼諸有情類，及羯羅藍、頞部曇、閉尸等位諸有情類，彼息應轉。然彼不轉。是故當知，要依身心，入出息轉，如其所應。

Ru-Yi Translation

The in-breath and out-breath have two conditions. What are the two? A drawing karma, and the cavity at the navel or the cavities of the upper body. … The in-breath and out-breath have two supports. What are the two? The body, and the mind. Why is this? Because the breath moves in dependence on both body and mind, as is fitting to each case. … If breath moved on the strength of the body alone, then beings in the attainment of no-perception, the attainment of cessation, and those born in the heaven of no-perception would still breathe. If breath moved on the strength of mind alone, then those in a formless absorption, or born in the formless realm, would still breathe. If breath moved simply because body and mind are both present, without regard to what is actually fitting, then those in the fourth absorption, or born there, and beings at the stages of the embryo's earliest weeks, would still breathe. Yet in none of these cases does breath move. Therefore know: breath moves in dependence on both body and mind, precisely as is fitting to each case.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a small masterclass in reasoned argument: the treatise proposes and then systematically refutes three tempting oversimplifications about what makes breathing happen. Body alone can't be sufficient, since a living body in deep meditative absorption without perception, in the still-deeper cessation-attainment available to advanced practitioners, or reborn into the heaven of no-perception, has no breath at all despite being fully physically present. Mind alone can't be sufficient either, since a mind absorbed in a formless-realm state, or reborn into the formless realm itself, is fully active yet likewise breathless. And simply having both body and mind present isn't sufficient on its own, since someone absorbed in the fourth meditative absorption — or reborn into its heaven — has both, and yet does not breathe; nor does an embryo in its earliest weeks in the womb, though its body and its sixth consciousness are both already present. The only complete answer, Cháng Bǎi explains, requires a further ingredient beyond mere body-and-mind: the specific karma that draws a being into a state where breath is "fitting" (如其所應) — some states of existence are simply not breath-supporting states, regardless of how fully body and mind are otherwise present. He notes the Abhidharma tradition explains the embryo and fourth-absorption cases further still: an early embryo's body is too insubstantial and its channels not yet open for breath to move without destroying it, while the fourth absorption's mind is so subtle, so fully turned inward, and so utterly still that the coarse agitation ordinary breath depends on simply has nothing left to arise from.

12. Two Loci, Four Names for the Breath

Chinese Source

一者、入息向下而行；二者、出息向上而行。入息、出息有二種地。何等為二？一、麤孔穴，二、細孔穴。云何麤孔穴？謂從臍處孔穴乃至面門、鼻門，復從面門、鼻門乃至臍處孔穴。云何細孔穴？謂於身中一切毛孔。入息、出息有四異名。何等為四？一名風，二名阿那波那，三名入息出息，四名身行。風名一種，是風共名；餘之三種，是不共名。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the in-breath travels downward; second, the out-breath travels upward. The in-breath and out-breath have two loci. What are the two? The coarse cavity, and the subtle cavity. What is the coarse cavity? It is the passage from the navel's cavity up to the face and nose, and from the face and nose back down to the navel's cavity. What is the subtle cavity? It is every pore throughout the body. The in-breath and out-breath have four other names. What are the four? First, wind; second, ānāpāna; third, in-breath and out-breath; fourth, bodily activity. "Wind" is the shared name, common to all kinds of wind; the other three are names unique to the breath alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes breath moves through two distinct channels at once: the coarse cavity — the windpipe's clear passage between navel and nose — and the subtle cavity, every pore of the skin, through which finer exchange with the outside air happens constantly, felt as a chill in the wind or warmth near a fire, even without conscious breathing. He then walks through the breath's four names, only one of which is shared: "wind" simply names it as one instance of the wind element generally, no different in kind from any other moving air; "ānāpāna" names it specifically as the object of this meditative practice — bringing in, carrying out; "in-breath and out-breath" names its two directions plainly; and "bodily activity" (身行) names the deeper point that the breath is what makes the body capable of movement at all — without this particular wind, nothing else in the body could move either.

13. Two Faults in Practice: Too Lax, Too Forced

Chinese Source

修入出息者有二過患。何等為二？一、太緩方便，二、太急方便。由太緩方便故，生起懈怠，或為惛沈睡眠纏擾其心，或令其心於外散亂。由太急方便故，或令其身生不平等，或令其心生不平等。云何令身生不平等？謂彊用力持入出息。由入出息被執持故，便令身中不平風轉。由此最初於諸支節皆生戰掉，名能戰掉。此戰掉風若增長時，能生疾病。由是因緣，於諸支節生諸疾病，是名令身生不平等。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are two faults in cultivating mindfulness of the breath. What are the two? Practicing too laxly, and practicing too forcefully. Through practicing too laxly, laziness arises; the mind becomes entangled in dullness and drowsiness, or else becomes scattered outward. Through practicing too forcefully, either the body or the mind becomes thrown out of balance. What is it for the body to become thrown out of balance? It is forcibly seizing hold of the breath. Because the breath is being forcibly controlled, an unbalanced wind arises within the body. From this, at first, trembling arises in the limbs and joints — this is called "trembling." If this trembling wind increases, it can produce illness. Through this condition, various afflictions arise in the limbs and joints — this is called the body becoming thrown out of balance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as practical, almost clinical advice, the kind a teacher gives a student who has just started sitting: practicing too laxly — going through the motions of counting the breath without real attention — leads either to dullness and drowsiness (nodding off mid-count) or to the mind simply wandering elsewhere, distracted by whatever crosses it. Practicing too forcefully is the opposite error, and arguably the more dangerous one for an eager beginner: gripping the breath tightly, trying to control or force it rather than simply observing it, disturbs the body's own natural wind and produces visible trembling in the hands, legs, or whole body; left uncorrected, this can develop into genuine physical illness. Cháng Bǎi's implicit teaching point, still to be completed with the matching account of mental imbalance: breath-mindfulness works only when it is neither slack nor strained — attentive but unforced, the same balance the treatise has praised throughout its account of meditative cultivation.

14. Milestone: The Two Faults Close; Five Modes of Cultivation Named

Mental imbalance closes the pair of faults from forcing the practice. The treatise then names all five modes into which breath-mindfulness can develop, before unpacking the first in detail: counting.

Chinese Source

云何令心生不平等？謂或令心生諸散亂，或為極重憂惱逼切。是名令心生不平等。……又此阿那波那念，應知略有五種修習。何等為五？一、算數修習，二、悟入諸蘊修習，三、悟入緣起修習，四、悟入聖諦修習，五、十六勝行修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is it for the mind to become thrown out of balance? It is either the mind giving rise to various kinds of scattering, or the mind being pressed by extremely heavy anxiety and distress. This is called the mind becoming thrown out of balance. … Know that this mindfulness of the breath, briefly stated, has five modes of cultivation. What are the five? First, cultivation through counting; second, cultivation through realizing the aggregates; third, cultivation through realizing dependent origination; fourth, cultivation through realizing the noble truths; fifth, cultivation through the sixteen excellent practices.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi completes the pair: where forcing the body produces trembling, forcing the mind produces either scattered anxiety (doubting whether one can ever attain concentration, whether one is even suited to the practice) or genuine distress heavy enough to make a practitioner want to abandon the method altogether. His practical takeaway: no meditative attainment arrives on a forced schedule — causes and conditions ripen at their own pace, and pressing for quick results only produces the very obstacles the practice is meant to remove.

With that groundwork laid, the treatise now names the five directions breath-mindfulness can develop into: simple counting (the entry point, detailed next); using the breath as a lens onto the five aggregates; using it to contemplate dependent origination directly; using it to realize the four noble truths; and a final, most refined mode — the "sixteen excellent practices," which Cháng Bǎi flags as the most complete and advanced of the five, still to come.

15. Counting the Breath: Two of Four Methods

Chinese Source

云何名為算數修習？謂略有四種算數修習。何等為四？一者、以一為一算數，二者、以二為一算數，三者、順算數，四者、逆算數。云何以一為一算數？謂若入息入時，由緣入息出息住念，數以為一。若入息滅，出息生，出向外時，數為第二。如是展轉數至其十。由此算數非略非廣，故唯至十。是名以一為一算數。云何以二為一算數？謂若入息入而已滅，出息生而已出，爾時總合數以為一。即由如是算數道理，數至其十，是名以二為一算數。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation through counting? Briefly, there are four methods of counting. What are the four? First, counting one for one; second, counting two as one; third, counting forward; fourth, counting backward. What is counting one for one? When the in-breath enters, attending to it with mindfulness that rests on the in-breath while the out-breath is stilled, one counts it as one. When the in-breath has ceased and the out-breath arises and moves outward, one counts it as two. Continuing this way, one counts up to ten. This count is neither too brief nor too long, and so goes only to ten. This is counting one for one. What is counting two as one? When the in-breath has entered and ceased, and the out-breath has arisen and gone out, at that point the two together are counted as one. Continuing by this same method up to ten — this is counting two as one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first two of four counting variants. "One for one" counts every single breath-movement separately — in-breath is one, out-breath is two, next in-breath is three, and so on up to ten, meaning five full in-out cycles complete one round. "Two as one" counts a complete in-and-out cycle as a single unit — one full breath (in plus out together) is one, the next full cycle is two, up to ten, meaning ten full cycles complete one round; alternatively, a practitioner may simply count only the in-breaths (or only the out-breaths) to ten, letting the other half of each cycle pass unnumbered — this is the more common approach in later meditation manuals such as the Śamatha-vipaśyanā text, which recommends counting only one direction. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise deliberately caps the count at ten precisely because it lands in the middle: counting only to two or three is too brief to gather a scattered mind, while counting to a hundred risks losing count itself through sheer length — ten is judged the ideal middle measure for settling attention without inviting further distraction.

16. Forward, Backward, and Advancing Counting

Chinese Source

云何順算數？謂或由以一為一算數，或由以二為一算數，順次展轉數至其十，名順算數。云何逆算數？謂即由前二種算數，逆次展轉從第十數，次九、次八……次數其一，名逆算數。……若時行者……於順算數及逆算數已串修習，於其中間心無散亂。無散亂心善算數已，復應為說勝進算數。云何名為勝進算數？謂或依以一為一算數，或依以二為一算數，合二為一而算數之。……如是後後漸增，乃至以百為一而算數之。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is forward counting? Whether by the one-for-one method or the two-as-one method, counting forward in sequence up to ten — this is forward counting. What is backward counting? By either of those same two methods, counting backward from ten — nine, eight … down to one — this is backward counting. … When a practitioner has thoroughly practiced forward and backward counting, by either method, such that the mind remains free of distraction throughout, and has mastered counting without distraction, one should then be taught advancing counting. What is advancing counting? Whether based on the one-for-one or the two-as-one method, one now counts two units as a single count. … Continuing to increase in this way, one eventually counts a hundred breaths as a single count.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Forward and backward counting simply apply the same one-for-one or two-as-one methods in either direction — one to ten, or ten down to one — and Cháng Bǎi notes different practitioners find different directions more effective at gathering a scattered mind; a more severely distracted mind often finds counting backward, being less habitual, more effective at holding attention. Once someone counts forward and backward, by either method, with genuinely zero distraction across a full round, they graduate to "advancing counting" — treating two breaths as one unit rather than one, then four as one, doubling repeatedly until, for the most disciplined practitioners, a full hundred breaths are held as a single count before advancing the number. Cháng Bǎi is candid that this most advanced degree isn't for everyone — it demands real memory and sustained attention, and most practitioners move from ordinary counting straight to simply following the breath rather than pushing counting this far; the treatise includes it, he says, because a few practitioners genuinely can do it.

17. Milestone: Mastery of Counting — Beyond the Need to Count

The treatise closes cultivation through counting: what to do when distraction breaks the count, and what happens when counting has been practiced so thoroughly that it becomes effortless and can finally be set aside.

Chinese Source

又此勤修數息念者，若於中間其心散亂，復應退還從初數起，或順、或逆。……若時算數極串習故，其心自然乘任運道，安住入息、出息所緣，無斷無間相續而轉。……如是展轉相續流注，無動無搖，無散亂行，有愛樂轉。齊此名為過算數地，不應復數。唯於入息、出息所緣，令心安住。……又鈍根者，應為宣說如是息念算數修習。彼由此故，於散亂處，令心安住、令心愛樂。……由正勤修數息念故，彼皆無有。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, in the midst of diligently cultivating mindfulness of the breath, the mind becomes distracted, one should withdraw and begin counting again from the start, whether forward or backward. … When counting has been so thoroughly practiced that the mind naturally rides an effortless path, abiding on the object of the in-breath and out-breath without break or interruption … flowing on continuously in this way, unmoved, unshaken, free of distraction, with a sense of genuine delight — at this point one has passed beyond the stage of counting, and should count no longer, but simply let the mind abide on the in-breath and out-breath as its object. … For a practitioner of dull faculties, this cultivation through counting should be taught, so that in the midst of distraction the mind may be made to abide, and made to delight in the practice. … Through correctly and diligently cultivating mindfulness of the breath in this way, these afflictions are altogether absent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The remedy for losing count mid-practice is simple and non-negotiable, per Cháng Bǎi: no picking up where one thinks one left off — genuinely start again from one (or from ten, if counting backward). Guessing invites exactly the sloppiness the practice exists to correct. But the real destination of counting practice, he stresses, is to make itself unnecessary: once a practitioner can hold the count through a full round with zero distraction, effortlessly, taking genuine pleasure in the practice rather than needing to force it, they have "passed beyond the stage of counting" — the numbers drop away, and only bare, continuous awareness of the breath itself remains, in and out, without further labeling. Cháng Bǎi closes by naming counting's proper audience: it exists specifically for practitioners of duller faculties, whose minds most need a concrete number to hold onto against distraction; for such a practitioner, faithfully practiced counting genuinely eliminates both the drowsiness and the outward scattering named earlier as the fault of practicing too laxly. Having thoroughly closed cultivation through counting, the treatise turns next to how sharper-facultied practitioners are taught — likely bypassing counting for a more direct method, which the text takes up next.

18. Milestone: Counting Closes Entirely; Aggregates Opens as Mode Two

The treatise briefly notes how sharp-facultied practitioners relate to counting, names the fruit of diligent practice for both types, formally closing mode one — then opens mode two: using the breath as a lens to directly recognize the five aggregates.

Chinese Source

若有利根，覺慧聰俊，不好乘此算數加行。若為宣說算數加行，亦能速疾無倒了達，然不愛樂。……於此加行若修、若習、若多修習為因緣故，起身輕安及心輕安，證一境性，於其所緣愛樂趣入。……如是彼於算數息念善修習已，復於所取、能取二事，作意思惟，悟入諸蘊。謂於入息、出息，及息所依身，作意思惟，悟入色蘊。於彼入息、出息能取念相應領納，作意思惟，悟入受蘊。即於彼念相應等了，作意思惟，悟入想蘊。即於彼念，若念相應思及慧等，作意思惟，悟入行蘊。若於彼念相應諸心、意、識，作意思惟，悟入識蘊。如是行者，於諸蘊中乃至多住，名已悟入。是名悟入諸蘊修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sharp-facultied practitioner, being naturally clear and quick of mind, does not take pleasure in this counting practice. Even if taught it, such a person understands it swiftly and without error, yet finds no delight in it. … Through practicing, repeating, and thoroughly cultivating this discipline, physical and mental pliancy arise, one-pointedness is realized, and the practitioner enters this object with genuine delight. … Once a practitioner has thoroughly cultivated mindfulness through counting, further attending to the object grasped and the mind that grasps it leads to realizing the aggregates. Attending to the in-breath, the out-breath, and the body they depend on realizes the aggregate of form. Attending to the sensation accompanying the mindfulness that grasps the breath realizes the aggregate of feeling. Attending to the recognition accompanying that mindfulness realizes the aggregate of perception. Attending to the intention and discernment accompanying that mindfulness realizes the aggregate of formations. Attending to the mind, mentation, and consciousness accompanying that mindfulness realizes the aggregate of consciousness. Dwelling extensively within this realization of the aggregates — this is called having truly realized them. This is cultivation through realizing the aggregates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp-faculty aside as a small but telling observation: a naturally quick mind grasps the mechanics of counting instantly but simply doesn't enjoy the exercise — counting is a tool for the duller-facultied, not a universal requirement. Both types, though, arrive at the same fruit through diligent practice: bodily and mental pliancy (輕安, the settled ease that precedes genuine absorption) and one-pointedness of mind, engaging the breath with real delight rather than effort. This closes mode one entirely.

Mode two, Cháng Bǎi explains, uses the very same breath-awareness as a springboard into recognizing the five aggregates directly, rather than as an abstract doctrine: the breath and the body it depends on are simply form (色); the felt sensations accompanying breath-awareness — long, short, cool, warm — are feeling (受); the act of recognizing "this is the in-breath, this is the out-breath" is perception (想); the volition and discernment choosing which counting method to apply, when to advance, when to restart, is formations (行); and the very mind, mentation, and consciousness doing all this attending is consciousness (識) itself. Nothing new is introduced — the same breath-practice a practitioner has already mastered turns out, on close inspection, to already contain all five aggregates in miniature. Dwelling in this recognition repeatedly is what "realizing the aggregates" means.

19. Milestone: Dependent Origination Opens as Mode Three

The third mode traces the breath backward through a chain of dependency — from breath, to body and mind, to the life faculty, to past karmic action — the beginning of a direct, experiential realization of dependent origination.

Chinese Source

若時無倒，能見能知唯有諸蘊、唯有諸行、唯事、唯法。彼於爾時，能於諸行悟入緣起。云何悟入？謂觀行者，如是尋求：此入出息何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：此入出息依身緣身、依心緣心。復更尋求：此身此心何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：此身此心依緣命根。復更尋求：如是命根何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：如是命根依緣先行。

Ru-Yi Translation

When, without distortion, one can see and know only aggregates, only conditioned formations, only events, only phenomena — at that time one is able to realize dependent origination with respect to these formations. How does one realize it? The contemplative practitioner inquires: on what does this in-breath and out-breath depend, and what is its condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this in-breath and out-breath depends on the body and is conditioned by the body, depends on the mind and is conditioned by the mind. One inquires further: on what do this body and this mind depend, and what is their condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this body and this mind depend on and are conditioned by the life faculty. One inquires yet further: on what does this life faculty depend, and what is its condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this life faculty depends on and is conditioned by prior karmic action.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames mode three as taking the same five-fold "only" analysis from the earlier dependent-origination object (fascicle 27's opening topic) and now grounding it in direct, first-person inquiry starting from the one thing always immediately available to attention: the breath itself. The chain of questioning unwinds backward one link at a time — the breath depends on body and mind; body and mind in turn depend on the life faculty (the karmic force that keeps a particular body-mind continuum running for its allotted span); and the life faculty itself depends on nothing more mysterious than past karmic action — volitional deeds performed in a previous life, whose accumulated force is what determined how long this life's "clock" was set to run. Cháng Bǎi notes the practitioner is meant to trace this regress experientially, not merely recite it: each step is something to be "truly realized" (如實悟入) through actual inquiry into one's own breath, not accepted as doctrine. The chain will continue further back, presumably toward the earlier links of the twelve-fold formula — ignorance and volitional formations — in what follows.
