Dependent Origination and the Six Elements

Fascicle 27 continues the second yogic process's third question — objects of meditation — picking up where fascicle 26 left off. Having closed impurity and loving-kindness, the treatise now gives full standalone treatment to the remaining purifying objects: the object of dependent origination, the object of the six elements, and mindfulness of the breath.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 27 complete Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 27 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Object of Dependent Origination Opens

Fascicle 27 opens as the second part of the second yogic process's continuous unfolding, resuming directly where fascicle 26 closed: two of five purifying objects (impurity, loving-kindness) are done; three remain (dependent origination, the elements, breath-mindfulness), followed by skillful objects and objects that purify affliction.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之二……云何緣性緣起所緣？謂於三世唯行、唯法、唯事、唯因、唯果，墮正道理。謂觀待道理、作用道理、證成道理、法爾道理。……唯有諸法能引諸法，無有作者及以受者，是名緣性緣起所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the second part of the second yogic process … What is the object of dependent origination? It is contemplating, across the three times, only conditioned formations, only phenomena, only events, only causes, and only effects — falling within the correct principles: the principle of dependence, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of intrinsic nature. … Only phenomena drawing forth further phenomena — there is no agent, and no one who experiences the result. This is called the object of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating this object precisely as the third of the five purifying objects, and immediately flags its unusual density: unlike impurity or loving-kindness, dependent origination is analyzed here through five successive "only" (唯) qualifiers, each stripping away a different layer of the illusion of a controlling self. "Only conditioned formations" (唯行) names the sheer arising-and-perishing of phenomena moment to moment — nothing here that doesn't immediately pass away. "Only phenomena" (唯法) names the fact that what we call "a life" is nothing but language and concept describing a process, with no experiencer standing behind it — the Saṃyukta Āgama's classic formula, "this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises," describes phenomena conditioning phenomena, not a person undergoing anything. "Only events" (唯事) names the five aggregates themselves as nothing but the ripened result of past karma — grasped-at aggregates, but grasped by no one in particular. "Only causes" (唯因) sorts the twelve links into two functional groups: a "drawing" cause (ignorance and volitional formations, planting the seeds of a next rebirth) and a "generating" cause (craving, grasping, and becoming, which water those seeds into actual rebirth) — the drawing cause distant, the generating cause immediate. "Only effects" (唯果) names the two kinds of result that follow: the basic fact of one's own existence (how long one lives, in what body) and the specific experiences — pleasant or unpleasant — one encounters within that existence, both simply the ripening of past karma, however unequal they may look from the outside.

Having established that nothing but this five-fold "only" can be found across past, present, and future, the treatise names four principles that make this contemplation properly reasoned rather than mere assertion: the principle of dependence (each link genuinely depends on the prior one — no ignorance, no volitional formations), the principle of function (each link actually does something — ignorance functions to generate formations, and reversing the chain functions to extinguish suffering), the principle of proof (established by the Buddha's own teaching, by direct realization, and by inference), and the principle of intrinsic nature (this is simply how things are, Buddha or no Buddha — not invented, only discovered). Cháng Bǎi closes this object by naming its clinical use, given next: whoever's dominant affliction is delusion — not understanding that life is without self and without a controlling agent — has that delusion specifically thinned by sustained contemplation of dependent origination this way.

2. The Object of the Six Elements Opens

Chinese Source

云何界差別所緣？謂六界差別。一、地界，二、水界，三、火界，四、風界，五、空界，六、識界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the object of the differentiation of elements? It is the differentiation of the six elements: the earth element, the water element, the fire element, the wind element, the space element, and the consciousness element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth purifying object, per Master Cháng Bǎi, exists specifically to counter pride — seeing the body as nothing but six impersonal constituents leaves nothing left to be proud of. The desire realm and form realm's bodies are composed of all six; the formless realm has no earth, water, fire, or wind at all, only the space and consciousness elements. Concretely: earth is whatever is hard in the body — hair, nails, teeth, skin, flesh, sinew, bone; water is whatever is moist — saliva, mucus, pus, blood, other bodily fluids, urine; fire is whatever is warm — the body's own heat; wind is whatever moves — the breath and all bodily motion (what's colloquially called "qi" is this wind element, and "blood" the water element — a body in good health is traditionally described as having good "wind and water," the same phrase used for geomantic harmony); space is the necessary gaps and cavities without which no body could function — nostrils, ears, throat, stomach, intestines; and consciousness is the awareness that, if withdrawn, leaves a corpse rather than a living body. Cháng Bǎi notes this sixfold list will now be defined element by element in what follows, beginning with the earth element.

3. The Earth Element: Internal and External

Chinese Source

云何地界？地界有二，一、內，二、外。內地界者，謂此身中內別堅性，堅硬所攝、地地所攝，親附、執受。外地界者，謂外堅性，堅硬所攝，地地所攝，非親附、非執受。……謂髮、毛、爪、齒、塵垢、皮、肉、骸骨、筋、脈、肝、膽、心、肺、脾、腎、肚、胃、大腸、小腸、生藏、熟藏，及糞穢等，名內地界。……謂瓦、木、塊、礫、樹、石、山、巖，如是等類，名外地界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the earth element? The earth element is twofold: internal and external. The internal earth element is whatever hard quality within this body belongs to hardness, belongs to earth, and is bound to and appropriated by consciousness. The external earth element is whatever hard quality outside the body belongs to hardness and to earth, but is neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Hair, body hair, nails, teeth, accumulated grime, skin, flesh, bone, sinew, blood vessels, liver, gallbladder, heart, lungs, spleen, kidneys, stomach lining, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, undigested and digested matter, and excrement — these are the internal earth element. … Tile, wood, clods, gravel, trees, stone, mountains, and cliffs — such things as these are the external earth element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the technical distinction the treatise draws between "bound to" (親附) and "appropriated by" (執受): some hard parts of the body are only loosely attached — trimmed nail or cut hair that grows beyond living tissue feels nothing, so it is bound to the body but not appropriated by consciousness — while the living root still connected to nerve and blood is genuinely appropriated, and cutting it hurts. External earth — a felled tree, a stone, a mountain — shares the identical physical quality of hardness with the body's own bones and teeth; the only difference is that consciousness never claims it as "mine," so no pain accompanies its destruction. The point of the whole exercise, per Cháng Bǎi, is to notice that internal and external earth are made of exactly the same stuff — only appropriation (the sense of "mine") makes one part of it feel like "self" and the rest like mere object.

4. The Water Element: Internal and External

Chinese Source

云何水界？水界有二，一、內，二、外。內水界者，謂此身中內別濕性，濕潤所攝、水水所攝，親附、執受。……謂淚、汗、洟、唾、肪、膏、脂、髓、熱痰、膿血、腦、膜、尿等，名內水界。外水界者，謂外濕性，濕潤所攝、水水所攝，非親附、非執受。……謂井、泉、池、沼、陂、湖、河、海，如是等類，名外水界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the water element? The water element is twofold: internal and external. The internal water element is whatever moist quality within this body belongs to moisture, belongs to water, and is bound to and appropriated by consciousness. … Tears, sweat, mucus, saliva, fat, tallow, marrow, phlegm, pus and blood, brain matter, membrane, and urine — these are the internal water element. The external water element is whatever moist quality outside the body belongs to moisture and to water, but is neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Wells, springs, ponds, marshes, reservoirs, lakes, rivers, and seas — such things as these are the external water element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The same internal/external, bound/appropriated structure repeats exactly for water: every fluid inside the body — tears, sweat, blood, marrow, urine — is chemically no different in kind from the water in a well or a river outside it. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise is building a deliberate rhythm across all six elements, so that by the time it reaches fire, wind, space, and consciousness, the reader has already internalized the pattern: whatever quality the body seems to "own," the identical quality exists freely in the world outside it, unclaimed and unfelt. Nothing in the body is uniquely the body's own material — only the appropriating consciousness makes the difference.

5. The Fire Element: Internal and External

Chinese Source

云何火界？火界有二，一、內，二、外。內火界者，謂此身中內別溫性，溫熱所攝、煖煖所攝，親附、執受。……謂於身中所有溫煖，能令身熱、等熱、遍熱。由是因緣，所食、所飲、所噉、所嘗，易正消變。……外火界者，謂外溫性，溫熱所攝、煖煖所攝，非親附、非執受。……謂於人間依鑽燧等、牛糞末等，以求其火。火既生已，能燒牛糞、或草、或薪……或城、城分、或國、國分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the fire element? The fire element is twofold: internal and external. The internal fire element is whatever warm quality within this body belongs to heat and to warmth, bound to and appropriated by consciousness. … All the warmth within the body, which makes the body hot in one place, hot evenly, or hot throughout. Through this condition, whatever is eaten, drunk, chewed, or tasted is readily digested and transformed. … The external fire element is whatever warm quality outside the body belongs to heat and to warmth, but is neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … In the human world, fire is sought by drilling friction-sticks, by grinding dried dung, and other such means. Once fire has arisen, it can burn dung, grass, firewood … a city or part of a city, a country or part of a country.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Internal fire is the body's own heat — what traditional medicine calls "stomach fire," "liver fire," or "heart fire" — whose main function, per the treatise, is digesting whatever is eaten or drunk; too much of it and the body overheats or sweats, too little and digestion fails. External fire, kindled by friction-sticks, dung, or a burning glass, has an unlimited destructive range — it can consume anything from a handful of grass up through a village, city, or entire country, given the chance. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the same internal/external and bound/appropriated pattern holds exactly as with earth and water: the fire that warms living flesh and the fire that burns a forest are, in their basic nature, identical.

6. The Wind Element Opens — Twelve Kinds of Internal Wind

Chinese Source

云何風界？風界有二，一、內，二、外。內風界者，謂此身中內別風性，風飄所攝，輕性、動性，非親附、非執受。……謂內身中，有上行風、有下行風、有脅臥風、有脊臥風、有腰間風、有髖間風、有小刀風、有大刀風、有針刺風、有畢鉢羅風、有入出息風、有隨支節風。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the wind element? The wind element is twofold: internal and external. The internal wind element is whatever windy quality within this body belongs to motion, being of a light and mobile nature — neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Within the body there is upward-moving wind, downward-moving wind, side-lying wind, spine-lying wind, waist wind, hip wind, small-blade wind, great-blade wind, needle-piercing wind, mottling wind, in-and-out breath wind, and limb-and-joint wind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi flags an important asymmetry here: unlike earth, water, and fire, the internal wind element is explicitly defined as neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness — even inside a living body, wind is too restless and impersonal to be claimed as "mine" the way solid bone or warm blood are. He then walks through the twelve traditional named "winds," most of which name specific bodily conditions rather than ordinary healthy function: upward wind (belching, gas rising), downward wind (flatulence), winds forcing one to lie only on one's side or only flat on the spine, waist and hip winds (what acupuncture calls the "sea of energy"), small-blade and great-blade winds (the sharp, knife-like pain of conditions such as gout), needle-piercing wind (a stabbing sensation), mottling wind (a condition producing dark blotches on the skin), the breath itself (in-and-out wind, the only universally healthy one on the list), and limb-and-joint wind (the ordinary capacity for voluntary movement — writing, walking). Cháng Bǎi observes that this is also where the common word "stroke" (中風, literally "struck by wind") comes from: paralysis, in traditional diagnosis, is exactly this internal wind failing to move freely through the body.

7. External Wind, Space, and Consciousness Close the Six Elements

Chinese Source

外風界者，謂外風性，風飄所攝，輕性、動性，非親附、非執受。……謂在身外，有東來風、有西來風、有南來風、有北來風、有有塵風、有無塵風、有狹小風、有廣大風、有毗濕婆風、有吠藍婆風、有風輪風。有時大風卒起積集，折樹、頹牆、崩山、蕩海……云何空界？謂眼、耳、鼻、口、咽喉等所有孔穴。由此吞咽、於此吞咽，既吞咽已，由此孔穴便下漏泄。云何識界？謂眼、耳、鼻、舌、身、意識。又心、意、識三種差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The external wind element is whatever windy quality outside the body belongs to motion, light and mobile in nature, neither bound to nor appropriated by consciousness. … Outside the body there is wind from the east, west, south, and north; wind carrying dust and wind free of dust; narrow, localized wind and vast, sweeping wind; the wind of primordial world-formation, the whirlwind, and the wind that encircles the world like a wheel. At times a great wind suddenly gathers and rises, snapping trees, toppling walls, collapsing mountains, and stirring seas … What is the space element? It is all the cavities and openings — the eyes, ears, nose, mouth, throat, and so on. Through these one swallows; having swallowed, waste is passed downward through these same openings. What is the consciousness element? It is the eye-, ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness — or, differently divided, mind, mentation, and consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

External wind closes with the same pattern as the other elements — the identical windy quality that moves the breath inside a living body also drives storms, dust clouds, and the vast planetary wind said to encircle the world, unclaimed by anyone. The space element is deliberately minimal: simply the necessary cavities and passages — eyes, ears, nose, mouth, throat, and by extension the whole digestive tract — without which nothing could be swallowed, absorbed, or expelled; a body with no gaps at all could not function. And the consciousness element closes the sixfold list by naming what makes the difference between a living body and a corpse: the six consciousnesses (or, in the finer threefold division Cháng Bǎi notes as the Mahāyāna elaboration, mind/mentation/consciousness — ālaya, the seventh consciousness, and the six active consciousnesses) — the one element among the six that is not itself physical, yet without which none of the other five could be appropriated as "mine" at all.

8. Section: The Six Elements Close — Antidote for Pride

Having defined all six elements individually, the treatise closes with their shared clinical function: dismantling the sense of the body as a single, solid, prideworthy whole.

Chinese Source

若諸慢行補特伽羅，於界差別作意思惟，便於身中離一合想，得不淨想。無復高舉，憍慢微薄，於諸慢行，心得清淨。是名慢行補特伽羅由界差別淨行所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a practitioner's dominant affliction is pride, attending to the differentiation of the elements dissolves, within the body, the notion of a single unified mass, giving rise instead to the perception of impurity. No further arrogance arises; conceit thins. This is called the mind of the pride-dominant practitioner being purified through the object of the differentiation of elements.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The whole point of the exercise, per Cháng Bǎi: pride depends entirely on perceiving the body as one solid, unified, special thing — "this is me, and I am impressive." Seeing it instead as six impersonal constituents, each identical in kind to something freely available outside the body and belonging to no one in particular, leaves nothing left to be proud of. "All phenomena are equal, with nothing higher or lower" — everyone's body is assembled from the same six elements in the same way, so what, precisely, is there to feel superior about? This closes the fourth of the five purifying objects. The fifth and final purifying object — mindfulness of the breath, ānāpānasati — opens next, the antidote for a scattered, restless mind.

9. Section: Mindfulness of the Breath Opens — the Fifth Purifying Object

The fifth and final purifying object opens: ānāpānasati, mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath, the antidote for a mind dominated by discursive scattering.

Chinese Source

云何阿那波那念所緣？……謂緣入息、出息念，是名阿那波那念。此念所緣入出息等，名阿那波那念所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the object of mindfulness of the breath? … Mindfulness that takes the in-breath and out-breath as its object is called mindfulness of the breath. What this mindfulness takes as its object — the in-breath, the out-breath, and so on — is called the object of mindfulness of the breath.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens with the etymology: ānā means "bringing in" (the in-breath), apāna means "carrying out" (the out-breath) — like a blacksmith's bellows, opening and closing, air drawn in and pushed out. The object itself is simple — the breath, moment to moment — but the treatise's "and so on" (等) signals something larger: from this single point of attention, the treatise says, five distinct modes of cultivation can be developed — counting the breath, and using the breath as a gateway into contemplating the aggregates, dependent origination, and the four noble truths through their sixteen aspects. What looks like the simplest of the five purifying objects, Cháng Bǎi notes, is in fact the one with the broadest reach: sustained breath-mindfulness can eventually open into every other major framework of insight the treatise teaches. Its immediate clinical use is naming a scattered, discursive mind and returning it, breath by breath, to a single point.

10. In-Breath and Out-Breath, and the Subtle Interval Between

Chinese Source

當知此中，入息有二。何等為二？一者、入息，二者、中間入息。出息亦二。何等為二？一者、出息，二者、中間出息。入息者，謂出息無間，內門風轉，始從面門、鼻門乃至臍處。中間入息者，謂入息滅已，乃至出息未生，於其中間在停息處，暫時相似微細風起，是名中間入息。如入息、中間入息，出息、中間出息，當知亦爾。此中差別者，謂入息無間，外門風轉，始從臍處，乃至面門、或至鼻端、或復出外。

Ru-Yi Translation

Know that here, the in-breath is twofold. What are the two? The in-breath itself, and the mid-interval in-breath. The out-breath is likewise twofold: the out-breath itself, and the mid-interval out-breath. The in-breath is: immediately following the out-breath, wind turns inward through the inner gate, moving from the face and nose down to the navel. The mid-interval in-breath is: once the in-breath has ceased, and before the out-breath has yet arisen, in that interval, at the point of rest, a faint and subtle wind-like motion briefly arises — this is called the mid-interval in-breath. The out-breath and its mid-interval should be understood the same way, with this difference: immediately following the in-breath, wind turns outward through the outer gate, moving from the navel up to the face, or to the tip of the nose, or beyond, out of the body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise's most technically precise account of the breath cycle anywhere: in-breath travels downward, from the face or nostrils to the navel — the traditional "sea of energy" — and out-breath travels upward, from the navel back out through the nose or mouth. Between each in-breath and out-breath, once the coarse movement has fully ceased and before the next coarse movement has begun, there is a genuinely still point — no motion in or out — where only a faint, subtle residue of wind-like activity remains; this is the "mid-interval breath." Cháng Bǎi notes this interval is far too subtle for a beginner to notice; only once the mind itself has settled into real stillness does the practitioner become able to perceive it directly. Locating this gap accurately is itself an index of how deep one's concentration has become.

11. What Makes Breath Possible: Karma, Body, and Mind Together

Chinese Source

入息、出息有二因緣。何等為二？一、牽引業；二、臍處孔穴，或上身分所有孔穴。……入息、出息有二所依。何等為二？一、身，二、心。所以者何？要依身心，入出息轉，如其所應。……若唯依身而息轉者，入無想定、入滅盡定、生無想天諸有情類，彼息應轉。若唯依心而息轉者，入無色定、生無色界，彼息應轉。若唯依身心而轉，非如其所應者，入第四靜慮，若生於彼諸有情類，及羯羅藍、頞部曇、閉尸等位諸有情類，彼息應轉。然彼不轉。是故當知，要依身心，入出息轉，如其所應。

Ru-Yi Translation

The in-breath and out-breath have two conditions. What are the two? A drawing karma, and the cavity at the navel or the cavities of the upper body. … The in-breath and out-breath have two supports. What are the two? The body, and the mind. Why is this? Because the breath moves in dependence on both body and mind, as is fitting to each case. … If breath moved on the strength of the body alone, then beings in the attainment of no-perception, the attainment of cessation, and those born in the heaven of no-perception would still breathe. If breath moved on the strength of mind alone, then those in a formless absorption, or born in the formless realm, would still breathe. If breath moved simply because body and mind are both present, without regard to what is actually fitting, then those in the fourth absorption, or born there, and beings at the stages of the embryo's earliest weeks, would still breathe. Yet in none of these cases does breath move. Therefore know: breath moves in dependence on both body and mind, precisely as is fitting to each case.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a small masterclass in reasoned argument: the treatise proposes and then systematically refutes three tempting oversimplifications about what makes breathing happen. Body alone can't be sufficient, since a living body in deep meditative absorption without perception, in the still-deeper cessation-attainment available to advanced practitioners, or reborn into the heaven of no-perception, has no breath at all despite being fully physically present. Mind alone can't be sufficient either, since a mind absorbed in a formless-realm state, or reborn into the formless realm itself, is fully active yet likewise breathless. And simply having both body and mind present isn't sufficient on its own, since someone absorbed in the fourth meditative absorption — or reborn into its heaven — has both, and yet does not breathe; nor does an embryo in its earliest weeks in the womb, though its body and its sixth consciousness are both already present. The only complete answer, Cháng Bǎi explains, requires a further ingredient beyond mere body-and-mind: the specific karma that draws a being into a state where breath is "fitting" (如其所應) — some states of existence are simply not breath-supporting states, regardless of how fully body and mind are otherwise present. He notes the Abhidharma tradition explains the embryo and fourth-absorption cases further still: an early embryo's body is too insubstantial and its channels not yet open for breath to move without destroying it, while the fourth absorption's mind is so subtle, so fully turned inward, and so utterly still that the coarse agitation ordinary breath depends on simply has nothing left to arise from.

12. Two Loci, Four Names for the Breath

Chinese Source

一者、入息向下而行；二者、出息向上而行。入息、出息有二種地。何等為二？一、麤孔穴，二、細孔穴。云何麤孔穴？謂從臍處孔穴乃至面門、鼻門，復從面門、鼻門乃至臍處孔穴。云何細孔穴？謂於身中一切毛孔。入息、出息有四異名。何等為四？一名風，二名阿那波那，三名入息出息，四名身行。風名一種，是風共名；餘之三種，是不共名。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the in-breath travels downward; second, the out-breath travels upward. The in-breath and out-breath have two loci. What are the two? The coarse cavity, and the subtle cavity. What is the coarse cavity? It is the passage from the navel's cavity up to the face and nose, and from the face and nose back down to the navel's cavity. What is the subtle cavity? It is every pore throughout the body. The in-breath and out-breath have four other names. What are the four? First, wind; second, ānāpāna; third, in-breath and out-breath; fourth, bodily activity. "Wind" is the shared name, common to all kinds of wind; the other three are names unique to the breath alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes breath moves through two distinct channels at once: the coarse cavity — the windpipe's clear passage between navel and nose — and the subtle cavity, every pore of the skin, through which finer exchange with the outside air happens constantly, felt as a chill in the wind or warmth near a fire, even without conscious breathing. He then walks through the breath's four names, only one of which is shared: "wind" simply names it as one instance of the wind element generally, no different in kind from any other moving air; "ānāpāna" names it specifically as the object of this meditative practice — bringing in, carrying out; "in-breath and out-breath" names its two directions plainly; and "bodily activity" (身行) names the deeper point that the breath is what makes the body capable of movement at all — without this particular wind, nothing else in the body could move either.

13. Two Faults in Practice: Too Lax, Too Forced

Chinese Source

修入出息者有二過患。何等為二？一、太緩方便，二、太急方便。由太緩方便故，生起懈怠，或為惛沈睡眠纏擾其心，或令其心於外散亂。由太急方便故，或令其身生不平等，或令其心生不平等。云何令身生不平等？謂彊用力持入出息。由入出息被執持故，便令身中不平風轉。由此最初於諸支節皆生戰掉，名能戰掉。此戰掉風若增長時，能生疾病。由是因緣，於諸支節生諸疾病，是名令身生不平等。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are two faults in cultivating mindfulness of the breath. What are the two? Practicing too laxly, and practicing too forcefully. Through practicing too laxly, laziness arises; the mind becomes entangled in dullness and drowsiness, or else becomes scattered outward. Through practicing too forcefully, either the body or the mind becomes thrown out of balance. What is it for the body to become thrown out of balance? It is forcibly seizing hold of the breath. Because the breath is being forcibly controlled, an unbalanced wind arises within the body. From this, at first, trembling arises in the limbs and joints — this is called "trembling." If this trembling wind increases, it can produce illness. Through this condition, various afflictions arise in the limbs and joints — this is called the body becoming thrown out of balance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as practical, almost clinical advice, the kind a teacher gives a student who has just started sitting: practicing too laxly — going through the motions of counting the breath without real attention — leads either to dullness and drowsiness (nodding off mid-count) or to the mind simply wandering elsewhere, distracted by whatever crosses it. Practicing too forcefully is the opposite error, and arguably the more dangerous one for an eager beginner: gripping the breath tightly, trying to control or force it rather than simply observing it, disturbs the body's own natural wind and produces visible trembling in the hands, legs, or whole body; left uncorrected, this can develop into genuine physical illness. Cháng Bǎi's implicit teaching point, still to be completed with the matching account of mental imbalance: breath-mindfulness works only when it is neither slack nor strained — attentive but unforced, the same balance the treatise has praised throughout its account of meditative cultivation.

14. Section: The Two Faults Close; Five Modes of Cultivation Named

Mental imbalance closes the pair of faults from forcing the practice. The treatise then names all five modes into which breath-mindfulness can develop, before unpacking the first in detail: counting.

Chinese Source

云何令心生不平等？謂或令心生諸散亂，或為極重憂惱逼切。是名令心生不平等。……又此阿那波那念，應知略有五種修習。何等為五？一、算數修習，二、悟入諸蘊修習，三、悟入緣起修習，四、悟入聖諦修習，五、十六勝行修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is it for the mind to become thrown out of balance? It is either the mind giving rise to various kinds of scattering, or the mind being pressed by extremely heavy anxiety and distress. This is called the mind becoming thrown out of balance. … Know that this mindfulness of the breath, briefly stated, has five modes of cultivation. What are the five? First, cultivation through counting; second, cultivation through realizing the aggregates; third, cultivation through realizing dependent origination; fourth, cultivation through realizing the noble truths; fifth, cultivation through the sixteen excellent practices.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi completes the pair: where forcing the body produces trembling, forcing the mind produces either scattered anxiety (doubting whether one can ever attain concentration, whether one is even suited to the practice) or genuine distress heavy enough to make a practitioner want to abandon the method altogether. His practical takeaway: no meditative attainment arrives on a forced schedule — causes and conditions ripen at their own pace, and pressing for quick results only produces the very obstacles the practice is meant to remove.

With that groundwork laid, the treatise now names the five directions breath-mindfulness can develop into: simple counting (the entry point, detailed next); using the breath as a lens onto the five aggregates; using it to contemplate dependent origination directly; using it to realize the four noble truths; and a final, most refined mode — the "sixteen excellent practices," which Cháng Bǎi flags as the most complete and advanced of the five, still to come.

15. Counting the Breath: Two of Four Methods

Chinese Source

云何名為算數修習？謂略有四種算數修習。何等為四？一者、以一為一算數，二者、以二為一算數，三者、順算數，四者、逆算數。云何以一為一算數？謂若入息入時，由緣入息出息住念，數以為一。若入息滅，出息生，出向外時，數為第二。如是展轉數至其十。由此算數非略非廣，故唯至十。是名以一為一算數。云何以二為一算數？謂若入息入而已滅，出息生而已出，爾時總合數以為一。即由如是算數道理，數至其十，是名以二為一算數。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivation through counting? Briefly, there are four methods of counting. What are the four? First, counting one for one; second, counting two as one; third, counting forward; fourth, counting backward. What is counting one for one? When the in-breath enters, attending to it with mindfulness that rests on the in-breath while the out-breath is stilled, one counts it as one. When the in-breath has ceased and the out-breath arises and moves outward, one counts it as two. Continuing this way, one counts up to ten. This count is neither too brief nor too long, and so goes only to ten. This is counting one for one. What is counting two as one? When the in-breath has entered and ceased, and the out-breath has arisen and gone out, at that point the two together are counted as one. Continuing by this same method up to ten — this is counting two as one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first two of four counting variants. "One for one" counts every single breath-movement separately — in-breath is one, out-breath is two, next in-breath is three, and so on up to ten, meaning five full in-out cycles complete one round. "Two as one" counts a complete in-and-out cycle as a single unit — one full breath (in plus out together) is one, the next full cycle is two, up to ten, meaning ten full cycles complete one round; alternatively, a practitioner may simply count only the in-breaths (or only the out-breaths) to ten, letting the other half of each cycle pass unnumbered — this is the more common approach in later meditation manuals such as the Śamatha-vipaśyanā text, which recommends counting only one direction. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise deliberately caps the count at ten precisely because it lands in the middle: counting only to two or three is too brief to gather a scattered mind, while counting to a hundred risks losing count itself through sheer length — ten is judged the ideal middle measure for settling attention without inviting further distraction.

16. Forward, Backward, and Advancing Counting

Chinese Source

云何順算數？謂或由以一為一算數，或由以二為一算數，順次展轉數至其十，名順算數。云何逆算數？謂即由前二種算數，逆次展轉從第十數，次九、次八……次數其一，名逆算數。……若時行者……於順算數及逆算數已串修習，於其中間心無散亂。無散亂心善算數已，復應為說勝進算數。云何名為勝進算數？謂或依以一為一算數，或依以二為一算數，合二為一而算數之。……如是後後漸增，乃至以百為一而算數之。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is forward counting? Whether by the one-for-one method or the two-as-one method, counting forward in sequence up to ten — this is forward counting. What is backward counting? By either of those same two methods, counting backward from ten — nine, eight … down to one — this is backward counting. … When a practitioner has thoroughly practiced forward and backward counting, by either method, such that the mind remains free of distraction throughout, and has mastered counting without distraction, one should then be taught advancing counting. What is advancing counting? Whether based on the one-for-one or the two-as-one method, one now counts two units as a single count. … Continuing to increase in this way, one eventually counts a hundred breaths as a single count.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Forward and backward counting simply apply the same one-for-one or two-as-one methods in either direction — one to ten, or ten down to one — and Cháng Bǎi notes different practitioners find different directions more effective at gathering a scattered mind; a more severely distracted mind often finds counting backward, being less habitual, more effective at holding attention. Once someone counts forward and backward, by either method, with genuinely zero distraction across a full round, they graduate to "advancing counting" — treating two breaths as one unit rather than one, then four as one, doubling repeatedly until, for the most disciplined practitioners, a full hundred breaths are held as a single count before advancing the number. Cháng Bǎi is candid that this most advanced degree isn't for everyone — it demands real memory and sustained attention, and most practitioners move from ordinary counting straight to simply following the breath rather than pushing counting this far; the treatise includes it, he says, because a few practitioners genuinely can do it.

17. Section: Mastery of Counting — Beyond the Need to Count

The treatise closes cultivation through counting: what to do when distraction breaks the count, and what happens when counting has been practiced so thoroughly that it becomes effortless and can finally be set aside.

Chinese Source

又此勤修數息念者，若於中間其心散亂，復應退還從初數起，或順、或逆。……若時算數極串習故，其心自然乘任運道，安住入息、出息所緣，無斷無間相續而轉。……如是展轉相續流注，無動無搖，無散亂行，有愛樂轉。齊此名為過算數地，不應復數。唯於入息、出息所緣，令心安住。……又鈍根者，應為宣說如是息念算數修習。彼由此故，於散亂處，令心安住、令心愛樂。……由正勤修數息念故，彼皆無有。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, in the midst of diligently cultivating mindfulness of the breath, the mind becomes distracted, one should withdraw and begin counting again from the start, whether forward or backward. … When counting has been so thoroughly practiced that the mind naturally rides an effortless path, abiding on the object of the in-breath and out-breath without break or interruption … flowing on continuously in this way, unmoved, unshaken, free of distraction, with a sense of genuine delight — at this point one has passed beyond the stage of counting, and should count no longer, but simply let the mind abide on the in-breath and out-breath as its object. … For a practitioner of dull faculties, this cultivation through counting should be taught, so that in the midst of distraction the mind may be made to abide, and made to delight in the practice. … Through correctly and diligently cultivating mindfulness of the breath in this way, these afflictions are altogether absent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The remedy for losing count mid-practice is simple and non-negotiable, per Cháng Bǎi: no picking up where one thinks one left off — genuinely start again from one (or from ten, if counting backward). Guessing invites exactly the sloppiness the practice exists to correct. But the real destination of counting practice, he stresses, is to make itself unnecessary: once a practitioner can hold the count through a full round with zero distraction, effortlessly, taking genuine pleasure in the practice rather than needing to force it, they have "passed beyond the stage of counting" — the numbers drop away, and only bare, continuous awareness of the breath itself remains, in and out, without further labeling. Cháng Bǎi closes by naming counting's proper audience: it exists specifically for practitioners of duller faculties, whose minds most need a concrete number to hold onto against distraction; for such a practitioner, faithfully practiced counting genuinely eliminates both the drowsiness and the outward scattering named earlier as the fault of practicing too laxly. Having thoroughly closed cultivation through counting, the treatise turns next to how sharper-facultied practitioners are taught — likely bypassing counting for a more direct method, which the text takes up next.

18. Section: Counting Closes Entirely; Aggregates Opens as Mode Two

The treatise briefly notes how sharp-facultied practitioners relate to counting, names the fruit of diligent practice for both types, formally closing mode one — then opens mode two: using the breath as a lens to directly recognize the five aggregates.

Chinese Source

若有利根，覺慧聰俊，不好乘此算數加行。若為宣說算數加行，亦能速疾無倒了達，然不愛樂。……於此加行若修、若習、若多修習為因緣故，起身輕安及心輕安，證一境性，於其所緣愛樂趣入。……如是彼於算數息念善修習已，復於所取、能取二事，作意思惟，悟入諸蘊。謂於入息、出息，及息所依身，作意思惟，悟入色蘊。於彼入息、出息能取念相應領納，作意思惟，悟入受蘊。即於彼念相應等了，作意思惟，悟入想蘊。即於彼念，若念相應思及慧等，作意思惟，悟入行蘊。若於彼念相應諸心、意、識，作意思惟，悟入識蘊。如是行者，於諸蘊中乃至多住，名已悟入。是名悟入諸蘊修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

A sharp-facultied practitioner, being naturally clear and quick of mind, does not take pleasure in this counting practice. Even if taught it, such a person understands it swiftly and without error, yet finds no delight in it. … Through practicing, repeating, and thoroughly cultivating this discipline, physical and mental pliancy arise, one-pointedness is realized, and the practitioner enters this object with genuine delight. … Once a practitioner has thoroughly cultivated mindfulness through counting, further attending to the object grasped and the mind that grasps it leads to realizing the aggregates. Attending to the in-breath, the out-breath, and the body they depend on realizes the aggregate of form. Attending to the sensation accompanying the mindfulness that grasps the breath realizes the aggregate of feeling. Attending to the recognition accompanying that mindfulness realizes the aggregate of perception. Attending to the intention and discernment accompanying that mindfulness realizes the aggregate of formations. Attending to the mind, mentation, and consciousness accompanying that mindfulness realizes the aggregate of consciousness. Dwelling extensively within this realization of the aggregates — this is called having truly realized them. This is cultivation through realizing the aggregates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp-faculty aside as a small but telling observation: a naturally quick mind grasps the mechanics of counting instantly but simply doesn't enjoy the exercise — counting is a tool for the duller-facultied, not a universal requirement. Both types, though, arrive at the same fruit through diligent practice: bodily and mental pliancy (輕安, the settled ease that precedes genuine absorption) and one-pointedness of mind, engaging the breath with real delight rather than effort. This closes mode one entirely.

Mode two, Cháng Bǎi explains, uses the very same breath-awareness as a springboard into recognizing the five aggregates directly, rather than as an abstract doctrine: the breath and the body it depends on are simply form (色); the felt sensations accompanying breath-awareness — long, short, cool, warm — are feeling (受); the act of recognizing "this is the in-breath, this is the out-breath" is perception (想); the volition and discernment choosing which counting method to apply, when to advance, when to restart, is formations (行); and the very mind, mentation, and consciousness doing all this attending is consciousness (識) itself. Nothing new is introduced — the same breath-practice a practitioner has already mastered turns out, on close inspection, to already contain all five aggregates in miniature. Dwelling in this recognition repeatedly is what "realizing the aggregates" means.

19. Section: Dependent Origination Opens as Mode Three

The third mode traces the breath backward through a chain of dependency — from breath, to body and mind, to the life faculty, to past karmic action — the beginning of a direct, experiential realization of dependent origination.

Chinese Source

若時無倒，能見能知唯有諸蘊、唯有諸行、唯事、唯法。彼於爾時，能於諸行悟入緣起。云何悟入？謂觀行者，如是尋求：此入出息何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：此入出息依身緣身、依心緣心。復更尋求：此身此心何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：此身此心依緣命根。復更尋求：如是命根何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：如是命根依緣先行。

Ru-Yi Translation

When, without distortion, one can see and know only aggregates, only conditioned formations, only events, only phenomena — at that time one is able to realize dependent origination with respect to these formations. How does one realize it? The contemplative practitioner inquires: on what does this in-breath and out-breath depend, and what is its condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this in-breath and out-breath depends on the body and is conditioned by the body, depends on the mind and is conditioned by the mind. One inquires further: on what do this body and this mind depend, and what is their condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this body and this mind depend on and are conditioned by the life faculty. One inquires yet further: on what does this life faculty depend, and what is its condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this life faculty depends on and is conditioned by prior karmic action.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames mode three as taking the same five-fold "only" analysis from the earlier dependent-origination object (fascicle 27's opening topic) and now grounding it in direct, first-person inquiry starting from the one thing always immediately available to attention: the breath itself. The chain of questioning unwinds backward one link at a time — the breath depends on body and mind; body and mind in turn depend on the life faculty (the karmic force that keeps a particular body-mind continuum running for its allotted span); and the life faculty itself depends on nothing more mysterious than past karmic action — volitional deeds performed in a previous life, whose accumulated force is what determined how long this life's "clock" was set to run. Cháng Bǎi notes the practitioner is meant to trace this regress experientially, not merely recite it: each step is something to be "truly realized" (如實悟入) through actual inquiry into one's own breath, not accepted as doctrine. The chain will continue further back, presumably toward the earlier links of the twelve-fold formula — ignorance and volitional formations — in what follows.

20. Section: Dependent Origination Closes — Arising, Cessation, and Mode Three Complete

The backward-tracing inquiry reaches ignorance, then reverses to show the full forward chain of arising and the corresponding chain of cessation, closing the third mode of cultivation.

Chinese Source

復更尋求：如是先行何依何緣？既尋求已，如實悟入：如是先行依緣無明。如是了知無明依緣先行，先行依緣命根，命根依緣身心，身心依緣入息、出息。又能了知無明滅故行滅，行滅故命根滅，命根滅故身心滅，身心滅故入出息滅。如是名為悟入緣起。彼於緣起悟入多住，名善習修。是名悟入緣起修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Inquiring further still: on what does this prior karmic action depend, and what is its condition? Having inquired, one truly realizes: this prior karmic action depends on and is conditioned by ignorance. Thus one comes to know: ignorance conditions prior karmic action; prior karmic action conditions the life faculty; the life faculty conditions body and mind; body and mind condition the in-breath and out-breath. And one further comes to know: with the cessation of ignorance, karmic action ceases; with the cessation of karmic action, the life faculty ceases; with the cessation of the life faculty, body and mind cease; with the cessation of body and mind, the in-breath and out-breath cease. This is called realizing dependent origination. Dwelling extensively in this realization is called thorough cultivation. This is cultivation through realizing dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The regress terminates exactly where the classical twelve-link formula begins: ignorance, the root condition for karmic action. Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the treatise's teaching method here — it first walks the chain backward, question by question, from the immediately available fact of one's own breathing all the way back to ignorance (this is the analytical, "tracing the reason" direction); it then restates the identical chain forward, from ignorance down to the breath, showing how existence actually unfolds moment to moment (the "arising" or saṃsāric direction); and finally states the same chain in reverse-cessation form — ignorance's ending unwinds the entire chain all the way down to the breath itself ceasing. A practitioner who has genuinely traced this chain through their own breath, Cháng Bǎi notes, has realized dependent origination far more vividly than one who has only memorized the twelve links as an abstract list — the same insight now discovered as one's own breath, one's own body, one's own present moment. This closes the third of five modes of cultivation.

21. Section: The Four Noble Truths Open and Close as Mode Four

The fourth mode carries breath-mindfulness into a direct realization of the four noble truths — suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path — completing what the treatise treats as a full path to liberation grown out of watching the breath.

Chinese Source

如是彼於緣起悟入善修習已，復於諸行如實了知從眾緣生，悟入無常。……即是生法、老法、病法、死法……即是其苦。若是其苦，即是無我，不得自在，遠離宰主。如是名為由無常、苦、空、無我行，悟入苦諦。……一切皆以貪愛為緣。……又正悟入即此能生眾苦貪愛若無餘斷，即是畢竟寂靜微妙。……我若於此如是了知、如是觀見、如是多住，當於貪愛能無餘斷。如是名能悟入集諦、滅諦、道諦。於此悟入能多住已，於諸諦中證得現觀。是名悟入聖諦修習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thoroughly cultivated the realization of dependent origination, one further truly knows that all conditioned formations arise from conditions, and realizes impermanence … which is subject to birth, aging, sickness, and death … which is suffering. Being suffering, it is without self, lacking autonomy, free of any controlling master. This is realizing the truth of suffering through the aspects of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self. … All of this has craving as its condition. … And truly realizing that this craving which produces every suffering, once cut off without remainder, is itself final, tranquil, and sublime peace [realizes the truth of cessation]. … If I come to know this, to see it directly, and to dwell in it extensively, craving will be cut off without remainder [realizing the truth of the path]. This is realizing the truths of origin, cessation, and path. Dwelling extensively in this realization, one attains direct insight into the truths. This is cultivation through realizing the noble truths.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats mode four as the natural continuation of mode three: having just watched every conditioned formation, including the breath itself, arise from conditions and pass away, the practitioner now reads that same impermanence through all four aspects of the truth of suffering — impermanent (arising from nothing, then dissolving), therefore subject to birth-aging-sickness-death, therefore suffering, therefore without any real self able to control or direct it. The truth of origin is compressed to its single essential cause: craving — the treatise elsewhere (fascicle 34) unpacks this into self-love, love of future existence, and delight in whatever is presently pleasant, but here states simply that every one of the twelve links ultimately runs on craving as its fuel. The truth of cessation is craving's complete removal, described as truly final peace rather than mere temporary relief. And the truth of the path is simply this entire process — knowing it, directly seeing it, and dwelling in it repeatedly — actually accomplishing that removal. Cháng Bǎi notes that sustained practice to this point yields "direct insight" (現觀), the non-conceptual realization traditionally marking entry into the stream — this is no longer intellectual understanding but lived attainment. This closes the fourth of five modes.

22. Section: The Sixteen Excellent Practices Open — the Fifth and Final Mode

Having realized the four noble truths and cut off the afflictions abandoned at the path of seeing, the practitioner takes up a final, more refined cultivation to remove what remains — the subtler afflictions abandoned only through the path of cultivation.

Chinese Source

如是於聖諦中善修習已，於見道所斷一切煩惱皆悉永斷，唯餘修道所斷煩惱。為斷彼故，復進修習十六勝行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thoroughly cultivated realization within the noble truths in this way, all afflictions abandoned at the path of seeing are permanently cut off; only the afflictions abandoned at the path of cultivation remain. In order to cut these off as well, one further advances into cultivating the sixteen excellent practices.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi locates this precisely within the traditional path structure: reaching direct insight into the four truths cuts off, all at once, the roughly one hundred twelve conceptually-constructed afflictions belonging to the three realms — or, in the more familiar five-lower-fetters framework, at minimum self-view, attachment to rites and rituals, and doubt. This is the path of seeing, traditionally marking stream-entry. What remains after that breakthrough are the subtler, deeply habitual afflictions — arising instinctively rather than through conceptual error — that only the sustained path of cultivation can remove, the road from stream-entry to full arhatship. The sixteen excellent practices are this final, most refined stage of breath-mindfulness, aimed at exactly this remaining subtle residue. Cháng Bǎi notes, based on how the text unfolds further, that despite being framed here as the practice of noble ones (those who have already seen the truths), the sixteen excellent practices are in fact accessible to ordinary unenlightened practitioners as well — its content, still to come, will make clear why.

23. The Sixteen Excellent Practices, Part One: Body and Feeling

Chinese Source

謂於念入息，我今能學念於入息；於念出息，我今能學念於出息。若長、若短。於覺了遍身入息，我今能學覺了遍身入息……於息除身行入息，我今能學息除身行入息……於覺了喜入息，我今能學覺了喜入息……於覺了樂入息，我今能學覺了樂入息……於覺了心行入息，我今能學覺了心行入息……於息除心行入息，我今能學息除心行入息……

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding mindfulness of the in-breath: I now train myself to be mindful of the in-breath. Regarding mindfulness of the out-breath: I now train myself to be mindful of the out-breath — whether long or short. Regarding awareness of the in-breath pervading the whole body: I now train myself in this awareness … Regarding calming the bodily formation on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding awareness of joy on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding awareness of bliss on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding awareness of the mental formation on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding calming the mental formation on the in-breath: I now train myself in this …

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the sixteen excellent practices as the treatise's version of the classical ānāpānasati formula, organized (per Zhìzhě Dàshī's Śamatha-Vipaśyanā and the Mahāyāna-saṃgraha-śāstra) into four groups of four, matching the four foundations of mindfulness. The first tetrad is the body foundation: knowing the breath is long or short (practices 1–2, roughly tracking a coarser or calmer mind); feeling the breath pervading every pore of the body, not merely the windpipe (practice 3, a subtler awareness reached only once the mind has settled); and calming the "bodily formation" — becoming aware of the breath's own natural pause at the mid-interval, the same subtle point named earlier in this fascicle (practice 4). The second tetrad is the feeling foundation: noticing the joy that accompanies the first two absorptions (practice 5); noticing the bliss that accompanies the third absorption, born from joy the way a settled body gives rise to ease and ease gives rise to bliss (practice 6); noticing "mental formation" — the feeling and recognition that color each moment of breath-awareness, and catching the subtle self-view or conceit that can quietly ride along with them even in a noble one who has forgotten mindfulness for a moment (practice 7); and actively removing that mental formation once caught, restoring undistorted awareness (practice 8). Each practice repeats the identical formula for both in-breath and out-breath — "I now train myself" — turning the breath into a running site of ever-more-refined self-observation, from coarse physical fact through to the subtlest correction of a fleeting conceit.

24. Section: Mind and Dharma Close the Sixteen Practices — All Five Purifying Objects Complete

The final eight practices — the mind foundation and the dharma foundation — carry the practitioner from mere awareness of mental states through their liberation, to contemplating impermanence, abandonment, dispassion, and finally cessation itself, closing breath-mindfulness, the fifth and last purifying object, and with it all five purifying objects entirely.

Chinese Source

於覺了心入息，我今能學覺了心入息……於喜悅心入息，我今能學喜悅心入息……於制持心入息，我今能學制持心入息……於解脫心入息，我今能學解脫心入息……於無常隨觀入息，我今能學無常隨觀入息……於斷隨觀入息，我今能學斷隨觀入息……於離欲隨觀入息，我今能學離欲隨觀入息……於滅隨觀入息，我今能學滅隨觀入息，於滅隨觀出息，我今能學滅隨觀出息。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding awareness of the mind on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding gladdening the mind on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding restraining and steadying the mind on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding liberating the mind on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding contemplating impermanence on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding contemplating abandonment on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding contemplating dispassion on the in-breath: I now train myself in this … Regarding contemplating cessation on the in-breath, I now train myself in this; regarding contemplating cessation on the out-breath, I now train myself in this.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third tetrad is the mind foundation. "Awareness of mind" (practice 9) means recognizing, in real time, which of twenty classical paired states the mind is currently in — Cháng Bǎi lists all ten pairs: with or without desire, with or without aversion, with or without delusion, contracted or scattered, dull or uplifted, agitated or calm, tranquil or untranquil, concentrated or unconcentrated, well-cultivated or poorly-cultivated, and — the culminating pair — well-liberated or not yet liberated, this last one naming arhatship itself. "Gladdening the mind" (practice 10) is the antidote for dullness and drowsiness — deliberately recalling an inspiring object (the virtues of the Buddha, one's own generosity or discipline) to lift a sinking mind. "Restraining the mind" (practice 11) is the antidote for the opposite fault, agitation and regret — pulling a scattered mind back to stillness. And "liberating the mind" (practice 12) names the mind actually freed from the five hindrances that block concentration.

The fourth and final tetrad is the dharma foundation, and it is where the practice culminates in full liberation. "Contemplating impermanence" (practice 13) sees every conditioned formation, including the breath itself, ceaselessly arising and perishing — cutting the latent seeds of affliction. "Contemplating abandonment" (practice 14) is the direct realization that permanently ends everything abandoned at the path of seeing — traditionally, stream-entry. "Contemplating dispassion" (practice 15) goes further, ending the coarser afflictions of the desire realm still remaining after stream-entry — traditionally, the third fruit, non-returning. And "contemplating cessation" (practice 16), the sixteenth and final practice, ends what remains of form-realm and formless-realm affliction as well — the complete uprooting of the five higher fetters, arhatship itself, entered while the body still lives (nirvana "with residue"). Master Cháng Bǎi closes with the full arc this makes visible: the very same breath a beginner first learns simply to count can, through this sixteen-step refinement, carry a practitioner the entire distance to arhatship — though only as far as the third absorption, since breath itself ceases in the fourth. This closes the fifth and final purifying object. All five — impurity, loving-kindness, dependent origination, the six elements, and breath-mindfulness — are now complete, and with them the second of the three great object-categories within topic three (所緣) of the second yogic process. Skillful objects and objects that purify affliction remain, continuing in what follows.

25. The Sixteen Practices Re-Explained Through the Path of Cultivation

Chinese Source

問：如是十六差別云何？答：有學見跡已得四念住等，於入出息所緣作意復更進修，為斷餘結。……又若得根本第一、第二、第三靜慮，彼定已得初靜慮近分未至依定，依此觀察所生起心。謂如實知、如實覺了，或有貪心、或離貪心、或有瞋心、或離瞋心、或有癡心、或離癡心、略心、散心、下心、舉心、有掉動心、無掉動心、有寂靜心、無寂靜心、有等引心、無等引心、善修習心、不善修習心、善解脫心、不善解脫心。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: What are the distinctions among these sixteen? Answer: A trainee who has already seen the trace of the path — having attained the four foundations of mindfulness and so on — further advances the attentive object of the in-breath and out-breath in order to cut off the remaining fetters. … And having attained the actual first, second, or third absorption — which entails also having attained the preparatory stage bordering the first absorption — one observes, based on this, whatever mind arises: truly knowing, truly recognizing, whether it is a mind with desire or free of desire, with aversion or free of aversion, with delusion or free of delusion, contracted or scattered, sunken or lifted, agitated or unagitated, tranquil or untranquil, absorbed or unabsorbed, well-cultivated or poorly cultivated, well-liberated or not yet liberated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having listed all sixteen practices, the treatise now re-explains them in far greater depth through a detailed question-and-answer, and Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the payoff: it maps the sixteen practices explicitly onto the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, the classical roadmap from the four foundations of mindfulness through the four right efforts, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment (mindfulness, investigation, energy, joy, pliancy, concentration, and equanimity — the seven qualities whose completion marks stream-entry), and finally the eightfold noble path. He notes these are not really seven separate practices but seven names for the same four-foundations-of-mindfulness cultivation at increasing degrees of maturity. A stream-enterer has, in this process, permanently cut only three of the five lower fetters — self-view, attachment to rites and rituals, and doubt — leaving craving, aversion, and the five higher fetters (form-realm and formless-realm attachment, restlessness, conceit, and subtle ignorance) still to be removed through the eightfold path's continued cultivation. Cháng Bǎi reiterates the twenty paired states of mind first named at practice nine, in their complete list, as the direct object of "awareness of mind": present or absent desire, aversion, and delusion; a contracted mind (attention narrowed in concentration) against a scattered one; a sunken, drowsy mind against one deliberately lifted by recalling an inspiring object; an agitated mind against a settled one; one still troubled by the five hindrances against one genuinely tranquil, meaning access concentration has been attained; one that has reached full absorption against one that has not; a mind skilled enough to enter, abide in, and exit concentration at will against one still clumsy at it; and, the final and highest pair, a mind fully liberated through arhatship against one not yet liberated. Recognizing exactly which of these twenty describes one's present mind, moment to moment while watching the breath, is what "awareness of mind" — the ninth of the sixteen practices — actually means in practice.

26. Section: The Five Purifying Objects Formally Named; Five Skillful Objects Open

After the sixteen practices' extended elaboration closes (including its fifth counterpart, curing discursive-thought-dominant practitioners), the treatise formally names the whole category "purifying objects" and immediately opens the third of four object-categories: skillful objects.

Chinese Source

是名第五多尋思行補特伽羅淨行所緣。如是總名淨行所緣。云何名為善巧所緣？謂此所緣略有五種。一、蘊善巧，二、界善巧，三、處善巧，四、緣起善巧，五、處非處善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is the fifth: the purifying object for practitioners dominated by discursive thought. Altogether, these are called the purifying objects. What are skillful objects? Briefly, this object is of five kinds: skillfulness regarding the aggregates, skillfulness regarding the elements, skillfulness regarding the sense-fields, skillfulness regarding dependent origination, and skillfulness regarding what is possible and impossible.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the five purifying objects with their complete matching, now that all five have been covered in full: impurity counters desire, loving-kindness counters aversion, dependent origination counters delusion, the six elements counter pride, and breath-mindfulness — as just shown at exhaustive length — counters a mind dominated by discursive scattering. Together these five form what the treatise names, as a formal category, "purifying objects" (淨行所緣).

Skillful objects open immediately after, and Cháng Bǎi notes this category serves a different purpose than the last: where purifying objects target a practitioner's dominant affliction, skillful objects build sheer analytical mastery over reality's basic categories — the five aggregates, the eighteen elements, the twelve sense-fields, dependent origination, and the distinction between what is possible and impossible. He flags that the treatise elsewhere (in the Decisions section) also teaches a sixth kind, skillfulness regarding the faculties, not included here — this fascicle's list of five reflects specifically what the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples emphasizes. He also previews the pattern to come: sharper-facultied practitioners tend toward aggregate-skillfulness, duller-facultied toward element-skillfulness, and those of middling faculty toward sense-field-skillfulness — the same faculty-matching logic seen throughout this fascicle.

27. The Five Aggregates Defined; Aggregate-Skillfulness Opens

Chinese Source

云何色蘊？謂諸所有色，一切皆是四大種及四大種所造。……云何受蘊？謂或順樂觸為緣諸受，或順苦觸為緣諸受，或順不苦不樂觸為緣諸受。復有六受身……云何想蘊？謂有相想、無相想、狹小想、廣大想、無量想、無諸所有無所有處想。復有六想身……云何行蘊？謂六思身……復有所餘，除受及想諸心法等……云何識蘊？謂心、意、識。復有六識身。……云何蘊善巧？謂善了知如所說蘊種種差別性、非一眾多性，除此法外，更無所得、無所分別，是名略說蘊善巧義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the aggregate of form? Whatever form there is, all of it is either the four great elements or what is derived from them. What is the aggregate of feeling? It is feeling conditioned by a contact tending toward pleasure, or toward suffering, or toward neither — together with the six bodies of feeling arising from the six sense-contacts. What is the aggregate of perception? It is perception with characteristic, without characteristic, narrow, vast, immeasurable, and the perception of nothing-whatsoever — together with the six bodies of perception. What is the aggregate of formations? It is the six bodies of volition, together with all other mental factors apart from feeling and perception. What is the aggregate of consciousness? It is mind, mentation, and consciousness, together with the six bodies of consciousness. What is skillfulness regarding the aggregates? It is thoroughly knowing that the aggregates, as described, each differ from one another and are each not single but manifold — and that apart from these phenomena, nothing further is to be found or discriminated. This, briefly, is the meaning of skillfulness regarding the aggregates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five aggregate definitions largely as review — form is simply the four great elements (earth, water, fire, wind) and everything built from them; feeling is pleasant, painful, or neutral sensation arising from the six sense-contacts; perception is the act of recognizing an object, spanning from ordinary conceptual recognition through the increasingly refined non-conceptual perceptions of the higher formless absorptions; formations is volition together with every mental factor besides feeling and perception — roughly fifty-one factors in total; and consciousness is the six sense-consciousnesses, or, in their finer threefold division, mind (ālaya), mentation (the seventh consciousness), and consciousness proper (the six active consciousnesses).

The real point, per Cháng Bǎi, is what "skillfulness" adds to this catalogue: recognizing that each aggregate genuinely differs from the others (form is not feeling, feeling is not perception, and so on) and that each aggregate is itself not a single simple thing but a whole family of variations — and, crucially, that searching through all this differentiation and multiplicity, nothing further can ever be found: no single, unified, controlling self standing apart from or underneath the five aggregates. Whether one claims the self simply is the aggregates, or claims the self is something separate from them, neither claim can be made good — there is only the differentiated, manifold process itself. Cháng Bǎi notes this isn't merely a doctrinal exercise: genuinely internalizing that there is no fixed "I" to defend loosens the grip of both pleasant and unpleasant experience, since there is no one there for either to threaten or gratify.

28. Element-Skillfulness: Eighteen Elements as Eighteen Seeds

Chinese Source

云何界？云何界善巧？謂界有十八。則眼界、色界、眼識界，耳界、聲界、耳識界，鼻界、香界、鼻識界，舌界、味界、舌識界，身界、觸界、身識界，意界、法界、意識界，是名為界。若復於彼十八種法，從別別界、別別種子、別別種性生起出現，如實了知、忍可、審察，名界善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the elements? What is skillfulness regarding the elements? The elements are eighteen: the eye element, the form element, the eye-consciousness element; the ear, sound, and ear-consciousness elements; the nose, smell, and nose-consciousness elements; the tongue, taste, and tongue-consciousness elements; the body, touch, and body-consciousness elements; and the mind, phenomena, and mind-consciousness elements. This is called the elements. Truly knowing, assenting to, and closely examining that these eighteen phenomena each arise from their own distinct element, their own distinct seed, their own distinct nature — this is called skillfulness regarding the elements.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighteen elements simply triple the six sense-faculties, six sense-objects, and six consciousnesses into a single list — Master Cháng Bǎi notes this framework exists specifically for practitioners still confused about the relationship between physical and mental phenomena together, where the five-aggregate framework suits those confused mainly about mental phenomena, and the twelve-sense-field framework (taken up next) suits those confused mainly about physical phenomena. "Element" (界) carries six technical senses, per the Decisions section Cháng Bǎi cites — cause, seed, intrinsic nature, potential lineage, subtlety, and support — but the essential point condenses to one: each of the eighteen arises from its own distinct karmic seed, not from any of the others. Eye-consciousness doesn't grow from the seed of ear-consciousness; the seed of form doesn't produce sound. Genuinely seeing this — that the body-mind complex is eighteen independently-seeded streams running in parallel, not one unified thing — is element-skillfulness, and it dissolves the same sense of a solid, controlling self that aggregate-skillfulness targets, from a different angle.

29. Sense-Field-Skillfulness: Twelve Fields and the Three Conditions

Chinese Source

云何處？云何處善巧？謂處有十二。則眼處、色處、耳處、聲處、鼻處、香處、舌處、味處、身處、觸處、意處、法處，是名為處。處善巧者，謂眼為增上緣，色為所緣緣，等無間滅意為等無間緣，生起眼識及相應法。……如是六識身及相應法，皆由三緣而得流轉。謂增上緣、所緣緣、等無間緣。……若於如是諸內外處緣得善巧，名處善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the sense-fields? What is skillfulness regarding them? The sense-fields are twelve: the eye and form fields, the ear and sound fields, the nose and smell fields, the tongue and taste fields, the body and touch fields, and the mind and phenomena fields. This is called the sense-fields. Skillfulness regarding the sense-fields is: the eye serves as the dominant condition, form as the object condition, and the immediately-preceding cessation of mind as the immediate condition, giving rise to eye-consciousness and its associated factors — and likewise for the remaining five consciousnesses, all six consciousnesses and their associated factors arising through these same three conditions: the dominant condition, the object condition, and the immediate condition. Attaining skillfulness regarding these three conditions across all the internal and external sense-fields is called skillfulness regarding the sense-fields.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the twelve sense-fields simply drop the six consciousnesses from the eighteen elements, leaving the six internal faculties and six external objects alone — the framework the Buddha taught specifically for those most confused about physical phenomena, since it emphasizes exactly the faculty-object structure a physicalist confusion clings to. Sense-field-skillfulness names something more precise than the bare list, though: understanding exactly how each of the six consciousnesses actually arises, through three distinct conditions working together — a dominant condition (the sense faculty itself, without which no perception could occur at all), an object condition (the very thing perceived, without which there'd be nothing to perceive), and an immediate condition (the just-ceased prior moment of consciousness, which must vacate before the next moment can arise — mind cannot generate two moments of the same stream simultaneously). No single one of these three is sufficient alone; consciousness is always the joint product of all three together. Cháng Bǎi observes that whoever masters element-skillfulness has effectively also mastered sense-field-skillfulness, since both ultimately describe the same causal process — element-skillfulness emphasizing the seed-cause behind each phenomenon, sense-field-skillfulness emphasizing the three conditions by which consciousness specifically arises. Neither leaves room for a controlling self anywhere in the process.

30. Dependent-Origination-Skillfulness: Craving as What Waters the Seeds

Chinese Source

云何緣起？云何緣起善巧？謂無明緣行，行緣識，識緣名色，名色緣六處，六處緣觸，觸緣受，受緣愛，愛緣取，取緣有，有緣生，生緣老死，乃至招集如是純大苦蘊，是名緣起。若復了知唯有諸法滋潤諸法，唯有諸法等潤諸法，唯有諸行引發諸行；而彼諸行因所生故、緣所生故，本無而有，有已散滅，體是無常。是無常故，即是生法，老法，病法，死法……則名為苦。由是苦故，不得自在，其力羸劣，由是因緣，定無有我。若於如是緣生法中……善巧了達，或無常智、或苦智、或無我智，是名緣起善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is dependent origination? What is skillfulness regarding it? Ignorance conditions volitional formations; formations condition consciousness; consciousness conditions name-and-form; name-and-form conditions the six sense-bases; the six sense-bases condition contact; contact conditions feeling; feeling conditions craving; craving conditions grasping; grasping conditions becoming; becoming conditions birth; birth conditions aging and death — thus gathering this whole mass of suffering. This is called dependent origination. Further knowing that only phenomena water other phenomena, only phenomena evenly saturate other phenomena, only formations draw forth further formations — and that these formations, being produced by causes and by conditions, are non-existent and then exist, and having existed then dissolve — their nature is impermanent. Being impermanent, this is subject to birth, aging, sickness, and death … and is therefore called suffering. Being suffering, it lacks autonomy, its power feeble and weak — and by this very reasoning, there is definitely no self. Skillfully realizing, within phenomena thus arisen from conditions, the wisdom of impermanence, or the wisdom of suffering, or the wisdom of non-self — this is called skillfulness regarding dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the twelve links themselves need no fresh explanation here, having already been unpacked at length earlier in this fascicle — what's new is the specific image the treatise adds: craving and grasping act as "watering" (滋潤) on the karmic seeds already planted by ignorance and volitional formations, and once that watering becomes complete and thorough ("evenly saturating," 等潤), those seeds sprout into an actual new rebirth. What dependent-origination-skillfulness adds beyond the earlier object of the same name, Cháng Bǎi explains, is drawing out its full logical consequence: since everything in this chain is caused, and therefore arises from nothing and eventually dissolves, it is impermanent; being impermanent, it is subject to birth-aging-sickness-death and thus suffering; and being suffering — genuinely without any power to control or secure itself — there can be no fixed self anywhere within it. Three insights (impermanence, suffering, non-self), one causal chain: this is the whole of dependent-origination-skillfulness.

31. Section: Possible-and-Impossible-Skillfulness Closes All Five Skillful Objects

The fifth and final skillful object is shown to be simply dependent origination viewed from one more angle — the guarantee that cause and effect are never arbitrary. The treatise then reduces all five skillful objects to two fundamental kinds of skillfulness.

Chinese Source

又處非處善巧，當知即是緣起善巧差別。此中差別者，謂由處非處善巧故，能正了知非不平等因果道理。則善不善法有果異熟。若諸善法能感可愛果異熟法，諸不善法能感非愛果異熟法。若能如是如實了知，名處非處善巧。……此五善巧略則為二。一、自相善巧，二、共相善巧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Skillfulness regarding what is possible and impossible should be known as simply a further distinction within skillfulness regarding dependent origination. The distinction here is this: through this skillfulness, one correctly knows that the principle of cause and effect is never unequal — that wholesome and unwholesome actions each have their own ripened result. Wholesome actions bring about a desirable ripened result; unwholesome actions bring about an undesirable ripened result. Truly knowing this is called skillfulness regarding what is possible and impossible. … These five skillfulnesses, briefly, reduce to two: skillfulness regarding particular characteristics, and skillfulness regarding shared characteristics.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "possible" (處) as whatever conforms to the correct principle of cause and effect, and "impossible" (非處) as whatever violates it — a wholesome action ripening into an unwholesome result, for instance, is simply impossible, not merely unlikely. He frames this as the treatise's quiet rebuttal of any theory of an uncaused first cause — a creator god who is himself simply "given," standing outside cause and effect while everything else remains subject to it: such a picture is exactly the "unequal" causality this skillfulness rules out. In genuine dependent origination, nothing stands outside the chain — cause and effect apply evenly, without exception, which is precisely why virtuous action reliably ripens as good and harmful action reliably ripens as harm. This closes all five skillful objects.

Cháng Bǎi then highlights the treatise's own summary of the whole category: all five reduce to two fundamental modes. Aggregate-skillfulness, element-skillfulness, and sense-field-skillfulness are all forms of particular-characteristic skillfulness (自相善巧) — mastering how each individual phenomenon differs from every other. Dependent-origination-skillfulness and possible-impossible-skillfulness are both forms of shared-characteristic skillfulness (共相善巧) — mastering the single causal principle that governs all phenomena alike, regardless of their individual differences. Between these two modes, nothing about reality's structure is left unaccounted for.

32. Section: Purifying-of-Affliction Objects Open — the Mundane Path

The fourth and final category of objects opens: those that actually purify affliction, beginning with the mundane path — comparing each level of existence against the one above it as coarse against calm.

Chinese Source

云何淨惑所緣？謂觀下地麤性、上地靜性。如欲界對初靜慮，乃至無所有處對非想非非想處。……體麤性者，謂欲界望初靜慮，雖皆具五蘊，而欲界中過患深重，苦住增上，最為鄙劣，甚可厭惡，是故說彼為體麤性。……以要言之，有過患義是麤性義。若彼彼地中過患增多，即由如是過患增多性故，名為麤性。若彼彼地中過患減少，即由如是過患減少性故，名為靜性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are purifying-of-affliction objects? It is contemplating the coarseness of the lower level and the calm of the level above it — the desire realm compared against the first absorption, and so on up through the sphere of nothing-whatsoever compared against the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Regarding coarseness of substance: comparing the desire realm to the first absorption, though both possess the five aggregates, the desire realm's faults and defects run deep, dwelling constantly in suffering, the basest and most contemptible of states, thoroughly worthy of aversion — hence it is called coarse in substance. … Stated briefly, having the quality of fault and defect is what "coarseness" means. Wherever faults and defects increase, by that very increase it is called coarse; wherever they decrease, by that very decrease it is called calm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes the mundane path's method as deceptively simple: nine levels of existence, from the desire realm up through the four form-realm absorptions and the four formless absorptions to the peak of existence, compared each against the one directly above it, always finding the lower coarse and the upper calm. Coarseness itself is analyzed two ways — coarseness of substance (the desire realm's five aggregates are simply riddled with more affliction and suffering than the first absorption's) and coarseness of quantity (the desire realm's aggregates come in vastly more varieties — five sense-faculties, five sense-objects, male and female forms, and so on — than the sparer, more unified aggregates of a higher realm). As the comparison climbs, each successive level has fewer afflictions, a longer lifespan, and less variety — until the very peak, neither-perception-nor-non-perception, which has no level above it left to find coarse by comparison. Cháng Bǎi notes the entire method reduces to one criterion: coarseness simply means "has more faults," calm simply means "has fewer" — a practitioner uses this to develop genuine revulsion toward the level they currently occupy and genuine longing for the one above it, and that revulsion-and-longing is what actually produces the deeper absorption.

33. Section: Only Temporary — the Warning of Udraka Rāmaputra

The treatise delivers a sobering verdict on the mundane path: it only suspends affliction, never permanently removes it — illustrated with a famous cautionary account contrasting a fallen meditation master with the Buddha's own murderous cousin.

Chinese Source

斷除下地所有煩惱，始從欲界乃至上極無所有處。此是暫斷，非究竟斷，以於後時更相續故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The afflictions of each lower level, from the desire realm all the way up to the sphere of nothing-whatsoever, are cut off by this method — but this is only a temporary cutting-off, not a final one, since these afflictions arise again afterward in continuity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this line as the whole point of including the mundane path at all: however far it climbs — even to the very summit of conditioned existence, the eighty-thousand-great-eon bliss of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — it only suspends affliction while the absorption lasts. The moment concentration is lost, the same desire-realm afflictions resume exactly where they left off, because their underlying seeds were never actually destroyed, only pressed down. He illustrates this with a citation the Abhidharma tradition treats as a genuine shock: "better to be Devadatta falling into the Avīci hell than Udraka Rāmaputra being born into the heaven of neither-perception-nor-non-perception." Devadatta, despite committing the five most heinous crimes and severing his own roots of goodness, will — the tradition holds — eventually attain awakening as a solitary buddha, sharper in faculty even than Śāriputra, once the karmic debt of Avīci is paid. Udraka Rāmaputra, by contrast, mastered every absorption up to the very peak of existence, came right to the threshold of the deathless, and spent eighty thousand great eons in the most refined bliss the conditioned universe has to offer — only to fall, upon that immense lifespan's end, by the force of leftover unwholesome karma, into rebirth as a flying, winged, fish-and-fowl-devouring predator, and from there straight into the hell of relentless suffering, with no scripture even recording when he is said to escape it. Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson directly: "though dwelling in the forest of ascetic practice, one can still be swept downstream; though caught in the midst of the five desires, one can still swim against the current" — mundane concentration alone, however sublime, is no refuge, and only the supramundane path, taken up next, actually breaks the cycle for good.

34. Section: The Supramundane Path Opens — Suffering and Its Origin

Where the mundane path only suspended affliction, the supramundane path — the four noble truths — actually ends it. The treatise opens with the eight sufferings, each analyzed into five aspects, and the fourfold craving that is their true cause.

Chinese Source

出世間道淨惑所緣復有四種。一、苦聖諦，二、集聖諦，三、滅聖諦，四、道聖諦。云何苦聖諦？謂生苦、老苦、病苦、死苦、怨憎會苦、愛別離苦、求不得苦，略說一切五取蘊苦，名苦聖諦。……云何集聖諦？謂若愛、若後有愛、若喜貪俱行愛、若彼彼喜樂愛等，名集聖諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The supramundane path's purifying-of-affliction objects are of four kinds: the noble truth of suffering, of its origin, of its cessation, and of the path. What is the noble truth of suffering? Birth, aging, sickness, and death; meeting the disliked; parting from the loved; not obtaining what is sought; and, in brief, the suffering of the five aggregates of grasping altogether — this is the noble truth of suffering. What is the noble truth of the origin? It is self-love, love of future existence, craving bound up with delight in what is presently held dear, and longing-craving for what is not yet obtained — these are the noble truth of the origin.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the classical eight sufferings as review, but flags the treatise's distinctive contribution: it analyzes each of the eight into five specific aspects. Birth is suffering because it is followed by further suffering, shadowed by the latent seeds of affliction, the basis all other suffering depends on, the basis affliction itself depends on, and inherently a separation from anything one might have wished to keep. Aging is suffering through the five-fold decline of appearance, strength, the senses, enjoyment of experience, and remaining lifespan. Sickness is suffering through bodily breakdown, mounting distress, the inability to enjoy what is pleasant, the forced endurance of what is not, and life shortened. Death is suffering through parting from wealth, friends, family, and one's own body, and through the acute distress of the dying process itself. Meeting the disliked, parting from the loved, and not obtaining what is sought each carry their own five-fold analysis in turn — Cháng Bǎi notes this level of structural precision is distinctive to this treatise among the canonical sources on suffering. And the aggregates of grasping themselves are suffering in five ways: as the very vessel that generates suffering, as what all suffering depends on, as the vessel of suffering-through-pain, the vessel of suffering-through-change, and simply by their own restless, ceaselessly-flowing nature.

The origin of all this, per the treatise, reduces to four kinds of craving: self-love (the deepest, attachment to one's own present existence, riding underneath even apparently selfless virtue until it's genuinely uprooted); love of future existence (hoping tomorrow's life will be better than today's — the very engine, Cháng Bǎi notes, behind most people's motivation to practice generosity or discipline at all); delight-bound craving for whatever pleasant thing is already at hand; and longing-craving reaching for whatever pleasant thing is not yet obtained. The first two are forms of self-love proper; the latter two are love of pleasant objects. Together, these four cravings are what actually manufacture every rebirth — cut the craving, and the entire apparatus of suffering has nothing left to run on.

35. Section: Cessation, Path, and the Black-and-White Framework Close the Four Truths

The treatise closes the four noble truths with cessation and the path, then reveals the underlying logic binding all four together: two truths describe the tainted "black" side of existence, two describe the pure "white" side — cause matched to cause, effect matched to effect.

Chinese Source

云何滅聖諦？謂即此愛等無餘斷滅，名滅聖諦。云何道聖諦？謂八支等聖道，名道聖諦。……當知此中，依黑品白品果因建立故，建立四聖諦。謂苦諦是黑品果；集諦是黑品因；滅諦是白品果；道諦是白品因，能得、能證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the noble truth of cessation? It is the complete, remainderless ending of this very craving — this is the noble truth of cessation. What is the noble truth of the path? It is the eightfold noble path and its allied factors — this is the noble truth of the path. Know that these four noble truths are established according to the relation of cause and effect within the tainted and the pure: the truth of suffering is the tainted effect; the truth of origin is the tainted cause; the truth of cessation is the pure effect; and the truth of the path is the pure cause, since it is what attains and what realizes cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Cessation, Cháng Bǎi notes, is stated with striking simplicity — not a place one goes, but simply craving's complete and final ending, with no remainder left to reignite. And the path is, in essence, the same thirty-seven factors of enlightenment already unpacked through the sixteen excellent practices earlier in this fascicle, here compressed to their classical eightfold form: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

The closing structural insight, per Cháng Bǎi, is what makes the four truths cohere as a single teaching rather than four separate topics: they form two cause-effect pairs, one tainted, one pure. Suffering is the tainted result; its origin (craving) is the tainted cause that produces it. Cessation is the pure result; the path is the pure cause that produces it. The order taught — result before cause in both pairs — is deliberate pedagogy: a practitioner must first recognize suffering as suffering (not mistake it for pleasure, as the unawakened routinely do) before investigating its cause will mean anything, and must first glimpse cessation as a genuinely desirable goal before undertaking the path toward it will seem worthwhile. This closes the supramundane path, the second of the two paths within purifying-of-affliction objects — and with it, all objects that purify affliction, the fourth and final category of objects within the second yogic process's third question.

36. The Medical Simile; Why "Truth," Why "Noble"

Chinese Source

又苦諦如病，初應遍知；集諦如病因緣，次應遠離；滅諦如無病，次應觸證；道諦如良藥，復應修習及多修習。……是如是實，非不如實；是無顛倒，非是顛倒；故名為諦。……答：唯諸聖者於是諸諦同謂為諦，如實了知、如實觀見；一切愚夫不如實知、不如實見；是故諸諦唯名聖諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The truth of suffering is like an illness, to first be fully known; the truth of the origin is like the illness's cause, next to be abandoned; the truth of cessation is like health restored, next to be personally attained; the truth of the path is like the medicine, to be practiced and thoroughly practiced. … They are thus, they are real, not otherwise than real; they are without distortion, not distorted — hence they are called truths. … Answer: only the noble ones regard these truths, alike, as truth — truly knowing them, truly seeing them directly. All the unenlightened neither truly know nor truly see them. This is why these truths are called, specifically, noble truths.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi favors the treatise's own medical analogy as the clearest summary of the whole four-truths structure: suffering is the illness, to be diagnosed and fully understood before anything else is possible; its origin is the illness's actual cause, to be identified and abandoned; cessation is health itself, restored, to be personally verified rather than merely believed in; and the path is the medicine — not a single dose, but a treatment to be taken repeatedly until the cure is complete. He notes the treatise even explains why "full knowledge" is specified only for the first truth: suffering alone must be known both in its particular forms (the eight specific sufferings) and its shared nature (impermanent, empty, without self) — the other three truths need only be known in their own single respective nature.

On why these four alone are called "noble" truths: Cháng Bǎi stresses this isn't honorific but literal — an unenlightened person may accept that "life is suffering" as something the Buddha said and trust it as true, but only a noble one (a stream-enterer or above) has actually seen it directly, with undistorted realization rather than secondhand belief. The ordinary person's truth is borrowed; the noble one's truth is personally verified. This is why the treatise insists these are truths "of the noble ones" specifically, not truths in general.

37. Section: All Four Objects of Meditation Formally Summarized

After brief closing distinctions — craving's three modes (for future existence, for what's already held, for what's not yet obtained), cessation's two modes (affliction ended, versus the aggregates themselves ended), and the path's two modes (trainee versus one beyond training) — the treatise delivers a complete recap of everything covered since fascicle 26 opened this third question of the second yogic process.

Chinese Source

滅有二種。一、煩惱滅，二、所依滅。……道有二種。一、有學道，二、無學道。如是當知名出世道淨惑所緣。如是已說四種所緣。一、遍滿所緣，二、淨行所緣，三、善巧所緣，四、淨惑所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cessation is of two kinds: the ending of affliction, and the ending of what affliction depends on. The path is of two kinds: the path of the trainee, and the path of one beyond training. Thus should the purifying-of-affliction objects of the supramundane path be known. Thus the four objects of meditation have now been explained: pervasive objects, purifying objects, skillful objects, and purifying-of-affliction objects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats cessation's two modes as marking the difference between nirvana attained while still living (affliction alone extinguished, the five aggregates continuing until natural death) and final nirvana at death (the aggregates themselves finally laid down, no further rebirth to follow) — and the path's two modes as simply naming everyone from stream-enterer through the very brink of arhatship as still "in training," with the arhat alone "beyond training," nothing further left to accomplish.

He then walks through the treatise's own capstone summary of the entire third question — what has been, across fascicles 26 and 27 together, an extraordinarily long unfolding: pervasive objects (four kinds — the image with discrimination for insight practice, the image without discrimination for calming practice, the exhaustive-particulars-and-universal-nature pairing, and the object of accomplished result once absorption is actually attained); purifying objects (five kinds, each matched to a specific affliction — impurity for desire, loving-kindness for aversion, dependent origination for delusion, the six elements for pride, and breath-mindfulness for a scattered, discursive mind); skillful objects (five kinds — the aggregates, the eighteen elements, the twelve sense-fields, dependent origination, and the possible-and-impossible, reducible to particular-characteristic and shared-characteristic skillfulness); and purifying-of-affliction objects (the mundane path's coarse-versus-calm comparison across the nine levels, which only suspends affliction, and the supramundane path's four noble truths, which end it for good). Cháng Bǎi calls this the single most complete map, anywhere in Buddhist literature he knows, of what a meditator can actually take as an object of attention — and notes that every one of the seventeen questions still to come will draw on this map, since a practitioner's whole progress depends on knowing which object to use, and when.

38. Section: Topic Three Closes; Instruction Opens as Topic Four

With all four objects of meditation now fully explained, the treatise moves to the fourth of the second yogic process's seventeen questions: what is instruction?

Chinese Source

云何教授？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is instruction?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi marks this as a genuine structural turn, closing out what has been by far the longest of the seventeen questions — object of meditation (所緣) has now run from partway through fascicle 26 through to nearly the end of fascicle 27, covering four major categories and dozens of sub-topics. Instruction (教授), the fourth question, turns from what a practitioner attends to toward how a teacher actually transmits the practice — the treatise, he notes, opens this topic with a fourfold framework, still to come. This fascicle's source material draws close to its own end; whatever of topic four fits within fascicle 27's remaining pages will open here, with the rest continuing into fascicle 28.

39. The Four Kinds of Instruction

Chinese Source

謂四教授。一、無倒教授，二、漸次教授，三、教教授，四、證教授。云何無倒教授？謂無顛倒宣說法義，令其受持、讀誦、修學，如實出離，正盡眾苦，作苦邊際。……云何漸次教授？謂稱時機宣說法義，先令受持讀誦淺近，後方令彼學深遠處。又為令入初諦現觀，先教苦諦，後集滅道。又為令得靜慮等至，先教最初靜慮等至，後教其餘靜慮等至。……云何教教授？謂從尊重、若似尊重，達解瑜伽軌範、親教……即如其教不增不減教授於他，名教教授。云何證教授？謂如自己獨處空閑所得、所觸、所證諸法，為欲令他得、觸、證故，方便教授，名證教授。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are four kinds of instruction: undistorted instruction, gradual instruction, teaching-instruction, and realization-instruction. What is undistorted instruction? It is expounding the teaching and its meaning without any distortion, so that the student retains it, recites it, and trains in it — thereby genuinely gaining release, fully ending all suffering, and reaching suffering's utter limit. What is gradual instruction? It is expounding the teaching in a manner suited to the occasion and the student's readiness — first having them retain and recite what is simple and near at hand, only later leading them to what is deep and far-reaching; teaching the truth of suffering first, before the truths of origin, cessation, and the path, so as to bring them into direct insight; and teaching the first absorption first, before the remaining stages of concentration. What is teaching-instruction? It is instruction received from a respected teacher, or one comparable in standing, a preceptor or mentor versed in the yogic disciplines — or ultimately from the Tathāgatas or the Buddha's disciples — and transmitted onward to others exactly as received, neither adding to it nor subtracting from it. What is realization-instruction? It is instructing others, through skillful means, so that they too may attain, touch, and realize the very things one has personally attained, touched, and realized in solitary retreat.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these four as a natural progression rather than four unrelated options. Undistorted instruction is the baseline requirement — teaching that tracks the five stages of the path faithfully, from the provisions and preparatory stages through seeing the path, cultivating it, and finally reaching the ultimate path of the arhat, with nothing garbled or mistaken at any stage. Gradual instruction adds pedagogical wisdom to that accuracy: matching the lesson to the student's actual readiness rather than the teacher's preference — simple stories before deep doctrine, suffering before its origin (since a student must first genuinely recognize suffering as suffering before its cause will mean anything), the first absorption before the higher ones. Teaching-instruction concerns lineage and fidelity: a student who has received the true teaching from a genuinely qualified teacher — ultimately traceable back to the Buddha himself — has an obligation to pass it on to others exactly as received, neither embellishing nor cutting corners, so the teaching survives intact across generations. And realization-instruction is the highest of the four: not secondhand transmission at all, but a teacher instructing from their own direct, personally verified attainment — having genuinely gained the first fruit, touched the second and third, and realized the fourth, they can then guide others toward the very same attainments through skillful means suited to each student's own capacity. Cháng Bǎi notes this fourfold framework itself now closes; the treatise moves next to instruction "complete in every respect" (諸相圓滿教授), a further refinement still to come.

40. Section: The Three Miraculous Powers Complete Instruction — Fascicle 27 Complete

The treatise closes with instruction "complete in every respect": three miraculous transformations that make the four kinds of instruction fully effective. Here, at the height of this teaching, fascicle 27's source draws to its own close.

Chinese Source

復有諸相圓滿教授。……謂由三種神變教授。三神變者，一、神境神變，二、記說神變，三、教誡神變。由神境神變，能現種種神通境界，令他於己生極尊重；由彼於己生尊重故，於屬耳聽瑜伽作意，極生恭敬。由記說神變，能尋求他心行差別。由教誡神變，如根、如行、如所悟入為說正法，於所修行能正教誡。故三神變能攝諸相圓滿教授。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is, further, instruction complete in every respect — through three miraculous transformations: the transformation of psychic power, the transformation of mind-reading, and the transformation of admonition. Through the transformation of psychic power, one manifests various miraculous states, causing others to hold one in the deepest reverence — and because of that reverence, they listen to the instruction on yogic mental engagement with the utmost respect. Through the transformation of mind-reading, one is able to discern the particular workings of another's mind. Through the transformation of admonition, one teaches the true dharma exactly suited to the student's faculty, conduct, and degree of realization, correctly admonishing them in their practice. Thus these three miraculous transformations encompass instruction complete in every respect.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes this final topic of fascicle 27 by matching each transformation to one of the six supernormal powers: the transformation of psychic power belongs to the power over one's own form and environment (able to shrink, expand, multiply, or move through what is normally solid) — its teaching function is winning genuine reverence, since a student who has witnessed something beyond ordinary capacity listens far more attentively than one who hasn't. The transformation of mind-reading belongs to the power of knowing others' minds — a teacher who can actually perceive a student's inner state, developed first by carefully observing one's own expressions and impulses and then verifying the same patterns in others, can teach with real precision rather than guesswork. And the transformation of admonition belongs to the power of the destruction of defilements, available only to one who has fully purified the mind — teaching calibrated exactly to this particular student's faculty, conduct, and depth of realization, not a generic lesson. Together, Cháng Bǎi notes, these three miraculous powers don't replace the four kinds of instruction named earlier — they complete them, adding the teacher's own attainment to what would otherwise be correct doctrine delivered without full effect.

The source text ends here. Fascicle 27 has carried the second yogic process from where fascicle 26 left it — closing all four objects of meditation (pervasive, purifying, skillful, and purifying-of-affliction, the third of the seventeen questions) in full, and opening the fourth question, instruction, through both its basic fourfold framework and this deeper account of instruction perfected by miraculous power. The fifth question, training, and all that follows continue in fascicle 28.
