Fascicle 33 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十三 · The Fourth Yogic Process Opens

The Mundane Path Through the Absorptions

Fascicle 33 opens the fourth and final yogic process of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples — the extensive exposition of the marks of cultivation. Having attained near-attainment concentration in fascicle 32, a practitioner now faces two paths forward: the mundane path toward the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions, detailed here through seven kinds of mental engagement, or the supramundane path toward the four noble truths, reserved for fascicle 34. This fascicle follows the mundane path in full.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 33 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Fourth Yogic Process Opens — Two Paths From Near-Attainment Concentration

Fascicle 33 opens the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples' fourth and final yogic process with two summary verses — one for the mundane path this fascicle will cover, one for the supramundane path reserved for fascicle 34 — before explaining that a practitioner who has attained near-attainment concentration now faces exactly two ways forward, and no third option.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第四瑜伽處之一。復次，此嗢柁南曰：七作意離欲　及諸定廣辯　二定五神通　生差別諸相。觀察於諸諦　如實而通達　廣分別於修　究竟為其後。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first part of the fourth yogic process. Further, the summary verse says: "Seven mental engagements free one from desire, and the absorptions are explained at length; two attainments, five superknowledges, and the differentiated marks of rebirth." "Investigating the truths, one penetrates them as they truly are; the varieties of cultivation are explained at length, culminating, at the very end, in the ultimate fruit."

Chinese Source

已得作意諸瑜伽師，已入如是少分樂斷；從此已後，唯有二趣，更無所餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

A teacher of yogic practice who has attained mental engagement has already entered this partial delight in severing affliction. From this point on, there are exactly two paths open, and no others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks both verses as tables of contents for fascicles 33 and 34 respectively. The mundane-path verse names, in order: the seven kinds of mental engagement that carry a practitioner from near-attainment concentration through freedom from desire (learning the object's mark, confident understanding, seclusion, gathering joy, examination, the culmination of effort, and its fruit); the eight absorptions this fascicle will detail — the four dhyānas and four formless spheres; two attainments without mind (the attainment of non-perception and the attainment of cessation); five of the six superknowledges (divine eye, divine ear, mind-reading, psychic power, and recollection of past lives — omitting only the knowledge of the exhaustion of contaminants, since that belongs only to the supramundane path); and finally the differentiated marks of those reborn in the form and formless heavens, along with the outward signs of someone who has genuinely become free of desire. The supramundane-path verse, held for fascicle 34, previews investigating the four noble truths through the identical seven mental engagements, culminating in arhatship itself. Cháng Bǎi frames near-attainment concentration as a genuine fork in the road: a practitioner who stops here and simply enjoys its partial peace makes no further progress; the only two ways forward are the mundane path toward ever-deeper absorption, or the supramundane path toward liberation itself — fascicles 21 through 34 together tracing a Hearer-disciple's entire journey from the provisions of the path through final attainment.

2. Milestone: Four Kinds of Practitioner Who Choose the Mundane Path

The treatise now identifies exactly who, having attained near-attainment concentration, chooses to pursue the mundane path of deepening absorption rather than the supramundane path toward liberation — four distinct kinds of practitioner, each with a different underlying reason.

Chinese Source

答：略有四種補特伽羅。何等為四？一、一切外道；二、於正法中根性羸劣，先修正行；三、根性雖利，善根未熟；四、一切菩薩樂當來世證大菩提，非於現法。如是四種補特伽羅，於現法中，樂往世間道發起加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: There are, in brief, four kinds of person. What are the four? First, every follower of an outside path. Second, one within the true teaching whose faculties are weak, who first cultivates correct conduct accordingly. Third, one whose faculties are sharp, but whose wholesome roots are not yet ripe. Fourth, every bodhisattva who wishes to realize great enlightenment in a future life, not in this present life. These four kinds of person, in this present life, delight in applying effort toward the mundane path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the four precisely: a follower of an outside path pursues the mundane path exclusively, since without the Buddha's teaching there is no concept of liberation to aim for at all, and such a practitioner typically mistakes deep absorption itself for final liberation; a practitioner within the true teaching but of weak faculties simply cannot yet move quickly enough toward direct realization, and so first strengthens concentration as preparation; one of sharp faculties but immature wholesome roots has the capacity but not yet the ripeness needed to actually enter the stream of the noble path; and a bodhisattva, uniquely, does not pursue the mundane path out of any limitation at all, but by deliberate choice — postponing personal liberation for countless future lives of benefiting beings, and cultivating the dhyānas' profound stillness partly because that same concentration strengthens the very capacity needed to guide others skillfully. Cháng Bǎi notes the second and third differ subtly: one lacks the raw capacity, the other has the capacity but not yet the maturity — a distinction worth knowing since the appropriate encouragement differs for each.

3. The Six-Aspect Method: Coarse Below, Peaceful Above

Chinese Source

謂先於欲界觀為麤性，於初靜慮若定、若生觀為靜性，發起加行，離欲界欲；如是乃至發起加行，離無所有處欲，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, one first observes the desire realm as coarse, and observes the first dhyāna — whether the concentration itself or the resulting rebirth there — as peaceful; applying effort in this way, one becomes free of desire for the desire realm. In just this way, applying effort onward, one becomes free of desire even for the sphere of nothingness — this should be understood identically at every stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the classical "six aspects" underlying this method: the lower level is seen as suffering, coarse, and an obstruction, while the level above is seen as peaceful, excellent, and a genuine escape — and this same comparative structure repeats at every single transition up the ladder of absorption: desire realm against first dhyāna, first against second, second against third, third against fourth, fourth against the sphere of infinite space, and onward through infinite consciousness, nothingness, and finally neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the very summit of conditioned existence. He stresses this is a genuinely gradual method — no level is skipped — and that it only ever suppresses the coarser afflictions associated with each lower level, never uprooting their underlying seeds; even the summit, the formless sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, with its eighty-thousand-eon lifespan, still ends in death and a fall back into the round of rebirth once that immense lifespan is exhausted. The mundane path, however far it climbs, never itself delivers final liberation.

4. Milestone: What the Mundane Path Yields — Attainments Without Mind, Five Superknowledges, and Heavenly Rebirth

Though the mundane path cannot deliver final liberation, it is far from fruitless: sustained cultivation of the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions gives rise to two rare attainments without mind, five superknowledges, and a range of genuine noble qualities.

Chinese Source

又依靜慮等，能引無想定等及發五神通等。又即依此若生、若相，皆當廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, relying on the dhyānas and so on, one is able to give rise to the attainment of non-perception and the like, and to develop the five superknowledges and so on. And, relying on these same attainments, both the resulting rebirth and the outward marks of accomplishment shall all be explained at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps out the yield of the mundane path in full: the fourth dhyāna can give rise to the attainment of non-perception, where the sixth consciousness's feeling and perception are entirely stilled; the peak of neither-perception-nor-non-perception can give rise to the far rarer attainment of cessation, where even the seventh consciousness's self-grasping activity is stilled alongside them. The four root dhyānas can also develop five of the six superknowledges — psychic power (shrinking, expanding, or transforming physical matter), recollection of past lives, the divine ear (hearing at any distance without obstruction), the divine eye (knowing where a being will be reborn), and mind-reading (knowing another's thoughts directly) — together with further noble qualities such as the samādhi of non-contention (Subhūti's practice of checking, before begging for alms, that his presence would trouble no one) and the samādhi of wish-fulfilling wisdom (instantly mastering a body of knowledge merely by wishing to understand it, rather than through laborious study). Cultivating the nine levels at low, middling, or high grade determines specific heavenly rebirth within each level's own sub-tiers — the first dhyāna's retinue-of-Brahmā, ministers-of-Brahmā, and Great Brahmā himself, for instance, corresponding to low, middling, and high cultivation respectively — and genuine freedom from desire, once attained, visibly changes a practitioner's outward bearing in body, speech, and mind, details the treatise promises to spell out further ahead.

5. Milestone: The Seven Kinds of Mental Engagement Named

The treatise now names, in full, the seven mental engagements that structure the entire mundane path — the same seven first introduced back in fascicle 30, now given their complete definitions as the operative method for actually ascending through the absorptions.

Chinese Source

為離欲界欲，勤修觀行諸瑜伽師，由七作意方能獲得離欲界欲。何等名為七種作意？謂了相作意、勝解作意、遠離作意、攝樂作意、觀察作意、加行究竟作意、加行究竟果作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

For a teacher of yogic practice diligently cultivating contemplation in order to become free of desire for the desire realm, it is through seven kinds of mental engagement that freedom from desire for the desire realm is actually attained. What are these seven kinds of mental engagement? Learning the object's mark, confident understanding, seclusion, gathering joy, examination, the culmination of effort, and the fruit of that culmination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews all seven in sequence: learning the object's mark correctly identifies the lower level's suffering, coarseness, and obstruction, and the upper level's peace, excellence, and genuine escape; confident understanding repeatedly generates vivid understanding of that same comparison; seclusion first abandons the lower level's grossest, most obvious afflictions; gathering joy abandons the lower level's middling afflictions, delighting in the process of letting go; examination checks whether the lower level's afflictions have genuinely been abandoned, guarding specifically against the false confidence of imagining oneself further along than one actually is; the culmination of effort abandons even the lower level's subtlest remaining afflictions, forming the final uninterrupted path immediately preceding attainment; and the fruit of that culmination is the actual moment of liberation from the lower level — the release itself, receiving in full whatever qualities the newly attained higher level brings. Cháng Bǎi notes this same seven-stage structure, first sketched in outline back when four kinds of mental engagement were introduced in fascicle 31, now receives its complete and definitive treatment — and will recur, essentially unchanged, when the supramundane path applies the identical seven engagements to the four noble truths in fascicle 34.

6. Milestone: Learning the Object's Mark Opens — the Meaning and Substance of Desire's Coarseness

The first of the seven mental engagements now receives its full treatment: investigating the desire realm's coarseness through the same six-things framework used throughout the treatise, beginning with what makes desire coarse in meaning, and what its underlying substance actually consists of.

Chinese Source

云何名為了相作意？謂若作意，能正覺了欲界麤相，初靜慮靜相。云何覺了欲界麤相？謂正尋思欲界六事：一、義，二、事，三、相，四、品，五、時，六、理。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called learning the object's mark? It is the mental engagement able to correctly recognize the desire realm's coarse mark and the first dhyāna's peaceful mark. How does one recognize the desire realm's coarse mark? By rightly investigating six things regarding the desire realm: meaning, substance, characteristic, category, time, and principle.

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：如是諸欲有多過患、有多損惱、有多疫癘、有多災害。於諸欲中多過患義，廣說乃至多災害義，是名麤義。謂正尋思：於諸欲中有內貪欲，於諸欲中有外貪欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: these desires carry much danger, much distress, much disease, and much calamity. Investigating desire's much danger, and so on at length through its much calamity — this is called its meaning. One rightly investigates: within these desires there is inner craving, and within these desires there is outer craving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks desire's fourfold danger in meaning: it brings little genuine satisfaction yet is condemned by the Buddha, it constantly stokes further craving rather than resolving it, and it drives beings toward unwholesome action; it breeds constant distress, since desire's very nature is to grieve when unfulfilled and grieve again when lost; it is the basis for countless diseases, since compulsive pursuit of sensory pleasure directly damages health; and it is the basis for the calamity of rebirth itself, since unabandoned desire simply perpetuates the entire cycle of old age, sickness, and death indefinitely. On substance, he distinguishes inner craving (attachment to one's own body and existence) from outer craving (attachment to external objects and other beings) as the two basic forms desire's underlying substance takes — the raw material this entire investigation is meant to dismantle.

7. Milestone: Desire's Particular Characteristics — Object-Desire, Affliction-Desire, and Three Kinds of Distortion

The treatise now distinguishes desire's object (the sensory thing itself) from desire's affliction (the inner craving that actually does the grasping) — and traces how each of the three kinds of feeling breeds its own characteristic distortion.

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：此為煩惱欲，此為事欲。此復三種：謂順樂受處、順苦受處、順不苦不樂受處。順樂受處是貪欲依處，是想、心倒依處；順苦受處是瞋恚依處，是忿、恨依處；順不苦不樂受處是愚癡依處，是覆、惱、誑、諂、無慚、無愧依處，是見倒依處。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: this is affliction-desire, this is object-desire. Object-desire is further threefold: what conduces to pleasant feeling, what conduces to painful feeling, and what conduces to neutral feeling. What conduces to pleasant feeling is the basis for craving, and the basis for distorted perception and distorted mind. What conduces to painful feeling is the basis for hatred, and the basis for indignation and resentment. What conduces to neutral feeling is the basis for delusion, and the basis for concealment, irritability, deceit, flattery, shamelessness, and lack of conscience — and the basis for distorted view.

Chinese Source

即正尋思：如是諸欲，極惡諸受之所隨逐，極惡煩惱之所隨逐。是名尋思諸欲自相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One then rightly investigates: these desires are pursued by the most severe kinds of feeling, and pursued by the most severe afflictions. This is called investigating the particular characteristic of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes object-desire (the sensory object itself — a sight, a sound, a person) from affliction-desire (the actual craving mind that grasps at it), citing the classical formula that "the world's fine objects are not truly desire; true desire is the discriminating craving of the mind" — a cup of coffee is only ever object-desire; the felt need for it is affliction-desire. He then traces three distinct distortions bred by the three feelings: pleasant feeling breeds "perceptual distortion" (mistaking an object's appearance for something genuinely fine) and "mental distortion" (the craving that follows from that misperception); painful feeling breeds no comparable distortion since its unpleasantness is at least accurately recognized, only the secondary reactions of indignation and resentment; and neutral feeling — the subtlest and most commonly overlooked — breeds delusion and, through it, an entire cluster of secondary afflictions (concealing one's faults out of fear of losing reputation, lashing out when disturbed, deceiving and flattering others for personal gain, and both shamelessness before oneself and indifference to others' judgment), because neutral feeling is where a being most easily overlooks the aggregates' own subtle, ever-present suffering of conditioning — mistaking a self-grasping "I" for genuinely, permanently existing beneath the placid surface. Only a noble one, Cháng Bǎi notes, actually recognizes this neutral feeling for the subtle suffering it truly is; every ordinary being takes it as simple, harmless neutrality, which is precisely why it breeds the deepest distortion of the three — distorted view itself.

8. Desire's Universal Characteristics: Suffering and Impermanence

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：此一切欲，生苦、老苦，廣說乃至求不得苦等所隨逐、等所隨縛。諸受欲者，於圓滿欲驅迫而轉，亦未解脫生等法故，雖彼諸欲勝妙圓滿，而暫時有。是名尋思諸欲共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: all these desires are pursued and bound by the suffering of birth, the suffering of old age, and so on at length through the suffering of not obtaining what is sought. Those who indulge in desire are driven and compelled in pursuit of ever more complete satisfaction, never freed from birth and the rest — so that even the most excellent, complete desires, however fine, are only ever temporary. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that whatever desire's particular flavor — pleasant, painful, or neutral — every single instance shares the same two universal marks: it is bound up with the classical sufferings (birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from what is loved, encountering what is hated, and failing to obtain what is sought), and it is impermanent, however complete or refined it may temporarily seem. He stresses the ordinary mind's characteristic error is treating desire's temporary completeness as though it were lasting security — wealth, a large family, a comfortable life all still end in exactly the same suffering and impermanence as anything else, a fact obvious to anyone who examines it honestly, yet one that desire itself, by its very nature, keeps the mind from actually examining. Only sustained investigation like this — precisely the exercise the first mental engagement demands — cuts through that habitual blindness.

9. Milestone: Desire's Category — Eight Similes, and the King Who Was Never Satisfied

The treatise now shows why desire belongs entirely to the dark category, through eight classical similes and a cautionary story of a king whose insatiable craving nearly destroyed him.

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：如是諸欲，皆墮黑品。猶如枯骨，如凝血肉，如草炬火，如一分炭火，如大毒蛇，如夢所見，如假借得諸莊嚴具，如樹端果。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: these desires all fall within the dark category. They are like a dry bone; like a lump of raw flesh; like a torch of grass on fire; like a single glowing ember; like a great venomous serpent; like what is seen in a dream; like ornaments borrowed for an occasion; like fruit hanging at a treetop's very end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks each image: a dry bone, gnawed at by a dog that can never actually be satisfied by it, since there is no meat left to satisfy anything; raw flesh, fought over by competing scavengers the instant it appears — illustrated by the Jin-dynasty story of the beautiful Lǜzhū, so fiercely contested between her master Shí Chóng and a rival that the dispute ended in Shí Chóng's execution and Lǜzhū's own suicide; a burning torch of grass, liable to scorch the very hand holding it; a single ember, small enough to seem harmless yet capable of burning down an entire forest, just as the smallest indulged desire can consume every accumulated merit; a venomous serpent, which even the wise keep their distance from; a dream, vivid while it lasts and gone the instant one wakes; borrowed ornaments, which must eventually be returned to their true owner, since nothing about them was ever truly one's own; and treetop fruit, so ripe it could fall and rot at any moment, unstable and impossible to rely upon. He tells the *Mahāvibhāṣā's* story of a tyrannical king named Duōqiú, deposed by his own ministers and reduced to selling straw sandals for a living — until his kind-hearted brother and successor took pity and granted him first one district to govern, then, as his ambition grew, two, three, four, and five, until he finally murdered his own brother to reclaim the throne outright. Indra, observing this from his heavenly assembly, disguised himself as a brahmin and lured the still-insatiable king with false promises of hidden treasure across the sea — leaving him to waste away in anxious, unfulfilled longing until the Buddha himself intervened, teaching that desire's pursuit brings joy only when satisfied and torment when it is not, and that a truly wise person recognizes an unmet promise for what it is rather than suffering over it. Cháng Bǎi treats the whole sequence — eight similes plus one cautionary tale — as cataloging exactly why desire, whatever momentary pleasure it offers, belongs entirely and irredeemably to the dark category.

10. Milestone: Desire's Principle — the Groom Who Priced His Bride Down to Nothing

The treatise closes its investigation of desire's danger through the principle of function — the body's needs are only ever medicine for an illness, never a pleasure to indulge — illustrated by the Buddha's own dismantling of a bridegroom's infatuation, item by item, until nothing of value remained.

Chinese Source

是故諸欲，唯能對治隨所生起種種苦惱，不應染著而受用之。唯應正念，譬如重病所逼切人，為除病故，服雜穢藥。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore, these desires are only able to counteract whatever suffering happens to arise — one should not indulge them with attachment. One should only, with right mindfulness, regard them as a person afflicted by grave illness regards taking a foul, bitter medicine solely to be rid of that illness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells the story of Nanda's kinsman, a young man of the Buddha's own clan, on his very wedding day — the Buddha, knowing the young man's readiness for liberation had ripened, arrived with Ānanda to claim him for the monastic life, brushing aside hostile onlookers' objections that a wedding was no place for renunciants. Later, having secretly caused the groom's ostentatious prayer to a manifested deity to appear answered, the Buddha asked how much the elaborate wedding had cost — and, learning that a full third of the sum had gone toward "purchasing" the bride, proceeded to ask, with disarming directness, what her hair alone might be worth, then her spit, her earwax, her internal organs, working through all thirty-six unclean substances one by one until the young man, tallying the true value of what he had paid so dearly for, realized he had been badly cheated by his own infatuation — and, his desire already weakened by ten thousand years of elemental-analysis practice carried over from past lives, found it fully collapse on the spot, whereupon the Buddha revealed his true form and the young man joined the monastic order immediately. Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson directly from the principle of function: the body's genuine needs — food for hunger, clothing for cold, shelter for exhaustion, medicine for illness — are entirely legitimate, but only ever as remedies for an affliction already present, never as objects of attachment in their own right; craving what merely functions as medicine is precisely the confusion this entire investigation is meant to dissolve.

11. Milestone: The First Dhyāna's Peaceful Mark — Learning the Mark Closes

Having thoroughly investigated the desire realm's coarseness through all six things, the treatise closes the first mental engagement with its positive counterpart: recognizing the first dhyāna's peaceful mark by simple contrast — everything coarse about the desire realm is, in the first dhyāna, simply absent.

Chinese Source

復能覺了初靜慮中所有靜相。謂欲界中一切麤性，於初靜慮皆無所有。由離欲界諸麤性故，初靜慮中說有靜性。是名覺了初靜慮中所有靜相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One is further able to recognize the peaceful mark present within the first dhyāna. That is, every coarse quality found in the desire realm is entirely absent in the first dhyāna; because it is free of every one of the desire realm's coarse qualities, the first dhyāna is said to possess a peaceful nature. This is called recognizing the peaceful mark present within the first dhyāna.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the elegant simplicity of this closing move: having spent such extensive effort cataloging the desire realm's danger, distress, disease, and calamity across meaning, substance, characteristic, category, time, and principle, the first dhyāna's peaceful mark requires no separate catalogue of its own — it is simply and exactly the absence of everything just described. This economy is itself instructive: genuine peace, in this analysis, is not some additional quality layered on top of ordinary experience, but precisely what remains once desire's coarseness has been thoroughly seen through and let go. With both the desire realm's coarse mark and the first dhyāna's peaceful mark now fully recognized, learning the object's mark — the first of the seven mental engagements — is complete, and the treatise moves next to the second engagement, confident understanding.

12. Milestone: Confident Understanding — Moving Beyond Words Into Direct Practice

The second mental engagement marks a genuine threshold: the practitioner no longer needs to consult teaching or texts to bring the coarse/peaceful comparison to mind — vivid understanding now arises directly, entirely through practice itself.

Chinese Source

彼既如是如理尋思，了知諸欲是其麤相，知初靜慮是其靜相；從此已後，超過聞思，唯用修行，於所緣相發起勝解，修奢摩他、毗鉢舍那。既修習已，如所尋思麤相、靜相，數起勝解，如是名為勝解作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus investigated soundly, and having come to know that desire is coarse and the first dhyāna is peaceful, from this point on one moves beyond hearing and reflection, relying solely on cultivation itself — generating vivid understanding with respect to the object, cultivating calm-abiding and insight. Having thus cultivated, one repeatedly generates vivid understanding of the coarse and peaceful marks just as investigated — this is called confident understanding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "moving beyond hearing and reflection" as a genuinely significant marker: at the first mental engagement, a practitioner still needs to actively recall teaching or consult a text to bring the coarse/peaceful comparison to mind; by the second, it arises directly through cultivation-born wisdom, immediately present the moment one sits, without any intervening step of remembering what was taught. He illustrates the resulting fluency with two anecdotes — two monks discussing wading through water, where the junior corrects the senior's phrasing from "not wetting the feet" to "the feet stay unwet," a subtle shift from active effort toward mind not chasing outward at all; and Guǎngqīn's rebuke to a disciple begrudging fruit given to monkeys ("isn't giving it to you the real waste?") — neither citing scripture directly, yet both perfectly in accord with its deepest point, precisely the fluency confident understanding is meant to produce: no longer parroting the teaching, but embodying its intent so thoroughly that speech naturally aligns with it unprompted.

13. Seclusion: The First Break From Coarse Affliction

Chinese Source

即此勝解善修、善習、善多修習為因緣故，最初生起斷煩惱道。即所生起斷煩惱道俱行作意，此中說名遠離作意。由能最初斷於欲界先所應斷諸煩惱故，及能除遣彼煩惱品麤重性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the causes and conditions of this same confident understanding, well cultivated, well practiced, and extensively cultivated, one first gives rise to the path that severs affliction. The mental engagement joined with this arising path that severs affliction is, here, called seclusion. Because it first severs the afflictions that ought first to be severed within the desire realm, and because it dispels the heaviness belonging to that category of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies what seclusion actually severs: the grossest, uppermost three grades (of the desire realm's nine total grades) of the discursive thoughts of desire, ill will, and harm — genuine severance, meaning these no longer arise even after rising from meditation, not merely suppressed in the moment through effort. This is the crucial distinction, he stresses, between simply redirecting attention away from a temptation and actually cutting off the affliction so it no longer surfaces at all — the treatise's own name for this stage, "seclusion," refers precisely to this genuine separation from what was formerly grasped.

14. Gathering Joy: The First Taste of Freedom's Pleasure

Chinese Source

從是已後，愛樂於斷、愛樂遠離，於諸斷中見勝功德，觸證少分遠離喜樂。於時時間，欣樂作意，而深慶悅；於時時間，厭離作意，而深厭患。為欲除遣惛沈睡眠、掉舉等故。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point on, one delights in severing affliction, delights in seclusion, sees excellent merit in each severance, and touches a small measure of the joy and happiness of seclusion. At fitting times, engaging the mind in delight, one deeply rejoices; at fitting times, engaging the mind in revulsion, one deeply feels aversion — in order to dispel dullness, drowsiness, restlessness, and the like.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies gathering joy as severing the desire realm's middle three grades of affliction, and notes its distinctive character: for the first time, the practitioner actually tastes the pleasure of freedom itself, not merely the absence of affliction — a small but genuine joy and happiness that makes continued practice self-reinforcing rather than merely disciplined. He explains the alternating delight-and-revulsion technique as targeted maintenance: delight (recollecting the Three Jewels and one's own progress) counters incipient dullness and drowsiness, while revulsion (returning to the desire realm's coarseness) counters incipient restlessness — the same two tools from the mental-engagement framework established back in fascicles 31 and 32, now applied specifically to sustaining momentum through this stage.

15. Milestone: Examination — the Tiger the Novice Monk Liked Best

Before advancing further, the treatise inserts a crucial reality check: deliberately summoning a tempting image to test whether desire has genuinely been overcome or merely gone quiet — a test that, revealingly, often turns up residual craving still very much alive.

Chinese Source

便作是念：我今為有於諸欲中貪欲煩惱不覺知耶？為無有耶？為審觀察如是事故，隨於一種可愛淨相作意思惟。由未永斷諸隨眠故，思惟如是淨妙相時，便復發起隨習近心、趣習近心、臨習近心，不能住捨，不能厭毀、制伏、違逆。

Ru-Yi Translation

One reflects thus: "Do I now have craving toward desires that I am simply unaware of? Or do I not?" In order to carefully examine this very matter, one deliberately directs attention to some attractive, pleasing mark. Because the underlying tendencies have not yet been permanently severed, when reflecting on such an attractive mark, a mind of inclining toward it, of moving toward it, of approaching it arises once again — unable to remain in equanimity, unable to feel revulsion toward it, to master it, or to resist it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi tells the classic story of an old monk warning his young disciple, sent down the mountain for supplies, to avoid a dangerous "tiger" — a woman with long hair and a skirt. A week later, asked what he liked best in the village, the boy answered without hesitation: the tiger. Cháng Bǎi reads examination as institutionalizing exactly this honest self-report: rather than assuming middling-grade affliction is now fully gone simply because gathering joy felt genuinely peaceful, the practitioner deliberately reconstructs a tempting image and watches, with real honesty, whether an inclination to approach it still arises — as it very often does, since only the underlying tendency's coarser and middling layers have actually been severed so far, not its deepest root. This stage exists precisely to prevent the false confidence of believing oneself further along the path than one genuinely is.

16. Milestone: The Culmination of Effort and Its Fruit — Entering the First Dhyāna

Having honestly confronted residual craving through examination, the practitioner redoubles effort to sever the desire realm's subtlest remaining afflictions — the culmination of effort — and, immediately following, actually enters the first dhyāna itself.

Chinese Source

從此倍更樂斷、樂修……由是因緣，從欲界繫一切煩惱，心得離繫。此由暫時伏斷方便，非是畢竟永害種子。當於爾時，初靜慮地前加行道已得究竟，一切煩惱對治作意已得生起，是名加行究竟作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point, delighting doubly in severing and in cultivating … through these causes and conditions, the mind becomes freed from every affliction bound to the desire realm. This is only a temporary means of suppression, not the ultimate, permanent destruction of the underlying seeds. At that time, the preparatory path preceding the first dhyāna has reached its culmination, and mental engagement that counters every affliction has fully arisen — this is called the culmination of effort.

Chinese Source

從此無間，由是因緣，證入根本初靜慮定。即此根本初靜慮定俱行作意，名加行究竟果作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Immediately following this, without interruption, through these causes and conditions, one enters the root absorption of the first dhyāna itself. The mental engagement joined with this root absorption of the first dhyāna is called the fruit of the culmination of effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the culmination of effort as severing the desire realm's lowest three grades of affliction — the subtlest and most stubborn remainder — through redoubled, meticulous practice that checks and rechecks what was already thought severed. He is careful to underscore the treatise's own explicit caveat: even this complete suppression of every desire-realm affliction is temporary, a matter of the underlying seeds being pressed down rather than destroyed, since only the supramundane path can accomplish genuine, permanent eradication. The culmination's fruit follows immediately and without any gap: the very next moment delivers actual entry into the first dhyāna's root absorption — the culmination of effort forming the final uninterrupted path, and its fruit the moment of liberation itself, exactly the two-part structure (uninterrupted path, then liberated path) that will recur identically when the same seven engagements are applied to the four noble truths in fascicle 34. With this, the complete seven-stage climb from near-attainment concentration to the first dhyāna proper — learning the mark, confident understanding, seclusion, gathering joy, examination, the culmination of effort, and its fruit — stands fully demonstrated as the treatise's worked example for the entire mundane path.

17. Milestone: The First Dhyāna's Five Factors, and the Seven Engagements' Distinct Functions

The treatise now names the five factors that fully define the first dhyāna, and closes the seven-engagement climb by specifying exactly what each individual engagement accomplishes — from first forming the aspiration through actually receiving the finished result.

Chinese Source

彼於爾時，遠離諸欲，遠離一切惡不善法，有尋有伺，離生喜樂。於初靜慮圓滿五支具足安住，名住欲界對治修果，名隨證得離欲界欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, one is free of desires, free of every unwholesome, unskillful state, endowed with applied and sustained thought, and possessed of the joy and happiness born of seclusion. Abiding with the first dhyāna's five factors fully perfected and complete is called abiding in the fruit of the practice that counters the desire realm, and is called thereby attaining freedom from desire for the desire realm.

Chinese Source

了相作意，於所應斷能正了知，於所應得能正了知，為斷應斷，為得應得，心生希願。勝解作意，為斷為得，正發加行。遠離作意，能捨所有上品煩惱。攝樂作意，能捨所有中品煩惱。觀察作意，能於所得離增上慢，安住其心。加行究竟作意，能捨所有下品煩惱。加行究竟果作意，能正領受彼諸作意善修習果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Learning the object's mark correctly knows what is to be abandoned and what is to be attained, and, wishing to abandon the one and attain the other, gives rise to genuine aspiration. Confident understanding, for the sake of that abandoning and attaining, actually initiates effort. Seclusion is able to abandon every uppermost grade of affliction. Gathering joy is able to abandon every middle grade of affliction. Examination is able to free what has been attained from false pride, settling the mind securely. The culmination of effort is able to abandon every lowest grade of affliction. The fruit of that culmination correctly receives the fruit that all these mental engagements, well cultivated, have produced.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first dhyāna's five defining factors — applied thought (coarse investigation comparing desire's coarseness to the first dhyāna's peace), sustained thought (the same comparison examined more finely), joy (a sixth-consciousness-linked delight — mental gladness, courage, ease, and pliancy all at once), happiness (an ālaya-consciousness-linked bodily ease pervading the whole physical body), and one-pointedness of mind (concentration's own defining stillness) — noting joy belongs to the mind and happiness to the body, together constituting the "joy and happiness born of seclusion" that gives the first dhyāna its name, intensifying in three stages from faint (at seclusion and gathering joy) through expanding (at the culmination of effort) to fully pervading the entire body without gap (at the culmination's fruit, actual entry into the first dhyāna). He then distills each engagement's single function into one clean verb: learning the mark forms the wish; confident understanding actually begins the work; seclusion, gathering joy, and the culmination of effort progressively abandon the upper, middle, and lower grades of affliction in turn; examination guards specifically against false pride at partial progress; and the fruit of culmination simply receives, complete, what the preceding six engagements together produced — a structure the treatise notes maps onto a broader forty-engagement scheme from the "Stage of Concentrative Equipoise," with learning-the-mark and confident-understanding together called "accordant engagement," seclusion/gathering-joy/culmination-of-effort together called "counteracting engagement," gathering joy doubling also as "purity-according engagement" (via the six recollections), and examination alone called "investigation-according engagement." With the entire seven-stage structure now fully defined, functionally distinguished, and mapped onto this wider scheme, the treatise's worked example of leaving the desire realm for the first dhyāna stands complete in every respect.

18. The Same Climb Repeats All the Way Up — and Two Universal Marks Explain Why

The treatise now generalizes: every remaining transition — second dhyāna, third, fourth, and all four formless absorptions — is climbed by the identical seven engagements just demonstrated. Two universal marks explain what "coarse" and "peaceful" actually mean at every single rung of that ladder.

Chinese Source

如初靜慮定有七種作意，如是第二、第三、第四靜慮定，及空無邊處、識無邊處、無所有處、非想非非想處定，當知各有七種作意……如是乃至為欲證入非想非非想處定，於地地中，隨其所應，當知皆有七種作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the first dhyāna's absorption has seven kinds of mental engagement, so too the second, third, and fourth dhyānas, and the spheres of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception should each be known to have their own seven kinds of mental engagement … and so on up to the wish to enter the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception itself: at every single stage, exactly as appropriate to that stage, all seven kinds of mental engagement should be known to be present.

Chinese Source

又彼麤相，遍在一切下地皆有。下從欲界，展轉上至無所有處……當知麤相略有二種，謂諸下地苦住增上，望上所住不寂靜故；及諸壽量時分短促，望上壽量轉減少故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, that coarse mark is present, in relative terms, in every lower stage — from the desire realm all the way up, in turn, to the sphere of nothingness … one should know the coarse mark is, in brief, of two kinds: that in every lower stage the abiding in suffering is heightened, being unpeaceful relative to what the stage above abides in; and that the span and duration of life is short and pressed, growing shorter still relative to the lifespan above.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise's own invitation to compress: having shown the complete seven-engagement climb once, in full analytical detail, for leaving the desire realm behind for the first dhyāna, it simply asserts by inference (比量) that the identical seven-stage structure — learning the mark, confident understanding, seclusion, gathering joy, examination, the culmination of effort, its fruit — governs every remaining rung of the ladder: first to second dhyāna, second to third, third to fourth, fourth dhyāna to the sphere of infinite space, and onward through infinite consciousness, nothingness, all the way to neither-perception-nor-non-perception — eight transitions in total, the coarse mark ending at the sphere of nothingness since nothing remains above neither-perception-nor-non-perception to compare it against. He draws out the pairing running underneath every single transition: the coarse mark is always exactly two things — more suffering and a shorter lifespan — while the peaceful mark above is always their mirror, less suffering and a longer lifespan (the desire realm's human century looks starved next to the highest heaven's eighty-thousand-eon span, yet even that summit still ends in death and falling back). Two illustrative anecdotes carry the point that dhyāna is a graded scale of actual stability, not a switch flipped on or off: the first dhyāna's heaven still burns in the eon-ending fire that the second dhyāna's heaven is entirely safe from, and — from Venerable Miàojìng's telling — a practitioner deep in absorption is genuinely unreachable by an alarm bell rung at their ear, since only their own wish to rise, never another's effort, can end the state. Learning-the-mark's task, at every single stage without exception, is simply to see this pair of facts about the stage one is leaving clearly enough to want to leave it.

19. The Second Dhyāna: Inner Clarity Replacing Applied and Sustained Thought

The treatise now defines the second dhyāna in its own canonical terms — what makes the applied and sustained thought that built the first dhyāna into precisely the "coarse" mark that must now be abandoned, and what inner clarity (內等淨) replaces it.

Chinese Source

於有尋有伺三摩地相，心能棄捨。於無尋無伺三摩地相，繫念安住……於諸怱務所行境界能正遠離，於不怱務所行境界安住其心，一味寂靜、極寂靜轉，是故說言：尋伺寂靜故、內等淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mind becomes able to relinquish the mark of concentration accompanied by applied and sustained thought, and fixes its mindfulness instead, abiding, on the mark of concentration free of both … it correctly withdraws from every object involving hurried activity, and settles the mind instead in an object free of such hurried activity, turning uniformly still, utterly still. For this reason it is said: because applied and sustained thought have become still, and because of inner, uniform purity.

Chinese Source

又彼即於無尋無伺三摩地中串修習故，超過尋伺有間闕位，能正獲得無間闕位，是故說言：心一趣故。無尋無伺者，一切尋伺悉皆斷故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And by repeatedly cultivating this concentration free of applied and sustained thought, one passes beyond the stage where such thought still intermittently intrudes, and genuinely attains the stage where it no longer intrudes at all — for this reason it is said: because the mind reaches a single direction. Free of applied and sustained thought means that all applied and sustained thought directed toward desire has been entirely cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful about a point easily misread: "free of applied and sustained thought" does not mean the mental factors of applied and sustained thought vanish from the mind altogether — they still operate whenever a second-dhyāna practitioner rises from absorption or is reborn in that heaven. What is actually cut off is only their use in the coarse, effortful business of seeking freedom from desire; once genuine inner purity (內等淨) has been reached, that laborious seeking is simply no longer needed. He glosses inner purity itself as the positive replacement doing the actual work here — an inward evenness and clarity that keeps applied and sustained thought's agitation from arising at all, compared in the *Xiǎnyáng* treatise to dispelling the muddiness those two mental factors otherwise stir up, so thoroughly stilled that Venerable Miàojìng's simile applies again at a deeper pitch: thunder overhead in this state goes entirely unheard, not merely unregarded. Where the first dhyāna's five factors were applied thought, sustained thought, joy, happiness, and one-pointedness, the second dhyāna keeps only four — inner purity replacing the first two entirely — a straightforward count that will recur, one further factor lost each time, through the third and fourth dhyānas ahead.

20. The Third Dhyāna: Equanimity Arrives, and Why Only Now

The treatise defines the third dhyāna through the abandoning of joy itself — the very delight that made the second dhyāna sweet becomes the next mark to be seen as coarse — and explains, via a classical question-and-answer, why equanimity, mindfulness, and clear comprehension are only named explicitly starting here, though present all along.

Chinese Source

彼於喜相深見過失，是故說言：於喜離欲……如是二法能擾亂心，障無間捨。初靜慮中有尋伺故，令無間捨不自在轉；第二靜慮由有喜故，令無間捨不自在轉。是故此捨，初二靜慮說名無有。由是因緣，修靜慮者，第三靜慮方名有捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

One sees deeply into the fault of the mark of joy, and for this reason it is said: one becomes free of desire for joy itself … these two things — applied-and-sustained-thought and joy — are each able to disturb the mind, obstructing uninterrupted equanimity. In the first dhyāna, because applied and sustained thought remain, uninterrupted equanimity cannot yet operate freely; in the second dhyāna, because joy remains, uninterrupted equanimity likewise cannot yet operate freely. For this reason this equanimity is said to be absent in the first and second dhyānas. On account of these causes and conditions, it is only in the third dhyāna that one cultivating dhyāna is said to possess equanimity.

Chinese Source

《披》問：念、正知、捨，一切處有，何故於初靜慮等不說耶？答：初靜慮中，由尋伺門所引發故，雖有不說。第二靜慮中，有踊躍自體之所作業，及心所有少分煩惱所纏覆故，總以內等淨名顯之。第三靜慮中，彼心所有少分煩惱皆遠離故，顯彼自相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Commentary — Question: mindfulness, clear comprehension, and equanimity are present everywhere; why are they left unmentioned at the first dhyāna and so on? Answer: in the first dhyāna, since it is brought about through the gateway of applied and sustained thought, though present, they go unmentioned. In the second dhyāna, since there is still joy's own exuberant activity and a slight remaining affliction still enveloping the mind, the whole is instead named simply by "inner purity." In the third dhyāna, since even that slight remaining affliction is entirely gone, its own distinct character can finally be shown.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats equanimity, mindfulness, and clear comprehension as present from the very start of practice — running continuously through every stage from the nine mental abidings up to the peak of existence — with only their prominence changing: applied and sustained thought so dominate the first dhyāna's business, and joy's exuberance the second's, that equanimity stays functionally invisible until the third dhyāna finally quiets both disturbances enough to let it stand forth in its own right, "superior equanimity" (上捨), fully self-possessed. He walks through the third dhyāna's remaining two named factors as this same equanimity's working parts: mindfulness is simply staying settled in the joy-free state once reached, and clear comprehension is catching joy's telltale companion — its associated perception and volition — the instant either flickers back into activity through a lapse of practice, and immediately rejecting, expelling, and disgorging it, restoring superior equanimity before it can take hold. Bodily happiness now also pervades the whole body for the first time, rather than joy's mind-only exuberance — the third dhyāna is called "the ground of joy-free wondrous happiness" for exactly this reason, and the treatise stresses this happiness is unique to the third dhyāna alone: the dhyānas below lack any happiness at all, while the stages above have equanimity but no happiness left to feel — a scale in which the third dhyāna, for one stage only, has both together.

21. The Fourth Dhyāna: Happiness Itself Abandoned, Every Storm Stilled

The treatise now abandons the third dhyāna's own happiness — the very bodily bliss just gained becomes the next coarse mark — arriving at a stage the treatise calls "purified equanimity, purified mindfulness," entirely free of the three cosmic disasters that still threaten every stage below it.

Chinese Source

修靜慮者，即於爾時，所有苦樂皆得超越……謂入第四靜慮定時，樂受斷故；入第二靜慮定時，苦受斷故；入第三靜慮定時，喜受沒故；入初靜慮定時，憂受沒故。

Ru-Yi Translation

One cultivating dhyāna, at that very time, transcends whatever suffering and happiness remain … that is: on entering the fourth dhyāna, the feeling of happiness is cut off; on entering the second dhyāna, the feeling of suffering is cut off; on entering the third dhyāna, the feeling of joy vanishes; on entering the first dhyāna, the feeling of grief vanishes.

Chinese Source

從初靜慮一切下地災患已斷。謂尋、伺、喜、樂、入息、出息。由彼斷故，此中捨念清淨鮮白……正入第四靜慮定時，心住無動，一切動亂皆悉遠離，是故說言：捨念清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every disturbance of the lower stages, from the first dhyāna onward, is now cut off — namely, applied thought, sustained thought, joy, happiness, in-breath, and out-breath. Because these are cut off, equanimity and mindfulness here become pure and bright … at the very moment of entering the fourth dhyāna, the mind abides utterly unmoved, every disturbance entirely left behind — for this reason it is said: purified equanimity, purified mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the elegant four-stage bookkeeping the treatise assembles here: grief goes at the first dhyāna, physical suffering at the second, joy's exuberance at the third, and finally happiness itself — the last positive feeling left — at the fourth, leaving only the neutral, neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling that will itself only be surrendered much later, on the supramundane path of the three concentrations of emptiness, markless­ness, and wishlessness. He connects this directly to the classical doctrine of the three cosmic disasters that periodically destroy the universe: fire reaches up to consume the first dhyāna's heaven (whose inhabitants still have applied-and-sustained-thought's inner "heat"), water reaches the second (whose inhabitants still have joy's inner turbulence), wind reaches the third (whose inhabitants still breathe, in-breath and out-breath being a subtle inner "wind"), and none of the three ever touches the fourth dhyāna's heaven, precisely because none of those six disturbances remain — outer catastrophe simply mirrors inner affliction. He closes with a reflection reaching outside the text: emotional volatility itself — grief, physical distress, exuberance, contentment cycling one into another — is what keeps ordinary beings' bodies in a kind of constant cellular conflict, prone to illness, while a settled, undisturbed mind supports a settled, resilient body (he cites Venerable Master Yìnshùn's frail constitution paired with an unusually long life, crediting the latter to the former's inner stillness) — dhyāna's calming of emotion, in other words, is not merely a spiritual attainment but something that shows up, quite literally, in how well the body itself holds up.
