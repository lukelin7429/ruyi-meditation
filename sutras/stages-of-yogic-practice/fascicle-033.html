Fascicle 33 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十三 · The Fourth Yogic Process Opens

The Mundane Path Through the Absorptions

Fascicle 33 opens the fourth and final yogic process of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples — the extensive exposition of the marks of cultivation. Having attained near-attainment concentration in fascicle 32, a practitioner now faces two paths forward: the mundane path toward the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions, detailed here through seven kinds of mental engagement, or the supramundane path toward the four noble truths, reserved for fascicle 34. This fascicle follows the mundane path in full.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 33 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Fourth Yogic Process Opens — Two Paths From Near-Attainment Concentration

Fascicle 33 opens the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples' fourth and final yogic process with two summary verses — one for the mundane path this fascicle will cover, one for the supramundane path reserved for fascicle 34 — before explaining that a practitioner who has attained near-attainment concentration now faces exactly two ways forward, and no third option.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第四瑜伽處之一。復次，此嗢柁南曰：七作意離欲　及諸定廣辯　二定五神通　生差別諸相。觀察於諸諦　如實而通達　廣分別於修　究竟為其後。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first part of the fourth yogic process. Further, the summary verse says: "Seven mental engagements free one from desire, and the absorptions are explained at length; two attainments, five superknowledges, and the differentiated marks of rebirth." "Investigating the truths, one penetrates them as they truly are; the varieties of cultivation are explained at length, culminating, at the very end, in the ultimate fruit."

Chinese Source

已得作意諸瑜伽師，已入如是少分樂斷；從此已後，唯有二趣，更無所餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

A teacher of yogic practice who has attained mental engagement has already entered this partial delight in severing affliction. From this point on, there are exactly two paths open, and no others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks both verses as tables of contents for fascicles 33 and 34 respectively. The mundane-path verse names, in order: the seven kinds of mental engagement that carry a practitioner from near-attainment concentration through freedom from desire (learning the object's mark, confident understanding, seclusion, gathering joy, examination, the culmination of effort, and its fruit); the eight absorptions this fascicle will detail — the four dhyānas and four formless spheres; two attainments without mind (the attainment of non-perception and the attainment of cessation); five of the six superknowledges (divine eye, divine ear, mind-reading, psychic power, and recollection of past lives — omitting only the knowledge of the exhaustion of contaminants, since that belongs only to the supramundane path); and finally the differentiated marks of those reborn in the form and formless heavens, along with the outward signs of someone who has genuinely become free of desire. The supramundane-path verse, held for fascicle 34, previews investigating the four noble truths through the identical seven mental engagements, culminating in arhatship itself. Cháng Bǎi frames near-attainment concentration as a genuine fork in the road: a practitioner who stops here and simply enjoys its partial peace makes no further progress; the only two ways forward are the mundane path toward ever-deeper absorption, or the supramundane path toward liberation itself — fascicles 21 through 34 together tracing a Hearer-disciple's entire journey from the provisions of the path through final attainment.

2. Milestone: Four Kinds of Practitioner Who Choose the Mundane Path

The treatise now identifies exactly who, having attained near-attainment concentration, chooses to pursue the mundane path of deepening absorption rather than the supramundane path toward liberation — four distinct kinds of practitioner, each with a different underlying reason.

Chinese Source

答：略有四種補特伽羅。何等為四？一、一切外道；二、於正法中根性羸劣，先修正行；三、根性雖利，善根未熟；四、一切菩薩樂當來世證大菩提，非於現法。如是四種補特伽羅，於現法中，樂往世間道發起加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: There are, in brief, four kinds of person. What are the four? First, every follower of an outside path. Second, one within the true teaching whose faculties are weak, who first cultivates correct conduct accordingly. Third, one whose faculties are sharp, but whose wholesome roots are not yet ripe. Fourth, every bodhisattva who wishes to realize great enlightenment in a future life, not in this present life. These four kinds of person, in this present life, delight in applying effort toward the mundane path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the four precisely: a follower of an outside path pursues the mundane path exclusively, since without the Buddha's teaching there is no concept of liberation to aim for at all, and such a practitioner typically mistakes deep absorption itself for final liberation; a practitioner within the true teaching but of weak faculties simply cannot yet move quickly enough toward direct realization, and so first strengthens concentration as preparation; one of sharp faculties but immature wholesome roots has the capacity but not yet the ripeness needed to actually enter the stream of the noble path; and a bodhisattva, uniquely, does not pursue the mundane path out of any limitation at all, but by deliberate choice — postponing personal liberation for countless future lives of benefiting beings, and cultivating the dhyānas' profound stillness partly because that same concentration strengthens the very capacity needed to guide others skillfully. Cháng Bǎi notes the second and third differ subtly: one lacks the raw capacity, the other has the capacity but not yet the maturity — a distinction worth knowing since the appropriate encouragement differs for each.

3. The Six-Aspect Method: Coarse Below, Peaceful Above

Chinese Source

謂先於欲界觀為麤性，於初靜慮若定、若生觀為靜性，發起加行，離欲界欲；如是乃至發起加行，離無所有處欲，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, one first observes the desire realm as coarse, and observes the first dhyāna — whether the concentration itself or the resulting rebirth there — as peaceful; applying effort in this way, one becomes free of desire for the desire realm. In just this way, applying effort onward, one becomes free of desire even for the sphere of nothingness — this should be understood identically at every stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the classical "six aspects" underlying this method: the lower level is seen as suffering, coarse, and an obstruction, while the level above is seen as peaceful, excellent, and a genuine escape — and this same comparative structure repeats at every single transition up the ladder of absorption: desire realm against first dhyāna, first against second, second against third, third against fourth, fourth against the sphere of infinite space, and onward through infinite consciousness, nothingness, and finally neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the very summit of conditioned existence. He stresses this is a genuinely gradual method — no level is skipped — and that it only ever suppresses the coarser afflictions associated with each lower level, never uprooting their underlying seeds; even the summit, the formless sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, with its eighty-thousand-eon lifespan, still ends in death and a fall back into the round of rebirth once that immense lifespan is exhausted. The mundane path, however far it climbs, never itself delivers final liberation.

4. Milestone: What the Mundane Path Yields — Attainments Without Mind, Five Superknowledges, and Heavenly Rebirth

Though the mundane path cannot deliver final liberation, it is far from fruitless: sustained cultivation of the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions gives rise to two rare attainments without mind, five superknowledges, and a range of genuine noble qualities.

Chinese Source

又依靜慮等，能引無想定等及發五神通等。又即依此若生、若相，皆當廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, relying on the dhyānas and so on, one is able to give rise to the attainment of non-perception and the like, and to develop the five superknowledges and so on. And, relying on these same attainments, both the resulting rebirth and the outward marks of accomplishment shall all be explained at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps out the yield of the mundane path in full: the fourth dhyāna can give rise to the attainment of non-perception, where the sixth consciousness's feeling and perception are entirely stilled; the peak of neither-perception-nor-non-perception can give rise to the far rarer attainment of cessation, where even the seventh consciousness's self-grasping activity is stilled alongside them. The four root dhyānas can also develop five of the six superknowledges — psychic power (shrinking, expanding, or transforming physical matter), recollection of past lives, the divine ear (hearing at any distance without obstruction), the divine eye (knowing where a being will be reborn), and mind-reading (knowing another's thoughts directly) — together with further noble qualities such as the samādhi of non-contention (Subhūti's practice of checking, before begging for alms, that his presence would trouble no one) and the samādhi of wish-fulfilling wisdom (instantly mastering a body of knowledge merely by wishing to understand it, rather than through laborious study). Cultivating the nine levels at low, middling, or high grade determines specific heavenly rebirth within each level's own sub-tiers — the first dhyāna's retinue-of-Brahmā, ministers-of-Brahmā, and Great Brahmā himself, for instance, corresponding to low, middling, and high cultivation respectively — and genuine freedom from desire, once attained, visibly changes a practitioner's outward bearing in body, speech, and mind, details the treatise promises to spell out further ahead.

5. Milestone: The Seven Kinds of Mental Engagement Named

The treatise now names, in full, the seven mental engagements that structure the entire mundane path — the same seven first introduced back in fascicle 30, now given their complete definitions as the operative method for actually ascending through the absorptions.

Chinese Source

為離欲界欲，勤修觀行諸瑜伽師，由七作意方能獲得離欲界欲。何等名為七種作意？謂了相作意、勝解作意、遠離作意、攝樂作意、觀察作意、加行究竟作意、加行究竟果作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

For a teacher of yogic practice diligently cultivating contemplation in order to become free of desire for the desire realm, it is through seven kinds of mental engagement that freedom from desire for the desire realm is actually attained. What are these seven kinds of mental engagement? Learning the object's mark, confident understanding, seclusion, gathering joy, examination, the culmination of effort, and the fruit of that culmination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews all seven in sequence: learning the object's mark correctly identifies the lower level's suffering, coarseness, and obstruction, and the upper level's peace, excellence, and genuine escape; confident understanding repeatedly generates vivid understanding of that same comparison; seclusion first abandons the lower level's grossest, most obvious afflictions; gathering joy abandons the lower level's middling afflictions, delighting in the process of letting go; examination checks whether the lower level's afflictions have genuinely been abandoned, guarding specifically against the false confidence of imagining oneself further along than one actually is; the culmination of effort abandons even the lower level's subtlest remaining afflictions, forming the final uninterrupted path immediately preceding attainment; and the fruit of that culmination is the actual moment of liberation from the lower level — the release itself, receiving in full whatever qualities the newly attained higher level brings. Cháng Bǎi notes this same seven-stage structure, first sketched in outline back when four kinds of mental engagement were introduced in fascicle 31, now receives its complete and definitive treatment — and will recur, essentially unchanged, when the supramundane path applies the identical seven engagements to the four noble truths in fascicle 34.

6. Milestone: Learning the Object's Mark Opens — the Meaning and Substance of Desire's Coarseness

The first of the seven mental engagements now receives its full treatment: investigating the desire realm's coarseness through the same six-things framework used throughout the treatise, beginning with what makes desire coarse in meaning, and what its underlying substance actually consists of.

Chinese Source

云何名為了相作意？謂若作意，能正覺了欲界麤相，初靜慮靜相。云何覺了欲界麤相？謂正尋思欲界六事：一、義，二、事，三、相，四、品，五、時，六、理。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called learning the object's mark? It is the mental engagement able to correctly recognize the desire realm's coarse mark and the first dhyāna's peaceful mark. How does one recognize the desire realm's coarse mark? By rightly investigating six things regarding the desire realm: meaning, substance, characteristic, category, time, and principle.

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：如是諸欲有多過患、有多損惱、有多疫癘、有多災害。於諸欲中多過患義，廣說乃至多災害義，是名麤義。謂正尋思：於諸欲中有內貪欲，於諸欲中有外貪欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: these desires carry much danger, much distress, much disease, and much calamity. Investigating desire's much danger, and so on at length through its much calamity — this is called its meaning. One rightly investigates: within these desires there is inner craving, and within these desires there is outer craving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks desire's fourfold danger in meaning: it brings little genuine satisfaction yet is condemned by the Buddha, it constantly stokes further craving rather than resolving it, and it drives beings toward unwholesome action; it breeds constant distress, since desire's very nature is to grieve when unfulfilled and grieve again when lost; it is the basis for countless diseases, since compulsive pursuit of sensory pleasure directly damages health; and it is the basis for the calamity of rebirth itself, since unabandoned desire simply perpetuates the entire cycle of old age, sickness, and death indefinitely. On substance, he distinguishes inner craving (attachment to one's own body and existence) from outer craving (attachment to external objects and other beings) as the two basic forms desire's underlying substance takes — the raw material this entire investigation is meant to dismantle.

7. Milestone: Desire's Particular Characteristics — Object-Desire, Affliction-Desire, and Three Kinds of Distortion

The treatise now distinguishes desire's object (the sensory thing itself) from desire's affliction (the inner craving that actually does the grasping) — and traces how each of the three kinds of feeling breeds its own characteristic distortion.

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：此為煩惱欲，此為事欲。此復三種：謂順樂受處、順苦受處、順不苦不樂受處。順樂受處是貪欲依處，是想、心倒依處；順苦受處是瞋恚依處，是忿、恨依處；順不苦不樂受處是愚癡依處，是覆、惱、誑、諂、無慚、無愧依處，是見倒依處。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: this is affliction-desire, this is object-desire. Object-desire is further threefold: what conduces to pleasant feeling, what conduces to painful feeling, and what conduces to neutral feeling. What conduces to pleasant feeling is the basis for craving, and the basis for distorted perception and distorted mind. What conduces to painful feeling is the basis for hatred, and the basis for indignation and resentment. What conduces to neutral feeling is the basis for delusion, and the basis for concealment, irritability, deceit, flattery, shamelessness, and lack of conscience — and the basis for distorted view.

Chinese Source

即正尋思：如是諸欲，極惡諸受之所隨逐，極惡煩惱之所隨逐。是名尋思諸欲自相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One then rightly investigates: these desires are pursued by the most severe kinds of feeling, and pursued by the most severe afflictions. This is called investigating the particular characteristic of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes object-desire (the sensory object itself — a sight, a sound, a person) from affliction-desire (the actual craving mind that grasps at it), citing the classical formula that "the world's fine objects are not truly desire; true desire is the discriminating craving of the mind" — a cup of coffee is only ever object-desire; the felt need for it is affliction-desire. He then traces three distinct distortions bred by the three feelings: pleasant feeling breeds "perceptual distortion" (mistaking an object's appearance for something genuinely fine) and "mental distortion" (the craving that follows from that misperception); painful feeling breeds no comparable distortion since its unpleasantness is at least accurately recognized, only the secondary reactions of indignation and resentment; and neutral feeling — the subtlest and most commonly overlooked — breeds delusion and, through it, an entire cluster of secondary afflictions (concealing one's faults out of fear of losing reputation, lashing out when disturbed, deceiving and flattering others for personal gain, and both shamelessness before oneself and indifference to others' judgment), because neutral feeling is where a being most easily overlooks the aggregates' own subtle, ever-present suffering of conditioning — mistaking a self-grasping "I" for genuinely, permanently existing beneath the placid surface. Only a noble one, Cháng Bǎi notes, actually recognizes this neutral feeling for the subtle suffering it truly is; every ordinary being takes it as simple, harmless neutrality, which is precisely why it breeds the deepest distortion of the three — distorted view itself.

8. Desire's Universal Characteristics: Suffering and Impermanence

Chinese Source

謂正尋思：此一切欲，生苦、老苦，廣說乃至求不得苦等所隨逐、等所隨縛。諸受欲者，於圓滿欲驅迫而轉，亦未解脫生等法故，雖彼諸欲勝妙圓滿，而暫時有。是名尋思諸欲共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One rightly investigates: all these desires are pursued and bound by the suffering of birth, the suffering of old age, and so on at length through the suffering of not obtaining what is sought. Those who indulge in desire are driven and compelled in pursuit of ever more complete satisfaction, never freed from birth and the rest — so that even the most excellent, complete desires, however fine, are only ever temporary. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that whatever desire's particular flavor — pleasant, painful, or neutral — every single instance shares the same two universal marks: it is bound up with the classical sufferings (birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from what is loved, encountering what is hated, and failing to obtain what is sought), and it is impermanent, however complete or refined it may temporarily seem. He stresses the ordinary mind's characteristic error is treating desire's temporary completeness as though it were lasting security — wealth, a large family, a comfortable life all still end in exactly the same suffering and impermanence as anything else, a fact obvious to anyone who examines it honestly, yet one that desire itself, by its very nature, keeps the mind from actually examining. Only sustained investigation like this — precisely the exercise the first mental engagement demands — cuts through that habitual blindness.
