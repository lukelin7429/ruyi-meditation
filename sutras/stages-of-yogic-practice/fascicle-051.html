Fascicle 51 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十一 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Opens

Eight Proofs for the Store-Consciousness

Having completed the entire seventeen-stage Basic Section across fascicles 1 through 50, the treatise now opens its second great division: the Chapter of Deciding Discernment, resolving the deeper, previously unexplained questions latent within those same seventeen stages. This fascicle begins with the single most consequential of these questions — why the Buddha never named the store-consciousness in the Āgamas, and how its existence can nonetheless be proven — through eight classical arguments that anchor the entire Yogācāra school's own account of consciousness and rebirth.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 51 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Opens — Why the Buddha Kept the Store-Consciousness a Secret

Fascicle 51 opens the treatise's second great division: the Chapter of Deciding Discernment, spanning fascicles 51 through 80, which resolves the deeper, previously unexplained meanings latent within the seventeen stages of the Basic Section just completed — matters the Basic Section either left unaddressed or explained only briefly, now unpacked through careful questions and answers. Where the Basic Section taught what is shared across all three vehicles alongside what the Great Vehicle alone should cultivate, this new division turns specifically to Consciousness-Only's own deepest teachings: why the store-consciousness must be established at all, how saṃsāra's own turning and reversal actually work, the store-consciousness's own essential nature and function and its relationship to active consciousness, how defilement is transformed into purity, and the treatise's own views on the three times, the four conditions, provisionally established phenomena, and the six kinds of skillful understanding. The chapter's own first section, spanning fascicles 51 through 57, resolves five topics regarding the Five Sense-Consciousness-Corresponding Ground and the Mind Ground together: the store-consciousness itself; the distinctions among the various forms of consciousness; the three times and four conditions; provisionally established phenomena; and the six kinds of skillful understanding. The treatise begins with the store-consciousness, posing three questions inherited directly from the Basic Section's own brief mention of it: why did the Buddha not explain, in the Five Sense-Consciousness-Corresponding Ground or the Mind Ground, that the store-consciousness truly exists? By what reasoning can its existence be known? And how should its nature, function, and full characteristics be understood in detail? The treatise answers the first question directly: this establishment is the Blessed One's own most profound, secret record, and for this reason was not taught — since the store-consciousness's own subtle, seemingly continuous activity risks being mistaken by ordinary beings, and even by śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas still carrying attachment to a real self within phenomena, for a genuine, abiding self, an error that would obstruct the very path to liberation or even lead to a fall into the lower destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi orients the reader to the sheer scale of what begins here: the Chapter of Deciding Discernment spans thirty full fascicles, nearly two-thirds the length of the entire Basic Section it now discusses, precisely because it must unpack, question by question, everything the seventeen stages left compressed or unstated — above all the store-consciousness itself, the single most consequential and most carefully guarded teaching in the entire treatise. He notes that this chapter's prose grows noticeably more technical and dialectical than the Basic Section's own more narrative style, requiring real patience from the reader, since entire arguments now turn on subtle distinctions a careless reading could easily miss.

He highlights the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own explanation, cited in the commentary, for exactly why the Buddha withheld this teaching from śrāvakas specifically: liberation from saṃsāra requires only severing self-grasping, attainable without ever knowing the store-consciousness's own subtle workings, whereas a bodhisattva vowed to liberate all beings genuinely needs the comprehensive wisdom of every realm, phenomenon, category, and time that only an understanding of the store-consciousness makes possible — the teaching withheld not from any lack of trust, but because śrāvakas simply had no need of it to reach their own goal. Having explained why the store-consciousness went unnamed in the Āgamas, he notes, the treatise turns next to how the Buddha did, in fact, gesture toward it — through a second name, and a celebrated verse.

2. Milestone: "Deep and Subtle Flows the Store of Every Seed" — The Celebrated Ādāna Verse

The treatise now cites the Blessed One's own words in direct support: the ādāna-consciousness is deep and subtle, every seed within it flowing like a raging torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate and cling to it as a self. The commentary explains that "deep and subtle" distinguishes this teaching from the shallow, superficial doctrine and unrealized practice of ordinary beings, and even from the two lesser vehicles' own real but incomplete depth — the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha having genuinely severed self-grasping and attained arhatship, yet still retaining attachment to a real self within phenomena, meaning the theory of the ādāna-consciousness surpasses even their own considerable attainment in subtlety. The image of a raging torrent is explained through three senses of "flowing": flowing inward, as every seed streams into the three realms of birth and death; flowing away, as beings drift and regress from one realm of existence into another, falling from the formless realms toward form, or from form toward desire, never settling in one destined place; and flowing out, as accumulated merit and virtuous roots — precept, concentration, and wisdom — seep away just as water leaks from a cracked pond that can no longer hold it. Because the ādāna-consciousness carries on so subtly, its own single characteristic continuing on in an unbroken, self-similar stream, those without proper understanding easily mistake it for a real, permanent, singular self, giving rise to both the view clinging to a personal self and the view clinging to a self within phenomena — and it is precisely to prevent this error that the Buddha withheld its deeper meaning from ordinary beings and from the two lesser vehicles alike.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, citing proof. As the Blessed One said: the ādāna-consciousness is deep and subtle, every seed within it like a raging torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate and cling to it as a self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this verse, drawn from the Sūtra Explaining the Profound Secrets' own Chapter on the Characteristics of Mind, Manas, and Consciousness, as among the most quoted four lines in the entire history of Yogācāra thought — cited repeatedly across the Compendium of the Great Vehicle and countless later commentaries precisely because it compresses the entire justification for the store-consciousness's own carefully guarded secrecy into a single, memorable stanza.

He highlights the threefold gloss on "flowing" as worth holding in mind throughout everything that follows in this chapter: flowing inward names how seeds continually pour into the three realms, flowing away names the instability of rebirth's own destination, and flowing out names how easily accumulated virtue can leak away unless carefully protected — three distinct dangers, all traceable back to the same restless, continuous activity of the seeds the ādāna-consciousness carries. He also draws out the etymology behind the name itself, already partly explained in the commentary's own gloss on "ādāna" as carrying three distinct functions — holding seeds so they are never lost, holding the physical sense faculties so the body retains sensation, and holding onto rebirth itself at the very moment of conception — an analysis the Establishment of Consciousness-Only Treatise develops further still. Having cited this celebrated verse confirming why the Buddha kept this teaching so carefully guarded, he notes, the treatise now turns to the second of the three original questions: by what reasoning can the store-consciousness's own existence actually be proven, introduced through a summary verse naming eight distinct arguments.

3. Milestone: Eight Arguments Named in Verse — Why Ordinary Consciousness Alone Cannot Explain a Living Body

The treatise now turns to proving that the store-consciousness genuinely exists, first presenting a summary verse: appropriation, the very first arising, clarity of cognition, having the nature of seeds, differences in karmic function, differences in bodily sensation, the attainment of mindless concentration, and consciousness at the moment of death — without the store-consciousness, none of these eight would make any sense at all. The treatise then explains each term in prose: through eight distinct characteristics, the store-consciousness is definitively proven to exist — for apart from the store-consciousness, the basis of appropriation makes no sense, the very first arising of consciousness makes no sense, the presence of clarity in cognition makes no sense, the nature of holding seeds makes no sense, differences in karmic function make no sense, differences in bodily sensation make no sense, abiding in mindless concentration makes no sense, and consciousness at the moment of death makes no sense. The treatise explains that Consciousness-Only thinkers hold that only by establishing the store-consciousness can the full range of life's own phenomena be adequately explained, whereas śrāvaka thinkers believe the six active forms of consciousness alone suffice to account for everything — the entire discussion that follows proceeds by comparing the six active consciousnesses against the store-consciousness on precisely these eight points. In brief: the six active consciousnesses require specific conditions to arise at all and are therefore intermittent, and they shift constantly among wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral qualities, so they cannot appropriate and sustain the physical sense faculties, nor can they retain the seeds of past action; only the store-consciousness, itself a uniformly neutral, fully ripened form of consciousness continuing without interruption throughout an entire lifetime, can appropriate the physical body, retain its seeds, sustain the body's own subtle ease even during mindless meditative attainments, and gradually withdraw from the body as death approaches, causing it to cool. Of the eight reasons, the treatise notes, the first arising, clarity of cognition, and karmic function serve chiefly to refute a further śrāvaka objection — that multiple forms of consciousness cannot possibly arise simultaneously — since if this objection stood, the store-consciousness could never coexist with the six active consciousnesses at all, making these three reasons an indirect line of proof supporting the other five.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this eightfold framework as the argumentative backbone of the entire store-consciousness discussion, and recommends keeping the whole list in view before descending into each individual proof: the six active consciousnesses, dependent on conditions and shifting constantly between wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral states, simply cannot do the jobs a living body plainly requires — sustaining the body moment to moment, retaining the seeds of past karma, keeping the body alive even in mindless meditative states, and gradually withdrawing at death — jobs only a continuous, uniformly neutral, fully ripened consciousness could ever perform.

He highlights the three "indirect" proofs — first arising, clarity of cognition, and karmic function — as answering not the main question directly but a prior objection that would otherwise block the whole argument: if forms of consciousness genuinely cannot coexist, as some śrāvaka thinkers held, then no store-consciousness could ever operate alongside the six active consciousnesses in the first place, making the case for simultaneous multiple consciousnesses itself a necessary preliminary battle before the five more direct proofs can even get underway. Having named all eight reasons, he notes, the treatise now works through each one individually, beginning with the first: the basis of appropriation.

4. Milestone: The First Proof Begins — Three Reasons Why Only the Store-Consciousness Can Sustain a Living Body

The treatise now works through the first of the eight proofs, appropriation, itself explained through five distinct reasons. Asked why appropriation makes no sense apart from the store-consciousness, the treatise answers: for five reasons. Asked what these five are, the treatise explains the first: the store-consciousness has past karmic action itself as its cause, while the active consciousnesses — the eye-consciousness and the rest — have the present, momentary coming together of conditions as their cause; as it is said, consciousness arises through the coming together of a sense faculty, an object, and attention, and so on at length. This means the store-consciousness, generated by karma performed in a past life, continues uninterrupted throughout an entire lifetime, sustaining the physical sense faculties without a single gap — sustaining a body destined by past karma to live fifty years, for instance, for the entirety of those fifty years — whereas the eye-consciousness and the other active forms of consciousness require present conditions to arise at all, and cease the moment any single required condition is missing, making them incapable of sustaining the physical body on their own. The second reason: the six active consciousnesses can be observed possessing wholesome, unwholesome, and other differing moral natures, shifting constantly between them, and are therefore unfit to serve as the basis of appropriation, since wholesome and unwholesome states are mutually exclusive and cannot embrace one another the way a genuinely neutral consciousness can. The third reason: the six active consciousnesses cannot be found to belong to the category of morally neutral, fully ripened consciousness in the way the store-consciousness does — "ripened" naming a karmic result that continues, without exception, for as long as a given rebirth lasts, regardless of momentary wholesome or unwholesome activity layered on top of it, a category the ever-shifting six active consciousnesses simply do not qualify for.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the classical enumeration of how many conditions each form of consciousness requires to arise — the eye-consciousness needing nine (its own seed, a visible object, light, spatial distance, the eye faculty itself, the mental-consciousness it depends on, the afflicted mental faculty, the root-consciousness, and attention), the ear-consciousness eight (omitting light), the nose, tongue, and body-consciousnesses seven each (omitting light and distance), the sixth consciousness five, and the seventh and eighth needing only four — a hierarchy he treats as itself proving the point: every one of the six active consciousnesses depends on some combination of present conditions and therefore inevitably breaks off whenever any single condition is missing, whereas the store-consciousness, generated purely by past karma, needs no such present assembly of conditions and so never stops.

He highlights the second and third reasons as reinforcing one another from slightly different angles: the second observes that the six active consciousnesses visibly shift between wholesome and unwholesome states, which cannot both simultaneously sustain a single body, while the third makes the sharper technical point that even when momentarily neutral, the six active consciousnesses still fail to qualify as "ripened" in the strict sense — a ripened result must continue steadily for an entire karmic lifespan regardless of what wholesome or unwholesome activity occurs on top of it, a stability only the store-consciousness itself actually possesses. Having given the first three of five reasons for why only the store-consciousness can serve as the basis of appropriation, he notes, the treatise turns to the fourth reason, addressing a direct objection from the Sautrāntika school of śrāvaka thought.
