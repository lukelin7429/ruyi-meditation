Fascicle 51 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十一 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Opens

Eight Proofs for the Store-Consciousness

Having completed the entire seventeen-stage Basic Section across fascicles 1 through 50, the treatise now opens its second great division: the Chapter of Deciding Discernment, resolving the deeper, previously unexplained questions latent within those same seventeen stages. This fascicle begins with the single most consequential of these questions — why the Buddha never named the store-consciousness in the Āgamas, and how its existence can nonetheless be proven — through eight classical arguments that anchor the entire Yogācāra school's own account of consciousness and rebirth, before moving through comprehensive self-knowledge of the mind, skillful means for its transformation, and a searching refutation of the Sarvāstivādin doctrine that past, present, and future are all equally real.

1. Section: The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Opens — Why the Buddha Kept the Store-Consciousness a Secret

Fascicle 51 opens the treatise's second great division: the Chapter of Deciding Discernment, spanning fascicles 51 through 80, which resolves the deeper, previously unexplained meanings latent within the seventeen stages of the Basic Section just completed — matters the Basic Section either left unaddressed or explained only briefly, now unpacked through careful questions and answers. Where the Basic Section taught what is shared across all three vehicles alongside what the Great Vehicle alone should cultivate, this new division turns specifically to Consciousness-Only's own deepest teachings: why the store-consciousness must be established at all, how saṃsāra's own turning and reversal actually work, the store-consciousness's own essential nature and function and its relationship to active consciousness, how defilement is transformed into purity, and the treatise's own views on the three times, the four conditions, provisionally established phenomena, and the six kinds of skillful understanding. The chapter's own first section, spanning fascicles 51 through 57, resolves five topics regarding the Five Sense-Consciousness-Corresponding Ground and the Mind Ground together: the store-consciousness itself; the distinctions among the various forms of consciousness; the three times and four conditions; provisionally established phenomena; and the six kinds of skillful understanding. The treatise begins with the store-consciousness, posing three questions inherited directly from the Basic Section's own brief mention of it: why did the Buddha not explain, in the Five Sense-Consciousness-Corresponding Ground or the Mind Ground, that the store-consciousness truly exists? By what reasoning can its existence be known? And how should its nature, function, and full characteristics be understood in detail? The treatise answers the first question directly: this establishment is the Blessed One's own most profound, secret record, and for this reason was not taught — since the store-consciousness's own subtle, seemingly continuous activity risks being mistaken by ordinary beings, and even by śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas still carrying attachment to a real self within phenomena, for a genuine, abiding self, an error that would obstruct the very path to liberation or even lead to a fall into the lower destinies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi orients the reader to the sheer scale of what begins here: the Chapter of Deciding Discernment spans thirty full fascicles, nearly two-thirds the length of the entire Basic Section it now discusses, precisely because it must unpack, question by question, everything the seventeen stages left compressed or unstated — above all the store-consciousness itself, the single most consequential and most carefully guarded teaching in the entire treatise. He notes that this chapter's prose grows noticeably more technical and dialectical than the Basic Section's own more narrative style, requiring real patience from the reader, since entire arguments now turn on subtle distinctions a careless reading could easily miss.

He highlights the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own explanation, cited in the commentary, for exactly why the Buddha withheld this teaching from śrāvakas specifically: liberation from saṃsāra requires only severing self-grasping, attainable without ever knowing the store-consciousness's own subtle workings, whereas a bodhisattva vowed to liberate all beings genuinely needs the comprehensive wisdom of every realm, phenomenon, category, and time that only an understanding of the store-consciousness makes possible — the teaching withheld not from any lack of trust, but because śrāvakas simply had no need of it to reach their own goal. Having explained why the store-consciousness went unnamed in the Āgamas, he notes, the treatise turns next to how the Buddha did, in fact, gesture toward it — through a second name, and a celebrated verse.

2. Section: "Deep and Subtle Flows the Store of Every Seed" — The Celebrated Ādāna Verse

The treatise now cites the Blessed One's own words in direct support: the ādāna-consciousness is deep and subtle, every seed within it flowing like a raging torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate and cling to it as a self. The commentary explains that "deep and subtle" distinguishes this teaching from the shallow, superficial doctrine and unrealized practice of ordinary beings, and even from the two lesser vehicles' own real but incomplete depth — the śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha having genuinely severed self-grasping and attained arhatship, yet still retaining attachment to a real self within phenomena, meaning the theory of the ādāna-consciousness surpasses even their own considerable attainment in subtlety. The image of a raging torrent is explained through three senses of "flowing": flowing inward, as every seed streams into the three realms of birth and death; flowing away, as beings drift and regress from one realm of existence into another, falling from the formless realms toward form, or from form toward desire, never settling in one destined place; and flowing out, as accumulated merit and virtuous roots — precept, concentration, and wisdom — seep away just as water leaks from a cracked pond that can no longer hold it. Because the ādāna-consciousness carries on so subtly, its own single characteristic continuing on in an unbroken, self-similar stream, those without proper understanding easily mistake it for a real, permanent, singular self, giving rise to both the view clinging to a personal self and the view clinging to a self within phenomena — and it is precisely to prevent this error that the Buddha withheld its deeper meaning from ordinary beings and from the two lesser vehicles alike.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this verse, drawn from the Sūtra Explaining the Profound Secrets' own Chapter on the Characteristics of Mind, Manas, and Consciousness, as among the most quoted four lines in the entire history of Yogācāra thought — cited repeatedly across the Compendium of the Great Vehicle and countless later commentaries precisely because it compresses the entire justification for the store-consciousness's own carefully guarded secrecy into a single, memorable stanza.

He highlights the threefold gloss on "flowing" as worth holding in mind throughout everything that follows in this chapter: flowing inward names how seeds continually pour into the three realms, flowing away names the instability of rebirth's own destination, and flowing out names how easily accumulated virtue can leak away unless carefully protected — three distinct dangers, all traceable back to the same restless, continuous activity of the seeds the ādāna-consciousness carries. He also draws out the etymology behind the name itself, already partly explained in the commentary's own gloss on "ādāna" as carrying three distinct functions — holding seeds so they are never lost, holding the physical sense faculties so the body retains sensation, and holding onto rebirth itself at the very moment of conception — an analysis the Establishment of Consciousness-Only Treatise develops further still. Having cited this celebrated verse confirming why the Buddha kept this teaching so carefully guarded, he notes, the treatise now turns to the second of the three original questions: by what reasoning can the store-consciousness's own existence actually be proven, introduced through a summary verse naming eight distinct arguments.

3. Section: Eight Arguments Named in Verse — Why Ordinary Consciousness Alone Cannot Explain a Living Body

The treatise now turns to proving that the store-consciousness genuinely exists, first presenting a summary verse: appropriation, the very first arising, clarity of cognition, having the nature of seeds, differences in karmic function, differences in bodily sensation, the attainment of mindless concentration, and consciousness at the moment of death — without the store-consciousness, none of these eight would make any sense at all. The treatise then explains each term in prose: through eight distinct characteristics, the store-consciousness is definitively proven to exist — for apart from the store-consciousness, the basis of appropriation makes no sense, the very first arising of consciousness makes no sense, the presence of clarity in cognition makes no sense, the nature of holding seeds makes no sense, differences in karmic function make no sense, differences in bodily sensation make no sense, abiding in mindless concentration makes no sense, and consciousness at the moment of death makes no sense. The treatise explains that Consciousness-Only thinkers hold that only by establishing the store-consciousness can the full range of life's own phenomena be adequately explained, whereas śrāvaka thinkers believe the six active forms of consciousness alone suffice to account for everything — the entire discussion that follows proceeds by comparing the six active consciousnesses against the store-consciousness on precisely these eight points. In brief: the six active consciousnesses require specific conditions to arise at all and are therefore intermittent, and they shift constantly among wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral qualities, so they cannot appropriate and sustain the physical sense faculties, nor can they retain the seeds of past action; only the store-consciousness, itself a uniformly neutral, fully ripened form of consciousness continuing without interruption throughout an entire lifetime, can appropriate the physical body, retain its seeds, sustain the body's own subtle ease even during mindless meditative attainments, and gradually withdraw from the body as death approaches, causing it to cool. Of the eight reasons, the treatise notes, the first arising, clarity of cognition, and karmic function serve chiefly to refute a further śrāvaka objection — that multiple forms of consciousness cannot possibly arise simultaneously — since if this objection stood, the store-consciousness could never coexist with the six active consciousnesses at all, making these three reasons an indirect line of proof supporting the other five.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this eightfold framework as the argumentative backbone of the entire store-consciousness discussion, and recommends keeping the whole list in view before descending into each individual proof: the six active consciousnesses, dependent on conditions and shifting constantly between wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral states, simply cannot do the jobs a living body plainly requires — sustaining the body moment to moment, retaining the seeds of past karma, keeping the body alive even in mindless meditative states, and gradually withdrawing at death — jobs only a continuous, uniformly neutral, fully ripened consciousness could ever perform.

He highlights the three "indirect" proofs — first arising, clarity of cognition, and karmic function — as answering not the main question directly but a prior objection that would otherwise block the whole argument: if forms of consciousness genuinely cannot coexist, as some śrāvaka thinkers held, then no store-consciousness could ever operate alongside the six active consciousnesses in the first place, making the case for simultaneous multiple consciousnesses itself a necessary preliminary battle before the five more direct proofs can even get underway. Having named all eight reasons, he notes, the treatise now works through each one individually, beginning with the first: the basis of appropriation.

4. Section: The First Proof Begins — Three Reasons Why Only the Store-Consciousness Can Sustain a Living Body

The treatise now works through the first of the eight proofs, appropriation, itself explained through five distinct reasons. Asked why appropriation makes no sense apart from the store-consciousness, the treatise answers: for five reasons. Asked what these five are, the treatise explains the first: the store-consciousness has past karmic action itself as its cause, while the active consciousnesses — the eye-consciousness and the rest — have the present, momentary coming together of conditions as their cause; as it is said, consciousness arises through the coming together of a sense faculty, an object, and attention, and so on at length. This means the store-consciousness, generated by karma performed in a past life, continues uninterrupted throughout an entire lifetime, sustaining the physical sense faculties without a single gap — sustaining a body destined by past karma to live fifty years, for instance, for the entirety of those fifty years — whereas the eye-consciousness and the other active forms of consciousness require present conditions to arise at all, and cease the moment any single required condition is missing, making them incapable of sustaining the physical body on their own. The second reason: the six active consciousnesses can be observed possessing wholesome, unwholesome, and other differing moral natures, shifting constantly between them, and are therefore unfit to serve as the basis of appropriation, since wholesome and unwholesome states are mutually exclusive and cannot embrace one another the way a genuinely neutral consciousness can. The third reason: the six active consciousnesses cannot be found to belong to the category of morally neutral, fully ripened consciousness in the way the store-consciousness does — "ripened" naming a karmic result that continues, without exception, for as long as a given rebirth lasts, regardless of momentary wholesome or unwholesome activity layered on top of it, a category the ever-shifting six active consciousnesses simply do not qualify for.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the classical enumeration of how many conditions each form of consciousness requires to arise — the eye-consciousness needing nine (its own seed, a visible object, light, spatial distance, the eye faculty itself, the mental-consciousness it depends on, the afflicted mental faculty, the root-consciousness, and attention), the ear-consciousness eight (omitting light), the nose, tongue, and body-consciousnesses seven each (omitting light and distance), the sixth consciousness five, and the seventh and eighth needing only four — a hierarchy he treats as itself proving the point: every one of the six active consciousnesses depends on some combination of present conditions and therefore inevitably breaks off whenever any single condition is missing, whereas the store-consciousness, generated purely by past karma, needs no such present assembly of conditions and so never stops.

He highlights the second and third reasons as reinforcing one another from slightly different angles: the second observes that the six active consciousnesses visibly shift between wholesome and unwholesome states, which cannot both simultaneously sustain a single body, while the third makes the sharper technical point that even when momentarily neutral, the six active consciousnesses still fail to qualify as "ripened" in the strict sense — a ripened result must continue steadily for an entire karmic lifespan regardless of what wholesome or unwholesome activity occurs on top of it, a stability only the store-consciousness itself actually possesses. Having given the first three of five reasons for why only the store-consciousness can serve as the basis of appropriation, he notes, the treatise turns to the fourth reason, addressing a direct objection from the Sautrāntika school of śrāvaka thought.

5. Section: Two Final Reasons — Why an Eye-Consciousness Cannot Sustain an Ear, and the First Proof Closes

The treatise now completes the first proof with two further reasons. Regarding a second objection from śrāvaka thinkers: even granting that only one of the six consciousnesses need be active at any moment to sustain appropriation, this too fails, since consciousness itself is localized — an eye-consciousness arising in dependence on the eye faculty has no presence wherever the ear faculty happens to be, and so cannot possibly appropriate that same ear; if the eye-consciousness alone were active while no ear-consciousness had arisen, claiming that the eye-consciousness thereby appropriates the ear makes no sense at all. This is the fourth reason, showing that only the store-consciousness, pervading the entire body without exception, can appropriate every sense faculty at once, whereas each active consciousness, tied to its own single faculty, could never appropriate faculties beyond its own. The fifth reason then addresses a further difficulty: if the six active consciousnesses could serve as the basis of appropriation, the body they sustain would suffer the fault of being repeatedly, intermittently appropriated and unappropriated by turns — since the eye-consciousness itself arises at one moment and fails to arise at another, and likewise for every other active consciousness, none of them sustaining continuous activity. Only the store-consciousness, continuing without a single interruption from the moment of conception until the moment of death, never once departing from the body in between, can avoid this fault and genuinely serve as the basis of appropriation. The treatise then draws together all five reasons into a single conclusion: because the store-consciousness has past karma as its cause while the six active consciousnesses depend on present conditions; because the six active consciousnesses display wholesome, unwholesome, and other differing natures; because the category of morally neutral, fully ripened consciousness cannot be found among them; because each active consciousness arises in dependence on its own separate faculty rather than pervading the whole body; and because appropriation through the six active consciousnesses would suffer the fault of repeated interruption — for all five reasons together, appropriation apart from the store-consciousness simply does not make sense.

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights Vasubandhu's own vivid summary of these same five reasons in the Establishment of Consciousness-Only Treatise: only the ripened mind — brought about by past karma, neither wholesome nor defiled, uniform in kind, pervading the entire body, and continuously appropriating the physical sense faculties without interruption — can accomplish what the eye-consciousness and other active consciousnesses plainly cannot. With the first of the eight proofs now fully established through all five reasons, he notes, the treatise turns to the second proof: the very first arising of consciousness — not, as it happens, a proof about conception at all, but rather a rebuttal of the śrāvaka claim that only one form of consciousness can ever be active at a time, a claim which, if true, would rule out the store-consciousness from ever coexisting alongside the six active consciousnesses in the first place.

6. Section: Watching, Listening, Tasting, and Smelling All at Once — Proving That Consciousnesses Truly Co-Arise

The treatise now turns to the second proof, the very first arising, addressing directly the śrāvaka view that consciousness arises strictly one at a time, in sequence, never two at once — a view carrying two faults at once: denying the store-consciousness altogether, and requiring some single, first consciousness to arise before any other. Asked why, apart from the store-consciousness, such a "very first arising" fails to make sense, the treatise presents the śrāvaka objection directly: if the store-consciousness truly existed, two forms of consciousness would have to arise simultaneously. The response: such a person raises a false objection where no actual fault exists, since it is entirely possible for two consciousnesses, or indeed several, to arise together whenever the corresponding conditions are all present. The treatise explains why this must be so: suppose a single person simultaneously wishes to see, to hear, and so on up through wishing to cognize — if, in such a case, only one consciousness could be said to arise first, this makes no sense at all, since attention itself is undivided in that same moment, and the corresponding faculties and objects are equally present without division; by what possible reasoning could the different consciousnesses fail to operate together? A single person walking through a garden may simultaneously look at the scenery, listen to music through headphones, taste a bite of food, smell a nearby flower's fragrance, and feel the pleasant lightness of a spring breeze on the skin — all these acts of consciousness plainly occurring together rather than in some strict, laborious sequence of one completing before the next begins.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the everyday example of walking through a garden while simultaneously seeing, hearing, tasting, smelling, and feeling as one of the treatise's own most immediately relatable arguments — anyone who has ever eaten a snack while listening to music and enjoying a view already has direct, personal evidence that consciousness does not politely wait its turn, faculty by faculty, but genuinely operates in parallel whenever the necessary conditions for each are simultaneously present.

He highlights the commentary's own careful clarification that this passage is not actually arguing for a "first arising" in the sense of conception itself, nor contradicting the twelve links of dependent origination's own sequential unfolding of consciousness conditioning name-and-form — rather, it exists purely to rebut the specific objection that consciousnesses cannot co-arise, since if that objection held, the store-consciousness could never operate simultaneously with the six active consciousnesses at all, making this entire argument an indirect but necessary defense clearing the way for the store-consciousness's own genuine coexistence with ordinary conscious experience. Having refuted the sequential-arising objection through the reality of simultaneous sense experience, he notes, the treatise turns to the third proof: how the mental consciousness's own clarity, when accompanying the five senses, offers yet further confirmation that multiple consciousnesses genuinely operate together.

7. Section: Why Recalling the Past Feels Hazy But Perceiving the Present Feels Sharp — The Third Proof Begins

The treatise now turns to the third proof: clarity of cognition. Asked why, if consciousnesses did not co-arise, the mental consciousness accompanying the five sense-consciousnesses would fail to possess its own characteristic clarity, the treatise answers by pointing to an observable fact: sometimes, when recollecting some past object once experienced, the mental consciousness's own activity in that moment is unclear and hazy; yet when the mental consciousness engages a genuinely present object, no such haziness is ever found — its activity is instead sharp and distinct. This contrast itself demonstrates that consciousnesses must co-arise: were the mental consciousness always operating entirely on its own, independent of the five senses, there would be no reason for it to be clear in one case and hazy in the other, since it would be the very same solitary consciousness in both instances; the only adequate explanation is that, when engaging a present object, the mental consciousness operates jointly alongside the corresponding sense-consciousness, and this joint operation is precisely what produces its characteristic clarity — a clarity entirely absent when the mental consciousness recalls a past object alone, unaccompanied by any sense-consciousness at all, since the object is no longer actually present to be jointly cognized. 21. Section: Waves on a Torrent, Reflections in a Mirror — Five Similes for Consciousnesses That Coexist Without Truly Corresponding

28. Section: "One Mass, One Heap, One Mere Collection" — The Contemplation That Finally Achieves Transformation of Basis

32. Section: Four Ways of Having or Lacking the Store-Consciousness — The Entire Discussion's Elegant Capstone

The treatise now closes its account of the store-consciousness with a final clarification, then poses one last, elegantly structured question. Regarding why śrāvaka teaching describes mind, manas, and consciousness differently: this reflects nothing more than skillful adaptation to the particular beings being taught, spoken for those of a more childlike wisdom as a provisional expedient specifically to make entry onto the path easier for them — which is why the Āgamas never mention the store-consciousness, manas, or the perceiving and perceived aspects of consciousness at all. The treatise then poses a final, four-cornered question completing this entire investigation: if one possesses the store-consciousness, does one also necessarily possess the active consciousnesses? And conversely, if one possesses the active consciousnesses, does one also necessarily possess the store-consciousness? The answer unfolds across four distinct cases. First, one may possess the store-consciousness without the six active consciousnesses: this occurs during dreamless sleep, fainting, the attainment without perception, the attainment of cessation, and rebirth into the heaven of no perception — five states where the six active consciousnesses cease entirely while the store-consciousness, together with manas, continues uninterrupted. Second, one may possess the active consciousnesses without the store-consciousness: this describes arhats, pratyekabuddhas, non-retrogressing bodhisattvas, and buddhas themselves, while abiding in a state with active mental consciousness, since these beings have already achieved transformation of basis and so no longer possess a genuinely defiled store-consciousness at all. Third, one may possess both together: this describes every other being, ordinary or still in training, while abiding in a state with active mental consciousness. And fourth, one may possess neither: this describes arhats, pratyekabuddhas, non-retrogressing bodhisattvas, and buddhas themselves while abiding in the attainment of cessation, or upon entering the realm of nirvāṇa without residue remaining, where even manas itself no longer operates in its ordinary, self-grasping mode.

33. Section: A New Topic Opens — Why Fire Is Named for Its Fuel, and Consciousness for Its Faculty and Object

45. Section: Refuting All Six — None of the Sarvāstivādin's Own Answers Can Explain How the Future Becomes Present

The treatise refutes all six positions in turn, first rejecting them collectively as unreasonable, then individually. Against mere transformation: what lacks any location or place whatsoever cannot coherently be said to move from one place to another, since the future — on the opponent's own admission — has no place where it could be observed. Against death-and-arising: what has not yet arisen at all cannot coherently be said to die, since dying presupposes having first existed in some observable way. Against conditioned arising: if the present arises by taking the future as a mere condition, then the present is a phenomenon distinct from that future, meaning the present is not the future's own arising at all — so the future can no longer be called "that same future coming to be." Against functional arising: since all phenomena, in ultimate truth, are free of any functional efficacy grounded in their own fixed characteristic, and functional efficacy is only ever conventionally established relative to a characteristic — if future and present are truly, equally real in their characteristic as claimed, there is no coherent way to say only the present possesses function; and further, saying function arises now when it did not previously exist directly means it did not exist before and now does, which is precisely the treatise's own position, not the opponent's. Against completed arising: this directly contradicts the Buddha's own teaching that all conditioned phenomena are impermanent, never fixed or eternal — for if function is impermanent while characteristic remains permanently, equally real across past and future, then the underlying nature of all conditioned things would have to be permanent after all, and the distinction between complete and incomplete characteristics would collapse into a single, undifferentiated characteristic. And against distinct-characteristic arising: if a distinct portion is itself claimed to be truly existent, then that distinct portion should likewise require its own further distinct portion without end; and if that distinct portion is said to arise only through originally not existing and now existing, this simply concedes the treatise's own thesis rather than establishing the opponent's — moreover, apart from form, feeling, and the other aggregates actually appearing before the mind, no further "future portion" can ever be found at all, and without a genuinely existent causal portion in the future, there remains no way for a resultant portion to arise in the present.

47. Section: Why Illusory Things Never Arise, Why Phenomena Don't All Arise at Once, and the Four Conditions Introduced

The treatise completes its account of where seeds reside prior to this treatise's own fuller doctrine of the store-consciousness: if consciousness were not followed by seeds of form, then an ordinary being reborn in the formless realm, upon exhausting that life's span and karma, could never again give rise to a body upon returning to the realm of desire or form, since no seed of form would remain — yet such a body definitely does arise again, proving that seeds of form must indeed reside latent within consciousness itself, consciousness serving as their condition for form to arise anew. The treatise then turns from analyzing by phenomena to analyzing by persons — ordinary beings and noble ones — distinguishing how each may either merely suppress or permanently destroy the seeds of affliction. 51. Section: How Noble Ones Permanently Destroy the Seed Itself — The Seven Mental Applications of the Transmundane Path

The treatise turns to noble disciples: through the transmundane path, they become free of the desire of the desire realm, up through complete freedom from the desire of all three realms — at which point all afflictive seeds of the three realms are permanently destroyed outright. The lecture explains why: the Śrāvaka Stage teaches that the transmundane path takes the four noble truths as its object and proceeds through seven mental applications. The first, application through apprehending the character, means clearly understanding the character of suffering, origination, cessation, and the path, the object to be contemplated appearing as clearly in the mind as the lines of one's own palm. The second, application through resolute understanding, means that having grasped the character, continued proper attention produces a firm, forceful conviction, planted deeply enough that it manifests spontaneously whenever an object is encountered. The third, application of renunciation, means that from this well-cultivated, well-practiced, well-repeated resolute understanding as condition, the path that first eradicates affliction arises, overcoming the strongest grade of affliction. The fourth, application of gathered joy, means the practitioner, having subdued this affliction, delights in continuing to practice, eradicating the middling grade. The fifth, application of observation, means examining whether affliction has genuinely been eradicated by deliberately generating within the mind an appearance that would accord with affliction, testing whether it truly no longer arises, and by this eradicating the weakest grade. The sixth, application culminating in effort, means continuing this observation until reaching the state just prior to full attainment, corresponding to the concentration on the very verge of the first meditative absorption. And the seventh, application culminating in the fruit of effort, means the fruit itself is actually attained — the first fruit of stream-entry, or the first meditative absorption — the sixth application being the uninterrupted path and the seventh the path of liberation, actual release from affliction. The lecture emphasizes that what makes the transmundane path uniquely capable of permanently destroying affliction, unlike the mundane path's mere weariness of the coarser realm below in favor of the subtler realm above, is that it strikes directly at the root of self-view and the view of what belongs to self, realizing all phenomena as ultimately empty of anything to cling to at all — a realization the mundane path, however deep its concentration, never reaches.

52. Section: The Sprouting-Seed Simile, the Learner Who Never Falls Back, and the Arhat's Final Extinguishing of Even Wholesome Seeds

The treatise explains why a noble disciple's eradication of affliction is permanent: having achieved this in the present life, they are no longer capable of falling back from freedom from desire so as to let the afflictions of a lower stage manifest again, and even if reborn in a higher realm, they are no longer capable of falling from there back down to a lower realm to be reborn. The lecture illustrates the difference between suppression and permanent destruction with a simile: grain or wheat seeds placed in an empty field or a dry container will not sprout for the time being, yet remain genuine seeds, still capable of sprouting the moment water and the right conditions arrive — this is suppression; but seeds consumed by fire can never again become seeds at all, no matter what conditions later arise — this is permanent destruction, and the destruction of afflictive seeds within one's own store-consciousness proceeds by exactly the same principle. For the learner — those between stream-entry and the third fruit still undergoing further training — once freedom from desire in some realm has been genuinely attained through the transmundane path, they never again fall back to that realm's afflictions, and if reborn in one of the four meditative absorptions, they never again return to a lower realm, since noble disciples do not take rebirth in the formless realm at all, where concentration runs deep but wisdom remains shallow, insufficient for cutting affliction at its root. For the arhat beyond further training, the treatise adds something further still: as they approach entry into the realm of nirvāṇa without residue remaining, not only their afflictive seeds but even their remaining wholesome and neutral seeds are rendered incapable of yielding fruit — the store-consciousness itself, the very support on which such seeds depended, being relinquished at that final transition, so that these seeds, though not literally destroyed, lose the condition without which they could never ripen again, no longer able to produce either a future retributive fruit or any further outflow of their own kind — this fourth kind of loss, distinct from the other three, is named the suppression that eliminates the very accomplice on which affliction's own arising depended.

53. Section: Which Seeds Follow Ordinary Beings, Learners, and Arhats — And the Historic Shift from Scattered Seeds to the Single Store-Consciousness

The treatise completes its account of which seeds follow which kind of person: an ordinary being's mind, whether wholesome, defiled, or neutral, is followed at all times by the seeds of all three moral natures, none yet severed; a learner — one still progressing from stream-entry toward the fourth fruit, not yet entirely free of the three realms' bonds — is followed, whenever a mundane wholesome, transmundane, defiled, or neutral mind arises, by whatever afflictive seeds still remain to be severed by the path of cultivation, together with the remaining wholesome and neutral seeds not yet severed; and an arhat beyond further training, having permanently severed all afflictive seeds, is followed in every mental arising — wholesome, transmundane, or neutral — solely by wholesome and neutral seeds, continuing in unbroken succession. Having completed this analysis of characteristics, the treatise turns to establishing the causal condition's own doctrinal placement: the entire preceding account of seeds residing in the sense faculties and consciousness should be understood as belonging to the teaching given before the store-consciousness had yet been established — that is, to the Śrāvaka vehicle's own Sautrāntika position. Once the store-consciousness has been established, however, one should know that, in brief, the seeds of all phenomena depend entirely upon the store-consciousness alone — precisely why the Compendium of Mahāyāna opens with the store-consciousness as "the basis of all that is known," every knowable phenomenon ultimately resting there rather than scattered across the sense faculties or the six active consciousnesses. Whatever wholesome, defiled, or neutral seeds remain unsevered, or need no severing at all, continue to follow the store-consciousness accordingly — though the store-consciousness of an ordinary being, a learner, and an arhat each contain a different configuration of seeds, since the first holds the full complement of all three realms' defiled seeds together with wholesome and neutral seeds, the learner has severed the conceptually-generated afflictions and, if a second-fruit or third-fruit practitioner, further grades of the desire realm's afflictions cultivated away, while the arhat retains only wholesome and neutral seeds.

54. Section: Two Kinds of "Falling Back" — Why an Arhat's Meditation May Retreat While the Noble Path Itself Never Does

The treatise raises a scriptural objection: the Blessed One himself said that an arhat may fall back from any one of the four levels of exalted mental dwelling in present bliss — yet if all their defiled seeds have truly been permanently destroyed, how could they possibly give rise to a lower stage's affliction again, and if they cannot, in what sense do they fall back at all? The treatise answers that falling back is of two kinds: falling back through the seed itself being merely suppressed rather than destroyed, and falling back merely from a state of meditative dwelling. The first belongs only to ordinary beings, whose seeds were never actually severed to begin with; the second belongs to both noble ones and ordinary beings alike, since even an arhat's meditative concentration can regress if, having eradicated affliction through the transmundane path, they become occupied with worldly affairs and fail to sustain proper attention, so that their previous ease of entering meditative bliss no longer arises as readily as before — yet the afflictions of the lower stage, already severed, never manifest again; this is falling back only from a state of dwelling, never a falling back through the seed itself. When the mundane path alone has suppressed affliction and it later reappears, both kinds of falling back occur together, since the affliction was never actually severed. The treatise establishes its conclusion by showing the alternative's absurdity: if an arhat's defiled seeds were not truly, permanently destroyed, how could such a person be called one whose mind is well liberated, whose outflows are forever exhausted? Since they are so called, and since anyone whose defiled seeds are truly destroyed could not even give rise to improper attention, let alone actual affliction, one must conclude that a person liberated through the transmundane path never truly falls back at all — only their meditative dwelling may.

55. Section: Time-Liberated and Unshakeable Arhats, the Monkey Who Became Mí Shèng, and the Multi-Headed Fish of Careless Speech

The lecture closes this extensive treatment of causal condition with an overview of the different kinds of arhats: time-liberated arhats require favorable circumstances — soft, adequate clothing, food, and bedding, a teacher able to instruct according to their own capacity, and companions who are agreeable and easy to live with — before they can sever affliction, and are divided into five further kinds according to how easily their meditative dwelling regresses, ranging from those prone to retreat, through those who must strive by reflection, guard their attainment vigilantly, or simply abide within it without further activity, to those who diligently sharpen a dull faculty into a sharp one; unshakeable arhats, by contrast, are unaffected by any external circumstance whatsoever, capable of attaining liberation from hearing even a single phrase from anyone at all, their meditative dwelling as secure as their liberation itself. The lecture notes that even an arhat retains habitual residual tendencies that do not impede their own liberation from affliction, yet do obstruct the further attainment of buddhahood, since it is precisely this residue that must also be eliminated on a bodhisattva's path to omniscience. The lecture then reviews causal condition's own historical development one final time: before the store-consciousness was established, the Sautrāntika school first proposed that seeds of the same kind are retained within phenomena of the same kind, form retaining its own seeds and mind its own — until challenged by the case of beings in the formless realm, who have no form at all, and beings in the two mindless meditative attainments, who have no consciousness at all — prompting a second proposal that form and mind seeds may be mutually retained across each other, which nevertheless still could not account for both absences occurring together, ultimately requiring the Mahāyāna's own doctrine of the store-consciousness as the sole, unbroken repository for every seed regardless of circumstance. The lecture closes with two illustrative stories: a monkey who joyfully offered the Buddha a bowl filled with wild honey was, upon dying, reborn as the son of a childless elder — the household's bowls found miraculously filled with honey at his birth, earning him the name Mí Shèng, "Honey Supreme" — and this child, ordained as promised, swiftly attained arhatship, the Buddha explaining that his rapid attainment stemmed directly from that offering, while his prior birth as a monkey stemmed from a single careless jest, in an even earlier life as a monk, mocking a fellow monk's climbing posture as resembling a monkey's; and the Repentance of Emperor Liang tells of merchants who encountered a many-headed fish at sea, its heads a punishment for a former life spent hurling insults comparing people to pigs and dogs — both stories illustrating that every act of body, speech, or mind leaves an ineffaceable trace, ripening in its own time, and that harsh or careless speech is never harmless, however lightly intended.
