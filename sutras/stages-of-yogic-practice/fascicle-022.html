Fascicle 22 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十二 · The Third Ground Opens

The Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation Begins

Fascicle 22 opens exactly where fascicle 21 left off: the Ground of Entry (趣入地) formally closes, and the third and largest of the three grounds comprising the hearer-disciple's first yogic process — the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation (出離地) — begins. This ground traces the two paths of detachment, mundane and supramundane, and the fourteen kinds of provisions a practitioner needs to walk either one, spanning fascicles 22 through 25.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 22 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation Opens

The treatise marks the transition plainly: the Ground of Entry has now been fully explained, and the third ground of the first yogic process begins. It opens with its own summary verse, naming the two paths of detachment — mundane and supramundane — together with the provisions both require.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之一……如是已說趣入地。云何出離地？……嗢柁南曰：若世間離欲　如是出世間　及此二資糧　是名出離地……謂若由世間道而趣離欲，若由出世道而趣離欲，若此二道所有資糧，總略為一，名出離地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the third chapter, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, part one. …Thus the Ground of Entry has already been explained. What is the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation? …The summary verse says: mundane detachment, and likewise supramundane detachment, together with the provisions for both — this is called the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation. …Whether one sets out toward detachment by the mundane path, or sets out toward detachment by the supramundane path, or whether it is the provisions belonging to both paths — all of this together, in brief, is called the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi orients the reader to this ground's scope before the root text unpacks it. Of the four "yogic processes" structuring the entire Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, this is still the first — spanning fascicles 21 through 25 — and having covered spiritual lineage and entry in fascicle 21, this third ground, setting out toward liberation, is by far the largest of the three, running from here through fascicle 25. It divides into four parts: the provisions for detachment (this fascicle's focus, continuing through fascicle 25), a classification of practitioner types, the actual sequence a teacher uses to guide a student's practice, and the detailed marks of practice itself. This particular fascicle picks up the fourth of fourteen kinds of provisions already being tracked since fascicle 21 — moral discipline, the root of the three trainings — while the remaining ten will follow in fascicles 23 through 25. The ground's own opening verse names its overall shape: detachment achieved by mundane means (through concentration alone, suppressing affliction without uprooting it — "like a stone pressing down grass," in the treatise's own image, since the affliction returns the moment the stone is lifted) and detachment achieved by supramundane means (through concentration paired with insight into the four noble truths, severing affliction at the root), together with everything both paths require to be walked at all.

2. Mundane Detachment: Coarse Below, Peaceful Above, Level by Level

The treatise details mundane detachment first: a practitioner ascends through the nine levels of the threefold world by repeatedly contrasting each level's faults against the relative peace of the one above it, using six specific contemplations to loosen attachment to wherever one currently stands.

Chinese Source

云何名為由世間道而趣離欲？謂如有一，於下欲界觀為麤相，於初靜慮離生喜樂若定、若生觀為靜相。彼由多住如是觀時，便於欲界而得離欲，亦能證入最初靜慮。如是復於初靜慮上，漸次如應，一切下地觀為麤相，一切上地觀為靜相。彼由多住如是觀時，便於乃至無所有處而得離欲，亦能證入乃至非想非非想處。如是名為由世間道而趣離欲。除此更無若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called setting out toward detachment by the mundane path? It is like one who observes the lower desire realm as coarse, and observes the joy and ease born of seclusion in the first absorption — whether attained through concentration or through rebirth there — as peaceful. Through repeatedly dwelling in this contemplation, one thereby attains detachment from the desire realm, and is able to enter the first absorption. In just this way, ascending progressively from the first absorption onward, one observes every lower level as coarse, and every higher level as peaceful. Through repeatedly dwelling in this contemplation, one thereby attains detachment all the way up to the sphere of nothingness, and is able to enter even the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. This is called setting out toward detachment by the mundane path. Beyond this there are no more, and none is superfluous.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "coarse" and "peaceful" as fundamentally relative terms: whichever level has more faults is called coarse, whichever has fewer is called peaceful, and every level in the threefold world's nine stages (the desire realm as one, the four form-realm absorptions, and the four formless spheres) is coarse relative to the one above it and peaceful relative to the one below — the desire realm coarse against the first absorption, the first absorption in turn coarse against the second, and so on all the way up to the very peak of existence. Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the *Śamatha-vipaśyanā* meditation manual tradition to spell out the six specific contemplations a practitioner cycles through at each transition, using the move from the desire realm to the first absorption as the model: contemplating the desire realm's causal coarseness (the five sense-objects breed every kind of evil) and its resultant coarseness (the body itself, made of thirty-six kinds of impure substance); its causal and resultant suffering (mental states bound to craving that cannot escape themselves, and a body subject to hunger, cold, sickness, and violence); its causal and resultant obstruction (affliction obscuring one's true nature, and a body's physical constraints preventing freedom); and then, in the opposite direction, delighting in the first absorption's causal and resultant excellence (concentration's superior joy, surpassing desire's inferior pleasure), its causal and resultant subtlety (a still, undistracted mind, and a luminous, unobstructed absorption-body, like a reflection in a mirror), and its causal and resultant liberation (freedom from the hindrances, and a body endowed with the five mundane superknowledges, moving without obstruction). Repeating this six-part contemplation — three reasons to feel revulsion toward what lies below, three reasons to feel joy toward what lies above — carries a practitioner level by level all the way from the coarsest desire realm to the most rarefied peak of existence, though, Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to note, this entire ascent only ever suppresses affliction through concentration's own force; it can never uproot it, since only supramundane insight into the four noble truths, covered next, can do that.

3. Supramundane Detachment: Seeds Stored Away Versus Seeds Burnt by Fire

Where mundane detachment only ever suppresses affliction, the supramundane path — direct realization of the four noble truths, carried from stream-entry through arhatship — genuinely uproots it. The treatise closes this contrast with one of its most memorable images: a suppressed affliction is a seed merely kept in dry storage, still capable of sprouting the moment conditions allow; a truly severed affliction is a seed already burnt to ash.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，親近善士，於聖法中已成聰慧，於聖法中已得調順。於苦聖諦如實知苦，於集聖諦如實知集，於滅聖諦如實知滅，於道聖諦如實知道。……既得成就有學智見，從此已後漸修聖道，遍於三界見修所斷一切法中，自能離繫、自得解脫。如是便能超過三界。如是名為由出世道而趣離欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like one who, drawing near a good teacher, becomes wise within the noble Dharma, and becomes well-tamed within the noble Dharma — knowing suffering, as it truly is, as the noble truth of suffering; knowing its origin, as it truly is, as the noble truth of origin; knowing its cessation, as it truly is, as the noble truth of cessation; and knowing the path, as it truly is, as the noble truth of the path. …Having thus attained a trainee's knowledge and vision, one thereafter gradually cultivates the noble path, and, throughout the three realms, with regard to everything to be abandoned by seeing and by cultivation, one is oneself able to become free of its bonds, oneself attaining liberation. In this way, one is able to transcend the three realms entirely. This is called setting out toward detachment by the supramundane path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces this path from its starting point — genuine direct realization of the four noble truths, marking stream-entry, "a trainee's knowledge and vision" — through continued cultivation of the noble eightfold path across every remaining affliction, whether cut by the initial seeing of truth or by the ongoing work of cultivation, until every one is severed and the entire threefold world is transcended: arhatship. The decisive contrast with mundane detachment, he stresses, is permanence. A practitioner who has only suppressed affliction through concentration alone can, at best, reach the peak of existence — but never beyond it — and even that suppression is temporary, since the underlying seeds remain fully intact, exactly like grain stored in a dry container: dormant for now, but still capable of sprouting the moment moisture returns. He cites the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī*'s own explanation: a noble one who has genuinely severed desire-realm and eventually all threefold-world craving through the supramundane path can never again regress into the coarser afflictions of a lower level, nor, having been reborn in the form realm through that path, ever fall back into the desire realm — precisely because the affliction-seeds themselves have been destroyed, not merely left dormant. The image the treatise reaches for is exact: mundane suppression is grain kept safely in storage, its capacity to sprout undiminished; supramundane severance is grain already consumed by fire, its capacity to sprout gone for good. This is why, Master Cháng Bǎi concludes, only the supramundane path can be called genuine, final liberation.

4. Milestone: The Fourteen Provisions Open — Moral Discipline, and Its Own Rich Sixfold Structure

With both paths of detachment named, the treatise turns to what both actually require: fourteen kinds of provisions, the first three of which (self-perfection, perfection by others, and a genuine wish for the good) were already covered in fascicle 21. This fascicle opens with the fourth — moral discipline — which the treatise unpacks through its own elaborate five-part outline before beginning with the first of six components: abiding in complete discipline.

Chinese Source

云何名為二道資糧？嗢柁南曰：自他圓滿善法欲　戒根律儀食知量　悎寤正知住善友　聞思無障捨莊嚴……當知此中，若自圓滿、若他圓滿、若善法欲，此三如前修集種子諸劣緣中，已辯其相。……云何戒律儀？嗢柁南曰：戒律儀當知　辯三虧滿十　六異門三淨　勝功德十種……戒律儀者，謂如有一，安住具戒，廣說乃至受學學處。云何名為安住具戒？謂於所受學所有學處，不虧身業，不虧語業，無闕，無穿。如是名為安住具戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are the provisions for both paths? The summary verse says: self-perfection, perfection by others, a wish for the good; moral discipline, restraint of the faculties, moderation in eating; wakefulness, clear awareness, abiding with good companions; hearing and reflecting on the Dharma, freedom from obstruction, and the adornments of renunciation. …It should be understood that, among these, self-perfection, perfection by others, and a wish for the good have already had their marks explained above, among the lesser conditions for cultivating the seed [of lineage]. …What is moral discipline in its proper restraint? The summary verse says: moral discipline should be understood through distinguishing three, ten marks of impairment and ten of fulfillment, six alternate names, three purities, and ten excellent virtues. …Moral discipline in its proper restraint is that one abides in complete discipline — and so on at length, down to training in every point of training. What is called abiding in complete discipline? It is that, regarding all the points of training one has undertaken, one's bodily conduct is not impaired, one's verbal conduct is not impaired, nothing is lacking, and nothing is breached. This is called abiding in complete discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi confirms the fourteen provisions named here match exactly the twelve "lesser conditions" already covered in fascicle 21 under lineage's establishment, plus the two named "superior conditions" folded in as their own separate entries — the same territory, reorganized slightly for this ground's own purposes. Since self-perfection, perfection by others, and the wish for the good were already detailed at length in fascicle 21, the treatise moves straight to the fourth provision, moral discipline, which turns out to have a rich structure of its own: distinguishing three (bodily, verbal, and mental conduct, alongside three further three-part breakdowns of what discipline actually consists in); ten marks each of impairment and of fulfillment; six alternate names discipline goes by depending on which of its qualities is being emphasized; three purities (examining bodily, verbal, and mental conduct across past, present, and future); and ten excellent virtues that flow from discipline genuinely kept pure — a cascade Master Cháng Bǎi previews running from freedom-from-regret through joy, ease, concentration, genuine insight, revulsion, dispassion, and finally liberation itself, each condition giving rise to the next.

The first of discipline's six components, abiding in complete discipline, means sustained, unbroken conformity in both bodily and verbal conduct to every precept received — "nothing lacking" referring, in one reading, to never violating a root precept, and "nothing breached" to never lapsing into habitual small infractions (or, if a lapse does occur, confessing and restoring purity promptly rather than letting it fester). Master Cháng Bǎi ties this concretely to the ten unwholesome courses of action: bodily conduct free of killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct; verbal conduct free of lying, divisive speech, harsh speech, and idle chatter — the basic, minimum standard beneath every one of the six components still to come.

5. Guarding Each Precept Individually, and the Fivefold Map of Monastic Law

Where the first component covered discipline as a whole, the second narrows to guarding each individual vow of the prātimokṣa — the specific precepts belonging to one's own station among the sevenfold assembly. The third component, perfected protocol, then opens with its own foundational distinction: conforming to ordinary social propriety, and conforming to the Vinaya itself, mapped through a fivefold classification of monastic law.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能守護別解律儀？謂能守護七眾所受別解律儀。即此律儀，眾差別故，成多律儀。今此義中，唯依苾芻律儀處說善能守護別解律儀。云何名為軌則圓滿？謂如有一，或於威儀路，或於所作事，或於善品加行處所，成就軌則。隨順世間，不越世間；隨順毗奈耶，不越毗奈耶。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called skillfully guarding the prātimokṣa restraint? It is being able to guard the prātimokṣa restraint belonging to the sevenfold assembly. Because this very restraint differs according to each assembly, it becomes many restraints. In the present sense, it is only with regard to the monk's and nun's restraint that this skillful guarding is spoken of. What is called perfected protocol? It is that, whether in the pathways of deportment, or in the tasks one performs, or in the settings for applying oneself to wholesome practice, one accomplishes proper protocol — conforming to worldly convention, not exceeding worldly convention; conforming to the Vinaya, not exceeding the Vinaya.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the "prātimokṣa restraint" differs by station within the sevenfold assembly — monks, nuns, probationary nuns, novice monks and nuns, laymen, and laywomen each hold a different set of vows, collapsing into three broad categories of restraint (monk, nun, and lay), though this passage focuses specifically on the monastic restraint, since novice and probationary vows are themselves subsumed under it. The point of naming it as its own component, distinct from "abiding in complete discipline," is granularity: not simply keeping the overall standard of conduct intact, but actively guarding each individual precept, one by one, as its own discrete commitment.

Perfected protocol opens a genuinely rich sub-topic: proper conduct within postures, activities, and practice settings, judged against two standards at once — ordinary social propriety and the Vinaya's own requirements. Master Cháng Bǎi introduces the treatise's fivefold map of monastic law here: "inherent faults" (acts wrong in themselves regardless of any Buddhist precept — killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying — carrying karmic consequence with or without ordination); "prohibited faults" (acts the Buddha specifically restricted because they tend to provoke criticism or undermine practice, even though not inherently evil — drinking alcohol being the classic example, permissible by ordinary secular standards but forbidden under the precepts because of what it tends to lead to); "prescription" (the actual precept the Buddha formulated in response to either category); "exception" (circumstances under which a precept may be set aside without harm — a sick monk permitted a special drink outside the normal eating schedule, for instance); and "practice" itself, which comes in only two forms, correct (never transgressing, or transgressing and then genuinely restoring purity through confession) and wrong (transgressing without restoration). Together, conforming to worldly convention and conforming to the Vinaya's own fivefold structure define what "perfected protocol" actually requires.

6. Perfected Protocol in Practice: Posture, and the Texture of Ordinary Monastic Life

The treatise now works through perfected protocol's concrete content: correct deportment across the four basic postures, and correct conduct across the countless small tasks of daily monastic life — dressing, hygiene, almsround, handling the bowl, washing the feet, and making one's bed — each governed by the same twin standard of matching both the occasion and the Vinaya's own requirements.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，於所應行、於如所行，即於此中如是而行。由是行故，不為世間之所譏毀；不為賢良、正至、善士、諸同法者、諸持律者、諸學律者之所呵責。如於所行，於其所住、所坐、所臥，當知亦爾。……謂如有一，於其所作，若衣服事、若便利事、若用水事、若楊枝事、若入聚落行乞食事、若受用事、若盪鉢事、若安置事、若洗足事、若為敷設臥具等事。即此略說衣事、鉢事，復有所餘如是等類諸所應作，名所作事。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is that one, regarding what should be done and how it should be done, acts accordingly in every case. Because of acting in this way, one is not disparaged by the world, and is not reproached by the virtuous, by those who have arrived at the truth, by good persons, by fellow practitioners, by those who uphold the Vinaya, or by those who study the Vinaya. Just as with walking, so too it should be understood regarding standing, sitting, and lying down. …It is that one, in what one does, attends to matters of clothing, of relieving oneself, of using water, of tooth-cleaning, of entering a village to beg for alms, of making use of things, of rinsing the bowl, of storing it, of washing the feet, and of arranging one's bedding and the like. This is a brief account of matters of robes and of the bowl; there are, further, other such matters that should likewise be attended to — these are called "the tasks one performs."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "the pathways of deportment" as covering the four classic postures — walking like the wind, sitting like a bell, lying like a bow, standing like a pine, in the traditional formula — matched to whatever the occasion, place, and time actually call for, neither excessive nor deficient, applied consistently whether one is attending a teaching, going on almsround, or simply moving through the monastery. "The tasks one performs" then works through the concrete texture of daily monastic life: dressing properly, so the robes hang evenly rather than bunching or sagging; basic hygiene; correct use of water and, in the Buddha's own era, chewing a twig in place of a toothbrush; conduct while begging for alms and receiving offerings; and, notably, "using things" — which the treatise elsewhere details as five kinds of impure and five kinds of pure use, covering items belonging to the Buddha specifically (never to be used personally, even by a monastic gravely ill), items belonging to the communal assembly (never to be taken without formal distribution), another's private property (never without explicit permission), items entrusted informally by a close companion (usable only where genuine mutual understanding exists), and items too soiled or otherwise unsuitable to touch at all. Rinsing and storing the bowl means never leaving it somewhere it might be soiled, lost, or damaged — not under a tree without checking for falling debris or insects, not near a cliff edge or ravine — washing the feet (barefoot practice being standard in the Buddha's own time) means attending properly to hygiene before entering the hall, including washing a senior's feet as an act of respect, and arranging bedding means keeping one's sleeping space orderly, folding blankets after rising, and, if using a borrowed space, leaving it as clean as it was found. Master Cháng Bǎi treats this entire catalogue not as an arbitrary list of rules but as training in mindful attention applied to literally everything — nothing in ordinary monastic life falls outside the scope of practice.

7. Protocol Completes: Wholesome Endeavors, Places to Avoid, and Real Fear of Even Small Faults

Perfected protocol's third and final domain covers conduct within wholesome endeavors themselves — study, service to elders, care for the sick, communal procedure, questioning teachers, mutual encouragement, teaching others, and formal meditation — closing out this third component of moral discipline. The treatise then moves to the fourth and fifth components: avoiding five specific places entirely unsuited to practice, and cultivating genuine dread even at the smallest fault.

Chinese Source

謂於種種善品加行，若於正法受持讀誦；若於尊長修和敬業，參覲承事；若於病者起慈悲心，殷重供侍；若於如法宣白加行，住慈悲心，展轉與欲；若於正法請問聽受，翹勤無惰；於諸有智同梵行者，盡其身力而修敬事；於他善品常勤讚勵；常樂為他宣說正法；入於靜室，結加趺坐，繫念思惟。……云何名為所行圓滿？謂諸苾芻，略有五種非所行處……一、唱令家，二、婬女家，三、酤酒家，四、國王家，五、旃荼羅、羯恥那家。……云何名為於微小罪見大怖畏？謂於諸小、隨小學處，若有所犯，可令還淨，名微小罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding various wholesome endeavors: upholding, reciting the true Dharma; attending on and serving elders through the practice of communal harmony; showing compassion and earnest care toward the sick; properly announcing matters in formal assembly, resting in compassion, and conveying consent when unable to attend; questioning and receiving the true Dharma with diligence, never idle; serving fellow practitioners of wisdom with the full strength of one's body; constantly praising and encouraging others' wholesome endeavors; delighting in teaching the true Dharma to others; and entering a quiet chamber, sitting cross-legged, and fixing the mind in contemplation. …What is called perfected conduct? It is that monks, in brief, have five kinds of place unsuited to their conduct … the household of one who proclaims [the slaughter of livestock]; the household of a prostitute; the household of a wine-seller; the household of a king; the household of an outcaste or an executioner. …What is called seeing great danger in even the slightest fault? It is that, regarding minor and subsidiary-minor points of training, should any transgression occur, it can be restored to purity — this is called a slight fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the nine wholesome endeavors this third domain covers: studying and reciting the Dharma; serving elders through the six practices of communal harmony; caring compassionately for sick fellow-practitioners; properly conveying formal consent when unable to attend a communal proceeding (traditionally requiring a designated proxy and, when raising a charge or expelling a member, the "five virtues" of right timing, truthfulness, benefit, gentle speech, and a genuinely compassionate motive); questioning teachers diligently; serving wise companions in practice; encouraging others' good conduct; teaching the Dharma freely; and formal seated meditation — this list, he notes, is illustrative rather than exhaustive, since literally any wholesome activity undertaken within the monastic community counts. With deportment, daily tasks, and wholesome endeavors all covered, "perfected protocol" — the third of discipline's six components — closes.

Perfected conduct, the fourth component, names five places a monastic should never enter: a household associated with public livestock slaughter, a brothel, a tavern, a royal court, or the household of an outcaste or executioner — largely the same five places already named in fascicle 21's own treatment of this component, here restated as this fascicle's own dedicated fourth section. Seeing great danger in even the slightest fault, the fifth component, defines its key term precisely: a "slight fault" is any transgression of the minor or subsidiary-minor points of training — the lowest tier of the traditional five-part offense classification for monastics, restorable through confession rather than permanently disqualifying, unlike the gravest first-tier offenses that end one's monastic standing outright. Master Cháng Bǎi notes these subsidiary-minor points number roughly a hundred, covering the countless small points of everyday deportment already surveyed under protocol — precisely the kind of fault easiest to dismiss as trivial, and precisely the kind this component insists must never be treated that way, since habitual carelessness with the small is exactly what erodes the capacity to keep the great.

8. Three Fears That Guard Against Complacency, and a Lifetime of Continued Study

The treatise names three specific fears that make "seeing great danger in even the slightest fault" more than an abstract ideal: fear of losing the very capacity for attainment, fear of disgraceful rebirth or open reproach, and fear of one's own bad name spreading. It then closes moral discipline's sixth and final component, training in every point, with a striking claim: the restraint one receives is never complete at the moment of ordination alone — it is earned only through a lifetime of continued learning.

Chinese Source

謂作是觀：勿我由此毀犯因緣，無復堪能得所未得、觸所未觸、證所未證。……勿我由此近諸惡趣，往諸惡趣。或當自責，或為大師、諸天、有智同梵行者以法呵責。……勿我由此遍諸方維惡名、惡稱、惡聲、惡頌遐邇流布。……云何名為受學學處？謂於先受別解脫戒……皆自誓言：一切當學。……以於一切所應學處皆受學故，說名獲得別解律儀。從此以後，於諸學處，若已善巧，便能無犯。設有所犯，尋如法悔。

Ru-Yi Translation

One reflects thus: may I not, through the cause of this transgression, lose the capacity ever to attain what has not yet been attained, to touch what has not yet been touched, to realize what has not yet been realized. …May I not, through this, draw near to or go toward the unfortunate rebirths; may I not bring reproach upon myself, or be reproached in accordance with the Dharma by the Teacher, by the gods, or by wise fellow practitioners. …May I not, through this, have an evil name, an evil report, an evil reputation, or evil verses about me spread far and near in every direction. …What is called training in every point of training? It is that, having first received the prātimokṣa precepts … one vows: "I shall train in all of them." …Because one trains in every point one should train in, this is called attaining the prātimokṣa restraint. From this point onward, having become skilled in these points of training, one is thereby able to be free of transgression; should any transgression occur, one promptly confesses it in accordance with the Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names the three fears in turn: fear of forfeiting the capacity for genuine attainment altogether — stream-entry not yet reached, the higher fruits not yet touched, arhatship not yet realized, all placed at risk by careless accumulation of small faults; fear of disgraceful consequence — rebirth in the lower realms, or open reproach from the Teacher, from unseen beings, or from wise companions in practice; and fear of infamy — a bad reputation, or even mocking verses, spreading in every direction, the opposite of the good name a genuinely careful practitioner earns. Together these three fears are what actually make "seeing great danger in even the slightest fault" operative rather than merely notional: a practitioner who takes even minor transgressions this seriously will never deliberately break a precept, even at the cost of life itself, and will confess and restore purity immediately should a lapse occur through simple forgetfulness.

Training in every point, the sixth and final component, makes an important claim: the restraint received at ordination is only a beginning, since a newly ordained monastic hears just a fraction of the full code from the preceptor in that single ceremony. Genuine mastery comes only through sustained further study — from the preceptor and instructing teacher afterward, from fellow reciters of the precepts, from the fortnightly recitation of the full monastic code (traditionally held every two weeks, since the Buddha observed that most people forget what they've learned within roughly that span), and from ongoing discussion with experienced companions — the whole process vowed to at ordination ("I shall train in all of them") but only actually fulfilled across a lifetime of continued practice. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that this is why "attaining the prātimokṣa restraint" is not a single completed event but an ongoing achievement: a practitioner who has genuinely grown skilled through this sustained study naturally avoids transgression, and swiftly restores purity through confession on the rare occasion a lapse does occur.

9. Reorganizing Six Into Three: Integrity, Essence, and Two Directions of Benefit

Correctly receiving both a teacher's instruction and its underlying meaning closes the sixth component, and with it, the detailed six-part explanation of moral discipline. The treatise now reorganizes this same material into three broader "marks," showing how each pair of components serves a distinct purpose — and, within the third mark, distinguishes benefit directed toward others from benefit directed toward oneself.

Chinese Source

謂於是中，世尊顯示戒蘊略義，有三種相：一者、無失壞相，二者、自性相，三者、自性功德相。……謂若說言：安住具戒。由此顯示尸羅律儀無失壞相。若復說言：能善守護別解律儀。由此顯示尸羅律儀自性相。若復說言：軌則、所行皆悉圓滿。由此顯示別解律儀，如其所受，觀他增上自性功德相。……若復說言：於微小罪見大怖畏、受學學處。由此顯示別解律儀，如其所受，觀自增上自性功德相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, the Bhagavat reveals the essential meaning of the aggregate of discipline through three marks: the mark of being free of loss or impairment; the mark of intrinsic nature; and the mark of intrinsic virtue. …When it is said "abiding in complete discipline," this reveals the mark of moral restraint being free of loss or impairment. When it is said "skillfully guarding the prātimokṣa restraint," this reveals the mark of moral restraint's intrinsic nature. When it is said "protocol and conduct are both entirely perfected," this reveals the mark of intrinsic virtue directed toward benefiting others, belonging to the prātimokṣa restraint as received. …When it is said "seeing great danger in even the slightest fault, and training in every point of training," this reveals the mark of intrinsic virtue directed toward benefiting oneself, belonging to the prātimokṣa restraint as received.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the six components already covered onto three broader marks. "Abiding in complete discipline" alone reveals discipline "free of loss or impairment" — the basic fact of unbroken conformity to what was received. "Skillfully guarding the prātimokṣa restraint" reveals discipline's own intrinsic nature — the precepts themselves, taken one by one. The remaining four components pair off to reveal "intrinsic virtue," split into two directions: perfected protocol and perfected conduct together reveal virtue directed outward, toward benefiting others — since a monastic's visibly impeccable deportment and choice of where to go is precisely what inspires confidence in observers, whether that confidence was already present or entirely new; Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to note this outward-facing virtue only accrues when protocol and conduct are genuinely, not merely superficially, complete, and warns against the opposite failure mode — a practitioner so preoccupied with policing others' conduct that everyone comes to avoid them. Seeing great danger in the smallest fault and training in every point, meanwhile, together reveal virtue directed inward, toward benefiting oneself — the practitioner's own capacity for genuine attainment, and their own favorable rebirth, both protected precisely by taking these two components seriously. Both directions of virtue matter, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses: protocol and conduct without genuine inward discipline leaves a hollow performance that collapses the moment a real test arises, while inward discipline without outward care for how one's conduct affects others' faith falls short of discipline's fuller purpose.

10. A Second Threefold Lens: Receiving, Liberating, and Cultivating Discipline — and Ten Ways It Can Falter

The same six components can be viewed through a second threefold lens entirely: discipline as something received, discipline as the very foundation of liberation itself (tied explicitly to the classic three trainings of discipline, concentration, and wisdom), and discipline as something requiring ongoing cultivation. The treatise then turns practical, naming ten specific ways discipline can become impaired — the mirror image of ten ways it can be genuinely fulfilled.

Chinese Source

謂佛世尊此中略顯三種戒性：一、受持戒性，二、出離戒性，三、修習戒性。……別解律儀所攝淨戒，當知說名增上戒學。即依如是增上戒學，修增上心、增上慧學；由此能得一切苦盡，究竟出離。如是出離，用增上戒以為前行所依止處，是故說此別解律儀名出離戒性。……如是一種尸羅律儀現前宣說，當知六種。又即如是尸羅律儀，由十因緣，當知虧損；即此相違十因緣故，當知圓滿。云何十種虧損因緣？一者、最初惡受尸羅律儀，二者、太極沈下，三者、太極浮散，四者、放逸懈怠所攝，五者、發起邪願，六者、軌則虧損所攝，七者、淨命虧損所攝，八者、墮在二邊，九者、不能出離，十者、所受失壞。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Bhagavat, in brief, reveals three natures of discipline here: the nature of receiving and upholding; the nature of leading to liberation; and the nature of ongoing cultivation. …The pure discipline comprised within the prātimokṣa restraint should be known as the higher training in discipline. Resting on this higher training in discipline, one cultivates the higher training in concentration and the higher training in wisdom; by this, one is able to exhaust all suffering and attain final liberation. Such liberation takes the higher training in discipline as its preliminary foundation — this is why the prātimokṣa restraint is called "the nature of leading to liberation." …Thus this single moral restraint, when set forth directly, should be understood as sixfold. Again, this same moral restraint, through ten causes and conditions, should be known to become impaired; and through the reverse of these same ten, should be known to become fulfilled. What are the ten causes of impairment? Wrongly receiving the moral restraint from the very start; being excessively lax; being excessively agitated; falling under heedlessness and laziness; giving rise to a misguided aspiration; impairment in protocol; impairment in one's pure livelihood; falling into one of two extremes; being unable to attain liberation; and letting what was received degrade entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps this second threefold lens onto the same six components differently: "receiving and upholding" corresponds to abiding in complete discipline alone; "leading to liberation" corresponds to skillfully guarding the prātimokṣa restraint, since keeping each precept individually is what actually functions as discipline, the foundational first of the three higher trainings — discipline supporting concentration, concentration supporting wisdom, wisdom finally exhausting all suffering; and "ongoing cultivation" corresponds to the remaining four components together (protocol, conduct, fear of small faults, and training in every point), since genuine cultivation requires all four working in concert. Whether described as one restraint, three marks, three natures, or six components, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses it is all the same underlying reality, simply viewed from different angles.

The treatise then turns to something more diagnostic: ten specific ways discipline actually falters in practice, each with an exact mirror-image way it can instead be fulfilled. The ten: taking up the precepts from the very start with a corrupted motive (coercion, escape, ulterior gain, rather than genuine aspiration); excessive laxity — a practitioner too shameless and sluggish to take practice seriously; excessive agitation — needless regret over things that don't call for it, or contempt toward others where none is warranted, repeated and never released; heedlessness and laziness — forgetting mindfulness, breaking precepts across past, present, and future, clinging to sleep and rest as pleasurable, avoiding communal duties, neglecting wise companions; a misguided aspiration — keeping precepts purely to be reborn in a heaven or to gain worldly support and veneration, rather than for liberation itself; impaired protocol — violating either worldly convention or the Vinaya's own requirements in deportment, tasks, or wholesome endeavors; impaired livelihood — obtaining robes, food, lodging, or medicine through improper means; falling into one of two extremes — either harsh austerity or comfortable indulgence in the name of discipline; an inability to attain liberation — mistakenly believing discipline alone, without concentration and wisdom, is sufficient for freedom; and, finally, letting what was received simply degrade — violating the precepts shamelessly and without any restraint at all. Master Cháng Bǎi will unpack each of these ten in turn, since recognizing exactly how discipline can quietly fail is precisely what makes its genuine fulfillment possible.

11. The First Four Ways Discipline Falters: Wrong Motive, Laxity, Agitation, and Unrepented Lapses

The treatise now details the first four of the ten impairments in turn. Wrongly-motivated ordination covers both outright coercion and a quieter failure of genuine aspiration; excessive laxity and excessive agitation describe two opposite ways a practitioner's basic disposition undermines discipline; and heedlessness names the simple failure to confess and restore purity after a lapse, across past, present, and future alike.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，王所逼迫而求出家，或為狂賊之所逼迫、或為債主之所逼迫、或為怖畏之所逼迫、或不活畏之所逼迫而求出家。不為沙門性、不為婆羅門性、不為自調伏、不為自寂靜、不為自涅槃而求出家。如是名為最初惡受尸羅律儀。……謂如有一，性無羞恥，惡作羸劣，為性慢緩，於諸學處所作慢緩。如是名為太極沈下。……謂如有一，堅執惡取，非處惡作，於不應作諸惡作中浪作惡作；非處於他起輕懱心、或惱害心，於其非處彊生曉悟。如是名為太極浮散。……謂如有一，由過去世毀犯所犯；於此毀犯，由失念故，一類不能如法還淨。如由過去，由未來世、由現在世當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like one who seeks ordination coerced by a king, or by fierce bandits, or by a creditor, or by fear, or by fear of destitution — seeking ordination not for the sake of the recluse's nature, not for the sake of the brahmin's nature, not to subdue oneself, not to quiet oneself, not to attain nirvāṇa. This is called wrongly receiving the moral restraint from the very start. …It is like one who is, by nature, without shame, whose remorse is feeble and weak, who is by nature sluggish, sluggish in every point of training. This is called being excessively lax. …It is like one who firmly clings to wrong views, feels regret where none is warranted, needlessly regrets what should not be regretted, and, out of place, feels contempt or ill will toward others, forcibly insisting one understands where one does not. This is called being excessively agitated. …It is like one who, having transgressed in some past time, fails, through loss of mindfulness, to restore purity in accordance with the Dharma regarding that transgression. As with the past, so too should it be understood regarding the future and the present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lists the five coercive circumstances behind wrongly-received precepts — royal decree, fleeing bandits, fleeing debt, superstitious fear, and fleeing poverty — the same five obstacles already covered in fascicle 21, here restated as this fascicle's own dedicated impairment cause, followed by five failures of genuine aspiration: not seeking the "recluse's nature" or "brahmin's nature" (both terms, he explains, ultimately meaning the same thing — a genuine aspiration toward liberation, since "brahmin" in this Buddhist usage means one who has reached nirvāṇa's far shore, exactly as "recluse" means one walking the path there), and not seeking to subdue, quiet, or ultimately liberate one's own mind. He notes the treatise's own fourfold gloss on "recluse" elsewhere: the term covers anyone renouncing ordinary life for a spiritual path (Buddhist or not), the intrinsic aspiration toward liberation itself, the noble eightfold path as the recluse's actual domain, and finally the fruit that path yields, whether described as the four traditional fruits or, more technically, the knowledge that no further rebirth follows.

Excessive laxity describes a temperament too shameless and sluggish to take discipline seriously — barely troubled by wrongdoing, slow to act even when action is called for, exactly the opposite of the urgency Master Cháng Bǎi notes ordinary people bring to worldly pursuits like earning money, which should if anything be exceeded when pursuing liberation. Excessive agitation describes the opposite failure: a mind cluttered with wrong view, prone to needless regret over things that were never actually wrong, and quick to judge others' conduct without genuinely understanding the precepts' own underlying spirit — restless and overconfident rather than sluggish, but equally corrosive to genuine practice. Heedlessness, the fourth impairment, names something simpler: a transgression, in the past, present, or future, that is never properly confessed and restored — Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise elsewhere classifies all monastic offenses into five tiers and prescribes a specific confession procedure (declaring the offense by name before a qualified elder or fellow practitioner capable of receiving it) for restoring purity, and stresses that a vow never actually renewed to avoid repeating a fault is precisely what lets heedlessness persist across all three times at once. He notes the extended discussion of "clear awareness in every activity" that follows in the source here simply restates, in the same words, material already covered in fascicle 21's own treatment of this quality — walking, glancing, bending a limb, wearing the robes, eating, the four postures, speaking or silent, even resting — and does not repeat it a second time.

12. Impairments Five and Six: Aspiring to the Wrong Reward, and Neglecting Protocol

Heedlessness closes with laziness's own signature — a love of sleep and reluctance to serve fellow practitioners. Two further impairments follow: keeping precepts purely to be reborn in a heaven or to attract worldly veneration rather than for liberation itself, and simply neglecting the protocol already covered at length.

Chinese Source

又自執取睡眠為樂、偃臥為樂、脅臥為樂；性不翹勤；為性懶惰；不具起發；於諸有智同梵行者，不能時時覲問供事。是名放逸懈怠所攝。云何名為發起邪願？謂如有一，依止邪願修行梵行。言我所有若戒、若禁，若常精勤、若修梵行，當得生天，或餘天處。或復愛樂利養恭敬，而修梵行。……如是名為發起邪願。云何軌則虧損所攝？謂如有一，於威儀路、或所作事、或諸善品加行處所所有軌則，不順世間，違越世間；不順毗奈耶，違越毗奈耶。準前廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, such a person clings to sleep as pleasurable, to lying supine as pleasurable, to lying on the side as pleasurable; is by nature reluctant, by nature lazy, never fully rousing effort; and is unable to attend on and serve wise fellow practitioners from time to time. This is called being subsumed under heedlessness and laziness. What is called giving rise to a misguided aspiration? It is like one who cultivates pure conduct resting on a misguided aspiration, saying: "Through all my discipline and restraint, through constant diligence, through cultivating pure conduct, may I be reborn in a heaven, or some other heavenly realm." Or one who cultivates pure conduct out of delight in support and veneration. …This is called giving rise to a misguided aspiration. What is called being subsumed under impairment of protocol? It is like one whose protocol, in the pathways of deportment, in the tasks performed, or in the settings for wholesome endeavor, does not conform to worldly convention and exceeds it, does not conform to the Vinaya and exceeds it — to be understood at length as stated before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes laziness's portrait with its most ordinary form: genuine attachment to sleep in any position, a reluctance to exert effort even when called for, and neglect of fellow practitioners who deserve attention and service. The fifth impairment, misguided aspiration, covers two related failures already anticipated earlier in this project: practicing purely to secure a favorable rebirth in some heaven rather than genuine liberation, and practicing in order to attract material support and public esteem — both technically "successful" by their own narrow measure, yet both, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, secretly bound up with craving rather than freed from it, which is exactly why they impair discipline even when the outward conduct looks impeccable. The sixth impairment, impaired protocol, simply names neglect of the deportment, daily tasks, and wholesome-endeavor protocol already detailed at length earlier in this same fascicle — violating either worldly convention or the Vinaya's own requirements, with nothing new to add beyond what was already covered.

13. Impairment Seven: Wrong Livelihood, and the Art of Faking Sanctity for Alms

The seventh impairment, impaired livelihood, opens one of the treatise's most vivid passages: a portrait of insatiable want, followed by a catalogue of specific deceptive tactics — feigned stillness, false claims of attainment, deliberately ragged robes — a monastic might use to extract material support under false pretenses.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，為性大欲，不知喜足，難養難滿。常以非法追求衣服、飲食、臥具、病緣醫藥及諸資具，不以正法。……又為貪求種種衣服、飲食、臥具、病緣醫藥資具因緣，方便顯己有勝功德，矯詐構集非常威儀。為誑他故，恆常詐現諸根無掉、諸根無動、諸根寂靜，由是令他謂其有德，當有所施、當有所作。……又多凶悖、彊口、矯傲、修飾其名、執恃種姓。……或求多聞，或任持法，為利養故，亦復為他宣說正法。……或於他前方便現相，為求衣服，或求隨一沙門資具。……雖無匱乏，而現被服故弊衣裳，為令淨信長者、居士、婆羅門等知其衣服有所匱乏，殷重承事，給施眾多上妙衣服。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like one who is, by nature, of great desire, never knowing contentment, hard to nourish, hard to satisfy — constantly seeking robes, food, bedding, medicine for illness, and other requisites through improper means, not through proper means. …Further, for the sake of robes, food, bedding, medicine, and other requisites, one contrives, by skillful means, to display an appearance of excellent virtue, fraudulently assembling an unusual deportment. To deceive others, one constantly, falsely displays faculties free of agitation, free of movement, utterly still — leading others to believe one possesses genuine virtue and ought to be given offerings and service. …Further, one is often fierce, obstinate, forcing one's words, feigning pride, embellishing one's reputation, relying on claims of noble lineage. …One seeks extensive learning, or retains the Dharma in memory, for the sake of material gain, and, on that basis, teaches the true Dharma to others. …Or, in front of others, one contrives to display a certain appearance in order to obtain robes, or some other monastic requisite. …Though not actually lacking, one displays worn, tattered robes, so that faithful elders, householders, and brahmins, believing one's robes to be genuinely lacking, will earnestly attend and provide an abundance of fine, excellent robes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens with the underlying temperament: a person genuinely never satisfied by whatever robes, food, or lodging circumstance provides, always wanting more, always wanting finer — the root condition that then drives five specific deceptive tactics the treatise catalogues in turn. The first, feigned deportment, means deliberately cultivating an outward stillness — unhurried movement, faculties apparently unmoved by anything, sitting for long stretches without apparent restlessness — precisely to project an image of deep meditative attainment that isn't actually there, since donors naturally want to support someone who looks genuinely accomplished. The second, false speech, covers both embellishing one's own reputation (letting others believe, or outright claiming, attainments never actually reached — the gravest form of lying in the entire monastic code) and relying on claims of prestigious birth to command undue respect; Master Cháng Bǎi adds a caution specifically for teachers here, that fluent, well-informed Dharma teaching can easily lead an audience to imagine a level of personal attainment behind it that the teacher never actually claimed but also never bothered to correct — itself a subtle form of the same deception. The third, staged appearances, covers the specific trick of wearing deliberately shabby robes despite owning perfectly good ones, precisely so that faithful donors, believing genuine need, respond with generous new gifts — Master Cháng Bǎi notes robes are used as the illustrative example here simply because they were, in the treatise's own era, an especially costly and hard-to-obtain requisite.
