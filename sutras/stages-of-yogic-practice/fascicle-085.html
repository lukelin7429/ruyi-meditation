Fascicle 85 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十五 · The Compendium of Bases Begins

Twenty-Four Kinds of Sūtra, and the Aggregates Section Opens

Fascicle 85 opens the Compendium of Bases (攝事分), the treatise's fifth and final major division, spanning sixteen fascicles in total (85 through 100). Where the Compendium of Synonyms sorted out the many names scattered through the sūtras, this final division grounds all of that in the actual textual sources themselves — the sūtras, the vinaya, and the treatises (摩怛理迦) that comment on them. Fascicle 85 begins with sūtra matters (契經事), the first and largest of the three, itself organized into four sections of analysis: the aggregates (行擇攝, fascicles 85–88), the sense-bases (處擇攝, fascicles 89–92), dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements (緣起食諦界擇攝, fascicles 93–96), and the factors of awakening (菩提分法擇攝, fascicles 97–98). After cataloguing all twenty-four kinds of sūtra and explaining the first four in detail, this fascicle completes element (界), the aggregates section's entire first topic — desire's eight similes, liberation's marks, the fourfold certainty of impermanence, and an extended prison simile among them — and opens brief teaching (略教), the second topic, carrying it through its own first five parts: brief teaching itself, its fourfold fruit, the six kinds of death, falling into conventional number, and threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off. The aggregates section continues in fascicle 86.

1. Section: The Compendium of Bases Opens — Sūtra Matters, the Aggregates Section, Part One

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the aggregates section, the first, part one.

Chinese Source

攝事分中契經事行擇攝第一之一

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the aggregates section, the first, part one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise's five major divisions unfold in sequence: the Basic Section explains the seventeen stages directly; the Determinations return to resolve what remained unclear there; the Compendium of Explanations then teaches how a sūtra's own text and meaning should be handled; the Compendium of Synonyms, just closed, sorts out the many names a single reality can bear; and now, finally, the Compendium of Bases grounds everything in the actual textual sources themselves — the sūtras, the vinaya, and the treatises that comment on them — since even sorting out names presupposes some text those names are drawn from. Spanning sixteen fascicles, the Compendium of Bases treats the sūtras first (fascicles 85–98, fourteen fascicles), then the vinaya (fascicles 99 through the first half of 100, a fascicle and a half), and finally the treatises (the second half of fascicle 100). Its sūtra section draws overwhelmingly on the Saṃyukta Āgama, with some material from the Ekottara, Madhyama, and Dīrgha Āgamas as well — the modern scholar Lü Cheng first demonstrated, in a 1923 paper, that this section follows the Saṃyukta Āgama's own sequence exactly, functioning as its mātṛkā (topical outline) though without quoting the sūtra text directly; Yin Shun later combined the two into a single reference work, the Combined Edition of the Saṃyukta Āgama and Its Treatise, which this fascicle's own notes draw on throughout. A sūtra (契經, "linking text") is prose narration of the Buddha's own teaching, distinct from verse — its own fascicle 81 defines it as running prose whose primary aim is to gather up the Buddha's intent through direct, connected exposition, and the Abhidharma-hṛdaya-śāstra offers five further glosses: like a person's birth, it can give rise to boundless further teaching; like a spring, it flows without exhaustion; it discloses many kinds of meaning; like a carpenter's inked line, it distinguishes right from wrong; and like a garland strung from many flowers, it threads scattered teachings into a single continuous whole. Sūtra matters (素怛纜事) is itself organized into four further sections of analysis: the aggregates (fascicles 85–88), the sense-bases (89–92), dependent origination together with food, the truths, and the elements (93–96), and the factors of awakening (97–98) — and since sūtra matters comes first among the three textual sources, and the aggregates section comes first among its four analyses, this fascicle is headed "the first, part one." The aggregates section itself unpacks the Saṃyukta Āgama's own chapter on the five aggregates, teaching how contemplating them rightly leads to liberation.

3. Section: Three Textual Sources Named — Closing the Overview

It should be known that bases are comprised through three sources: first, sūtra matters; second, vinaya matters; third, mātṛkā matters.

Chinese Source

謂由三處，應知攝事。一者、素怛纜事，二者、毗奈耶事，三者、摩怛理迦事。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that bases are comprised through three sources: first, sūtra matters; second, vinaya matters; third, mātṛkā matters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 100 and the Śrāvaka Stage

Sūtra matters treats the sūtras themselves; vinaya matters treats the monastic code; and mātṛkā matters treats the treatises that comment on both — fascicle 100 defines mātṛkā simply as any treatise commenting on the sūtras or the vinaya, gathered together under this single name. Sorted by the twelvefold division of the canon, the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 25, assigns sūtra, verse teaching, prophecy, verse, spontaneous utterance, parable, past-life account, birth-story, extended teaching, and unprecedented dharma all to the sūtra basket; causal narrative to the vinaya basket; and discursive treatise to the abhidharma basket. With this, the three textual sources this final division rests on are named in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a brief catalogue of the sūtras themselves, twenty-four kinds in all.

Ru-Yi Translation

What are sūtra matters? By twenty-four categories, every sūtra is briefly comprised. First, the Prātimokṣa Sūtra. Second, sūtras of narrative matter. Third, sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle. Fourth, sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle. Fifth, sūtras that render explicit a meaning not yet made explicit. Sixth, sūtras that further clarify and purify a meaning already made explicit. Seventh, sūtras spoken beforehand as preparation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Synonyms directly

Sūtra matters catalogues every sūtra found across the canon under twenty-four headings — the first four sorted by the sūtra's own textual category, the remaining twenty by its meaning, the first four serving as root and the latter twenty as their branches, each implying the other. The Prātimokṣa Sūtra is the vinaya's own textual portion, teaching that individually keeping each precept individually releases a corresponding affliction. Sūtras of narrative matter are the four āgamas themselves — Saṃyukta, Madhyama, Ekottara, and Dīrgha. Sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle teach the four noble truths and dependent origination for an audience of śrāvakas — the four āgamas again fit this description. Sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle address the great vehicle's own capacity directly, such as the Vimalakīrti, Lotus, Avataṃsaka, Ratnakūṭa, and Mahāsaṃnipāta sūtras, classed among the extended teachings of the twelvefold canon. Sūtras that render explicit a meaning not yet made explicit disclose what a prior teaching left concealed — the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, for instance, unpacks the Prajñāpāramitā's own profound, hidden meaning for those of duller faculties unable to enter emptiness through the Middle Way's reasoning alone, the Buddha delivering it in his third turning of the wheel precisely for this purpose. And sūtras spoken beforehand as preparation teach the causal link between generosity, discipline, and rebirth among gods and humans, laying the ground before the deeper teaching follows — exactly the "preamble" the Compendium of Synonyms itself already described: a skillful teacher first speaks of giving, discipline, and heavenly rebirth to draw out a listener's own genuine interest before turning to their full capacity and offering the deeper teaching earnestly and without reserve.

Eighth, sūtras of praise. Ninth, sūtras revealing the dark portion. Tenth, sūtras revealing the bright portion. Eleventh, sūtras of provisional meaning. Twelfth, sūtras of definitive meaning. Thirteenth, sūtras brief in meaning yet extensive in wording. Fourteenth, sūtras extensive in meaning yet brief in wording. Fifteenth, sūtras brief in both meaning and wording. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Sūtras of praise extol the three jewels' own virtue, such as Aśvaghoṣa's Acts of the Buddha. Sūtras revealing the dark portion expose affliction, karma, and suffering, or the ten unwholesome deeds and the three evil destinies, as the Kṣitigarbha Sūtra does. Sūtras revealing the bright portion teach wholesome dharmas instead, the form and formless heavens or the ten wholesome deeds, as the Sūtra on the Ten Wholesome Ways of Action does. Sūtras of provisional meaning leave their own deepest intent unstated, as texts aligned with the Middle Way's own reasoning leave the hidden meaning of essence-less dependent origination unspoken, such as the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra; sūtras of definitive meaning instead spell out every characteristic of every dharma directly, as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra does. The following pairs — brief or extensive in meaning, brief or extensive in wording, profound or plain in either — show that meaning and wording can combine in every possible way: sometimes a simple point is unpacked at great length or compressed to a few words; sometimes a profound truth is delivered in plain language accessible to all, or matched with equally dense and demanding wording — the Buddha spoke sometimes with utmost economy, sometimes at great expository length, always according to his listener's own capacity. Sūtras that free one from a fault yet to come address some prophesied future danger, as the Sūtra of Ānanda's Seven Dreams does — the Buddha, nearing his own final nirvāṇa, lamented that the true dharma's ruin would come not from outside attack but from within, "worms born in the lion's own body devouring the lion's own flesh," a warning his disciples were meant to heed. Sūtras that free one from a fault already present instead counter affliction actively present right now — the two paired against each other, future versus present. Sūtras that dispel doubt as it arises resolve uncertainty about the three jewels, or about one's own possession of buddha-nature, as the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra teaches that every being without exception possesses it. And sūtras that cause the true dharma to abide long in the world include, beyond the vinaya itself, every teaching on the emptiness of self and of dharmas, on consciousness-only, or on the perfection of wisdom. With this, all twenty-four kinds of sūtra are named in their full enumeration. The treatise turns next to explaining the first four in detail, beginning with the Prātimokṣa Sūtra.

6. Section: Explaining the First Four Begins — the Prātimokṣa Sūtra

The Prātimokṣa Sūtra means that within it, resting on the five categories of offense and on release from the five categories of offense, more than two hundred and fifty training precepts are taught, so that good sons who genuinely love themselves might diligently train in them.

Chinese Source

別解脫契經者，謂於是中，依五犯聚及出五犯聚，說過二百五十學處，為令自愛諸善男子精勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Prātimokṣa Sūtra means that within it, resting on the five categories of offense and on release from the five categories of offense, more than two hundred and fifty training precepts are taught, so that good sons who genuinely love themselves might diligently train in them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Discipline Section and the Śrāvaka Stage

Having catalogued all twenty-four kinds, the treatise now unpacks the first four individually, beginning with the Prātimokṣa Sūtra, the monastic precept text itself, sorting every offense from heaviest to lightest into five categories, and teaching, alongside them, how each offense is properly released through confession. Though loosely reckoned at two hundred and fifty precepts, the actual number of subtle rules exceeds this round figure considerably. The Discipline Section names the five categories directly: defeat, the gravest category, in which affliction has fully overcome the practitioner; remainder, a lesser fault still recoverable, like a beheading that leaves the throat somehow intact, curable by a skilled physician; downfall, offenses serious enough to lead toward the evil destinies — thirty each for monks and nuns concerning material goods, ninety more for monks and one hundred seventy-eight more for nuns concerning other matters, though confession can still purify them; confession-to-another, a lighter fault cleared simply by confessing individually to a fellow monastic; and wrongdoing, covering minor training rules and careless speech, born of a lapse in mindfulness, cleared through simple confession to oneself or to another. The Discipline Section, fascicle 99, treats all five at length; grouped by severity, the first two count as heavy offenses, the third and fourth as intermediate, and the fifth alone as light. The precise historical count of two hundred fifty precepts remains debated among commentators; the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 22, confirms that a monastic, having heard the fuller Prātimokṣa from a preceptor or teacher beyond the core precepts already known, vows in each case: "in all of this, I shall train."

Sūtras of narrative matter mean the four āgamas: first, the Saṃyukta Āgama; second, the Madhyama Āgama; third, the Dīrgha Āgama; fourth, the Ekottara Āgama.

Chinese Source

事契經者，謂四阿笈摩。一者、雜阿笈摩，二者、中阿笈摩，三者、長阿笈摩，四者、增一阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtras of narrative matter mean the four āgamas: first, the Saṃyukta Āgama; second, the Madhyama Āgama; third, the Dīrgha Āgama; fourth, the Ekottara Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of the Mind Ground

Āgama means "transmission" — teachings passed down unaltered from the Buddha through his disciples in turn. The Stage of the Mind Ground, fascicle 3, explains that every word the buddhas speak is comprised within nine matters: the matter of sentient beings, namely the five grasping aggregates; the matter of enjoyment, namely the twelve bases; the matter of arising, namely the twelvefold links of dependent origination and what is dependently arisen; the matter of abiding, namely the four kinds of food; the matter of defilement and purity, namely the four noble truths; the matter of distinctions, namely the boundless elements; the matter of the speaker, namely the Buddha and his disciples; the matter of what is spoken, namely the factors of awakening beginning with the four foundations of mindfulness; and the matter of the assembly, namely the eight kinds of assembly — each of the four āgamas draws on this same ninefold framework throughout.

8. Section: The Saṃyukta Āgama Begins — Correspondence With Its Own Subject Matter

The Saṃyukta Āgama means that within it, the World-Honored One, according to whichever beings he was teaching, proclaimed correspondences with what the Tathāgata and his disciples themselves taught; correspondences with the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases; correspondences with dependent origination, food, and the truths; and correspondences with the foundations of mindfulness, the right efforts, the bases of power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of awakening, the path-factors, mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith.

Chinese Source

雜阿笈摩者，謂於是中，世尊觀待彼彼所化，宣說如來及諸弟子所說相應，蘊、界、處相應，緣起、食、諦相應，念住、正斷、神足、根、力、覺支、道支、入出息念、學、證淨等相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Saṃyukta Āgama means that within it, the World-Honored One, according to whichever beings he was teaching, proclaimed correspondences with what the Tathāgata and his disciples themselves taught; correspondences with the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases; correspondences with dependent origination, food, and the truths; and correspondences with the foundations of mindfulness, the right efforts, the bases of power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of awakening, the path-factors, mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Explanations and Yin Shun's own reorganization

The Saṃyukta Āgama takes its very name from this principle of correspondence (相應) — teachings gathered by shared theme, whether the aggregates, the sense-bases, dependent origination, the noble truths, and so on. Yin Shun's Combined Edition sorts its contents into seven recitations comprising fifty-one such correspondences: the aggregates recitation (correspondence 1); the six sense-bases recitation (2); the mixed-causes recitation (3–6); the path-factors recitation (7–16); the eight-assemblies recitation (17–27); the disciples' own teachings recitation (28–33); and the Tathāgata's own teachings recitation (34–51, the largest, spanning eighteen correspondences alone, since eminent disciples such as Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, Aniruddha, Mahākātyāyana, Ānanda, and Citra each contributed their own recorded teachings as well). Sorted instead by content rather than speaker, this same material covers the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases (the aggregates and six-bases recitations); dependent origination and the food that sustains a being from moment to moment, itself absorbed within the mixed-causes recitation in Yin Shun's own reworking, alongside the noble truths (a hundred fifty sūtras) and the eighteen elements (thirty-seven sūtras); and the path-factors recitation's own further ten correspondences — mindfulness, right effort, the bases of power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of awakening, the path-factors, mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith — totaling four hundred five sūtras across widely varying lengths, from fifty-four sūtras on mindfulness alone to a mere handful surviving for the bases of power. The Compendium of Explanations, fascicle 81, already explained "what is taught" as whatever accords with the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, the possible and the impossible, and the foundations of mindfulness — the very same content named here as correspondence.

9. Section: Correspondence With the Eight Assemblies — Closing Correspondence's Twofold Account

And further, resting on the eight assemblies, correspondence with the assembly is spoken of.

Chinese Source

又依八眾說眾相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, resting on the eight assemblies, correspondence with the assembly is spoken of.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of the Mind Ground

Beyond who speaks and what is spoken, correspondence with the assembly names whom a teaching was spoken for — the Stage of the Mind Ground, fascicle 3, names eight such assemblies: khattiyas (the warrior-noble caste); brahmins (India's priestly caste, custodians of the Vedas); householders (wealthy or venerable laypeople, such as the elder Anāthapiṇḍada, or the aged Nakula, said to have reached one hundred twenty years); renunciants, who have given up worldly wealth for the homeless life; the four great heavenly kings, lowest of the six desire-realm heavens; the thirty-three gods, the second desire-realm heaven atop Mount Sumeru, ruled by Śakra from his central palace, surrounded by thirty-two further deva-kings for the thirty-three total; Yama's own heaven, the third desire-realm heaven, and the lowest heaven whose inhabitants no longer dwell on solid ground; and the Brahmā heavens, the first absorption's own form-realm gods, comprising Great Brahmā himself, his ministers, and his general retinue. Yin Shun's own Combined Edition further divides the eight-assemblies recitation into eleven correspondences: monks; Māra, including his own recorded exchanges with the Buddha; Śakra, the thirty-three gods' devoted protector, alongside Great Brahmā, in contrast to Māra's own frequent opposition; khattiyas; brahmins; the Brahmā heavens; nuns; the venerable Vaṅgīsa, famed among the Buddha's disciples both for his threefold knowledge and for composing spontaneous verses in praise of the three jewels; the various heavens; yakṣas, spirits dwelling on earth or in the air who may harass or protect the true dharma; and the forest, recording events that took place there, such as forest deities urging monks on toward diligent practice. With this, correspondence — with the Buddha's and his disciples' own teaching, with its content, and with the eight assemblies it was taught to — is closed in its full twofold account. The treatise turns next to the Saṃyukta Āgama's own compilation.

10. Section: Its Compilation Explained — the Summary Verses

Its later compilation, in order that the noble teaching might abide long, gathered summary verses, arranged in sequence according to what was fitting.

Chinese Source

後結集者，為令聖教久住，結嗢柁南頌，隨其所應次第安布。

Ru-Yi Translation

Its later compilation, in order that the noble teaching might abide long, gathered summary verses, arranged in sequence according to what was fitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 87 and Yin Shun directly

After the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, the venerable Mahākāśyapa proposed compiling the teachings, and five hundred arhats gathered to recite the three baskets together from memory, since no writing yet preserved them; to aid recitation and ensure the teaching's long survival, the elders composed a brief summary verse after every ten sūtras, gathering their essential content into a single memorable stanza. The first ten sūtras of the aggregates correspondence, for instance, are summarized: "impermanence, and suffering, and emptiness; not-self, and right reflection; ignorance and its like, four kinds; and joy taken in form" — each phrase capturing one sūtra's own central teaching in turn: the first taught impermanence, the second suffering, the third emptiness, the fourth not-self, the fifth right reflection, the sixth through ninth various kinds of ignorance, and the tenth delight taken in form. Fascicle 87 confirms: "the later compilers, within this teaching, are called the World-Honored One's own summary verses." Yin Shun's own Combined Edition cites a further source, the Treatise Distinguishing Merit: "having finished compiling the three baskets, they recorded ten sūtras as one verse, so that future reciters, fearing they might forget or err, could see the name and recall the source, reflecting and awakening to it themselves."

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that every correspondence of this kind rests, in brief, on three marks. What are the three? First, the one who speaks; second, what is spoken; third, whom it is spoken for.

Every correspondence throughout the Saṃyukta Āgama can be reduced to three simple marks: the Buddha or a noble disciple as the one who genuinely, correctly speaks; the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, food, the truths, and the path-factors as what is spoken; and the eight assemblies as whom it is spoken for.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether the Tathāgata or a disciple of the Tathāgata, this is the one who speaks — as with what the disciples taught, and the portion the Buddha himself taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond the Buddha himself, his noble disciples too count as genuine speakers of the teaching — exactly the disciples' own teachings and the Tathāgata's own teachings recorded across their respective recitations.

13. Section: What Is Spoken

Whether what is to be known or the very faculty that knows it, this is what is spoken — as with the five grasping aggregates, the six bases, the portion on dependent origination, and the portion on the path-factors.

Chinese Source

若所了知、若能了知，是所說。如五取蘊、六處、因緣相應分及道品分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether what is to be known or the very faculty that knows it, this is what is spoken — as with the five grasping aggregates, the six bases, the portion on dependent origination, and the portion on the path-factors.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

What is spoken divides in two: the object to be known, namely the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements taught across their own recitations, together with dependent origination taught in the mixed-causes recitation; and the knowing faculty itself, namely the wisdom cultivated through the path-factors recitation's own foundations of mindfulness, right efforts, and bases of power, since only by cultivating the noble path can a practitioner truly know the object contemplated. In short, what is to be known is the object of contemplation; what does the knowing is the noble path itself.

14. Section: Whom It Is Spoken For — Closing the Threefold Essential Meaning Entirely

Whether monastics or Māra's own assembly, this is whom it is spoken for — as with the compilation chapter.

Chinese Source

若諸苾芻、天魔等眾，是所為說。如結集品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether monastics or Māra's own assembly, this is whom it is spoken for — as with the compilation chapter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha taught according to each of the eight assemblies' own capacity — monks, Māra, Śakra, and the rest — exactly the eight-assemblies recitation's own many correspondences, each teaching pitched to its own particular audience. With this, the threefold essential meaning — the one who speaks, what is spoken, and whom it is spoken for — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement naming the Saṃyukta Āgama.

15. Section: Naming the Saṃyukta Āgama — Closing Its Full Account Entirely

All of this, broadly and briefly setting forth the one who speaks, what is spoken, and whom it is spoken for — this same teaching on correspondence with matters, interspersed in due sequence and gathered together — is therefore named the Saṃyukta Āgama.

Chinese Source

如是一切，麤略標舉能說、所說及所為說，即彼一切事相應教間廁鳩集，是故說名雜阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

All of this, broadly and briefly setting forth the one who speaks, what is spoken, and whom it is spoken for — this same teaching on correspondence with matters, interspersed in due sequence and gathered together — is therefore named the Saṃyukta Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing Yin Shun directly

"Interspersed in due sequence" describes how the Saṃyukta Āgama's own many teachings — those on who speaks, spoken by the Tathāgata's own recitation and his disciples' recitation, and those on what is spoken, sorted by aggregates, sense-bases, mixed causes, and path-factors in turn — were gathered by shared theme into an orderly sequence, sūtras on the aggregates grouped together, sūtras on dependent origination grouped together, and so on through the noble truths and the eighteen elements, everything interspersed yet ultimately unified into a single coherent whole. "Mixed" here does not mean disordered but abundant and interwoven — Yin Shun's own Combined Edition notes that in Chinese, this same character need not mean chaos at all; "interspersed" precisely captures its intended sense of orderly interweaving. With this, the Saṃyukta Āgama — its correspondences, its compilation, its threefold essential meaning, and this concluding statement of its own name — is closed in its entirety, completing the first of the four āgamas. The treatise turns next to the Madhyama Āgama.

16. Section: The Madhyama Āgama — Closing the Second Āgama

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken again through further marks at middling length, is therefore named the Madhyama Āgama.

Chinese Source

即彼相應教，復以餘相處中而說，是故說名中阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken again through further marks at middling length, is therefore named the Madhyama Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All four āgamas ultimately teach the very same aggregates, sense-bases, elements, and dependent origination, corresponding to the same ultimate truth — yet since listeners' own capacities differ, the same content is delivered at different lengths. The Madhyama Āgama's own sūtras run neither especially brief nor especially long, a middling length, hence its name; in content, it dwells at particular length on meditative concentration and its methods of cultivation. With this, the Madhyama Āgama is closed, completing the second of the four āgamas. The treatise turns next to the Dīrgha Āgama.

17. Section: The Dīrgha Āgama — Closing the Third Āgama

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through further marks at great length, is therefore named the Dīrgha Āgama.

Chinese Source

即彼相應教，更以餘相廣長而說，是故說名長阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through further marks at great length, is therefore named the Dīrgha Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Certain portions of the same teaching required considerably more length to unpack — the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, for instance, recounts the Buddha's own life at length, from his birth through his teaching of disciples — and such extended treatments were gathered into the Dīrgha Āgama, named simply for its own greater length, its content thorough and detailed yet never exceeding the Saṃyukta Āgama's own foundational scope. With this, the Dīrgha Āgama is closed, completing the third of the four āgamas. The treatise turns next to the Ekottara Āgama.

18. Section: The Ekottara Āgama — Closing All Four Āgamas in Their Entirety

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through the reasoning of gradually increasing categories — one, two, three, and so on — is therefore named the Ekottara Āgama.

Chinese Source

即彼相應教，更以一二三等漸增分數道理而說，是故說名增一阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through the reasoning of gradually increasing categories — one, two, three, and so on — is therefore named the Ekottara Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Ekottara Āgama likewise rests on the Saṃyukta Āgama's own foundation, drawing out whatever content lends itself to numerical extension — wholesomeness itself, for instance, unpacked first as a single overall category, freedom from fault, then divided into two, then three, four, five, six, and onward through ten or eleven distinct kinds, the same underlying principle examined through gradually increasing enumeration; this same treatise itself often teaches in exactly this incremental style. Among the four āgamas, the Saṃyukta Āgama is root and origin, earliest of all, while the remaining three each further explain and extend it — content of middling length forming the Madhyama Āgama, more extensively worded content forming the Dīrgha Āgama, and content expressed through gradually increasing numerical categories forming the Ekottara Āgama, yet none departing from what the Saṃyukta Āgama itself already established. With this, the Ekottara Āgama is closed, completing all four āgamas — the Saṃyukta, Madhyama, Dīrgha, and Ekottara alike — in their entirety. The treatise turns next to explaining the term āgama itself.

19. Section: Explaining the Term Āgama — Closing Its Own Meaning

These four kinds, passed down from teacher to disciple in unbroken succession, have arrived down to the present day. Through this very principle, they are therefore called āgama.

Chinese Source

如是四種，師弟展轉傳來於今。由此道理，是故說名阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

These four kinds, passed down from teacher to disciple in unbroken succession, have arrived down to the present day. Through this very principle, they are therefore called āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Āgama means "transmission," carrying the sense of unbroken succession, teacher instructing disciple across generations — precisely why these four collections, handed down from the Buddha and his noble disciples all the way to the present, bear this shared name. With this, the term āgama itself is closed, its own meaning fully explained. The treatise turns next to a concluding summary naming sūtras of narrative matter.

20. Section: A Concluding Summary — Closing Sūtras of Narrative Matter Entirely

This is called sūtras of narrative matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As the treatise's own earlier catalogue named it, sūtras of narrative matter form the second of the twenty-four kinds of sūtra — namely the four āgamas just explained in full. With this, sūtras of narrative matter is closed in its entirety, completing the second of the four kinds explained in detail. The treatise turns next to sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle and the great vehicle together, the third and fourth kinds.

21. Section: Śrāvaka and Great Vehicle Sūtras Begin — What Each Comprises

Among the twelvefold division of the canon, apart from the extended teachings, the rest are named sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle. The extended teachings themselves are named sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The canon's twelve textual genres sort here into two groups: the extended teachings (方廣, vaipulya) alone comprise sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle, while sūtra, verse teaching, prophecy, verse, spontaneous utterance, causal narrative, past-life account, birth-story, unprecedented dharma, parable, and discursive treatise together comprise sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle.

22. Section: A Reference to Fuller Treatment — Closing Śrāvaka and Great Vehicle Sūtras, and All Four Kinds Explained in Detail

This same distinction should be understood exactly as already explained before.

Chinese Source

此分別義，如前應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

This same distinction should be understood exactly as already explained before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 38 and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Fascicle 38, in the Bodhisattva Stage, already taught this same division: the extended teachings alone belong to the bodhisattva basket, while every remaining genre belongs to the śrāvaka basket. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes record several further accounts of how the twelve genres divide between the two vehicles: the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 3, assigns nine genres to the great vehicle, omitting only causal narrative, parable, and discursive treatise; the Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 1, assigns nine to the śrāvaka vehicle, omitting only extended teachings, spontaneous utterance, and prophecy; and the Compendium of Mixed Topics offers a fourfold sorting instead — sūtra, verse teaching, prophecy, verse, and spontaneous utterance belong to the śrāvaka basket's sūtra division; causal narrative, parable, past-life account, and birth-story belong to the vinaya division shared by both vehicles; extended teachings and unprecedented dharma belong to the bodhisattva basket's own sūtra division; and discursive treatise belongs to the abhidharma shared by both — noting, further, that in truth both the greater and lesser baskets each contain all twelve genres in full, these various groupings simply emphasizing different angles of classification. With this, śrāvaka and great vehicle sūtras is closed in its entirety, completing all four of the twenty-four kinds explained in detail — the Prātimokṣa Sūtra, sūtras of narrative matter, and sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka and great vehicles alike. The treatise turns next to the remaining twenty kinds, left to the reader's own inference.

As for these four kinds of sūtra, the remaining twenty kinds — sūtras that render explicit a meaning not yet made explicit, and the rest — their marks should each be understood accordingly, in the same manner.

With this, the full catalogue of twenty-four kinds of sūtra, together with detailed treatment of the first four, is closed in its entirety, completing sūtra matters' own opening account. 24. Section: A Detailed Examination of the Three Sources Begins — Why the Sūtra-Commentary Was Composed

從是已後，依此所說四種契經，當說契經摩怛理迦，為欲決擇如來所說、如來所稱、所讚、所美先聖契經。

From this point onward, resting on these four kinds of sūtra already explained, the treatise commenting on the sūtras shall now be taught, in order to determine and discern the sūtras of the ancient sages that the Tathāgata himself spoke, endorsed, praised, and commended.

The treatise now turns to examine all three textual sources in careful detail, beginning with the mātṛkā, or root-treatise, that comments on the sūtras themselves — its purpose is precisely to determine and clarify the true intent of the sūtras the Buddha himself spoke and endorsed. The Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 25, defines a sūtra broadly as the World-Honored One, in various places, teaching various beings he was training, according to their own particular conduct, proclaiming boundless discourse corresponding to the aggregates, the sense-bases, dependent origination, food, the truths, the elements, the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, the buddha vehicle, the foundations of mindfulness through the path-factors, and mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith. This sūtra-commentary undertakes to determine and discern exactly this discourse — hence the four major sections that follow: the aggregates section, the sense-bases section, the section on dependent origination together with food, the truths, and the elements, and the section on the factors of awakening — unpacking the four āgamas' own full content in turn.

25. Section: Why It Is Called Mātṛkā

Just as, without the root-mother of writing, meaning remains unclear, so too a sūtra not comprised within its own root-mother remains obscure, its meaning unclear. Where this is not so, meaning becomes clear — hence it is called mātṛkā.

Chinese Source

譬如無本母字，義不明了。如是本母所不攝經，其義隱昧，義不明了。與此相違，義即明了，是故說名摩怛理迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as, without the root-mother of writing, meaning remains unclear, so too a sūtra not comprised within its own root-mother remains obscure, its meaning unclear. Where this is not so, meaning becomes clear — hence it is called mātṛkā.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Mātṛkā means "root-mother," the mother of wisdom itself — without such a root-treatise to draw out its structure, a sūtra's own text and meaning are far from easy to grasp; portions of the Saṃyukta Āgama, read alone, often remain genuinely obscure. Only by studying this very fascicle alongside the Saṃyukta Āgama itself does its meaning become truly accessible — had Maitreya never composed this treatise to unlock it, disciples would be left to interpret the bare text unaided, its most profound, hidden meaning forever out of reach; with a treatise to illuminate it, that same meaning becomes clear and wisdom can genuinely arise in the reader — precisely why this kind of commentary earns the name mātṛkā, root-mother of the text it explains.

27. Section: Element Begins — Its Own Particular Summary Verse

The particular summary verse says: element, teaching, preparation, observation, and result; delusion's own mark, the impermanence that fixes the boundary of element; the two kinds of gradual sequence should be known; neither cut off nor permanent, and defilement and purity.

Chinese Source

別嗢柁南曰：界說前行觀察果　愚相無常等定界

二種漸次應當知　非斷非常及染淨

Ru-Yi Translation

The particular summary verse says: element, teaching, preparation, observation, and result; delusion's own mark, the impermanence that fixes the boundary of element; the two kinds of gradual sequence should be known; neither cut off nor permanent, and defilement and purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's first topic, element, unfolds across eleven further parts, this verse naming each: element, teaching, preparation, observation, result, delusion's own mark, the impermanence that fixes element's boundary, element again, the two kinds of gradual sequence, neither cut off nor permanent, and defilement and purity. Maitreya explains the opening five sūtras of the Saṃyukta Āgama's own aggregates recitation through the first three of these alone — element, teaching, and preparation. Element carries the sense of a seed: the Buddha spoke these five sūtras because four kinds of being carry four kinds of wrong view, each needing right view — observing the five aggregates as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — to counter it. Teaching names the three features that distinguish the Buddha's own teaching from every non-Buddhist path: genuine liberation itself, the means to it, and the personal realization that follows once it is reached — contemplating the five aggregates through impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, both in their full extent and in their true nature, leads a practitioner all the way to genuine, ultimate liberation. And preparation names the preliminary practice leading up to the path of seeing, and the preliminary practice leading up to arhatship itself.

28. Section: The Prose Explanation Begins — Element's Wrong Kind — a Question Posed

There are four kinds of being trained who have, through long habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation. What are the four?

有四種所化有情先數習邪解脫見所集成界。

何等為四？

There are four kinds of being trained who have, through long habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation. What are the four?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The prose explanation unfolds across eleven sections matching the verse's own eleven parts, the first of which, element, first distinguishes its own wrong kind. Four kinds of being, prior to gaining right view, have habituated a wrong view of liberation in a past life or earlier in this one, forming four distinct seeds of wrong element — the treatise poses the question of what these four are before answering it in turn.

29. Section: The First Wrong View — Eternalism

The first: resting on a prior existence, a prior life, a prior body, or a prior attainment of one's own being, having heard a teaching dominated by the view of eternalism, and through the ascendant power of improper attention, now, through that very view as cause and that very attention as condition, one has, through repeated habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation.

Chinese Source

謂於先有、先世、先身、先所得自體中，聽聞常見增上不正法、不如理作意增上力故，於今由彼為因、由彼為緣，數習邪解脫見所集成界。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first: resting on a prior existence, a prior life, a prior body, or a prior attainment of one's own being, having heard a teaching dominated by the view of eternalism, and through the ascendant power of improper attention, now, through that very view as cause and that very attention as condition, one has, through repeated habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first wrong view, eternalism, takes root when a being, in some prior existence, life, body, or resultant state, hears and absorbs a teaching dominated by the view that self and world are permanent, giving rise to improper attention that reinforces it — through the combined power of that view as cause and that attention as condition, repeated habituation gathers together a seed, an "element," of this same wrong view of liberation, carried forward into the present.

30. Section: The Remaining Three Wrong Views — Annihilationism, Present-Life Nirvāṇa, and the View of a Perishable Self — Closing Element's Wrong Kind Entirely

As is said of eternalism, so too, at length, should annihilationism, the view of nirvāṇa in this very life, and the view of a perishable self be understood.

Chinese Source

如說由常見，如是由斷見、由現法涅槃見、由薩迦耶見，廣說亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

As is said of eternalism, so too, at length, should annihilationism, the view of nirvāṇa in this very life, and the view of a perishable self be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Annihilationism clings to the opposite extreme, that self and world are cut off entirely at death or eventual cosmic dissolution, with nothing whatsoever continuing after. The view of nirvāṇa in this very life takes one of two forms: some non-Buddhist ascetics, materialists among them, take present enjoyment of the five sense pleasures itself to be nirvāṇa, and so indulge them without restraint; others mistake the joy, ease, or equanimity attained through meditative absorption for the noble fruit itself — traditionally enumerated as five distinct versions of this same wrong view, tied to enjoyment in the desire realm and to each of the four form-realm absorptions in turn. And the view of a perishable self is simply the view of self, root of the sixty-two speculative views tradition catalogues, here reduced to these same four broad categories: eternalism, annihilationism, the view of present-life nirvāṇa, and the view of a perishable self. With this, element's own wrong kind — all four views, eternalism and its three companions — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to how the Buddha subdues each of these four wrong elements in turn.

31. Section: Subduing These Views Begins — a General Statement

Here, the World-Honored One, through the ascendant power of his manifold wisdom regarding resolve and his manifold wisdom regarding elements, seeks out a being's own prior resolve and its resulting element, and, according to what is fitting, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element, for the most part turns the wheel of four kinds of teaching.

Chinese Source

此中世尊，由種種勝解智力、種種界智力增上力故，尋求彼先勝解及彼後界，如其所應，為調伏彼邪勝解界故，多分為轉四種法教。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the World-Honored One, through the ascendant power of his manifold wisdom regarding resolve and his manifold wisdom regarding elements, seeks out a being's own prior resolve and its resulting element, and, according to what is fitting, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element, for the most part turns the wheel of four kinds of teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Possessing the wisdom to know every being's own disposition and the wisdom to know every being's own underlying element, the Buddha, in meditative absorption, directly perceives a being's habituated wrong view from a past life together with its seed still present in the mind today, and, matching his teaching to what that being needs, proclaims four teachings — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — to counter eternalism, annihilationism, the view of present-life nirvāṇa, and the view of a perishable self in turn. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm these same four teachings are meant, applied respectively to subdue each of the four wrong views named above.

32. Section: Countering Eternalism — Teaching Impermanence Through the Cessation of Formations

For beings fallen into the first wrong element, it is taught that because the cause ceases, formations cease — through this approach of formations' own exhaustion, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為初邪界有情，說因滅故行滅，由行盡門說無常性，為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the first wrong element, it is taught that because the cause ceases, formations cease — through this approach of formations' own exhaustion, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

To subdue a being habituated to eternalism, the Buddha teaches impermanence through the angle of cessation: once affliction, the cause, ceases, the formations it produces cease as well — this ceasing, that ceases — directly dismantling the belief that self or world persists unchanged.

33. Section: Countering Annihilationism — Teaching Impermanence Through the Arising of Formations

For beings fallen into the second wrong element, it is taught that because the cause gathers, formations gather — through this approach of formations' own arising, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為墮第二邪界有情，說因集故行集，由行起門說無常性，為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the second wrong element, it is taught that because the cause gathers, formations gather — through this approach of formations' own arising, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Against the belief that death is like a lamp snuffed out, the Buddha teaches impermanence through the opposite angle, arising: this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises — so long as affliction's own cause remains, formations continue to form and the five aggregates continue to arise in succession, death settling nothing at all; where the first teaching stressed "this ceasing, that ceases" against eternalism, this one stresses "this arising, that arises" against annihilationism, each countering its own particular error.

34. Section: Countering the View of Present-Life Nirvāṇa — Teaching Formations as Suffering

For beings fallen into the third wrong element, the true dharma is turned through the approach of every formation's own suffering, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為墮第三邪界有情，由諸行苦門轉正法教，為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the third wrong element, the true dharma is turned through the approach of every formation's own suffering, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Against the mistaken belief that pleasant feeling itself, or the joy and ease of meditative absorption, constitutes genuine nirvāṇa, the Buddha teaches that every formation, every feeling, is suffering — joy and pleasure are themselves the suffering of change, and even neutral feeling is the suffering inherent in all conditioned things; none of it is nirvāṇa at all.

35. Section: Countering the View of a Perishable Self — Teaching Emptiness or No-Self According to the Kind of Self-View — Closing the Four Teachings and Element Entirely

For beings fallen into the fourth wrong element: for those whose view of a perishable self arises apart from the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of the formations' own emptiness; for those whose view of a perishable self arises identified with the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of no-self — in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為墮第四邪界有情，若離諸行起薩迦耶見行者，由諸行空門轉正法教；若即諸行起薩迦耶見行者，由無我門轉正法教；為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the fourth wrong element: for those whose view of a perishable self arises apart from the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of the formations' own emptiness; for those whose view of a perishable self arises identified with the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of no-self — in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The view of self takes two shapes: one clings to a self existing apart from the five aggregates entirely, unrelated to them — for such a person, the Buddha teaches that formations beyond the five aggregates are likewise impermanent and empty, no fixed, unchanging self to be found anywhere; the other clings to the five aggregates themselves as self — for such a person, the Buddha teaches that the aggregates, arising from causes and conditions, are impermanent and never self-governing, and being impermanent, cannot be self, no unchanging self to be found within them either. With this, all four teachings matched to the four wrong views are closed in their entirety, completing element's own first section — both its wrong kind, fourfold, and its subduing, general and specific alike — in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to teaching, the second of the eleven parts.

36. Section: Teaching Begins — Three Features Unique to the Buddha's Own Dharma and Discipline

Further, a well-taught dharma and discipline rests, in brief, on three features unique to it, not shared with any non-Buddhist path, and thereby falls within the category of what is well taught. First, it teaches genuine, ultimate liberation. Second, it teaches the very means to it. Third, it teaches personal realization, inwardly witnessed, of that same liberation.

Chinese Source

復次，善說法律略由三種不共支故，不共外道，墮善說數。一者、宣說真實究竟解脫故；二者、宣說即彼方便故；三者、宣說即彼自內所證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, a well-taught dharma and discipline rests, in brief, on three features unique to it, not shared with any non-Buddhist path, and thereby falls within the category of what is well taught. First, it teaches genuine, ultimate liberation. Second, it teaches the very means to it. Third, it teaches personal realization, inwardly witnessed, of that same liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's second part, teaching, names three marks distinguishing the Buddha's own teaching from every non-Buddhist path. First, genuine and ultimate liberation: stream-entry is genuine liberation, arhatship is ultimate liberation, and contemplating the five aggregates through impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, as the Buddha taught, leads a practitioner to both in turn. Second, the very means to it: right view and right reflection, correctly contemplating or reflecting on the five aggregates through these same four aspects, serve as the preliminary practice through which liberation is actually reached. And third, personal, inwardly-witnessed realization: a practitioner who genuinely practices in accordance with the dharma personally realizes the noble path directly — the Buddha, never speaking falsely, guarantees that whoever practices exactly as taught will certainly realize the path and attain liberation.

37. Section: Unpacking Each Feature Begins — What Is Genuine and Ultimate Liberation?

What is genuine and ultimate liberation?

云何真實究竟解脫？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is genuine and ultimate liberation?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having named the three unique features, the treatise now unpacks each individually across three further sections, beginning with a direct question: what, precisely, constitutes genuine and ultimate liberation?

38. Section: Two Kinds of Liberation Named

This means ultimate liberation and liberation in every respect — namely, the fruit of the path of seeing, and, following after it, the fruit of the path of cultivation, worldly and transcendent alike.

Chinese Source

謂畢竟解脫及一切解脫，即是見道果，及此後所得世出世修道果。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means ultimate liberation and liberation in every respect — namely, the fruit of the path of seeing, and, following after it, the fruit of the path of cultivation, worldly and transcendent alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Genuine liberation names ultimate liberation, the fruit of the path of seeing — an ordinary being's own affliction genuinely overcome, freedom from the three evil destinies' own suffering truly realized and never again lost, stream-entry itself. Yet this is not yet complete: a practitioner must continue cultivating insight through the path of cultivation, worldly and transcendent, advancing through the second and third fruits to arhatship, liberation in every respect, every affliction without exception finally released.

39. Section: A Further Distinction — Why Stream-Entry Is Genuine Yet Not Ultimate — Closing the Twofold Account of Liberation

Here, the fruit of the path of seeing, through being ultimate in its own respect, is named genuine — yet it is not ultimate in every respect, since there still remains something further to be done regarding liberation in every respect.

Chinese Source

此中見道果，由畢竟故得名真實，而非究竟，於一切解脫猶有所應作故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the fruit of the path of seeing, through being ultimate in its own respect, is named genuine — yet it is not ultimate in every respect, since there still remains something further to be done regarding liberation in every respect.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A stream-enterer, never again to fall back into an ordinary being, is genuinely liberated — the distinctly-arisen seeds of affliction are truly cut off, never to arise again. Yet sixteen innately-arisen afflictions of craving and view remain: six in the desire realm (the view of a perishable self, extreme views, craving, hatred, conceit, and ignorance — every distinctly-arisen affliction already removed), and five each in the form and formless realms (the same list minus hatred) — all sixteen still awaiting the path of cultivation's own further work. A stream-enterer must therefore continue cultivating calm and insight to remove what remains — genuine liberation, yet not the ultimate liberation only an arhat achieves. With this, the twofold account of liberation — ultimate liberation and liberation in every respect — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a threefold account of liberation.

40. Section: A Threefold Account of Liberation Begins — Three Kinds Named

And further, liberation is of three kinds: first, worldly liberation; second, a trainee's liberation; third, liberation beyond training.

Chinese Source

又解脫有三種。一、世間解脫，二、有學解脫，三、無學解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, liberation is of three kinds: first, worldly liberation; second, a trainee's liberation; third, liberation beyond training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second, threefold sorting of liberation now follows the twofold account just closed, naming worldly liberation, a trainee's liberation, and liberation beyond training in turn.

41. Section: Distinguishing the Three — Only the One Beyond Training Possesses Both Genuine and Ultimate Liberation — Closing Genuine and Ultimate Liberation Entirely

Worldly liberation is not genuine, since it is subject to regression. A trainee's liberation, though genuine, is not ultimate, since something further still remains to be done. It should be known that what remains possesses both.

Chinese Source

世間解脫非是真實，有退轉故。有學解脫雖是真實，而非究竟，猶有所作故。當知所餘，具足二種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Worldly liberation is not genuine, since it is subject to regression. A trainee's liberation, though genuine, is not ultimate, since something further still remains to be done. It should be known that what remains possesses both.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Worldly liberation names meditative attainment — form-realm absorption frees one from the desire realm's own affliction, formless attainment frees one from both desire and form; yet this liberation only suppresses affliction's active manifestation, never uprooting its underlying seed, and once concentration itself is lost, the practitioner falls back to the desire realm once more — not genuine liberation at all. A trainee — the first through third fruits — genuinely cuts off the distinctly-arisen afflictions by their very root, like pulling a weed out whole, where worldly liberation merely mows the grass down to the soil, leaving the root to sprout again after the next rain; yet even this genuine cutting falls short of the ultimate, since the remaining innately-arisen afflictions still call for further diligent practice. Only what remains — liberation beyond training, arhatship itself — possesses both together, every affliction of the three realms, distinctly-arisen and innately-arisen alike, cut off entirely; only this liberation beyond training is truly ultimate. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm that "what remains" here refers specifically to liberation beyond training. With this, genuine and ultimate liberation, examined through both the twofold and threefold accounts, is closed in its entirety, completing the first of teaching's three unique features. The treatise turns next to the means that leads to it.

42. Section: The Means to Liberation Begins — a Question Posed

What is the means?

Chinese Source

云何方便？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the means?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Teaching's second unique feature poses directly what method actually leads a practitioner to genuine and ultimate liberation alike.

43. Section: The Means to Genuine Liberation — Contemplating Formations Through Their Full Extent and True Nature

This means that, among the formations, resting on their true nature and their full extent, one cultivates the perception of impermanence; resting on impermanence, one cultivates the perception of suffering; resting on suffering, one cultivates the perception of emptiness and no-self — through this, upon entering the direct realization of the truths, through correctly observing the object to be known, right view is attained.

Chinese Source

謂於諸行中，依如所有性及盡所有性，修無常想；依無常，修苦想；依苦，修空、無我想；因此得入諦現觀時，由正觀察所知境故，獲得正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that, among the formations, resting on their true nature and their full extent, one cultivates the perception of impermanence; resting on impermanence, one cultivates the perception of suffering; resting on suffering, one cultivates the perception of emptiness and no-self — through this, upon entering the direct realization of the truths, through correctly observing the object to be known, right view is attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations' Bodhisattva Stage

The means to genuine, stream-entry liberation rests on contemplating the five aggregates through both their true nature and their full extent. True nature contemplates the aggregates' own shared mark — impermanent, suffering, empty, without self — the Determinations, Bodhisattva Stage, fascicle 77, defining true nature as suchness itself, present in every defiled and pure dharma alike: reflecting on impermanence gives rise to suffering, genuinely felt; resting on suffering, emptiness follows — no self apart from the aggregates to be found (empty), and no governing, permanent function within the aggregates either (no-self). Full extent instead contemplates every possible instance of the aggregates without exception — past, present, or future, fine or coarse, superior or inferior, near or far, desire-realm or form- or formless-realm — every single one alike impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self. Cultivating these four perceptions in meditation leads a practitioner into direct realization of the four noble truths; this account specifically describes the truth of suffering's own contemplation at the level of forbearance and supreme worldly dharma, the final steps before the path of seeing itself — a practitioner still at the level of heat or summit must additionally contemplate the truth of origination through cause, arising, and condition, the truth of the path through path, suchness, accomplishment, and deliverance, and the truth of cessation through cessation, peace, excellence, and freedom, the complete preparatory contemplation leading up to the path of seeing.

44. Section: The Means to Ultimate Liberation Begins — Dwelling in the Perception of Aversion

Resting on this very right view as support, within the path of cultivation, one dwells pervasively in the perception of aversion toward every formation.

Chinese Source

由此正見為依止故，修道位中，遍於諸行住厭逆想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on this very right view as support, within the path of cultivation, one dwells pervasively in the perception of aversion toward every formation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The means to ultimate liberation, arhatship, unfolds across three further steps. Resting on the right view already gained through contemplating the aggregates' true nature and full extent, a trainee at the path of cultivation continues reflecting on the aggregates as sickness, an abscess, an arrow, and a source of harm, dwelling pervasively in aversion toward them — no longer delighting in the aggregates, turning against them, no longer wishing for the resultant fruit of further birth-and-death.

45. Section: Extinguishing the Active Manifestation of Joy and Craving Begins — While Dwelling in Stillness

While dwelling in that state, though feeling correspondent to it, recollecting, and reflecting on an object not currently present, brings it vividly before the mind, joy does not arise.

Chinese Source

彼於住時，雖由彼相應受，憶念思惟不現前境明了現前，而不生喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

While dwelling in that state, though feeling correspondent to it, recollecting, and reflecting on an object not currently present, brings it vividly before the mind, joy does not arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second step, extinguishing the active manifestation of joy and craving, unfolds first while dwelling in stillness: even deliberately calling to mind some pleasing object of the five aggregates during seated calm and insight, vividly present though not actually before the senses, no attachment or delight arises — the aversion cultivated through right view holds firm even under deliberate testing, no longer drawn toward the impure, unlovely body or its pleasures. This corresponds to the application of attention called examination, among the seven applications of attention.

46. Section: While in Motion — No Defiled Attachment Arises Even Toward What Is Genuinely Present

Through the ascendant power of no joy arising, while in motion, toward the very object that feeling takes as its support, no defiled attachment arises. Toward every object one might seek, having attained equanimity, one does not even long for it, let alone indulge it.

Chinese Source

由不生喜增上力故，彼於行時，即於彼受所緣境界不生染著。彼於一切所求境界，得處中故，尚不希求，何況耽著。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the ascendant power of no joy arising, while in motion, toward the very object that feeling takes as its support, no defiled attachment arises. Toward every object one might seek, having attained equanimity, one does not even long for it, let alone indulge it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Where the previous step concerned deliberately-imagined objects during seated stillness, this step concerns genuinely encountered objects during ordinary activity — walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, a practitioner may actually meet a pleasing circumstance, yet the same firmly-rooted aversion, strengthened by the absence of joy already cultivated, prevents any defiled attachment from arising toward it, self or other alike. Toward whatever the world prizes — lasting youth, a long life — a practitioner who knows it is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, mastering neither it nor the desire for it, remains in equanimity, neither seeking nor rejecting, resting always in the highest equanimity — not even longing for such fine circumstances, let alone becoming attached to them. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss equanimity as dwelling evenly within a neutral, unbiased state. Whether in motion or at rest, this practice extinguishes the active manifestation of joy and craving alike.

47. Section: A Summary Statement — Closing Extinguishing Joy and Craving's Active Manifestation Entirely

Through dwelling and moving in this way, one swiftly extinguishes the entanglement of joy and craving entirely, the mind abiding in purity.

Chinese Source

彼由如是若住、若行，於喜貪纏速能滅盡，心清淨住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through dwelling and moving in this way, one swiftly extinguishes the entanglement of joy and craving entirely, the mind abiding in purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Through this sustained training in both stillness and activity, the active manifestation of joy and craving is swiftly and thoroughly extinguished, the mind settling into lasting purity — mindfulness strengthened, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self genuinely realized, nothing left in the world worth chasing or being troubled by. With this, extinguishing the active manifestation of joy and craving — both while dwelling and while in motion — is closed in its entirety, completing the second of ultimate liberation's three steps. The treatise turns next to permanently uprooting the latent tendency itself.

48. Section: Permanently Uprooting the Latent Tendency — Closing the Means to Both Genuine and Ultimate Liberation Entirely

And, resting on that very path already attained, through the cause and condition of cultivating it most thoroughly, one permanently uproots the coarse latent tendency belonging to that class of affliction, attaining genuine and ultimate liberation — this should be known as the mind's own excellent liberation.

Chinese Source

又即於彼如所得道極多修習為因緣故，永拔彼品麤重隨眠，獲得真實究竟解脫，當知即是心善解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

And, resting on that very path already attained, through the cause and condition of cultivating it most thoroughly, one permanently uproots the coarse latent tendency belonging to that class of affliction, attaining genuine and ultimate liberation — this should be known as the mind's own excellent liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As a practitioner's own path-power deepens through repeated cultivation of the same contemplation — the aggregates' true nature and full extent, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, applied again and again, not once or twice but countless times over — the coarse, incapable latent tendency of innate desire and craving is finally uprooted entirely, its very seed extinguished, genuine and ultimate liberation both attained at once: arhatship, the mind's own excellent liberation. The content contemplated never changes, only the depth of realization — reading a text for the first time and the tenth time differ profoundly, though the words themselves are identical; speaking a teaching once and speaking it a hundred times carry entirely different power. With this, the means to both genuine and ultimate liberation — contemplation's own true nature and full extent, dwelling in aversion, extinguishing joy and craving's active manifestation, and permanently uprooting its latent tendency — is closed in its entirety, completing teaching's second unique feature. The treatise turns next to personal, inwardly-witnessed realization, the third unique feature.

49. Section: Personal, Inwardly-Witnessed Realization Begins — a Question Posed

What is personal, inwardly-witnessed realization?

Chinese Source

云何自內所證？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is personal, inwardly-witnessed realization?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Teaching's third and final unique feature poses the question of what a practitioner actually experiences, personally and directly, upon genuinely realizing the path.

50. Section: Four Marks Named

It should be known that there are four marks.

Chinese Source

當知有四種相。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that there are four marks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Realizing the path, from stream-entry through arhatship, carries four distinct marks, each to be examined by whether it belongs to a trainee or to one beyond training.

51. Section: The Trainee's Stage Begins — Two Marks Named

When turning within a trainee's own liberation, inward wisdom directly touches and confirms two marks.

Chinese Source

若於有學解脫轉時，由二種相，內慧觸證。

Ru-Yi Translation

When turning within a trainee's own liberation, inward wisdom directly touches and confirms two marks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The two positions distinguished here, trainee and one beyond training, are read two ways by the tradition: the Yogācārabhūmi Notes take "trainee" as the first fruit specifically, matching the surrounding contrast with arhatship; the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation instead reads it as spanning the first three fruits together. Since the passages before and after both concern the specific contrast between stream-entry and arhatship, the noble path divides here into two broad stages — stream-entry marking the pivotal shift from ordinary being to noble one, and arhatship marking the śrāvaka path's own complete fulfillment — following the Yogācārabhūmi Notes' own angle of reading.

52. Section: The Two Marks Explained — Freedom From the Evil Destinies, and an Unregressing Path

This means: I have exhausted every formation that would otherwise arise within the evil destinies. And I have exhausted every remaining formation of future existence apart from seven more births, two more births, or one more birth — and I now abide in an unregressing path that will bring this to its ultimate end.

Chinese Source

謂我已盡諸惡趣中所生諸行，又我已盡除其七生、二生、一生所餘後有所生諸行，又我已住能究竟盡無退轉道。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means: I have exhausted every formation that would otherwise arise within the evil destinies. And I have exhausted every remaining formation of future existence apart from seven more births, two more births, or one more birth — and I now abide in an unregressing path that will bring this to its ultimate end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation

A stream-enterer directly knows, through personal realization, that the three evil destinies are forever closed — not through deliberate wisdom cutting them off, but simply because the cause that would lead there no longer exists, the discriminated affliction that alone could produce such a rebirth already gone, even though innate affliction still remains. Commentators differ on how to read "seven births, two births, one birth, and the remaining formations of future existence." Read through the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation's own account, a stream-enterer, not yet fully free of desire, knows their remaining innate affliction still permits at most seven further rounds of birth between the human and heavenly realms, or two, or one, never an eighth existence — certain, in every case, to reach arhatship, the noble path itself never again subject to regression. All three figures apply specifically to rebirth among humans and gods in the desire realm, corresponding to three traditional patterns: seven returns between the human and heavenly realms before arhatship (the "seven births" case); the "family-to-family" pattern, cutting off three of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction, six grades still remaining, needing at most three further human-heaven-human rounds (the pattern the "two births" phrase points to, in an equal reading — three heavenly and three human rebirths, or three human and three heavenly — or an unequal reading — three heavenly and two human, or three human and two heavenly); and a second family-to-family pattern, cutting off four of the nine grades, five grades remaining, needing at most two further rounds, again divided into an equal reading (two heavenly and two human, or two human and two heavenly, corresponding to "two births") and an unequal reading (two heavenly and one human, or one human and two heavenly, corresponding to "one birth"). The Yogācārabhūmi Notes offer a different, simpler correspondence instead: seven births belongs to the stream-enterer as a whole, two births to the once-returner, and one birth to the non-returner — a cleaner mapping across the first three fruits themselves rather than these more granular family-to-family sub-patterns. With this, the trainee's own stage — its two marks, freedom from the evil destinies and an unregressing path toward at most seven, two, or one further birth in all its detailed patterns — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the stage of the one beyond training.

53. Section: The Stage Beyond Training Begins — Two Marks Named

Arhatship, too, is confirmed through personal realization by two marks of its own, parallel to the trainee's own two — each to be explained in turn.

54. Section: The Two Marks Explained — What Was to Be Trained Is Complete, and Confirming It for Others — Closing the Stage Beyond Training, and Both Stages Entirely

This means: I have accomplished what was to be trained in order to cut off every remaining affliction; I now have not even one further birth remaining, let alone two, let alone seven. And, according to what is fitting, I am also able to confirm this directly and accurately for others.

Chinese Source

謂我已作為斷其餘一切煩惱所應學事，我今尚無餘一生在，況二、況七。又隨所樂，亦能為他如實記別。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means: I have accomplished what was to be trained in order to cut off every remaining affliction; I now have not even one further birth remaining, let alone two, let alone seven. And, according to what is fitting, I am also able to confirm this directly and accurately for others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An arhat knows directly that every task is done, every affliction subject to the path of cultivation entirely cut off, the śrāvaka path's own training complete — birth, aging, sickness, and death across the three realms and five destinies ultimately exhausted, no further existence to come, not even a single rebirth, let alone two or seven. Beyond this self-knowledge, a noble one beyond training can also, according to another practitioner's own progress, genuinely confirm whether they have reached the first, second, or third fruit, or arhatship itself. These two marks, then: an arhat's own knowledge that every affliction a trainee still carries has now been fully removed, the task complete; and the capacity, beyond simply knowing one's own freedom from further existence, to confirm the same for others. With this, the stage beyond training is closed in its entirety, completing both stages, trainee and beyond training, together. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement.

55. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing Personal Realization, and Teaching in Its Complete Entirety

This is what is called personal, inwardly-witnessed realization.

如是名為自內所證。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is what is called personal, inwardly-witnessed realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A trainee at the first, second, or third fruit knows directly that the evil destinies are closed and the noble path secure against regression; an arhat, whose path-power runs deeper still, knows this same freedom and, further, can confirm it accurately in others as well. With this, personal, inwardly-witnessed realization — its four marks across both the trainee's and the one-beyond-training's own stage — is closed in its entirety, completing teaching, the aggregates section's second topic, across all three of its unique features in their complete entirety. The treatise turns next to preparation, the third topic.

56. Section: Preparation Begins — Two Kinds Named

Further, that very liberation carries two kinds of preparatory practice: first, the preparatory practice for seeing; second, the preparatory practice for the path's own fruit.

復次，即彼解脫有二種前行法。一者、見前行法，二者、道果前行法。

Further, that very liberation carries two kinds of preparatory practice: first, the preparatory practice for seeing; second, the preparatory practice for the path's own fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's third topic, preparation, names two stages of preliminary practice: the preparation leading to the path of seeing, stream-entry itself, and the preparation leading beyond it, all the way to arhatship's own fruit.

57. Section: The Preparation for Seeing Explained

The preparatory practice for seeing means that, through the ascendant power of liberation, its means, and personal realization, one gives rise, from hearing another's own voice, to the wondrous, skillful, proper attention accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation — and thereby, not yet having entered the true nature that transcends birth, one is able to enter it, gaining the genuine, transcendent right view that sees things as they truly are.

Chinese Source

見前行法者，謂由解脫及彼方便自內所證增上力故，從他言音，起聞思修所成妙善如理作意，未入正性離生能入正性離生，得如實見出世正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

The preparatory practice for seeing means that, through the ascendant power of liberation, its means, and personal realization, one gives rise, from hearing another's own voice, to the wondrous, skillful, proper attention accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation — and thereby, not yet having entered the true nature that transcends birth, one is able to enter it, gaining the genuine, transcendent right view that sees things as they truly are.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Hearing the Buddha or a noble disciple skillfully teach liberation, its means, and personal realization gives rise, through their combined power, to the wondrous proper attention accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation in turn — resting on calm, contemplating the aggregates' full extent (all eleven marks: past, present, future, near, far, and the rest) and true nature (impermanent, suffering, empty, without self). Diligently cultivated until conditions ripen, this attention carries a practitioner not yet free of birth into freedom from it, genuine undefiled insight and transcendent right view finally attained. "True nature that transcends birth" names the undefiled noble path or nirvāṇa itself as "true nature"; "birth" names either affliction or an immature wholesome root, and "transcending" it means cutting off the discriminated affliction born of wrong view, or leaving behind roots not yet mature enough to bear the noble path. The Determinations, Stage of the Wisdom Born of Hearing, fascicle 64, name five ways of entering this transcendence: not yet free of desire, halfway free of desire, already free of desire, the pratyekabuddha's own entry, and the bodhisattva's own entry. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm that "ascendant power" here names precisely the three features unique to the Buddha's own teaching already explained — liberation, its means, and personal realization. Together, element, teaching, and preparation explain the aggregates recitation's own first five sūtras: the first teaches that observing form as impermanent is itself right view, leading through weariness, freedom from desire, and the mind's own liberation, to the declaration "birth is exhausted, the chaste life established, what was to be done is done, no further existence to be known" — "birth is exhausted" describing the stream-enterer, whose ordinary rebirth now has a final limit of at most seven returns; "the chaste life established" describing the non-returner, free of desire and no longer returning to the desire realm; and "what was to be done is done" describing the arhat, this present life the very last. The second through fourth sūtras repeat the same teaching applied to suffering, emptiness, and no-self in turn, each equally capable of leading to the very same realization. The fifth sūtra shifts from right view to right reflection — even a stream-enterer must continue reflecting properly on form's own impermanence to fully release craving for it, exactly as an ordinary being must, sustained proper reflection alone eventually freeing the mind from all five aggregates in turn.

58. Section: The Preparation for the Path's Own Fruit — Closing Preparation Entirely

The preparatory practice for the path's own fruit means that, having already gained this right view, one further gives rise to right reflection and the rest, whether arising together with it or arising afterward, as preparatory practice for the path, in order to cut off every remaining affliction.

Chinese Source

道果前行法者，謂得如是正見已，復起所餘正思惟等，或同時生、或後時生道前行法，為斷所餘諸煩惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The preparatory practice for the path's own fruit means that, having already gained this right view, one further gives rise to right reflection and the rest, whether arising together with it or arising afterward, as preparatory practice for the path, in order to cut off every remaining affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once the undefiled right view of stream-entry is gained, a practitioner continues generating right reflection, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration — reflecting on the aggregates as sickness, an abscess, and the rest, growing weary of the three realms' own aggregates, these further path-factors arising either together with right view or in its wake, all directed at cutting off whatever affliction remains until arhatship itself is finally reached. With this, preparation — the preparatory practice for seeing and the preparatory practice for the path's own fruit — is closed in its entirety, completing the aggregates section's third topic. The treatise turns next to observation, the fourth topic.

59. Section: Observation Begins — Eight Things to Be Observed

Further, in order to realize a liberation not yet attained, one should observe eight things: observing, among the formations, their relish, their danger, and escape from them; and observing hearing, reflection, the power of discernment, the path of seeing, and the path of cultivation.

Chinese Source

復次，為欲證得所未得解脫故，應觀察八事。謂於諸行中愛味、過患、出離觀察，及聞、思、思擇力、見道、修道觀察。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, in order to realize a liberation not yet attained, one should observe eight things: observing, among the formations, their relish, their danger, and escape from them; and observing hearing, reflection, the power of discernment, the path of seeing, and the path of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of Cultivated Wisdom

The aggregates section's fourth topic names eight things a practitioner must observe in sequence, from ordinary being to noble one: relish taken in the aggregates, the danger that relish conceals, escape from that very relish, and then the wisdom of hearing, of reflection, of discernment (a further sharpening of reflective wisdom capable of examining even worldly cultivated wisdom itself), and finally the path of seeing and the path of cultivation themselves. The Stage of Cultivated Wisdom, fascicle 19, describes escape directly: a practitioner who is able to subdue, cut off, and transcend every craving toward form, and toward every further object of touch, is said to have achieved escape from desire.

60. Section: The First Three Things Begin — Observing Relish

When observing relish among the formations, one becomes able to skillfully penetrate the formations' own particular mark of relish.

Chinese Source

於諸行中觀察愛味時，能善通達諸行愛味所有自相。

Ru-Yi Translation

When observing relish among the formations, one becomes able to skillfully penetrate the formations' own particular mark of relish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Observing whether relish is present toward a pleasing body, pleasing sensory objects, pleasant feeling, endorsed thoughts, a seemingly resolute and self-governing will, and the consciousnesses that discern every object in turn — reflecting on this repeatedly gradually penetrates relish's own particular mark within each of the five aggregates; even the slightest trace of such relish still marks an ordinary being caught in birth-and-death.

61. Section: Observing Danger

When observing danger among those very formations, one becomes able to skillfully know the shared mark of danger present across the three feelings' own distinct conditions — namely, that relish found there is very little, while danger found there is manifold.

Chinese Source

即於諸行觀察過患時，能善了知三受分位過患共相。謂於是中甚少愛味，多諸過患。

Ru-Yi Translation

When observing danger among those very formations, one becomes able to skillfully know the shared mark of danger present across the three feelings' own distinct conditions — namely, that relish found there is very little, while danger found there is manifold.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Observing danger penetrates all three feelings' shared mark as suffering — painful feeling as the suffering of suffering, pleasant feeling as the suffering of change, neutral feeling as the suffering inherent in every conditioned formation; sustained observation of this kind reveals how little the aggregates genuinely offer and how much danger they actually conceal. Where there is a head, there is headache; where there is a heart, it can be wounded; where there is feeling, there is discomfort; where there is perception, grasping at marks and finer characteristics makes right mindfulness difficult to sustain; where there is volition, decisions often go wrong; and where there is consciousness, false discrimination follows, subject and object seemingly split apart, unaware that the object itself is nothing but consciousness's own manifestation — form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness alike offer very little worth wanting and a great deal of danger, pleasure fleeting and pain seemingly endless: truly, very little relish, manifold danger.

62. Section: Observing Escape Begins — Its Distinct Mark Named

Having thus understood relish, defiled clinging, and their manifold shared danger, whatever craving and greed then arises toward any formation once relished can, in that very moment, be dispelled, suppressed, and cut off and abandoned.

Chinese Source

如是了知愛味染著多諸過患共相應已，於所愛味一切行中，隨所生起欲貪煩惱，即能除遣、制伏、斷捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus understood relish, defiled clinging, and their manifold shared danger, whatever craving and greed then arises toward any formation once relished can, in that very moment, be dispelled, suppressed, and cut off and abandoned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once a practitioner sees clearly how relish and defiled clinging are bound up with manifold danger, the very craving that relish produces toward any formation can be dispelled, suppressed, and cut off — though it should be understood that this alone only suppresses affliction's active manifestation, not yet its underlying seed, since this is still an early stage of practice; only deeper path-power can genuinely uproot the seed itself.

63. Section: What "Cutting Off" Actually Means — Closing the First Three Things Observed

Because this craving and greed no longer manifests, it is called cut off — not because desire has been permanently left behind that it is called cut off. And, regarding that very matter, the mind is not yet liberated; only once the latent tendency itself is ultimately transcended is desire permanently left behind, the mind genuinely liberated.

Chinese Source

於此欲貪不現行故，說名為斷，非永離欲故名為斷。又於彼事心未解脫，若於隨眠究竟超越，乃永離欲，心得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because this craving and greed no longer manifests, it is called cut off — not because desire has been permanently left behind that it is called cut off. And, regarding that very matter, the mind is not yet liberated; only once the latent tendency itself is ultimately transcended is desire permanently left behind, the mind genuinely liberated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise clarifies precisely what "cutting off" means at this preliminary stage: craving and greed merely fail to manifest, called cut off in only this limited sense — not that desire is permanently abandoned. Only once the latent seed itself is finally, completely transcended does genuine, permanent freedom from desire arrive, the mind truly liberated at last. With this, the first three of the eight things observed — relish, danger, and escape, together with a careful clarification of what "cutting off" truly means at each stage — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement on these first three.

64. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the First Three Things as a Single Gate of Observation

This is called a single gate of distinguishing observation.

Chinese Source

是名一門觀察差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called a single gate of distinguishing observation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Relish, danger, and escape together form a single unified gate of observation. With this, the first three of the eight things to be observed are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the remaining five, beginning with the wisdom born of hearing.

65. Section: The Remaining Five Things Begin — the Wisdom Born of Hearing

And further, when a practitioner rightly observes those very formations, first, through the wisdom accomplished by hearing, following the āgama, one comes to know that the formations' own nature is impermanent — impermanent, and therefore suffering; suffering, and therefore empty and without self.

Chinese Source

又修行者，於彼諸行正觀察時，先以聞所成慧，如阿笈摩，了知諸行體是無常，無常故苦，苦故空及無我。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, when a practitioner rightly observes those very formations, first, through the wisdom accomplished by hearing, following the āgama, one comes to know that the formations' own nature is impermanent — impermanent, and therefore suffering; suffering, and therefore empty and without self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The remaining five of the eight things observed begin with the wisdom born of hearing — the very first wisdom a practitioner accomplishes, learning from the āgamas themselves that the aggregates are impermanent, a mass of manifold suffering, ungoverned and without self, karma's own cycling alone. This is why the wisdom that abides in the dharma always precedes the wisdom of nirvāṇa — learning must come first; without knowing, understanding, cutting off, and becoming free of desire in turn, liberation is impossible, and knowing itself must come before any of the rest — one who has never even heard the teaching may not even recognize their own fault at all, which is one reason even the most diligent non-Buddhist practitioner cannot reach liberation: without hearing, there is no knowing, no understanding, no cutting off.

66. Section: The Wisdom Born of Reflection

Following the noble teaching, one thus resolves upon it, thus penetrates it. Having penetrated it, one further, through the subtle attention accomplished by inferential reflection, truly comes to know that very object as it is.

Chinese Source

彼隨聖教如是勝解、如是通達。既通達已，復以推度相應思惟所成微細作意，即於彼境如實了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Following the noble teaching, one thus resolves upon it, thus penetrates it. Having penetrated it, one further, through the subtle attention accomplished by inferential reflection, truly comes to know that very object as it is.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Following the Buddha's own teaching, a practitioner resolves upon and penetrates the formations' own impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self through hearing, then further reflects on this same truth through inferential reasoning, arriving at the subtle attention that genuinely knows the object as it is. Hearing's wisdom simply "knows" the terms; reflection's wisdom "clarifies" their meaning more deeply, resolving upon them and then discerning them through reasoned reflection, forming the preliminary attention that leads toward genuine cultivated insight.

67. Section: The Power of Discernment, the Path of Seeing, and the Path of Cultivation Begin — Discernment Itself

Through the ascendant power of this very penetration and understanding, one truly observes the danger, present and future, that accompanies affliction's own manifestation toward that object. Resting on the power of discernment as support, even should affliction arise again, one does not genuinely cling to it, and is thereby able to abandon it.

Chinese Source

即由如是通達了知增上力故，於彼相應煩惱現行現法、當來所有過患，如實觀察。由思擇力為依止故，設復生起而不實著，即能捨離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the ascendant power of this very penetration and understanding, one truly observes the danger, present and future, that accompanies affliction's own manifestation toward that object. Resting on the power of discernment as support, even should affliction arise again, one does not genuinely cling to it, and is thereby able to abandon it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Resting on the strength of penetrating impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, a practitioner genuinely observes the danger affliction's own active manifestation brings, now and in whatever future rebirth it produces — and through the power of discernment this observation cultivates, even should affliction stir again, it is no longer genuinely clung to, swiftly reproved and set aside before it can take hold.

68. Section: The Path of Seeing

Through cultivating this very penetration, understanding, and power of discernment at length, one becomes able to enter the true nature that transcends birth.

Chinese Source

彼由如是通達了知及思擇力多修習故，能入正性離生。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through cultivating this very penetration, understanding, and power of discernment at length, one becomes able to enter the true nature that transcends birth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Through sustained, repeated cultivation of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, together with the power of discernment that sets affliction aside, a practitioner reaches supreme worldly dharma's own final instant and, in that single moment, enters the true nature that transcends birth — the noble path directly touched and confirmed, every distinctly-arisen affliction of the three realms cut off, the evil destinies closed forever.

69. Section: The Path of Cultivation

Having entered the true nature that transcends birth, through the power of the path of cultivation, one gradually becomes free of desire.

Chinese Source

既入正性離生已，由修道力，漸離諸欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having entered the true nature that transcends birth, through the power of the path of cultivation, one gradually becomes free of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

After stream-entry, innate affliction still remains, and the path of cultivation continues the same contemplation — the aggregates as sickness, an abscess, an arrow, and a source of harm — gradually loosening the grip of the three realms' own remaining craving born of relish in the formations. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm that "desire" here means precisely this same craving spanning the three realms.

70. Section: A Further Distinction — How Each Power Cuts Off Affliction Differently

Through these two powers, discernment and the path of seeing, one cuts off whichever affliction is fitting, according to what applies — this meaning that some are cut off through no longer manifesting, and some are cut off in part. Through the power of the path of cultivation, freedom from desire is made ultimate.

Chinese Source

彼由思擇、見道二種力故，隨其所應斷諸煩惱，謂不現行斷故，及一分斷故。由修道力，究竟離欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through these two powers, discernment and the path of seeing, one cuts off whichever affliction is fitting, according to what applies — this meaning that some are cut off through no longer manifesting, and some are cut off in part. Through the power of the path of cultivation, freedom from desire is made ultimate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discernment's power suppresses affliction so it no longer manifests; the path of seeing's power — belonging to one moving toward or having just reached stream-entry — cuts off every distinctly-arisen affliction of the three realms outright, though innate affliction remains; only the path of cultivation's own further power finally brings freedom from desire to completion, all the way to arhatship.

71. Section: A Brief Summary — Closing Observation, the Aggregates Section's Fourth Topic, in Its Entirety

Thus, through the first two powers, one gradually becomes free of craving and desire; through the power of the path of cultivation, the mind attains liberation.

Chinese Source

如是由前二種，漸離欲貪；由修道力，心得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, through the first two powers, one gradually becomes free of craving and desire; through the power of the path of cultivation, the mind attains liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discernment and the path of seeing together only gradually loosen craving and desire, not yet complete liberation; only the path of cultivation's own power, culminating in arhatship, finally liberates the mind from craving and desire entirely. With this, the remaining five of the eight things observed — the wisdom of hearing, the wisdom of reflection, the power of discernment, the path of seeing, and the path of cultivation — are closed in their entirety, completing observation, the aggregates section's fourth topic, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to result, the fifth topic.

72. Section: Result Begins — Two Kinds Named

Further, there are two kinds of result: the result of affliction cut off, and the result of suffering extinguished.

Chinese Source

復次，有二種煩惱斷果及苦滅果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are two kinds of result: the result of affliction cut off, and the result of suffering extinguished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's fifth topic, result, names the fruit that actually follows from the observation just closed — the result of cutting off affliction through the path of seeing and the path of cultivation in turn, and the result of suffering itself finally extinguished.

73. Section: The Result of Cutting Off Affliction Begins — What the Path of Seeing Cuts Off

First, the result of what the path of seeing cuts off. Through realizing it, one is able to know for oneself: I have permanently exhausted the hells, the animal realm, and the hungry ghosts; I have now attained stream-entry's own freedom from regression — and so on, spoken of at length.

Chinese Source

一者、見所斷果。由證彼故，能自了知：我已永盡捺落迦、傍生、餓鬼，我今證得預流無退墮法，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the result of what the path of seeing cuts off. Through realizing it, one is able to know for oneself: I have permanently exhausted the hells, the animal realm, and the hungry ghosts; I have now attained stream-entry's own freedom from regression — and so on, spoken of at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

A practitioner who has cut off the three realms' own distinctly-arisen affliction and entered the true nature that transcends birth knows directly: the aggregates proper to hell, the animal realm, and the hungry ghosts are forever closed to me, never to be taken up again; and stream-entry's own freedom from regression is now attained, never again to fall back into an ordinary being, certain of at most seven more returns between the heavens and the human realm before arhatship's own final fruit. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss "stream-entry's own freedom from regression" as abiding in the unregressing path that brings this to its ultimate end, and gloss "spoken of at length" as the same self-knowledge already explained above — permanently exhausting whatever remains of seven, two, or one further birth.

74. Section: What the Path of Cultivation Cuts Off — Closing the Result of Affliction Cut Off Entirely

Second, the result of what the path of cultivation cuts off. Through realizing it, one is able to know for oneself: this present body alone sustains me for the time being; a second existence and the rest will never again turn.

Chinese Source

二者、修所斷果。由證彼故，能自了知：我最後身暫時支持，第二有等永不復轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the result of what the path of cultivation cuts off. Through realizing it, one is able to know for oneself: this present body alone sustains me for the time being; a second existence and the rest will never again turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes on the term

An arhat knows directly that this present body is the very last, merely sustained a little longer by past karma already set in motion, never to turn toward a second existence again — nirvāṇa with residue. "And the rest" includes birth, aging, death, and every further such suffering — an arhat's own future is entirely free of them, no further round of the seven or eight sufferings ever to arise again. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm: never turning toward a second existence is precisely nirvāṇa with residue. With this, the result of affliction cut off — through the path of seeing and the path of cultivation alike — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the result of suffering extinguished.

75. Section: The Result of Suffering Extinguished Begins — Distinguished by Cause

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, the present serving as cause, future suffering extinguished; second, the past serving as cause, present suffering extinguished.

Chinese Source

復有二種苦滅。一者、現在為因，未來苦滅；二者、過去為因，現在苦滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, the present serving as cause, future suffering extinguished; second, the past serving as cause, present suffering extinguished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Suffering's own extinguishing is distinguished first by cause: an arhat, no longer possessing present craving and views as cause, no longer creates the karma of birth-and-death, and so future suffering is already extinguished — nirvāṇa with residue's own mark; and once nirvāṇa without residue is entered, the present suffering-body itself, the fruit of past karma as its cause, is finally extinguished as well, the aggregates of this final moment giving way to nothing further.

76. Section: Distinguished by Basis — Mental Suffering and Bodily Suffering

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, mental suffering extinguished; second, bodily suffering extinguished.

Chinese Source

復有二種苦滅。一者、心苦滅，二者、身苦滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, mental suffering extinguished; second, bodily suffering extinguished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second distinction sorts by what suffering rests on: mental suffering, tied to the sixth consciousness, is extinguished already in nirvāṇa with residue — though past karma still brings bodily pain and illness in this life, the sixth consciousness's own affliction no longer troubles the mind; bodily suffering, tied to the five sense-consciousnesses, still remains so long as any residue persists, and is extinguished only in nirvāṇa without residue, once the body itself is finally laid down.

77. Section: Distinguished by Feeling — the Suffering of Change and Suffering Itself, and the Suffering Inherent in Formations

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, the suffering of change and suffering itself extinguished; second, the suffering inherent in all conditioned formations extinguished.

Chinese Source

復有二種苦滅。一者、壞苦、苦苦苦滅，二者、行苦苦滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, the suffering of change and suffering itself extinguished; second, the suffering inherent in all conditioned formations extinguished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A third distinction sorts by the three feelings: an arhat extinguishes the suffering of change, born of pleasant feeling's own eventual loss, and suffering itself, born directly of painful feeling; and extinguishes as well the suffering inherent in all conditioned formations, tied to neutral feeling, the restless flux underlying every formation whatsoever — with arhatship, all three feelings' own corresponding sufferings are finally stilled together.

78. Section: Distinguished by Resultant Karma — Closing the Fourfold Account of Suffering's Extinguishing

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, the suffering of an unwanted karmic result extinguished; second, the suffering of a wanted karmic result extinguished.

Chinese Source

復有二種苦滅。一者、非愛業果苦滅，二者、可愛業果苦滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, two kinds of suffering's extinguishing: first, the suffering of an unwanted karmic result extinguished; second, the suffering of a wanted karmic result extinguished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A fourth distinction sorts by resultant karma: an unwanted karmic result — the three evil destinies — is already extinguished at the path of seeing, since distinctly-arisen affliction is cut off and rebirth there impossible; a wanted karmic result — human or heavenly rebirth, which a trainee may still undergo up to seven, two, or one further time — still carries its own suffering, the suffering of change and the suffering inherent in formations alike, extinguished completely only once arhatship is reached and nirvāṇa without residue entered. With this, suffering's extinguishing, distinguished across all four angles — by cause, by basis, by feeling, and by resultant karma — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a further result some trainees still experience.

79. Section: A Further Result Distinguished — Dread That Remains Even After Stream-Entry — Closing Result, the Aggregates Section's Fifth Topic, in Its Entirety

There remain some few noble disciples who have already seen the track of the truths — though they have already surpassed every dread belonging to the suffering of the evil destinies, since they have not yet permanently exhausted every fetter, their minds still carry a dread of future birth, aging, and death shared with every ordinary being. In order to cut this off, they are able to arouse intense aspiration and desire, sustained right mindfulness, and freedom from negligence, diligently cultivating the practice of insight.

Chinese Source

復有少分已見諦跡諸聖弟子，雖已超過諸惡道苦所有怖畏，由未永盡一切結故，其心猶有於當來世共諸異生生老死怖。為斷彼故，而能發起猛利樂欲，乃至正念及無放逸，勤修觀行。

Ru-Yi Translation

There remain some few noble disciples who have already seen the track of the truths — though they have already surpassed every dread belonging to the suffering of the evil destinies, since they have not yet permanently exhausted every fetter, their minds still carry a dread of future birth, aging, and death shared with every ordinary being. In order to cut this off, they are able to arouse intense aspiration and desire, sustained right mindfulness, and freedom from negligence, diligently cultivating the practice of insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Even a stream-enterer, having genuinely seen the four noble truths' own track, remains free of any dread of falling to the evil destinies, yet since innate affliction still remains uncut, some corner of the mind still shares an ordinary being's own dread of future birth, aging, sickness, and death in the human realm. To cut off even this remaining dread, such a disciple arouses intense aspiration, sustains mindfulness of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, and diligently cultivates calm and insight until arhatship itself is directly realized, entering nirvāṇa without residue — only then is even this last trace of dread permanently stilled. With this, result — both kinds of affliction cut off and the fourfold, further-distinguished account of suffering extinguished — is closed in its entirety, completing result, the aggregates section's fifth topic, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to delusion's own mark, the sixth topic.

80. Section: Delusion's Mark Begins — Two Marks Named, a Question Posed

Further, there are two marks of the foolish, ordinary being. What are the two?

Chinese Source

復次，有二種愚夫之相。何等為二？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are two marks of the foolish, ordinary being. What are the two?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's sixth topic, delusion's mark, names two ways an ordinary being's own confusion shows itself, posing the question of what these two marks are before answering directly.

81. Section: The Two Marks Listed

First, failing to truly know what should genuinely be sought. Second, pursuing instead what should not be sought at all.

Chinese Source

一者、於所應求不如實知，二者、非所應求而反生起。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, failing to truly know what should genuinely be sought. Second, pursuing instead what should not be sought at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first mark names failing to understand nirvāṇa itself, the true nature of all dharmas, that the aggregates are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — as when someone dismisses Buddhist practice as too solemn or restrictive, and so never comes to understand its actual truth. The second names pursuing instead exactly what should not be pursued — worldly glory, wealth, sensory pleasure, reputation, food, and sleep — clinging to precisely what ought to be released.

82. Section: The First Mark Explained Begins — What Should Genuinely Be Sought

What is called what should genuinely be sought? Namely, nirvāṇa, the permanent extinguishing of every formation.

Chinese Source

何等名為是所應求？所謂涅槃諸行永滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called what should genuinely be sought? Namely, nirvāṇa, the permanent extinguishing of every formation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

What every being genuinely blessed with a precious human life should seek and grasp is release from every suffering, nirvāṇa itself, since only nirvāṇa can permanently extinguish the formations that produce suffering; a bodhisattva, further, seeks the Buddha's own unrestricted great nirvāṇa, beginning with the awakening-mind and unfolding through the whole of the bodhisattva path — this is what every disciple of the Buddha should genuinely pursue.

83. Section: Failing to Truly Know It — Closing the First Mark Entirely

Yet ordinary, foolish beings hold no wish whatsoever for the non-arising of formations in a life to come, taking joy only in their continued arising. Through this cause and condition, they fail to truly know what should genuinely be sought, and fail to truly know the manifold suffering that the arising of formations itself produces.

Chinese Source

而諸愚夫，於當來世諸行不生都無樂欲，於諸行生唯有欣樂。由是因緣，於所應求及諸行生所有眾苦不如實知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet ordinary, foolish beings hold no wish whatsoever for the non-arising of formations in a life to come, taking joy only in their continued arising. Through this cause and condition, they fail to truly know what should genuinely be sought, and fail to truly know the manifold suffering that the arising of formations itself produces.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An unlearned, foolish being holds no wish for nirvāṇa's own stilling of the aggregates in a future life, delighting instead in their continued arising — one without children wishes for children, one without grandchildren wishes for grandchildren, hoping the family line will simply continue. Driven by exactly this delight, worldly beings scramble after ever more houses, wealth, and grain, never truly understanding what should genuinely be sought, nor the manifold suffering that the aggregates' own continued arising produces — and not understanding birth-and-death's own suffering, there is nothing to grow weary of, and so beings remain content to keep sinking in its vast ocean. With this, the first mark — failing to truly know what should genuinely be sought — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the second mark, pursuing what should not be sought.

84. Section: The Second Mark Begins — a Question Posed

What is called pursuing what should not be sought at all?

何等名為非所應求而反生起？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called pursuing what should not be sought at all?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second mark poses directly what dharma an ordinary being should never pursue, yet clings to and refuses to release regardless.

85. Section: What Should Not Be Sought Named

What should not be sought means aging, sickness, and death; the union of what is unloved, the separation of what is loved, the deprivation of what is desired, and the sorrow, lament, grief, suffering, and every further manner of distress that follows.

Chinese Source

非所求者，諸老、病、死、非愛合會、所愛別離、所欲匱乏、愁、歎、憂、苦種種熱惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

What should not be sought means aging, sickness, and death; the union of what is unloved, the separation of what is loved, the deprivation of what is desired, and the sorrow, lament, grief, suffering, and every further manner of distress that follows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

No one truly wishes for aging, sickness, or death, and yet, not understanding that pursuing life itself guarantees them, an unlearned being pursues birth all the same; not knowing that meeting the unloved should never be courted, beings continually make enemies through their own rivalry, unable to escape ever crossing paths with them again; what is genuinely loved — person, circumstance, or thing — is precisely what so often cannot be kept; and the deprivation of what is desired, wanting wealth and having none, wanting companionship and having none, wanting anything at all and finding it always just out of reach, brings its own bitter suffering — every one of these depends on life itself arising in the first place, and none of them should ever genuinely be sought.

86. Section: Yet Delight Arises All the Same

Toward these very formations' own arising, delight arises instead. Deep joy and attachment take root in every formation whose basis is birth, and karma is created and accumulated with birth as its very root. Through this cause and condition, one finds no release from the suffering of existence, and from the manifold distinct sufferings — aging, sickness, death, and the rest — that take birth as their own root.

Chinese Source

彼於如是諸行生起反生欣樂，於生為本一切行中深起樂著，於生為本所有諸業造作積集。由是因緣，於有生苦及生為本老病死等眾苦差別不得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward these very formations' own arising, delight arises instead. Deep joy and attachment take root in every formation whose basis is birth, and karma is created and accumulated with birth as its very root. Through this cause and condition, one finds no release from the suffering of existence, and from the manifold distinct sufferings — aging, sickness, death, and the rest — that take birth as their own root.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An unlearned being takes birth itself to be beautiful, celebrating it in literature, art, dance, and song — and, delighting in existence, willingly creates the very karma that guarantees its own continuation, dying without a shred of regret. Bound by exactly this delight, such a being finds no release whatsoever from future birth's own suffering, or from the aging, sickness, and death that inevitably follow from it.

87. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Second Mark, and Delusion's Mark Entirely

This is what is called pursuing what should not be sought at all.

Chinese Source

如是名為非所應求而反生起。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is what is called pursuing what should not be sought at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The dharma teaches this plainly, yet an ordinary being pursues exactly what should not be sought, only to reap suffering from it afterward — mistaking some fleeting, illusory pleasure for genuine happiness while it conceals grave danger within, indulging in songs, laughter, and dance without recognizing the affliction such indulgence breeds — this very inversion is delusion's own mark. With this, the second mark — what should not be sought, and delight arising toward it all the same — is closed in its entirety, completing delusion's mark, the aggregates section's sixth topic, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to the fourfold certainty of impermanence, the seventh topic.

88. Section: The Fourfold Certainty Begins — Four Certainties Named

Further, among the formations there are four certainties: first, the certainty of impermanence; second, the certainty of suffering; third, the certainty of emptiness; fourth, the certainty of no-self.

Chinese Source

復次，於諸行中有四決定。一、無常決定，二、苦決定，三、空決定，四、無我決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, among the formations there are four certainties: first, the certainty of impermanence; second, the certainty of suffering; third, the certainty of emptiness; fourth, the certainty of no-self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's seventh topic names four qualities that hold true of the five aggregates without exception: they are certainly impermanent; certainly suffering, never genuinely pleasurable; certainly empty, no true self to be found whether identified with or apart from the aggregates; and certainly without self, every conditioned formation arising from causes and conditions, never self-governing or within one's own control.

89. Section: Unpacking the Four Certainties Begins — Why Formations Are Certainly Impermanent, a Question Posed

Why are the formations certainly impermanent?

Chinese Source

云何諸行無常決定？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are the formations certainly impermanent?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Unpacking the four certainties begins with impermanence itself, examined first through three further marks, posing directly why the conditioned aggregates of form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness must, without exception, be impermanent.

90. Section: The Explanation Begins — Three Marks Named, and Why

Through three marks, it should be known that formations of the past and future are already certainly impermanent — how much more so the present. What are the three?

Chinese Source

由三種相，當知過去、未來諸行尚定無常，何況現在。何等為三？

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three marks, it should be known that formations of the past and future are already certainly impermanent — how much more so the present. What are the three?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise argues for impermanence across all three times at once, beginning with past and future — reasoning that if even these two, seemingly more stable, are certainly impermanent, the present, forever in flux, must be all the more so.

91. Section: Three Marks Listed

These are: first, not existing before and then existing; second, existing before and then not existing; third, being joined with both arising and cessation.

Chinese Source

謂先無而有故，先有而無故，起盡相應故。

Ru-Yi Translation

These are: first, not existing before and then existing; second, existing before and then not existing; third, being joined with both arising and cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Viewed from the present moment: not existing before and then existing describes the future aggregates, not yet arrived and so presently non-existent, yet destined to exist; existing before and then not existing describes the past aggregates, once present but now entirely ceased; and being joined with both arising and cessation describes the present aggregates themselves, arising and perishing in the very same instant — together, all three times alike are proven certainly impermanent.

92. Section: Not Existing Before and Then Existing Begins — an Argument From the Reverse

If future formations, not previously existing, were certainly non-existent, then they could not be said to not exist before and then exist — and impermanence itself would then not be certain.

Chinese Source

若未來行先所未有定非有者，是即應非先無而有，如是應非無常決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

If future formations, not previously existing, were certainly non-existent, then they could not be said to not exist before and then exist — and impermanence itself would then not be certain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Reasoning from the reverse: were the future aggregates' present non-existence a fixed, permanent state, they could never actually arise, and so could never be truly described as "not existing before and then existing" — impermanence itself would collapse.

93. Section: A Positive Demonstration — Closing "Not Existing Before and Then Existing" Entirely

Because that very future is provisionally posited on the basis of what is not yet existent, taking non-existence as prior and existence as coming only afterward, future formations are therefore certainly impermanent.

Chinese Source

由彼先時施設非有，非有為先，後時方有，是故未來諸行無常決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because that very future is provisionally posited on the basis of what is not yet existent, taking non-existence as prior and existence as coming only afterward, future formations are therefore certainly impermanent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The future is only ever provisionally posited relative to the present — the future aggregates do not exist now, and will exist only later, precisely the pattern of "not existing before and then existing" that proves their own certain impermanence. With this, not existing before and then existing is closed in its entirety, completing the first of three marks. The treatise turns next to existing before and then not existing.

94. Section: Existing Before and Then Not Existing Begins — an Argument From the Reverse

If present formations, once arisen from conditions, were certainly, fixedly existent, then they could not be said to have existed before and then not exist — future formations would then not be certainly impermanent either, and present formations would not be joined with both arising and cessation.

Chinese Source

若現在行從緣生已決定有者，是即應非先有而無，未來諸行便應非是無常決定，現在諸行亦應不與起盡相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

If present formations, once arisen from conditions, were certainly, fixedly existent, then they could not be said to have existed before and then not exist — future formations would then not be certainly impermanent either, and present formations would not be joined with both arising and cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Were the present aggregates, once arisen from conditions, fixed and permanently existent, the present could never actually become the past — never "existing before and then not existing" — and by the same logic the future could never truly be "not existing before and then existing" either, since present formations would then possess no arising or ceasing mark at all.

95. Section: A Positive Demonstration — Closing "Existing Before and Then Not Existing" Entirely

Because present formations, once arisen from conditions, are not certainly, fixedly existent, taking existence as prior, non-existence is provisionally posited as following it — past formations are therefore certainly impermanent.

Chinese Source

由現在行從緣生已，非決定有，以有為先，施設非有，是故過去諸行無常決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because present formations, once arisen from conditions, are not certainly, fixedly existent, taking existence as prior, non-existence is provisionally posited as following it — past formations are therefore certainly impermanent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The present aggregates, once arisen from conditions, are never fixed or permanently existent, but impermanent — taking their present existence as prior, non-existence is posited as following it, moment by moment moving toward extinction, exactly the pattern that proves the past aggregates' own certain impermanence. With this, existing before and then not existing is closed in its entirety, completing the second of three marks. The treatise turns next to being joined with both arising and cessation.

96. Section: Being Joined With Both Arising and Cessation — Closing All Three Marks Entirely

Thus present formations, because future formations do not exist before and then exist, and because past formations existed before and then do not exist, are, through this very reasoning, posited as joined with both arising and cessation.

Chinese Source

如是現在諸行，因未來行先無而有，因過去行先有而無，由此施設起盡相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus present formations, because future formations do not exist before and then exist, and because past formations existed before and then do not exist, are, through this very reasoning, posited as joined with both arising and cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The present aggregates' own moment-to-moment arising and ceasing is established precisely because the future's own "not existing before and then existing" and the past's own "existing before and then not existing" are already proven — the present, standing exactly between them, is thereby shown to be joined with both arising and cessation at once. With this, all three marks — not existing before and then existing, existing before and then not existing, and being joined with both arising and cessation — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement.

97. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Certainty of Impermanence Entirely

Hence it is said: it should be known that the impermanent nature of formations past and future is already certain — how much more so the present. This is called the certainty of impermanence among the formations.

Chinese Source

是故說言：當知去來諸行無常性尚決定，何況現在。是名諸行無常決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hence it is said: it should be known that the impermanent nature of formations past and future is already certain — how much more so the present. This is called the certainty of impermanence among the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Since past and future formations are both certainly impermanent through this threefold reasoning, and since the present itself is defined only relative to past and future, the formations of all three times together are certainly impermanent. With this, the certainty of impermanence, the first of the aggregates section's four certainties, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the certainty of suffering, the second.

98. Section: The Certainty of Suffering Begins — a Question Posed

Why are the formations certainly of the nature of suffering?

Chinese Source

云何諸行苦性決定？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are the formations certainly of the nature of suffering?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second certainty poses why the aggregates, across all three times, must without exception be of the nature of suffering.

99. Section: A Summary Statement

This means that even the past and future formations are already dharmas of the suffering of birth and the rest — how much more so the present. Why is this so?

Chinese Source

謂去來諸行尚是生等苦法，何況現在。所以者何？

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that even the past and future formations are already dharmas of the suffering of birth and the rest — how much more so the present. Why is this so?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Past aggregates already carried the sufferings of birth, aging, sickness, and death; future aggregates, should they arise, will carry the very same, including separation from what is loved; and present aggregates carry the three sufferings, the eight sufferings, and every further immeasurable suffering all at once, gathered together, scarcely possible to escape.

100. Section: The Reason Explained — Past, Future, and Present Suffering Distinguished — Closing the Certainty of Suffering Entirely

Past formations are suffering already passed through; future formations are suffering not yet arrived; present formations are suffering directly present. This is called the certainty of suffering among the formations.

Chinese Source

過去諸行是已度苦，未來諸行是未至苦，現在諸行是現前苦。是名諸行苦性決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Past formations are suffering already passed through; future formations are suffering not yet arrived; present formations are suffering directly present. This is called the certainty of suffering among the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The three times, sorted by suffering itself: the past carried the three, eight, and immeasurable sufferings already, now passed through; the future, should it come, will carry the very same, not yet arrived; and the present carries suffering directly, right now — every time alike, without exception, is certainly suffering. With this, the certainty of suffering, the second of the four certainties, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the certainty of emptiness, the third.

101. Section: The Certainty of Emptiness Begins — a Question Posed

Why are the formations certainly empty?

Chinese Source

云何諸行空性決定？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are the formations certainly empty?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third certainty poses why the aggregates, across all three times, must without exception be certainly empty of any true, substantial self.

102. Section: A Summary Statement

This means that even the past and future formations are already certainly empty — how much more so the present. Why is this so?

Chinese Source

謂去來諸行尚定空性，何況現在。所以者何？

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that even the past and future formations are already certainly empty — how much more so the present. Why is this so?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

No self is to be found in the aggregates of past or future — already certainly empty — how much more so the present.

103. Section: The Reason Explained — Past, Future, and Present All Empty

Future formations, their nature not yet existing, are therefore empty. Past formations, their nature already ceased, are therefore empty. Present formations, though existing and not yet ceased, are removed from what the ultimate truth and ultimate reality itself establish — and are therefore empty as well.

Chinese Source

未來諸行其性未有，由此故空；過去諸行其性已滅，由此故空；現在諸行雖有未滅，諦義、勝義性所遠離，由此故空。

Ru-Yi Translation

Future formations, their nature not yet existing, are therefore empty. Past formations, their nature already ceased, are therefore empty. Present formations, though existing and not yet ceased, are removed from what the ultimate truth and ultimate reality itself establish — and are therefore empty as well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Future formations have not yet arisen at all, hence empty in the sense of non-existence; past formations have already ceased, no longer findable, hence empty as well; and present formations, though seemingly present and not yet gone, are still empty in the ultimate sense a noble one realizes — the present itself only ever posited relative to past and future, and since neither past nor future can ultimately be found, the present cannot ultimately be found either.

104. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Certainty of Emptiness Entirely

This is called the certainty of emptiness among the formations.

Chinese Source

是名諸行空性決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called the certainty of emptiness among the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes clarify "removed from ultimate truth and reality": present formations' own mark can indeed be perceived, yet their nature is not genuinely, substantially real — exactly like a conjured illusion, an illusionist's horse or rabbit visibly present yet never truly real. With this, the certainty of emptiness, the third of the four certainties, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the certainty of no-self, the fourth.

105. Section: The Certainty of No-Self Begins — a Question Posed

Why are the formations certainly without self?

Chinese Source

云何諸行無我決定？

Ru-Yi Translation

Why are the formations certainly without self?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth certainty poses why the aggregates, across all three times, must without exception be certainly without self — self meaning something permanent, unchanging, and genuinely self-governing.

106. Section: A Summary Statement

This means that even the past and future formations are already certainly without self — how much more so the present.

Chinese Source

謂去來諸行尚定無我，何況現在。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that even the past and future formations are already certainly without self — how much more so the present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Neither past nor future formations can master themselves — how much less could the present, forever in flux, ever be genuinely self-governing.

107. Section: The Reason Explained — No Self Anywhere Across the Three Times

Why is this so? Future formations' own mark of being without self lies in their not yet being present at all. Past formations' own mark of being without self lies in their already having passed beyond. Present formations' own mark of being without self lies in their being present only for this very instant.

Chinese Source

所以者何？未來諸行非我之相，未現前故；過去諸行非我之相，已越度故；現在諸行非我之相，正現前故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this so? Future formations' own mark of being without self lies in their not yet being present at all. Past formations' own mark of being without self lies in their already having passed beyond. Present formations' own mark of being without self lies in their being present only for this very instant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Future formations have not yet appeared at all — where could any self reside? Past formations have already passed — no self remains to be found there either. And present formations, though seemingly present, vanish the very instant they arise, forever in flux, no permanent, unchanging self ever to be found within them — past, present, and future alike, every formation is without self, without any true governing power.

108. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Certainty of No-Self, and All Four Certainties Examined Through the Three-Times Method Entirely

This is called the certainty of no-self among the formations.

Chinese Source

是名諸行無我決定。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called the certainty of no-self among the formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

The three times' own aggregates are thus shown certainly without self. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes add: present formations, though their mark can indeed be perceived, are not thereby self, since only that momentary mark is genuinely present before us — through this very reasoning, no-self itself is certain. With this, the certainty of no-self, the fourth and final certainty, is closed in its entirety — and with it, all four certainties, examined through the three-times method, are closed in their complete entirety. The treatise turns next to the same four certainties examined again, through a second method of only two marks.

109. Section: A Second Method Begins — the Certainty of Impermanence Through Two Marks

And further, through two marks, it should be known that the formations are certainly impermanent: first, because the past has already been destroyed; second, because the future and the present are dharmas certain to be destroyed.

A second, simpler method now re-examines the same four certainties through only two marks apiece rather than three. Impermanence: the past has already been destroyed and no longer exists at all; and though the present has not yet been destroyed, and the future has not yet even arisen, both are certain, without exception, to eventually be destroyed — a simpler two-part reasoning arriving at the very same certainty of impermanence.

110. Section: The Certainty of Suffering Through Two Marks

No permanent, unchanging, self-governing self, and nothing genuinely belonging to such a self, can ever be found among the conditioned formations — utterly and ultimately empty of both, mark and substance alike absent from the very start.

113. Section: Freedom Reached Only Later — Closing the Certainty of Emptiness Through Two Marks Entirely

With this, the certainty of emptiness, examined through two marks, is closed in its entirety, completing the third of the four certainties under this second method. The treatise turns next to the certainty of no-self, examined the same way.

114. Section: The Certainty of No-Self Through Two Marks — Closing the Second Method Entirely

These marks and the rest should be understood exactly as already explained at length in the Śrāvaka Stage.

復由十相，當知諸行四相決定。謂由敗壞、變易、別離、相應、法性相故，非可樂、不安隱、相應、遠離、異相相故。如是等相，如前聲聞地已廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, through ten marks, it should be known that the four marks of the formations are certain — namely, through the marks of decay, change, separation, conjunction, and inherent nature; and through the marks of what is unpleasing, what is insecure, conjunction, distance, and what is other. These marks and the rest should be understood exactly as already explained at length in the Śrāvaka Stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Stage directly

A third method distributes ten marks across the same four certainties: impermanence receives five — decay (formations certainly decay), change (formations shift moment to moment), separation (nothing loved, inner or outer, can be kept forever), conjunction (even a chance meeting of conditions is impermanent), and inherent nature (the very nature of formations is impermanent); suffering receives three — what is unpleasing (the suffering of suffering itself), what is insecure (the suffering inherent in formations, itself unstable and bound to meet further suffering and change), and conjunction (the suffering of change, since greed typically accompanies pleasant feeling); emptiness receives one — distance, observing that no self, being, person, or life-force, nothing possessing the permanence and governing power beings cling to as self, is ever found among the aggregates, hence entering emptiness through this very absence; and no-self receives one — what is other, since the aggregates, impermanent and arisen from conditions, are never self-governing and so are other than any true self, entering no-self through this same lack of self-mastery. Distance examines emptiness through substance — no self findable anywhere within or beyond the aggregates; what is other examines no-self through function — the aggregates' own inability to govern themselves. The Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 34, teaches these same ten practices for observing the truth of suffering directly: change, decay, separation, inherent nature, conjunction, bondage, what is unloved, what is insecure, what is ungraspable, and what is not self-governing — mapped here respectively to decay-impermanence, change-impermanence, separation-impermanence, conjunction-impermanence, inherent-nature-impermanence, what-is-unpleasing-suffering, what-is-insecure-suffering, conjunction-suffering (bondage), distance-emptiness (the ungraspable), and what-is-other-no-self (what is not self-governing) — three complete, equally valid methods for the very same fourfold certainty: three marks, two marks, or these ten, each capable of cultivating the insight that actually sees it. With this, the fourfold certainty — examined through the three-times method, the two-marks method, and this ten-marks method alike — is closed in its entirety, completing the aggregates section's seventh topic in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to element, examined again as the eighth topic.

The aggregates section's eighth topic returns to element, this time naming five categories freed from affliction's bondage, reached by cultivating the seven applications of attention on the four truths' sixteen aspects — each to be explained individually in what follows.

117. Section: The First Two Realms Explained — Cutting-Off and Freedom From Desire

This means: because the formations cut off by the path of seeing are cut off, it is named the realm of cutting-off. Because the formations cut off by the path of cultivation are cut off, it is named the realm free of desire.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

This means: because the formations cut off by the path of seeing are cut off, it is named the realm of cutting-off. Because the formations cut off by the path of cultivation are cut off, it is named the realm free of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A noble one at the path of seeing, directly perceiving the true nature of all dharmas, cuts off every distinctly-arisen affliction of craving and view entirely, entering stream-entry — the realm of cutting-off. Continuing on through the path of cultivation, cutting off the desire realm's own innate affliction — the non-returner's own attainment — is the realm free of desire.

118. Section: The Remaining Three Realms — Closing Element's Five Categories Entirely

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

This same realm, possessing only a remaining residue, is named the realm of nirvāṇa with residue. That very residue then extinguished, it is named the realm of cessation, also named the realm of nirvāṇa without residue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 17

An arhat who has cut off every affliction of the three realms entirely, this present life's own resultant body — the fruit of past karma — still remaining though affliction no longer manifests, dwells in the realm of nirvāṇa with residue; once this final lifespan ends and even that resultant body is laid down, body and mind alike no longer findable, this is the realm of cessation, also called nirvāṇa without residue. Fascicle 17 confirms: perfected higher rebirth means proceeding to a fortunate destiny; perfected decisive excellence means craving exhausted, freed of desire, still and extinguished in nirvāṇa. With this, element's own five categories — cutting-off, freedom from desire, cessation, nirvāṇa with residue, and nirvāṇa without residue — are closed in their entirety, completing the classification of kinds. The treatise turns next to their further names, six in all.

119. Section: Six Further Names Begin — the Stilling of Every Formation

Chinese Source

即此五界，由一切行永寂靜故，名諸行止。

Ru-Yi Translation

These same five categories, because every formation is permanently stilled, are named the stilling of every formation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Across all five categories, in ascending order, ever more of the aggregates' own formations are permanently stilled: the realm of cutting-off stills the three evil destinies' own aggregates forever; the realm free of desire stills the desire realm's own aggregates as well; the realm of cessation and nirvāṇa with residue still every seed and active manifestation of the three realms' own future aggregates; and nirvāṇa without residue stills even this present life's own aggregates entirely, nothing whatsoever left to arise again — the stilling of every formation, in its most complete and final sense.

120. Section: Emptiness

Because self, what belongs to self, the conceit of self, clinging to these, and their latent tendency are all alike removed, it is named emptiness.

Chinese Source

由我、我所、我慢、執著及與隨眠皆遠離故，說名為空。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because self, what belongs to self, the conceit of self, clinging to these, and their latent tendency are all alike removed, it is named emptiness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Synonyms directly

Each of the five categories removes self-clinging to an ever-deeper degree: the realm of cutting-off removes the distinctly-arisen seeds of self, what belongs to self, and the conceit of self; the realm free of desire removes the same within the desire realm entirely; the realm of cessation and nirvāṇa with residue remove both distinctly-arisen and innately-arisen self-clinging together; and nirvāṇa without residue, every formation stilled, leaves self-clinging's own active manifestation and seed alike permanently unable to arise — emptiness, at ever-deepening levels across the five. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss self and what belongs to self as the view of a perishable self; the conceit of self as the conceit arising from that very view; active clinging as its manifest entanglement; and the latent tendency as its own coarse, dormant seed — exactly as the Compendium of Synonyms, fascicle 83, already explained.

121. Section: Nothing Gained

Chinese Source

由一切相皆遠離故，名無所得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because every mark whatsoever is removed, it is named nothing gained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A noble one who has realized non-discriminating wisdom, free of every conceptual mark and false discrimination, no longer grasps at any characteristic whatsoever in the mind — hence also named nothing gained.

122. Section: Craving Exhausted

Within the realm of cutting-off, because every craving toward whatever accords with a defiled dharma is entirely removed, it is named craving exhausted.

Chinese Source

於斷界中，一切隨順有漏法上所有貪愛皆遠離故，名為愛盡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the realm of cutting-off, because every craving toward whatever accords with a defiled dharma is entirely removed, it is named craving exhausted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Specifically within stream-entry's own realm of cutting-off, distinctly-arisen craving toward every pleasing object of the three realms is entirely removed — hence this fourth name, craving exhausted, applies to this realm in particular.

123. Section: Freedom From Desire

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the realm free of desire, because every desire and craving present there is entirely removed, it is named freedom from desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A noble one at the third fruit and beyond has cut off even the innately-arisen seed of desire-realm craving entirely — hence this fifth name, freedom from desire, applies specifically to this realm.

124. Section: Cessation and Nirvāṇa — Closing All Six Further Names Entirely

Within the realm of cessation, and within the realms of nirvāṇa with and without residue, according to what applies, since everything is permanently extinguished and everything is stilled, they are named, in their proper sequence, cessation, and also nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

於滅界中，及於有餘依、無餘依涅槃界中，如其所應皆永滅故、皆寂靜故，隨其次第，說名為滅，亦名涅槃。

Within the realm of cessation, and within the realms of nirvāṇa with and without residue, according to what applies, since everything is permanently extinguished and everything is stilled, they are named, in their proper sequence, cessation, and also nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Within these last three realms, whatever affliction and future suffering remains is permanently extinguished and stilled in turn — cessation names the remaining affliction finally exhausted, arhatship reached; and nirvāṇa names that same stillness, the mind at peace though a resultant body still remains in nirvāṇa with residue, and body and mind alike permanently extinguished in nirvāṇa without residue. With this, all six further names for the five categories — the stilling of every formation, emptiness, nothing gained, craving exhausted, freedom from desire, and cessation together with nirvāṇa — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to how these five categories are actually cultivated.

130. Section: Impermanence, and Therefore Suffering

This means that, among the formations, one first gives rise to the wisdom of impermanence, through discerning the principle of their own arising and ceasing. Next, one observes, regarding those very formations correspondent with arising, that they are dharmas of birth, dharmas of aging, and so on through the dharmas of sorrow, suffering, and feverish distress — through this cause and condition, all of it is suffering. This is precisely how, resting on the earlier wisdom of impermanence, the later wisdom of suffering is born.

Chinese Source

謂於諸行中，先起無常智，由思擇彼生滅道理故；次後於彼生相應行，觀為生法、老法，乃至憂苦熱惱等法；由是因緣，一切皆苦。此即依先無常智，生後苦智。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that, among the formations, one first gives rise to the wisdom of impermanence, through discerning the principle of their own arising and ceasing. Next, one observes, regarding those very formations correspondent with arising, that they are dharmas of birth, dharmas of aging, and so on through the dharmas of sorrow, suffering, and feverish distress — through this cause and condition, all of it is suffering. This is precisely how, resting on the earlier wisdom of impermanence, the later wisdom of suffering is born.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

A practitioner, through hearing's own wisdom, first learns that the aggregates are impermanent — reflecting through three, two, or ten marks, as already explained — discerning their own arising and ceasing. Observing next how the aggregates' own arising brings birth, aging, and every further sorrow, grief, and distress in its wake, that same impermanence is seen to be, through and through, suffering — the wisdom of impermanence giving birth to the wisdom of suffering that follows it. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes note that "dharmas correspondent with arising" here omits death by using "and so on," since death belongs properly to the contemplation of cessation, and likewise omits lament by writing simply "and the rest."

131. Section: Suffering, and Therefore No-Self

And further, since those very formations are dharmas of birth through dharmas of feverish distress, this is exactly the principle of death and birth's own dependent arising, cycling on in succession, never self-governing — hence there is no self at all. This is precisely how, resting on the earlier wisdom of suffering, the later wisdom of no-self is born.

Chinese Source

又彼諸行，由是生法乃至是熱惱法故，即是死生緣起展轉流轉不得自在行相道理，故無有我。此即依先苦智，生後無我智。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, since those very formations are dharmas of birth through dharmas of feverish distress, this is exactly the principle of death and birth's own dependent arising, cycling on in succession, never self-governing — hence there is no self at all. This is precisely how, resting on the earlier wisdom of suffering, the later wisdom of no-self is born.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Observing further that the aggregates are nothing but death following birth, birth following death, cycling endlessly through dependent origination, bound entirely to causes and conditions, never able to remain healthy and happy simply as one might wish — no permanent, governing self or function is anywhere to be found in this — the wisdom of suffering giving birth, in turn, to the wisdom of no-self.

132. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Gradual Sequence of Wisdom Entirely

Chinese Source

如是觀無常故苦，苦故無我，是名智漸次。

Ru-Yi Translation

Observing in this way that impermanence brings suffering, and suffering brings no-self, this is called the gradual sequence of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beginning with hearing's own wisdom, a practitioner first observes impermanence's own arising and ceasing, giving rise to the wisdom of impermanence; observing the aggregates' impermanence gives rise to the wisdom of suffering; and observing that every formation is suffering, without any governing self, gives rise finally to the wisdom of no-self. With this, the gradual sequence by which wisdom itself unfolds is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the gradual sequence of wisdom's own fruit.

This means: aversion, freedom from desire, liberation, and complete liberation.

Wisdom's own fruit unfolds through four stages in turn: aversion, freedom from desire, liberation, and complete liberation — the first three to be examined next through two distinct readings, the fourth to follow after.

135. Section: Aversion, Freedom From Desire, and Liberation Begin — a First Reading — Aversion Itself

What is aversion? This means that, once the antidote is actually present, the perception of aversion arises, so that afflictions no longer manifest.

A first reading of aversion, freedom from desire, and liberation together: when craving arises, bringing the antidote actually to mind — the contemplation of impurity, or impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — gives rise to the perception of aversion, affliction's own active manifestation suppressed, though its underlying seed remains untouched at this stage.

136. Section: Freedom From Desire

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once aversion has been cultivated enough to gain real discerning power, greed no longer manifests toward any pleasing object even without deliberately invoking the antidote — the strength of suppression already firmly established, though only regarding affliction's active manifestation, not yet its seed.

137. Section: Liberation

Ru-Yi Translation

What is liberation? This means that, through cultivating this very suppression and antidote at length, the latent tendency itself is permanently uprooted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Continuing to contemplate impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, affliction's active manifestation suppressed again and again until that suppression grows strong enough to join with undefiled cultivated wisdom, the affliction's own seed is finally cut off as well — genuine liberation, suppression deepening from shallow to complete across these three stages.

138. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the First Reading Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called the first reading of aversion, freedom from desire, and liberation.

139. Section: A Second Reading — Sorted by the Noble Fruits Themselves — Closing Aversion, Freedom From Desire, and Liberation Entirely

Resting on that very aversion, once freedom from desire is attained in every lower stage apart from the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the stage of freedom from desire is thereby established, hence it is named freedom from desire. Once freedom from desire is attained even within the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, the stage of liberation is thereby established, hence it is named liberation. This is called the second reading of aversion, freedom from desire, and liberation.

Chinese Source

復有差別。謂於厭位斷界極成滿，故名厭。即依止厭，除非想非非想處，於餘下地得離欲時，施設離欲位，故名離欲。於非想非非想處得離欲時，施設解脫位，故名解脫。是名厭、離欲、解脫第二差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a further distinction. At the stage of aversion, the realm of cutting-off reaches its own utmost completion, hence it is named aversion. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of Concentrated Equipoise

A second reading matches these same three terms to the noble fruits themselves. Aversion now names stream-entry directly — the Stage of Concentrated Equipoise, fascicle 13, confirms that formations cut off by the path of seeing being cut off is exactly the realm of cutting-off — a stream-enterer's own aversion toward the aggregates having reached its utmost completion, since every distinctly-arisen affliction is now cut off, never again to manifest toward any object encountered. Freedom from desire now names the non-returner and the arhat-in-training, resting on that same aversion to gradually release the lower eight stages' own desire — the four form-realm absorptions and the three lower formless attainments — apart from the very peak of existence, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception; a stream-enterer, and even the family-to-family patterns already discussed, may loosely be included here as well, though freedom from desire is properly counted only from the third fruit onward. And liberation now names arhatship specifically, once even the peak of existence's own subtle craving is finally released — only then is the stage of liberation genuinely established. With this, the second reading of aversion, freedom from desire, and liberation — aversion as stream-entry, freedom from desire as the non-returner's own progressive release, and liberation as arhatship itself — is closed in its entirety, completing both readings of these first three of the four fruits. The treatise turns next to complete liberation, the fourth and final fruit.

140. Section: Complete Liberation — Closing All Four Fruits Entirely

云何遍解脫？謂由如是煩惱雜染解脫故，生等諸苦雜染亦普解脫，是名遍解脫。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once liberated from the three realms' own affliction, an arhat is thereby liberated as well from every future defilement of birth, aging, sickness, and death — complete liberation, arhatship's own fullest sense. Tradition distinguishes two kinds of arhat here: liberated through wisdom alone, or liberated both through wisdom and through meditative absorption together. With this, aversion, freedom from desire, liberation, and complete liberation — all four fruits, individually explained — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement naming this whole sequence.

Thus, through the ascendant power of wisdom, aversion arises among the formations; through cultivating aversion, freedom from desire is attained; through cultivating freedom from desire, liberation and complete liberation are attained. This is called the gradual sequence of wisdom's own fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, growing steadily stronger through resting on concentration and cultivating insight into the aggregates, produces its own fruit in strict sequence: aversion arises first; sustained aversion yields freedom from desire, no longer clinging the instant an object is encountered; and sustained freedom from desire yields liberation, and finally complete liberation, every affliction of even a future life fully released. With this, the gradual sequence of wisdom's own fruit — its four stages fully unpacked — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to what this sequence actually cuts off.

142. Section: What Is Cut Off Begins — Four Wrong Views Named

Here, there are, further, four kinds of wrong clinging. What are the four?

Chinese Source

此中復有四種邪執。何等為四？

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, there are, further, four kinds of wrong clinging. What are the four?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wisdom's own gradual fruit cuts off, in turn, four kinds of wrong clinging that stand in its way — the treatise poses what these four are before naming them directly.

143. Section: The Four Wrong Views Listed

First, the wrong clinging of view; second, the wrong clinging of conceit; third, inwardly-arisen wrong clinging; fourth, wrong clinging taught by another.

Chinese Source

一、見邪執，二、慢邪執，三、自內邪執，四、他教邪執。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the wrong clinging of view; second, the wrong clinging of conceit; third, inwardly-arisen wrong clinging; fourth, wrong clinging taught by another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner may fall into any of these four kinds of wrong clinging, each to be examined individually in what follows.

144. Section: The Wrong Clinging of View

The wrong clinging of view means clinging, among the formations, to self and to what belongs to self.

Chinese Source

見邪執者，謂於諸行中執我、我所。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wrong clinging of view means clinging, among the formations, to self and to what belongs to self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8

Fascicle 8 defines this clinging directly: drawing near to unwholesome company, hearing improper teaching, and reflecting improperly, a being takes the view of a perishable self, extreme views, and wrong view — together with their supporting faculties, objects, and seed-causes — and, comparing against others' own views, holds their own to be supreme, superior, wondrous, and foremost, this whole clinging comprised of nothing but discriminated, defiled wisdom. Before the path of seeing, clinging to the aggregates as self, or as what belongs to self, resting on the view of a perishable self, is exactly this wrong clinging of view.

145. Section: The Wrong Clinging of Conceit — Closing the First Two Kinds' Own Marks

The wrong clinging of conceit means giving rise, among the formations, to the conceit of self.

Chinese Source

慢邪執者，謂於諸行中起我慢執。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wrong clinging of conceit means giving rise, among the formations, to the conceit of self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8

Fascicle 8 defines conceit similarly: drawing near to unwholesome company, hearing improper teaching, improper attention, and simply drifting into forgetful mindlessness, a being's comparison of superiority and inferiority, toward self or others, whether deliberate or entirely unreflective, swells into an arrogant self-elevation — this is its very substance. So long as innate self-clinging remains uncut, conceit readily arises the moment any small advantage over another is perceived — the wrong clinging of conceit. With this, the marks of both the wrong clinging of view and the wrong clinging of conceit are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a further distinction between the two.

146. Section: A Further Distinction — Which Path Cuts Off Which — Closing the First Two Kinds Entirely

The former, the wrong clinging of view, obstructs direct realization of the truths; the latter, the wrong clinging of conceit, obstructs the cutting-off that belongs to the path of cultivation.

Chinese Source

前見邪執，障諦現觀；後我慢邪執，障修所斷煩惱等斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

The former, the wrong clinging of view, obstructs direct realization of the truths; the latter, the wrong clinging of conceit, obstructs the cutting-off that belongs to the path of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The wrong clinging of view, distinctly-arisen self-clinging, obstructs the direct realization of the four truths and entry into the true nature that transcends birth, cut off at the path of seeing; the wrong clinging of conceit, here representing innately-arisen affliction, obstructs what the path of cultivation must remove. In the fullest account, ten afflictions divide this way — the view of a perishable self, extreme views, wrong view, view-clinging, clinging to rules and vows, greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt, already detailed in fascicle 8 — of which wrong view, view-clinging, clinging to rules and vows, and doubt are purely distinctly-arisen, while the remaining six admit both distinctly- and innately-arisen forms; here the treatise simply selects view-clinging and conceit-clinging as the two most representative examples. With this, the first two kinds of wrong clinging — view and conceit, together with this distinction of which path cuts off which — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the remaining two kinds.

147. Section: The Remaining Two Kinds Begin — Inwardly-Arisen Wrong Clinging

Inwardly-arisen wrong clinging means that, dwelling alone in solitude, resting on improper discrimination, one clings to a real self existing — whether the wrong clinging of view or the wrong clinging of conceit.

Chinese Source

自內邪執者，謂獨處空閑，不正分別為依止故，執有實我，或見邪執、或慢邪執。

Ru-Yi Translation

Inwardly-arisen wrong clinging means that, dwelling alone in solitude, resting on improper discrimination, one clings to a real self existing — whether the wrong clinging of view or the wrong clinging of conceit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Sitting alone without proper reflection, resting on the view of a perishable self, a being spontaneously clings to the aggregates as self and what belongs to self, giving rise to either view-clinging or conceit-clinging without any outside teaching at all — a genuinely innate clinging, arising even without anyone to instruct it.

148. Section: Wrong Clinging Taught by Another — Closing the Marks of the Remaining Two Kinds

Wrong clinging taught by another means that, through another's teaching, wrong clinging arises — namely, that this is self, this belongs to self, the conceit of self thereby set in motion.

Chinese Source

他教邪執者，謂由他教，起邪執著，謂此是我、此是我所，我慢行轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wrong clinging taught by another means that, through another's teaching, wrong clinging arises — namely, that this is self, this belongs to self, the conceit of self thereby set in motion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Learned from a worldly teacher's own instruction, a being comes to believe this very body is self, its own possession — self, what belongs to self, and the conceit of self all arising from this taught clinging, distinctly-arisen in nature, in contrast to inwardly-arisen clinging's own innate character. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes clarify: self and what belongs to self are view's own mark, while conceit resting on that same view is conceit's own mark — both mentioned together here since both arise jointly from the same taught cause. With this, the marks of inwardly-arisen and other-taught wrong clinging are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a further distinction between them.

149. Section: Distinguishing the Inward From the Taught — Closing All Four Wrong Views Entirely

And further, improper discrimination arising inwardly, clinging to self and to what belongs to self, is named inward wrong clinging, also named wrong clinging not taught by another.

Chinese Source

又於內起不正分別，執我、我所，名內邪執，亦名非他教邪執。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, improper discrimination arising inwardly, clinging to self and to what belongs to self, is named inward wrong clinging, also named wrong clinging not taught by another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Clinging that arises entirely from within, without any outside instruction, is inward wrong clinging — a small child, innocent and untaught, still cries the instant a toy is taken away, and though no one ever taught it, will insist "this is mine" all the same. Wrong clinging taught by another, by contrast, arises specifically through an unwholesome teacher's own instruction. With this, all four kinds of wrong clinging — view, conceit, inward, and other-taught, together with every distinction drawn between them — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to what wisdom's own fruit actually accomplishes regarding them.

150. Section: Wisdom's Fruit Explained — Closing What Is Cut Off, and the Two Kinds of Gradual Sequence, the Aggregates Section's Ninth Topic, in Their Complete Entirety

When every such wrong clinging is permanently cut off, it should be known that this is called wisdom's own fruit.

Chinese Source

如是一切邪執永斷，當知是名智果。

Ru-Yi Translation

When every such wrong clinging is permanently cut off, it should be known that this is called wisdom's own fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A noble one at the first fruit and beyond has cut off the distinctly-arisen wrong clinging of view (taught by another); through the path of cultivation, the innately-arisen wrong clinging of conceit (arising inwardly) and its own remaining seeds are gradually cut off as well, arhatship finally reached — this whole permanent cutting-off is wisdom's own fruit. With this, what is cut off — all four kinds of wrong clinging, and wisdom's own fruit in overcoming them — is closed in its entirety, completing the two kinds of gradual sequence, the aggregates section's ninth topic, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to neither cut off nor permanent, the tenth topic.

151. Section: Neither Cut Off Nor Permanent Begins — Three Marks Named, a Question Posed

Further, through three marks, it should be known that the formations are neither cut off nor permanent. What are the three?

Chinese Source

復次，由三種相，應知諸行非斷、非常。何等為三？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through three marks, it should be known that the formations are neither cut off nor permanent. What are the three?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's tenth topic guards against two further extremes beyond eternalism and annihilationism already discussed: a practitioner must avoid clinging to any dharma as permanent, but must equally avoid clinging to the opposite extreme, that impermanence itself names some further real, existing thing — right view must be established correctly before genuine practice can proceed. The treatise poses what three marks reveal the formations as neither cut off nor permanent.

152. Section: Three Marks Listed

First, because it takes as its cause a formation that does not abide. Second, because once arisen, it possesses no cause of abiding. Third, because future formations' own causal nature will itself cease.

Chinese Source

一、以無住行為因故；二、生已無住因故；三、未來諸行因性滅故。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, because it takes as its cause a formation that does not abide. Second, because once arisen, it possesses no cause of abiding. Third, because future formations' own causal nature will itself cease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes directly

Three marks span the three times: taking as its cause a formation that does not abide looks to the past — this present body itself the fruit of past karma, ceaselessly active though that karma is now itself gone; possessing no cause of abiding looks to the present — the present aggregates, once arisen, cannot remain fixed, changing moment to moment without pause; and future formations' own causal nature ceasing looks to the future — present activity seeds a cause for what is yet to come, and though that active seeding itself ceases, its seed persists, ready to produce a future result once conditions ripen. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain this third mark in detail: the seed capable of producing future formations is itself called "future formations," produced through the present formations' own habituation, which is therefore called its causal nature — the active habituating formation is the cause, the seed it plants is the fruit; though the active formation itself ceases, its seed continues on in unbroken succession — hence the treatise's own conclusion below: since the present causal nature that produces future formations does indeed cease, formations should be known as not cut off. Past, present, and future are thus shown mutually dependent, cause and fruit endlessly conditioning one another — proof, across all three times at once, that formations are neither cut off nor permanent: were they permanent, the future could never arise at all; precisely because the present is itself impermanent, the future is able to arise in its wake.

153. Section: The Explanation Begins — Establishing "Not Permanent"

Here, because the cause of the formations is itself impermanent, and because, once they have arisen, no cause of their abiding can be found, it should be known that the formations are not permanent.

Chinese Source

此中諸行因無常故，生已住因不可得故，當知諸行非常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, because the cause of the formations is itself impermanent, and because, once they have arisen, no cause of their abiding can be found, it should be known that the formations are not permanent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The present resultant body is formed from a past cause already impermanent, moment by moment; and the fruit that cause produces cannot itself remain fixed for even an instant, equally subject to moment-to-moment cessation — established, across both past and present, that formations are certainly not permanent.

154. Section: Establishing "Not Cut Off" — Closing the Three Marks Entirely

Because the present causal nature that is able to produce future formations does indeed cease, it should be known that the formations are not cut off.

Chinese Source

能生未來諸行現在因性滅故，當知諸行非斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the present causal nature that is able to produce future formations does indeed cease, it should be known that the formations are not cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The present, ceasing moment to moment, gives way directly to the future in its place — today passes, yet tomorrow surely comes; this present two o'clock has already passed by the time it becomes two-forty-three, and yet three o'clock reliably follows. Were the present never to cease, time itself would simply stop, and the formations would then genuinely be cut off, no future ever arriving; but because the present does cease, the future is continually able to arrive in succession — hence not cut off at all. With this, the three marks proving formations neither cut off nor permanent are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a second method, examining the same neither-cut-off-nor-permanent nature through four conditions.

155. Section: Four Conditions Begin — a Question Posed

There are, further, four conditions able to keep the formations cycling on in succession. What are the four?

Chinese Source

復有四緣，能令諸行展轉流轉。何等為四？

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, four conditions able to keep the formations cycling on in succession. What are the four?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second method now examines the same neither-cut-off-nor-permanent nature through the four conditions that drive every conditioned dharma's own arising, posing what these four are before naming them.

156. Section: The Four Conditions Listed

First, the causal condition; second, the condition of immediate succession; third, the condition of the object; fourth, the dominant condition.

Chinese Source

一、因緣，二、等無間緣，三、所緣緣，四、增上緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the causal condition; second, the condition of immediate succession; third, the condition of the object; fourth, the dominant condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Every conditioned dharma arises through these four conditions together — the very principle by which formations continue cycling on in unbroken succession.

157. Section: A Brief Summary — Reduced to Two

These same four conditions can be briefly reduced to two: first, cause; second, condition. Cause names only the causal condition; the remaining three are condition alone.

Chinese Source

即此四緣，略有二種。一、因，二、緣。因唯因緣，餘三唯緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

These same four conditions can be briefly reduced to two: first, cause; second, condition. Cause names only the causal condition; the remaining three are condition alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Of the four, only the causal condition serves as the primary cause; the condition of immediate succession, the condition of the object, and the dominant condition are all merely supporting conditions — one leading role, three supporting.

158. Section: Each Condition Explained Begins — the Causal Condition

And the causal condition means the formations' own seed.

Chinese Source

又因緣者，謂諸行種子。

Ru-Yi Translation

And the causal condition means the formations' own seed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Every conditioned dharma has its own seed — the eye faculty its own seed, eye-consciousness its own seed, and so through every dharma whatsoever — and that very seed is the causal condition itself.

159. Section: The Condition of Immediate Succession

The condition of immediate succession means that the prior six consciousnesses and the rest, together with their correspondent dharmas, cease without any gap, and the subsequent six consciousnesses and the rest, together with their correspondent dharmas, arise without any gap.

Chinese Source

等無間緣者，謂前六識等及相應法等無間滅，後六識等及相應法等無間生。

Ru-Yi Translation

The condition of immediate succession means that the prior six consciousnesses and the rest, together with their correspondent dharmas, cease without any gap, and the subsequent six consciousnesses and the rest, together with their correspondent dharmas, arise without any gap.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Only mind and mental factors possess this condition; material form does not. Sorting the five aggregates into form and mind, mind king and mental factors alike continue in unbroken succession within their own kind — this is the condition of immediate succession. "The prior six consciousnesses and the rest" here includes the seventh and eighth consciousnesses as well, together with their correspondent mental factors, each ceasing without gap so that the next moment's consciousnesses and factors can arise without gap in their place — one moment must cease before the next can arise; without that vacated place, no subsequent moment could ever occur. The prior moment is thus the immediate condition for the moment that follows it.

160. Section: The Condition of the Object

The condition of the object means that the group of five consciousnesses and the rest take the five distinct objects as their own object; the sixth consciousness and the rest take every dharma whatsoever as its object.

Chinese Source

所緣緣者，謂五識身等，以五別境為所緣；第六識身等，以一切法為所緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The condition of the object means that the group of five consciousnesses and the rest take the five distinct objects as their own object; the sixth consciousness and the rest take every dharma whatsoever as its object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The condition of the object names whatever a consciousness actually takes as its object of cognition — the five sense-consciousnesses each confined to their own distinct domain, the eye only discerning form, the ear only sound, the nose only scent, the tongue only taste, the body only touch; while the sixth consciousness takes every dharma whatsoever as its object, both the five sense-objects shared with the first five consciousnesses and the further mental objects proper to itself alone.

161. Section: The Dominant Condition Begins — Faculty in Relation to Consciousness

The dominant condition means that the group of five consciousnesses takes the eye and the other distinct supporting faculties as its dominant condition, and takes the attention capable of giving rise to it and the rest as its dominant condition as well; the mental consciousness and the rest take the four great elements' own body, and the attention capable of giving rise to it and the rest, as its dominant condition.

Chinese Source

增上緣者，謂五識等，以眼等各別所依為增上緣，及以能生作意等為增上緣；意識身等，以四大種身，及能生作意等為增上緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The dominant condition means that the group of five consciousnesses takes the eye and the other distinct supporting faculties as its dominant condition, and takes the attention capable of giving rise to it and the rest as its dominant condition as well; the mental consciousness and the rest take the four great elements' own body, and the attention capable of giving rise to it and the rest, as its dominant condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Verses Supplementing the Eight Consciousnesses' Rules

The dominant condition unfolds first through faculty and consciousness together: each of the first five consciousnesses depends on its own corresponding faculty — eye-consciousness on the eye faculty, and so on — this faculty itself serving as its dominant condition; and each further depends on attention, and the further mental factors it draws in its wake (touch, feeling, perception, and volition among the five omnipresent factors, and beyond), for its very arising — the dominant condition behind each moment's own false discrimination. Beyond the causal condition, the condition of immediate succession, and the condition of the object, every remaining factor is comprised within the dominant condition — the Verses Supplementing the Eight Consciousnesses' Rules describes the great vehicle's own more elaborate account of how many conditions each consciousness requires (nine for the eye, eight for the ear, seven for the nose, tongue, and body, and so on), though this treatise, following the āgamas' own more basic teaching, does not follow that fuller scheme here. The mental consciousness, in turn, requires five conditions to grasp its object: the faculty condition (the seventh consciousness, both as its root faculty and as the basis of its own purity or defilement), the object condition, the attention condition, the fundamental support condition, and the seed-support condition — apart from the object condition (already the condition of the object) and the seed-support condition (already the causal condition), every remaining condition here, too, is comprised within the dominant condition. This concludes faculty examined in relation to consciousness.

162. Section: Karma in Relation to Its Fruit

And further, karma created in the past, in relation to the wanted or unwanted result it produces, should also be known as a dominant condition.

Chinese Source

又先所造業，望所生愛非愛果，當知亦是增上緣。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, karma created in the past, in relation to the wanted or unwanted result it produces, should also be known as a dominant condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Keeping the five precepts and cultivating merit in a past life brings a pleasing human rebirth; the ten wholesome deeds bring a heavenly rebirth; and karma driven by greed, hatred, and delusion, in varying degrees, brings rebirth in one of the three evil destinies — karma itself, relative to the fruit it produces, is exactly this same dominant condition. Since the sūtras themselves speak of an impermanent cause and impermanent conditions, the treatise here examines each of the four conditions in turn: the seed itself is impermanent, arising and ceasing moment to moment; the condition of immediate succession is likewise impermanent, only its own unbroken succession allowing continuity at all; the condition of the object is likewise impermanent, form, sound, scent, taste, touch, and mental objects all alike impermanent; and the dominant condition — the faculties, attention, and every further mental factor — is likewise impermanent. Since cause and condition alike are impermanent, the fruit they produce — the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, and their six corresponding objects — must certainly be impermanent as well, a deeper way of understanding the earlier passage on the certainty of impermanence.

163. Section: Provisions in Relation to the Path, and the Path in Relation to Nirvāṇa — Closing the Four Conditions Entirely, and Neither Cut Off Nor Permanent, the Aggregates Section's Tenth Topic, in Its Complete Entirety

In just this way, provisions are a dominant condition in relation to the path, and the path is a dominant condition in relation to attaining nirvāṇa — this too should be known as comprised within the dominant condition.

Chinese Source

如是資糧望道，道望得涅槃，當知亦是增上緣攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

In just this way, provisions are a dominant condition in relation to the path, and the path is a dominant condition in relation to attaining nirvāṇa — this too should be known as comprised within the dominant condition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Just as the provisions gathered for the worldly and transcendent paths serve as their own dominant condition, so too the transcendent path itself serves as the dominant condition for actually attaining nirvāṇa. Examined across the three times, all four conditions of the past have already ceased, hence impermanent; the dharmas the four conditions presently produce likewise cannot remain even a single instant, equally impermanent — past and present alike proving the formations impermanent; and precisely because they are impermanent, further formations continue to arise in the future, proving them not cut off either. Combining the three-times analysis with this fourfold-condition analysis together: formations produced by an impermanent cause and impermanent conditions are certainly, thoroughly neither cut off nor permanent. With this, the four conditions — the causal condition, the condition of immediate succession, the condition of the object, and the dominant condition, examined through faculty and consciousness, karma and its fruit, and provisions and the path alike — are closed in their entirety, completing neither cut off nor permanent, the aggregates section's tenth topic, in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to defilement and purity, the eleventh and final topic of element's own eleven parts.

164. Section: Defilement and Purity Begin — an Overall Statement

Further, through three matters and two marks, defilement and purity should be observed.

Chinese Source

復次，由三種事、二種相，應當觀察雜染、清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through three matters and two marks, defilement and purity should be observed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Element's eleventh and final topic, defilement and purity, opens by naming two complementary methods of observation, each to be unpacked in turn: three matters, and two marks. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm this simply means observing every defiled and every pure dharma through both approaches together.

165. Section: The Three Matters Begin — a Question Posed

How, through three matters, are all defilement and purity observed?

Chinese Source

云何由三種事，觀察一切雜染、清淨？

Ru-Yi Translation

How, through three matters, are all defilement and purity observed?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first method poses how three specific matters reveal whether the mind is genuinely defiled or genuinely pure.

166. Section: The Three Matters Listed — Relish, Danger, and Escape

First, observing, among the formations, the cause and condition of defilement — this means observing that their relish is indeed relish. Second, observing, among the formations, the cause and condition of purity — this means observing that their danger is indeed danger. Third, observing, among the formations, purity itself — this means observing that escape from them is indeed escape.

Chinese Source

一者、於諸行中觀察雜染因緣，謂觀彼愛味為愛味故。二者、於諸行中觀察清淨因緣，謂觀彼過患為過患故。三者、於諸行中觀察清淨，謂觀彼出離為出離故。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, observing, among the formations, the cause and condition of defilement — this means observing that their relish is indeed relish. Second, observing, among the formations, the cause and condition of purity — this means observing that their danger is indeed danger. Third, observing, among the formations, purity itself — this means observing that escape from them is indeed escape.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Relish toward the aggregates is exactly defilement's own cause and condition — to relish is already to cling to suffering, as already explained, and clinging to suffering can never be released from suffering. Observing the aggregates' own manifold danger — impermanent, suffering, empty, without self — is purity's own cause and condition, since only recognizing genuine danger allows genuine aversion to arise; a noble one regards the aggregates as a great malignant growth, eager to be rid of them entirely — the venerable Maudgalyāyana, at the moment of his own death, released the aggregates exactly as one sets down a poisoned vessel, without the slightest attachment — while an ordinary being instead treasures them more dearly than diamonds, clinging so firmly that purity becomes almost impossible without repeated, urgent reflection on their danger, as though putting out a fire that has caught one's own hair. And observing escape itself — whether affliction has genuinely been subdued toward one's own or another's body, whether some measure of purity, awakening, or accordance with awakening and genuine insight has actually been reached — is purity examined directly, escape from defilement itself. Relish is defilement's cause; observing danger is purity's cause; and purity itself is escape from relish, affliction's own active manifestation and underlying seed both finally released.

167. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing the Three Matters Entirely

All of this, gathered together as one, is called observing all defilement and purity through three matters.

Chinese Source

如是一切總略為一，名由三事，觀察一切雜染、清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

All of this, gathered together as one, is called observing all defilement and purity through three matters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These three approaches — relish, danger, and escape — together form a single unified gate for observing one's own defilement and purity among the formations. With this, the first method, three matters, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a second method, two marks.

168. Section: Two Marks Begin — True Nature and Full Extent Named

How, through two marks, are all defilement and purity observed? First, through their true nature; second, through their full extent.

Chinese Source

云何由二種相，觀察一切雜染、清淨？一者、由如所有性故；二者、由盡所有性故。

Ru-Yi Translation

How, through two marks, are all defilement and purity observed? First, through their true nature; second, through their full extent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second method observes the formations, and thereby moves from defilement to purity, through these same two marks already familiar from earlier in this fascicle — true nature and full extent — each to be explained afresh in this new context.

169. Section: True Nature Explained

True nature means, among the formations, their relish, their danger, and escape from them.

Chinese Source

如所有性者，謂於諸行中，若愛味、若過患、若出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

True nature means, among the formations, their relish, their danger, and escape from them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

True nature here observes the aggregates' own shared marks — relish, danger, and escape from them — examined in general, apart from any of their particular instances.

170. Section: Full Extent Explained — Closing True Nature and Full Extent Entirely

Full extent means, among the formations, the entirety of their relish, the entirety of their danger, and the entirety of escape from them.

Chinese Source

盡所有性者，謂於諸行中，盡所有愛味、盡所有過患、盡所有出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Full extent means, among the formations, the entirety of their relish, the entirety of their danger, and the entirety of escape from them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Full extent instead gathers every possible instance of the aggregates without exception — past, present, or future; inner or outer; coarse or fine; inferior or superior; far or near — and observes relish, danger, and escape across every single one of them. With this, true nature and full extent, the two marks that observe defilement and purity, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to how observation actually proceeds through each mark.

171. Section: How Observation Proceeds Begins — True Nature's Own Relish

Here, observing that the formations, taken as a condition, give rise to pleasure and give rise to joy — this is called their relish. And this relish is extremely small. Through these two marks, true nature's own relish is thus observed.

Chinese Source

此中觀察諸行為緣，生樂、生喜，是名於彼愛味。又此愛味極為狹小。如是由二種相，觀察如所有性所謂愛味。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, observing that the formations, taken as a condition, give rise to pleasure and give rise to joy — this is called their relish. And this relish is extremely small. Through these two marks, true nature's own relish is thus observed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Relish is observed through two further marks: first, wherever the aggregates give rise to pleasure and joy, relish is present; second, relish itself, though the cause, is extremely small compared to the vast danger it eventually produces as its fruit — a small ember capable of igniting an enormous fire, a trifling relish carrying the power to bring about the whole of birth-and-death's own suffering.

172. Section: True Nature's Own Danger

And further, observing that the formations are impermanent, suffering, and dharmas subject to change and ruin — this is called their danger. And this danger is extremely vast. Through these two marks, true nature's own danger is thus observed.

Chinese Source

又觀察諸行是無常、苦、變壞之法，是名於彼過患。又此過患極為廣大。如是由二種相，觀察如所有性所謂過患。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, observing that the formations are impermanent, suffering, and dharmas subject to change and ruin — this is called their danger. And this danger is extremely vast. Through these two marks, true nature's own danger is thus observed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Danger, likewise through two marks: first, cultivating insight, one observes the aggregates as impermanent, suffering, and forever subject to change and ruin, moment to moment unstable; second, the danger that this small relish ultimately produces is itself vast beyond measure, pervading the three realms entirely, every formation everywhere equally impermanent, suffering, and subject to ruin.

173. Section: True Nature's Own Escape — Closing True Nature's Threefold Observation Entirely

And further, observing, among the formations, that craving and desire are extinguished, cut off, and transcended — this is called escape from them. And this escape is peaceful, unsurpassed, and ultimately secure. Through these two marks, true nature's own escape is thus observed.

Chinese Source

又復觀察於諸行中欲貪滅、欲貪斷、欲貪出，是名於彼出離。又此出離寂靜無上，畢竟安隱。如是由二種相，觀察如所有性所謂出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, observing, among the formations, that craving and desire are extinguished, cut off, and transcended — this is called escape from them. And this escape is peaceful, unsurpassed, and ultimately secure. Through these two marks, true nature's own escape is thus observed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Synonyms directly

Escape, likewise through two marks: first, observing whether craving and desire toward the aggregates has been extinguished, cut off, or fully transcended — the Compendium of Synonyms, fascicle 83, already matched these same three terms to discipline subduing active manifestation, then the applications of withdrawal and the rest cutting off the underlying seed, then the application of ultimate accomplishment transcending it entirely, deepening progressively from stream-entry all the way to arhatship; second, this escape, once ultimately achieved across the three realms, is itself peaceful, unsurpassed, and altogether secure — nirvāṇa's own genuine, final rest. With this, true nature's own threefold observation — relish extremely small, danger extremely vast, and escape peaceful and unsurpassed — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to full extent's own observation.

174. Section: Full Extent's Own Observation — Closing How Observation Proceeds, and the Entire Explanation of Observing Defilement and Purity, in Their Complete Entirety

And this very relish, danger, and escape, among the formations, whether past, future, or present, whether inner or outer, whether coarse or fine, whether inferior or superior, whether far or near, should be carefully and thoroughly observed — it should be known that this is called observing full extent's own relish, danger, and escape.

Chinese Source

又即此愛味、即此過患、即此出離，於諸行中，若過去、若未來、若現在、若內、若外、若麤、若細、若劣、若勝、若遠、若近審諦觀察，當知是名於彼觀察盡所有性所謂愛味、過患、出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

And this very relish, danger, and escape, among the formations, whether past, future, or present, whether inner or outer, whether coarse or fine, whether inferior or superior, whether far or near, should be carefully and thoroughly observed — it should be known that this is called observing full extent's own relish, danger, and escape.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Full extent's own observation examines this same relish, danger, and escape across every possible instance of the aggregates — past, present, or future; inner or outer, one's own or another's; coarse or fine; the lowlier desire realm or the superior form and formless realms; distant or immediate — leaving nothing whatsoever unexamined. With this, how observation actually proceeds through both true nature and full extent is closed in its entirety — and with it, the entire method of observing defilement and purity, through three matters and two marks alike, is closed in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to what these three matters actually reveal about the nature of beings themselves.

175. Section: Revealing Their Own Nature Begins — Three Kinds of Being Distinguished

And further, in order to know that the very nature of these three matters genuinely exists, it should be known that there are three distinct kinds of being: first, those defiled and attached toward desire; second, those who keep their distance from desire; third, those freed entirely of desire's own bondage.

Chinese Source

又為了知如是三事體性是有，應知三種有情眾別。一、於諸欲染著眾，二、於諸欲遠離眾，三、於諸欲離繫眾。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, in order to know that the very nature of these three matters genuinely exists, it should be known that there are three distinct kinds of being: first, those defiled and attached toward desire; second, those who keep their distance from desire; third, those freed entirely of desire's own bondage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

To confirm that relish, danger, and escape are not mere abstractions but genuinely correspond to real beings, three kinds are distinguished, each corresponding to one of the three matters: those defiled and attached toward the five sense-desires — ordinary beings, relishing form, sound, scent, taste, and touch in their own or another's body and its surrounding world; those who keep their distance from desire — practitioners who recognize desire's own danger and know it should be avoided; and those freed entirely of desire's own bondage — practitioners of a still higher order, who, through hearing, reflection, and cultivation, gradually subdue affliction and escape the three realms entirely. Genuine practice means continually reworking one's own direction and one's own conduct toward others — treasuring every fleeting moment of this precious human life, refusing to give up the struggle between purity and defilement, no longer manufacturing fresh affliction, and, at the very least, clearing away old affliction faster than new affliction accumulates; dwelling on the past, whether fondly or with regret, serves nothing, since it can never return — the present alone offers the real opportunity to turn, moment by moment, toward an awakened way of living.

176. Section: Three Kinds of Delusion Begin — Named Across Three Domains

Within these very three domains, there are, further, three kinds of worldly delusion: that of the heavenly world; that of renunciants and brahmins; and that of gods and humans.

Chinese Source

於此三處，復有三種世間愚癡。謂若天世間，若沙門、婆羅門，若諸天人。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within these very three domains, there are, further, three kinds of worldly delusion: that of the heavenly world; that of renunciants and brahmins; and that of gods and humans.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Saṃyukta Āgama's own 19th and 20th sūtras

Corresponding to the three kinds of being just named, these three domains carry their own particular delusion, gathering the companion sūtra's own threefold list — "gods, or Māra, or Brahmā; renunciants and brahmins; the multitude of gods and humans" — none of them able to escape their own inverted delusion, since none of the three, relish, danger, and escape, is genuinely understood.

177. Section: How These Three Worlds Are Established — Closing the Three Kinds of Delusion's Classification Entirely

These three worlds, it should be known, are established through three causes and conditions: first, through attaining mastery over desire and mastery over purity — namely, the world of Māra and of Brahmā. Second, through diligently cultivating practice in order to attain that very state — namely, renunciants and brahmins. Third, through proceeding toward manifold karmic causes and their fruits — namely, gods and humans.

Chinese Source

如是三種世間，由三因緣，應知安立。一、由得欲自在及淨自在故，謂若魔、若梵世間。二、由勤修得彼因故，謂若沙門、婆羅門。三、趣種種業因果故，謂若諸天人。

Ru-Yi Translation

These three worlds, it should be known, are established through three causes and conditions: first, through attaining mastery over desire and mastery over purity — namely, the world of Māra and of Brahmā. Second, through diligently cultivating practice in order to attain that very state — namely, renunciants and brahmins. Third, through proceeding toward manifold karmic causes and their fruits — namely, gods and humans.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Mastery over desire belongs to Māra, ruler of the heaven of controlling others' emanations, whose vast merit — built on generosity alone, without wisdom — lets him enjoy desires effortlessly conjured by others, though remaining unliberated and still deeply attached; mastery over purity belongs to Brahmā's own heaven, the first absorption's inhabitants who have left the desire realm's own coarse unwholesome states behind, yet whose affliction-seed remains uncut, their attainment likewise incomplete. Diligently cultivating practice in order to attain that very state describes renunciants and learned brahmins who practice not toward the three vehicles' own awakening, but merely toward becoming a Māra or a Brahmā themselves — itself another form of delusion. And proceeding toward manifold karmic causes and their fruits describes ordinary gods and humans, born to varying fortune according to superior, middling, or inferior karma — merit alone, without wisdom, remains incomplete. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm: manifold karma is the approach taken, its ripened fruit the destination reached — together, this is exactly why gods and humans alike are called deluded. With this, all three kinds of worldly delusion, and how each is established, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to how affliction is actually cut off and realization actually witnessed.

178. Section: Cutting Off and Witnessing — Two Paths That Free the Mind From the Four Inversions — Closing This Section Entirely

And further, within these same three domains, according to what is fitting, affliction can be cut off and realization can be witnessed, through two paths that free the mind from the four inversions — namely, one who has already entered the stage of seeing, and one who continues cultivating extensively at the stage of cultivation beyond it.

Chinese Source

又於此三處，隨其所應能斷、作證，有二種道離四倒心。謂已入見地，及於上修道多修習住。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, within these same three domains, according to what is fitting, affliction can be cut off and realization can be witnessed, through two paths that free the mind from the four inversions — namely, one who has already entered the stage of seeing, and one who continues cultivating extensively at the stage of cultivation beyond it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 8 and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Against these three kinds of delusion, the path of seeing and the path of cultivation together free the mind from the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity. Fascicle 8 maps each inversion to a specific affliction: the view of a perishable self is the inversion of self where no self exists; part of the extreme views (eternalism) is the inversion of permanence where nothing is permanent; view-clinging is the inversion of purity where nothing is genuinely pure; clinging to rules and vows is the inversion of pleasure regarding what is actually suffering; and greed spans both the purity-inversion and the pleasure-inversion at once. A stream-enterer's view of self mistakes the selfless aggregates, whether identified with or apart from them, for a genuine self; eternalism mistakes what is impermanent for permanent; view-clinging mistakes what is impure — the aggregates themselves, or one's own distorted views — for pure; and clinging to rules and vows mistakes painful, meaningless ascetic practices, such as imitating a cow's or dog's own conduct, for a path toward future happiness. Wherever craving arises, both the purity-inversion and the pleasure-inversion typically arise together — delighting in the aggregates as though genuinely pure, and delighting in relish itself as though reasonable rather than recognizing it as suffering. With this, cutting off affliction and witnessing realization through the path of seeing and the path of cultivation, freeing the mind from all four inversions, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to distinguishing liberation's own marks in detail.

179. Section: Liberation's Marks Begin — Four Marks Listed

And these two paths bring the mind's own liberation through four marks: first, the mark of liberation from the bondage of greed and hatred; second, the mark of extinguishing, cutting off, and escaping desire and craving; third, the mark of freedom from the bondage of the nine fetters; fourth, the mark of liberation from birth and every further suffering.

Chinese Source

又此二種道，有四種相心解脫果。一、貪瞋縛解脫相，二、欲貪滅斷出離相，三、九結離繫相，四、生等諸苦解脫相。

Ru-Yi Translation

And these two paths bring the mind's own liberation through four marks: first, the mark of liberation from the bondage of greed and hatred; second, the mark of extinguishing, cutting off, and escaping desire and craving; third, the mark of freedom from the bondage of the nine fetters; fourth, the mark of liberation from birth and every further suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The mark of liberation from greed and hatred's own bondage names stream-entry, cutting off distinctly-arisen greed and hatred, active manifestation and seed alike. The mark of extinguishing, cutting off, and escaping desire names the non-returner's own attainment at the path of cultivation, desire-realm craving fully removed, never again to return there. The mark of freedom from the nine fetters' own bondage — craving, hatred, conceit, ignorance, view, clinging, doubt, envy, and stinginess — names arhatship, every innately-arisen affliction of the three realms finally cut off, the realm of cessation reached. And the mark of liberation from birth and every further suffering names an arhat's own future birth, aging, sickness, and death extinguished at nirvāṇa with residue, and this present life's own suffering extinguished as well at nirvāṇa without residue — ultimate liberation itself.

180. Section: A Further Distinction — Three Marks Concern the Cause, One Concerns the Fruit — Closing Liberation's Marks Entirely

Among these, the first three marks reveal liberation from affliction at the level of cause; the last mark reveals liberation from suffering at the level of fruit.

Chinese Source

此中前三相，顯示因處煩惱解脫；後一相，顯示果處諸苦解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, the first three marks reveal liberation from affliction at the level of cause; the last mark reveals liberation from suffering at the level of fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Greed and hatred's own bondage, desire's own extinguishing, and the nine fetters together name birth-and-death's own cause, affliction itself, simply described from three different angles; the final mark, freedom from birth and every further suffering, instead names the resultant fruit — liberation from the aggregates' own resultant suffering directly. With this, liberation's own four marks, and this distinction between cause and fruit, are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to an extended simile illustrating this same liberation.

181. Section: An Extended Simile Begins — the Prisoner Bound by Manifold Fetters

Regarding this very meaning, it is like a person confined in prison, bound by manifold fetters — whether of wood, of rope, or of iron.

Chinese Source

於此義中，譬如有人處在囹圄，為種種縛之所繫縛，所謂或木、或索、或鐵。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding this very meaning, it is like a person confined in prison, bound by manifold fetters — whether of wood, of rope, or of iron.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A person imprisoned and bound by wooden, roped, and iron fetters images greed, hatred, and delusion respectively — wood for greed, rope for hatred, iron for delusion — a being imprisoned within the five aggregates, greed, hatred, and delusion constantly arising there like fetters tightly binding, the prisoner scarcely even aware of their own confinement.

182. Section: Guarded by a Watchful Keeper

Chinese Source

又置餘人令其防守。或設有彼從幽縶處逃至遠所，還執將來；或有尚不令彼轉動，況得逃避。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, another person is stationed to guard this prisoner. Should the prisoner attempt to flee this confinement to some distant place, that guard seizes and returns them; or, in some cases, the prisoner is not even allowed to move at all, let alone attempt any escape.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The prison's own watchful guard images improper discursive thought and improper attention, together with affliction's own uncut seed — this inward guard alone is enough to keep a being trapped within the three realms indefinitely, with no genuine hope of escape.

183. Section: The Enmity of the Four Māras — Closing All Four Fetters Entirely

Having thus been tightly bound by manifold fetters, tightly bound by the fetter of skillful vigilance, and tightly bound by the most secure fetter of all, one is further harmed, according to their own inclination, by the enmity of four Māras, through the suffering of birth and every further suffering.

Chinese Source

彼既如是為種種縛極所密縛，善方便縛之所密縛，最堅牢縛之所密縛，復四魔怨隨其所欲，以生等苦而加害之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus been tightly bound by manifold fetters, tightly bound by the fetter of skillful vigilance, and tightly bound by the most secure fetter of all, one is further harmed, according to their own inclination, by the enmity of four Māras, through the suffering of birth and every further suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Bound tightly by greed, hatred, and delusion; bound by improper discursive thought and affliction's own uncut seed; and bound by the nine fetters' own desirable objects, willingly embraced — a being is further assailed by four Māras: the Māra of the aggregates, the Māra of death, the heavenly Māra, and the Māra of affliction, each inflicting birth, aging, sickness, death, and every further suffering according to its own particular design. With this, all four fetters — manifold bondage, the guard of improper thought, the most secure bondage of desirable objects, and the enmity of the four Māras — are closed in their entirety, matched fully to their actual dharma-referents. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement on genuine liberation.

184. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing Defilement and Purity, and Element, the Aggregates Section's Entire First Topic, in Its Complete Entirety

Only one who can be genuinely liberated from these four fetters may truly be called liberated from every bondage whatsoever.

Chinese Source

若能從彼四種繫縛善解脫者，乃可名為從一切縛而得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only one who can be genuinely liberated from these four fetters may truly be called liberated from every bondage whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing companion sūtras 19 and 20 directly

Only genuine liberation from all four fetters — corresponding to the four marks of liberation already named: freedom from greed and hatred's bondage, the extinguishing of desire, freedom from the nine fetters, and freedom from birth's own suffering — truly deserves the name liberation from every bondage; for the śrāvaka path, this means nothing less than arhatship itself. Companion sūtras 19 and 20 confirm this whole topic directly: a being who does not relish the six faculties and their objects remains undefiled; relishing them is exactly what produces defilement. A being's very body brings danger — aging, sickness, death, and manifold sorrow — and recognizing this danger is what allows genuine aversion, purity's own cause, to arise; and since the aggregates are themselves illusory rather than substantially real, escape from them remains genuinely possible at all. The Buddha declares directly: without truly knowing relish, danger, and escape regarding the five grasping aggregates, no being whatsoever — god, Māra, Brahmā, renunciant, brahmin, or ordinary person — could ever realize unsurpassed, complete awakening; only through genuinely knowing all three, according with that knowledge in ever-deepening insight, can affliction's fetters finally be released, the four inversions left behind, and buddhahood itself ultimately attained. With this, defilement and purity — the observation that establishes it, the beings and delusions it corresponds to, and this extended prison simile matched fully to its own dharma — is closed in its entirety, completing the eleventh and final topic of element (界攝), the aggregates section's very first of eleven overall topics, in its complete entirety. As the source material's own lecture confirms directly: here concludes the prose explanation of the aggregates section's first particular verse. The treatise turns next to brief teaching (略教), the aggregates section's second overall topic.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's second overall topic, brief teaching, unfolds across eleven further parts of its own: brief teaching, its fruit, its ending, the numbered stages, threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off, bondage, liberation, view and conceit's own defilement, purifying phrases, freedom from four requisites, and threefold perfection — this particular verse previewing each in turn, matched to the Saṃyukta Āgama's own twenty-first sūtra and those that follow it.

Through three causes and conditions, some śrāvakas go before the great teacher to request brief instruction. What are the three?

Brief teaching, the first of the eleven parts, names three distinct reasons a disciple might seek concise instruction directly from the Buddha, each to be examined in what follows.

187. Section: The First Reason — Arriving at a New Resolve

This means one who has taken extensive learning alone as the final goal, growing averse to every other kind of practice, now gives rise to this very resolve: mere brief hearing of the dharma is already sufficient to reach my own true aim — why rely on extensive learning as the final goal at all? Genuine practice itself must be cultivated as what is truly real — and, further, one thereby relinquishes the very desire to take extensive learning as the final goal.

Chinese Source

謂唯多聞為究竟者，於諸餘行而厭背者，生如是解：但略聞法足得自義，何藉多聞以為究竟。要修正行為真實故，又棄捨多聞究竟欲故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means one who has taken extensive learning alone as the final goal, growing averse to every other kind of practice, now gives rise to this very resolve: mere brief hearing of the dharma is already sufficient to reach my own true aim — why rely on extensive learning as the final goal at all? Genuine practice itself must be cultivated as what is truly real — and, further, one thereby relinquishes the very desire to take extensive learning as the final goal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

A practitioner deeply rooted in scriptural learning, wisdom that abides in the dharma well matured, may nonetheless have spent nearly all their effort on study alone, neglecting to actually apply proper attention, practice in accordance with the dharma, the noble eightfold path, or the four foundations of mindfulness. Once genuine resolve arises to actually practice rather than merely study further, such a disciple goes before the Buddha to request brief instruction, and the Buddha, matching the teaching to that disciple's own capacity, offers precisely the practice needed to make realization swift and direct. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm: one who once took extensive learning as final, growing averse to genuine practice in accordance with the dharma, now resolves instead to actually practice, no longer resting content with mere learning — hence seeking brief instruction from the great teacher directly.

188. Section: The Second Reason Begins — Dread of Too Much to Do

And there are further those who dread the many undertakings involved in actually entering the gate of practice, and so seek a skillful means by which to enter it directly.

Chinese Source

又有怖畏於所入門多所作者，為善方便而得入故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And there are further those who dread the many undertakings involved in actually entering the gate of practice, and so seek a skillful means by which to enter it directly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Some practitioners, uncertain which of the many practices and provisions genuinely suits their own capacity, hesitate before the sheer number of undertakings practice seems to demand, and so directly request the Buddha's own skillful guidance toward whichever gate truly fits them, hoping to enter it and realize the truth of all dharmas swiftly. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm: seeking entry into the fitting gate of practice, requesting brief instruction to serve as one's own reliable support — undistorted guidance of this kind is exactly what skillful means means here.

189. Section: Dread of a Scattered Mind — Closing Dread's Own Two Reasons Entirely

Or there are those who, having already skillfully heard and skillfully reflected on manifold dharmas, think to themselves: I have already skillfully heard and reflected on manifold dharmas — if I now take everything I have heard and reflected upon as my own support, wishing to fix the mind on both an abiding object and an object of liberation at once, will this not leave my very attention scattered? If so, not even a settled mind could be attained, let alone liberation itself.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Or there are those who, having already skillfully heard and skillfully reflected on manifold dharmas, think to themselves: I have already skillfully heard and reflected on manifold dharmas — if I now take everything I have heard and reflected upon as my own support, wishing to fix the mind on both an abiding object and an object of liberation at once, will this not leave my very attention scattered? If so, not even a settled mind could be attained, let alone liberation itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A practitioner who has genuinely studied and reflected on many different teachings may worry that taking all of them at once as an object of concentration and liberation would only scatter attention further, making even basic mental stability difficult, let alone liberation itself — and so seeks the Buddha's own brief instruction to resolve this very worry. With this, dread's own two reasons — dread of too much to do, and dread of a scattered mind — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to the third reason, uncertainty itself.

190. Section: The Third Reason — Uncertainty Itself — Closing Brief Teaching's Three Reasons Entirely

And there are further those who, among everything they have heard and reflected upon, remain unable to reach any certainty: on what should I rely to swiftly realize penetrating wisdom? On what should I rely to swiftly attain escape? On what object should the mind be fixed in order to attain stability? On what object should the mind be fixed in order to attain liberation? Being thus themselves uncertain, upon actually encountering the great teacher, or a widely recognized disciple of the Tathāgata, they go directly to request brief instruction.

Chinese Source

又於如是所聞、所思一切法中不得決定，當依何者速證通慧？當依何者速得出離？當緣何境而得住心？當緣何境而得解脫？彼既如是自不決定，若於大師、或眾所識如來弟子現前見已，便即往詣請略教授。

Ru-Yi Translation

And there are further those who, among everything they have heard and reflected upon, remain unable to reach any certainty: on what should I rely to swiftly realize penetrating wisdom? On what should I rely to swiftly attain escape? On what object should the mind be fixed in order to attain stability? On what object should the mind be fixed in order to attain liberation? Being thus themselves uncertain, upon actually encountering the great teacher, or a widely recognized disciple of the Tathāgata, they go directly to request brief instruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Despite extensive hearing and reflection, some practitioners remain genuinely uncertain which object of concentration will swiftly bring realization, swiftly bring escape from craving and views, swiftly bring mental stability, or swiftly bring liberation itself — and so, whenever they happen to encounter the Buddha, or a widely respected disciple possessing the five superknowledges, they seize the opportunity to request brief, direct guidance. With this, all three reasons a disciple might seek brief teaching — a newly arisen resolve to actually practice, dread before practice's own demands, and unresolved uncertainty — are closed in their entirety, completing brief teaching, the first of eleven parts. The treatise turns next to brief teaching's own fruit, the second part.

191. Section: Brief Teaching's Fruit Begins — Four Kinds Named

Further, it should be known that proper instruction brings four kinds of fruit, each realizing one's own true aim.

Chinese Source

復次，當知正教授，有四種自義果得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, it should be known that proper instruction brings four kinds of fruit, each realizing one's own true aim.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Genuine, correct instruction from a true teacher yields, in the end, four distinct kinds of nirvāṇa-fruit, each to be named in what follows.

192. Section: Proper Instruction Explained — Four Kinds of Completeness

This means: renouncing the world for this very reason, and renouncing it in this very manner — namely, complete in outward form, complete in undertaking, complete in aspiration, and complete in what is renounced and what is taken up.

Chinese Source

謂為此出家及如此出家，即形相具足、事業具足、意樂具足、處捨取具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means: renouncing the world for this very reason, and renouncing it in this very manner — namely, complete in outward form, complete in undertaking, complete in aspiration, and complete in what is renounced and what is taken up.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes at length

Proper instruction first confirms that a disciple has genuinely renounced the world for the right reason — nirvāṇa itself, not lesser motives — and has renounced it in the right manner, complete across four further respects. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes unpack each: complete in outward form means resolving to accept a humble appearance — shaving the head and beard, giving up worldly adornment, wearing plain, unostentatious robes. Complete in undertaking means faithfully accomplishing every proper monastic duty and observance. Complete in aspiration means renouncing the world solely for the sake of nirvāṇa — for a śrāvaka, nirvāṇa as the very aim of renunciation; for a Mahāyāna disciple, unsurpassed awakening and unrestricted nirvāṇa as that same aim. And complete in what is renounced and what is taken up means resolving to uphold the restraining precepts, diligently and unceasingly cultivating wholesome dharmas without interruption — perfecting discipline, concentration, and wisdom together, extinguishing greed, hatred, and delusion, cutting off the unwholesome and gathering the wholesome in their place. Among these, completeness in aspiration explains "for this very reason" — renouncing the world with the correct underlying motive — while the remaining three completenesses explain "in this very manner" — the concrete respects in which that renunciation must be fully carried out to actually reach the aim it was undertaken for.

193. Section: The Fruit of One's Own True Aim Begins — Four Kinds of Attainment Named

Resting on this, one attains the unsurpassed attainment, the attainment realized in this very life, the attainment realized spontaneously, and the attainment personally, inwardly witnessed.

Chinese Source

依此故得無上得、現法得、自然得、內證得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on this, one attains the unsurpassed attainment, the attainment realized in this very life, the attainment realized spontaneously, and the attainment personally, inwardly witnessed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A disciple who genuinely understands their own reason for renouncing the world, and who diligently practices in accordance with the proper instruction already received, will in time certainly reach nirvāṇa itself — unsurpassed, since nothing whatsoever exceeds it, hence the unsurpassed attainment; and, for some, reached already within this very lifetime, the attainment realized in this very life. The remaining two attainments — realized spontaneously, without any teacher at all, as the Buddha or a pratyekabuddha realizes the thirty-seven factors of awakening unaided; and personally, inwardly witnessed — depend on each practitioner's own particular capacity, to be explained further in what follows.

194. Section: The Attainment Personally, Inwardly Witnessed — Closing Brief Teaching's Fruit Entirely

And once non-discriminating wisdom is realized, the mind becomes genuinely free of all idle conceptual proliferation — this is called the attainment personally, inwardly witnessed.

Chinese Source

（無上得者，得般涅槃故；現法得者，即於此生得般涅槃故；自然得者，無師自能修三十七菩提分法而證解脫故；內證得者，解脫自性唯內所證，無諸戲論故。）

Ru-Yi Translation

And once non-discriminating wisdom is realized, the mind becomes genuinely free of all idle conceptual proliferation — this is called the attainment personally, inwardly witnessed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes directly

The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss all four attainments together: reaching nirvāṇa itself is the unsurpassed attainment; reaching it within this very life is the attainment realized in this very life; realizing liberation through the thirty-seven factors of awakening without any teacher at all is the attainment realized spontaneously; and liberation's own essence, witnessed only inwardly, entirely free of idle conceptual proliferation, is the attainment personally, inwardly witnessed. All four ultimately point to the same nirvāṇa, simply emphasized from different angles — yet whichever angle is stressed, all rest finally on this same inward witnessing. A practitioner who never loses sight of proper instruction, who continually asks whether their own renunciation is genuine, and who steadily gathers the provisions merit and wisdom require, will in time see all four fruits realized in turn. With this, brief teaching's own fourfold fruit — its instruction, its completeness, and all four attainments it produces — is closed in its entirety, completing the second of eleven parts. The treatise turns next to ending, the third part.

195. Section: Ending Begins — Six Kinds of Death Named

Further, there are six kinds of death: death in the past, death in the present, untamed death, tamed death, death of the same kind, and death of a different kind.

Chinese Source

復次，有六種死。謂過去死、現在死、不調伏死、調伏死、同分死、不同分死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are six kinds of death: death in the past, death in the present, untamed death, tamed death, death of the same kind, and death of a different kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Brief teaching's third part, ending, names death itself through three complementary pairs: past and present, untamed and tamed, and same-kind and different-kind — six kinds in total, each to be explained individually in what follows.

196. Section: Death in the Past

Death in the past means that the formations of a past life have perished, down through the life faculty itself having ceased — this is death.

Chinese Source

過去死者，謂過去諸行沒，乃至命根滅故死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Death in the past means that the formations of a past life have perished, down through the life faculty itself having ceased — this is death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations

Death in the past names the prior life's own aggregates already gone, its life faculty already extinguished — this present life exists only because that past life first came to its own end. The Determinations, Stage Correspondent With the Five Sense-Consciousnesses and the Mind Ground, fascicle 52, define the life faculty directly: resting on past karma, the span, force, and condition that sustains a resultant body wherever it arises is called lifespan, vital breath, and the life faculty — lifespan, warmth, and consciousness united together constitute it.

197. Section: Death in the Present

Death in the present means that the formations of this present life have perished, down through the life faculty itself having ceased — this is death.

Chinese Source

現在死者，謂現在諸行沒，乃至命根滅故死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Death in the present means that the formations of this present life have perished, down through the life faculty itself having ceased — this is death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 84 and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Death in the present names this very life's own aggregates perishing, its life faculty finally ceasing — whether through merit exhausted, lifespan exhausted, or an untimely, violent end, once the store-consciousness has fully departed the body, this is present death. Fascicle 84 already explained this same progression in careful detail: passing means giving up the body's form; reaching one's end means the moment death arrives; bereavement means the corpse still remains, perishing means it has been destroyed; bereavement rests on the physical body, perishing on the name-body; the lifespan and warmth receding means, as death nears, some remaining mind still lingers; the life faculty's own extinguishing means every measure of lifespan is exhausted; and death itself means consciousness abandons the region of the heart and chest entirely. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm this same account: once formations have ceased and no longer continue, this is called perishing; as the lifespan and warmth recede, some remaining mind still lingers, until every measure of lifespan is finally exhausted — the life faculty's own ceasing; and consciousness abandoning the region of the heart and chest at last is death itself, exactly as the Compendium of Synonyms, fascicle 84, already explained.

198. Section: Untamed Death Begins — Its Cause Explained

Untamed death means that, in a past life, remaining untamed and unsubdued, carrying the latent tendency's own activity, one dies still carrying the conventional, worldly latent tendency of personhood, passing on in unbroken succession — and, in the present life, becoming bound again to birth and continuity, one is born possessing this very latent activity as one's own being; and in the present life too, down through the exhaustion of the lifespan, one remains, in just the same way, untamed and unsubdued, and so on, spoken of at length, until, at death, a future being arises once more, again taking up this same latent activity.

Chinese Source

不調伏死者，謂於過去世不調不伏，有隨眠行，展轉隨眠世俗言說士夫隨眠而命終已，於現在世結生相續，有隨眠行所攝自體而得生起；於現在世乃至壽盡，亦復如是不調不伏，廣說乃至而命終已，未來自體復得生起，又能攝取有隨眠行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Untamed death means that, in a past life, remaining untamed and unsubdued, carrying the latent tendency's own activity, one dies still carrying the conventional, worldly latent tendency of personhood, passing on in unbroken succession — and, in the present life, becoming bound again to birth and continuity, one is born possessing this very latent activity as one's own being; and in the present life too, down through the exhaustion of the lifespan, one remains, in just the same way, untamed and unsubdued, and so on, spoken of at length, until, at death, a future being arises once more, again taking up this same latent activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Itivṛttaka and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes at length

Untamed death names dying still gripped by affliction's own uncut seed — carrying, above all, self-clinging itself, since worldly convention constantly reinforces "I" and "mine" through everyday speech, seeding self-clinging afresh with every such utterance, life after life, this clinging never once addressed. The Itivṛttaka, fascicle 5, describes it vividly: an unlearned ordinary being, never having drawn near a genuine spiritual friend or learned their teaching, still clings to form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness as self, or as belonging to self; seeing a form, grasping its mark and its finer characteristics, unable to guard the eye faculty with mindfulness, craving and displeasure arise unchecked, countless unwholesome states flowing in without restraint — indulging the eye, clinging to pleasing form, entangling the mind, and, through that very craving, enduring suffering long into the night, suffering both fierce and impoverishing, cycling endlessly through the hells, the animal realm, the hungry ghosts, the asuras, and the human and heavenly realms alike, all originating from this one uncontrolled faculty; and the same holds for ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Untamed death, the sūtra concludes, is no noble path at all — it cannot lead to escape, cannot lead toward nirvāṇa, cannot bring weariness, cessation, stillness, penetrating wisdom, awakening, or the realization of nirvāṇa, and instead draws forth birth, aging, sickness, death, sorrow, and every further feverish affliction — hence the Tathāgata never once praises it, only warns against it, wishing beings to recognize and abandon it. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes explain the "conventional, worldly latent tendency of personhood" directly: worldly speech continually seeds self-clinging through such conventions as one's own name, kind, lineage, food, experience of pleasure and pain, longevity, endurance, and ultimate lifespan — the very "eight phrases of convention" the Bodhisattva Stage, fascicle 49, names explicitly: name, birth-kind, lineage, food and drink, the manner of experiencing pleasure and pain, longevity, endurance, and the boundary of one's own lifespan — each utterance of "I" reinforcing self-clinging afresh, an unbroken habituation the treatise calls "passing on in succession."

199. Section: Untamed Death's Own Result — Closing Untamed Death Entirely

Through taking up that very affliction as one's cause, one is bound by birth and every further manifold suffering, and is likewise bound by the great bondage of greed and the rest.

Chinese Source

由攝取彼以為因故，便為生等眾苦所縛，亦為貪等大縛所縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through taking up that very affliction as one's cause, one is bound by birth and every further manifold suffering, and is likewise bound by the great bondage of greed and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Taking affliction itself as cause guarantees the eight sufferings and every further bondage of greed, hatred, and delusion in their wake — untamed death's own inevitable fruit. With this, untamed death — its cause and its resultant bondage — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to tamed death, its direct counterpart.

200. Section: Tamed Death Begins — Its Cause Explained

Tamed death means that, in the present life, already tamed and already subdued, free of any latent tendency, one dies without carrying it — and no future being arises again, nor does one take up any further latent activity at all.

Chinese Source

調伏死者，謂於現在世已調已伏，無有隨眠而命終已，未來自體不復生起，亦不攝取有隨眠行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Tamed death means that, in the present life, already tamed and already subdued, free of any latent tendency, one dies without carrying it — and no future being arises again, nor does one take up any further latent activity at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Itivṛttaka at length

Tamed death names arhatship's own final passing, every affliction, active and latent alike, already cut off, guaranteeing no further existence whatsoever. The Itivṛttaka, fascicle 5, describes its opposite mirror-image directly: a noble, learned disciple, having drawn near a genuine spiritual friend and mastered their teaching, no longer clings to the aggregates as self or as belonging to self; seeing a form, grasping neither its mark nor its finer characteristics, guarding the eye faculty well with mindfulness, no craving or displeasure arises, every unwholesome state held firmly in check — never indulging the eye, never clinging to pleasing form, the mind untangled, free of that same long suffering, never again cycling through the hells, the animal realm, or any of the further destinies, precisely because the eye faculty has been well tamed; and the same holds for ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind. Tamed death, the sūtra concludes, is the very noble path itself — leading to permanent escape, leading toward nirvāṇa, capable of weariness, cessation, stillness, penetrating wisdom, awakening, and the realization of nirvāṇa, transcending birth, aging, sickness, death, and every feverish affliction entirely — hence the Tathāgata praises it without reservation.

201. Section: Tamed Death's Own Result — Closing Tamed Death Entirely

Through not taking up that affliction as one's cause, one is liberated from birth and every further distinct suffering, and is likewise liberated from the great bondage of greed and the rest.

Chinese Source

不攝取彼以為因故，解脫生等眾苦差別，亦復解脫貪等大縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through not taking up that affliction as one's cause, one is liberated from birth and every further distinct suffering, and is likewise liberated from the great bondage of greed and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Never taking up the conventional latent tendency of self-clinging as cause, an arhat is fully liberated from birth's own suffering and every bondage of greed, hatred, and delusion alike — such a one's death is genuinely tamed death. With this, tamed death — its cause and its resultant liberation — is closed in its entirety, completing both untamed and tamed death together. The treatise turns next to death of the same kind and death of a different kind.

202. Section: Death of the Same and Different Kind Begin — Sorted by Whether Life Was Once Given Up Before

Death of the same kind means that, exactly as in a past life untamed and unsubdued, one once gave up body and life before — and in the present life, in just the same way, one gives up body and life again. It should be known that this is called death of the same kind, death that resembles it, and death that follows accordingly.

Chinese Source

同分死者，謂如過去不調不伏，曾捨身命，於現在世亦復如是而捨身命，當知如此名同分死、名相似死、名隨順死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Death of the same kind means that, exactly as in a past life untamed and unsubdued, one once gave up body and life before — and in the present life, in just the same way, one gives up body and life again. It should be known that this is called death of the same kind, death that resembles it, and death that follows accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth and sixth kinds of death draw together everything already explained — past, present, untamed, and tamed death — into a final pair. A being untamed in a past life, having given up that life still ungoverned by affliction, and now giving up this present life in exactly the same untamed condition, dies a death of the same kind: resembling the death before it, and, since the same affliction and its seed persist unbroken, a death that simply follows the pattern already set.

203. Section: Death of a Different Kind — Closing This First Distinction Entirely

If, having been untamed and unsubdued in a past life and having already given up body and life then, one is, in the present life, already tamed and already subdued when giving up body and life now, it should be known that this is called death of a different kind, death unlike it, and death that does not follow the same pattern.

Chinese Source

若於過去不調不伏，捨身命已，於現在世已調已伏而捨身命，當知此名不同分死、不相似死、不隨順死。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, having been untamed and unsubdued in a past life and having already given up body and life then, one is, in the present life, already tamed and already subdued when giving up body and life now, it should be known that this is called death of a different kind, death unlike it, and death that does not follow the same pattern.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A being untamed in a past life who, in this present life, encounters the true dharma, draws near a genuine teacher, hears and properly reflects on the teaching, and practices accordingly, realizing anywhere from the first fruit to the fourth, now dies having genuinely tamed affliction — a death entirely unlike the one before it, since the past carried affliction and the present does not, never again destined for the evil destinies or any further round of the three realms' own resultant existence: death of a different kind, unresembling, and not following the same pattern at all. With this, death of the same and different kind, distinguished by whether life was once given up while still untamed, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the same distinction examined instead through the presence or absence of the latent tendency itself.

204. Section: A Second Distinction Begins — By the Presence or Absence of the Latent Tendency

If, in the present life, still carrying the latent tendency's own activity, passing it on in succession, one dies in just the same way as death in the past, this too is named death of the same kind and death that follows accordingly.

Chinese Source

若於現在有隨眠行，展轉隨眠而命終時，如過去死，名同分死及隨順死。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, in the present life, still carrying the latent tendency's own activity, passing it on in succession, one dies in just the same way as death in the past, this too is named death of the same kind and death that follows accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This second distinction differs slightly from the first: where the first spoke in terms of the resultant body actually given up, this one speaks directly in terms of the latent tendency's own presence — a present life still carrying affliction's own unbroken seed, dying exactly as an untamed past life once did, is likewise death of the same kind, following accordingly — the same underlying reality examined from a slightly different angle.

205. Section: Death of a Different Kind — Closing All Six Kinds of Death Entirely

Not dying as one died in the past, and thereby unable to take up the formations of the same kind that would otherwise bind one to future birth and continuity, this is named death of a different kind, and death that does not follow the same pattern.

Chinese Source

不如過去而命終時，不能攝取當所結生未來相續同分諸行，名不同分死及不隨順死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Not dying as one died in the past, and thereby unable to take up the formations of the same kind that would otherwise bind one to future birth and continuity, this is named death of a different kind, and death that does not follow the same pattern.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

One whose affliction-seed has genuinely been cut off in this present life no longer possesses any cause that could draw a further resultant existence in the three evil destinies or the three realms alike — unable, therefore, to take up any formation of the same kind that would bind one to a future rebirth resembling the past. With this, all six kinds of death — past, present, untamed, tamed, same-kind, and different-kind, examined both by whether life was once given up while still untamed and by the presence or absence of the latent tendency itself — are closed in their entirety, completing the classification of kinds. The treatise turns next to a brief summary of their marks.

206. Section: A Brief Summary Begins — Two Marks Named

And it should be known that these six kinds of death carry two marks: the mark of the formations' own dangerous cycling, and the mark of the formations' own excellent return to cessation.

Chinese Source

又此六種死，當知有二種相。謂諸行流轉過患相，及諸行還滅勝利相。

Ru-Yi Translation

And it should be known that these six kinds of death carry two marks: the mark of the formations' own dangerous cycling, and the mark of the formations' own excellent return to cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All six kinds of death reduce, in the end, to just two marks: formations cycling on through birth-and-death, affliction and karma and suffering their own inevitable danger; or formations returning to cessation, the excellent benefit of cutting off the very affliction and karma that would otherwise lead to the evil destinies, restored instead to nirvāṇa's own peaceful stillness.

207. Section: The Mark of Dangerous Cycling

If, in both the past and the present, remaining untamed and unsubdued, one dies a death of the same kind, and thereby takes up birth and every further suffering in the future, bound by the bondage of greed and the rest, this is named the mark of the formations' own dangerous cycling.

Chinese Source

若於過去及於現在不調不伏，同分而死，復於未來取生等苦，及為貪等煩惱縛者，名諸行流轉過患相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, in both the past and the present, remaining untamed and unsubdued, one dies a death of the same kind, and thereby takes up birth and every further suffering in the future, bound by the bondage of greed and the rest, this is named the mark of the formations' own dangerous cycling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A being who leaves affliction untamed across both a past and a present life, dying the same way each time, inevitably takes up a further resultant existence, birth, aging, sickness, and death all still to come, the mind tightly bound by greed, hatred, and delusion — the very danger inherent in birth-and-death's own endless cycling.

208. Section: The Mark of Excellent Return to Cessation — Closing the Brief Summary, and Ending, the Third Part, in Its Complete Entirety

If, in the present life, already tamed and already subdued, one dies a death of a different kind, and thereby takes up no further suffering in the future, liberated entirely from every bondage of affliction, this is named the mark of the formations' own excellent return to cessation.

Chinese Source

若於現在已調已伏，不同分死，又於未來不取眾苦，解脫一切煩惱縛者，名諸行還滅勝利相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, in the present life, already tamed and already subdued, one dies a death of a different kind, and thereby takes up no further suffering in the future, liberated entirely from every bondage of affliction, this is named the mark of the formations' own excellent return to cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra directly

One who has genuinely tamed affliction in this present life, both its active manifestation and its underlying seed, no longer draws forth any further resultant existence in the evil destinies or the three realms at all — every suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death, and every bondage of affliction whatsoever, entirely released: nirvāṇa with residue reached in this very life, nirvāṇa without residue reached once the lifespan itself is exhausted, this cycle of birth-and-death never to continue again. A companion sūtra records a monk requesting brief instruction from the Buddha directly, resolving to dwell alone in solitude thereafter and practice with diligence until realizing "birth is exhausted, the chaste life established, what was to be done is done, no further existence to be known"; the Buddha teaches him simply that whatever dharma should not genuinely be his own must be cut off at once, since doing so brings nirvāṇa's own benefit and lasting ease through birth-and-death's long night — the monk, understanding that the five aggregates themselves are exactly this dharma to be cut off, departs, practices accordingly, and swiftly realizes arhatship. With this, ending — all six kinds of death, and the two marks that summarize them — is closed in its entirety, completing the third of eleven parts. The treatise turns next to falling into number, the fourth part.

209. Section: Falling Into Number Begins — Eight Marks of Conventional Speech

Further, through eight marks, one comes to be included among the conventionally-spoken categories through which the formations' own arising is described — namely, such a name, such a kind, such a lineage, such food, such experience of pleasure or pain, such longevity, such endurance, and such a boundary to one's own lifespan.

Chinese Source

復次，由八種相，得入於彼諸行生起世俗言說士夫數中。謂如是名、如是種類、如是族姓、如是飲食、如是領受若苦若樂、如是長壽、如是久住、如是所有壽量邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through eight marks, one comes to be included among the conventionally-spoken categories through which the formations' own arising is described — namely, such a name, such a kind, such a lineage, such food, such experience of pleasure or pain, such longevity, such endurance, and such a boundary to one's own lifespan.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Falling into number, the fourth part, names eight conventional marks by which ordinary speech describes any person: one's own name in a past life; birth as a brahmin, khattiya, vaiśya, or śūdra; family lineage; habitual diet; which life was more painful or more pleasant; longevity, endurance, and lifespan's own final boundary together — beyond fifty years counting as long life, beyond eighty as enduring, and, for a stream-enterer, this present life itself already marked as the very last, or, for other trainees, the certainty of nirvāṇa or arhatship without any further existence to come — this final phrase marking that same boundary directly.

210. Section: A Reference to Fuller Treatment — Closing Falling Into Number Entirely

These same marks should be understood exactly as already explained at length under recollection of past lives in the Bodhisattva Stage.

Chinese Source

如是諸相，於菩薩地宿住念中，當知如前已廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

These same marks should be understood exactly as already explained at length under recollection of past lives in the Bodhisattva Stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Bodhisattva Stage and a companion sūtra directly

The Bodhisattva Stage, fascicle 49, already named these same eight phrases, and further reduces them to six practical categories: calling someone by name; distinguishing khattiya and the other castes by outward kind; distinguishing parentage; distinguishing customary manner of eating; distinguishing flourishing from decline in the experience of pleasure and pain; and distinguishing lifespan itself — a bodhisattva possessing recollection of past lives can discern each of these for any being directly. Left untamed, a being simply continues falling into this same conventional cycle, life after life, ever bound by the same eight marks. A companion sūtra confirms the same teaching as before, with one key addition: following affliction's own compulsion without taming it increases the count of birth-and-death's own cycling through the desire, form, and formless realms alike — "falling into number" precisely names this ongoing accumulation; ceasing to follow affliction's compulsion, by contrast, adds nothing further to that same count at all. With this, falling into number, the fourth of eleven parts, is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off, the fifth part.

211. Section: Threefold Complete Wisdom Begins — Three Marks Named, a Question Posed

Further, through three marks, it should be known, among the formations, complete wisdom regarding no-self, and its cutting-off. What are the three?

Chinese Source

復次，由三種相，於諸行中，應知無我遍智及斷。何等為三？

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through three marks, it should be known, among the formations, complete wisdom regarding no-self, and its cutting-off. What are the three?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off, the fifth part, names three marks through which a practitioner comes to fully penetrate no-self across every aspect of the aggregates and thereby cut off self-clinging entirely, posing what these three marks are before naming them.

212. Section: The Three Marks Listed

First, complete wisdom regarding what is inward; second, complete wisdom regarding what is outward; third, complete wisdom regarding both inward and outward together. Their cutting-off follows the very same threefold pattern, according to what applies.

Chinese Source

一、於內遍智，二、於外遍智，三、於內外遍智。斷亦如是，隨其所應。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, complete wisdom regarding what is inward; second, complete wisdom regarding what is outward; third, complete wisdom regarding both inward and outward together. Their cutting-off follows the very same threefold pattern, according to what applies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 9

Fascicle 9 already named these same three kinds of ignorance this wisdom counters: ignorance regarding what is inward, improperly clinging to one's own aggregates as self; ignorance regarding what is outward, improperly clinging to non-sentient external things — a table, a chair, a house — as belonging to self; and ignorance regarding both together, improperly discriminating another being's own aggregates as friend, enemy, or stranger. Complete wisdom regarding what is inward penetrates that the aggregates possess no self at all; complete wisdom regarding what is outward penetrates that nothing external genuinely belongs to self either; and complete wisdom regarding both together penetrates that no being should be regarded with friendship, enmity, or indifference on the basis of any such clinging. Cutting off these same three kinds of ignorance in turn is precisely threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off together.

213. Section: Explained Further — Closing Threefold Complete Wisdom and Cutting-Off's Classification Entirely

This means that the formations altogether possess no self, no belonging to self, and no further mutual entanglement with any other. It should be known that this is precisely complete wisdom and cutting-off regarding what is inward, outward, and both together.

Chinese Source

所謂諸行都無有我，無有我所，亦無有餘互相繫屬。當知如是於內、外、俱遍智及斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that the formations altogether possess no self, no belonging to self, and no further mutual entanglement with any other. It should be known that this is precisely complete wisdom and cutting-off regarding what is inward, outward, and both together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"The formations possess no self" names complete wisdom regarding what is inward directly; "no belonging to self" names complete wisdom regarding what is outward; and "no further mutual entanglement with any other" names complete wisdom regarding both together, free of friendship, enmity, or indifference toward any other being whatsoever. Only by genuinely penetrating that inner body, outer possession, and other beings alike are all without self and without what belongs to self can this threefold complete wisdom, and the threefold cutting-off of ignorance it accomplishes, truly be realized. With this, the classification of threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the causes and conditions through which it is actually attained.

214. Section: The Causes of Attainment Begin — Distinguished by Stage

Here, through the wisdom that abides in the dharma, one attains this same decisive complete wisdom — and, through habituating it repeatedly, one releases the latent tendency correspondent with it, attaining its ultimate cutting-off.

Chinese Source

此中由法住智得決定遍智，數習此故，捨彼相應所有隨眠，得畢竟斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, through the wisdom that abides in the dharma, one attains this same decisive complete wisdom — and, through habituating it repeatedly, one releases the latent tendency correspondent with it, attaining its ultimate cutting-off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Bases directly

The wisdom that abides in the dharma names the wisdom capable of penetrating the Buddha's own teaching, expressed through language, giving rise to decisive complete wisdom — the four noble truths, and the truth of no-self and nothing belonging to self, genuinely penetrated together; sustained habituation of that same wisdom, resting on calm and cultivating insight, then cuts off the correspondent affliction's own seed, reaching nirvāṇa's own wisdom, ultimate cutting-off — practice, in other words, always proceeds first through the wisdom that abides in the dharma, and only afterward through nirvāṇa's own wisdom. The Compendium of Bases, fascicle 87, confirms this same pair directly: two kinds of wisdom bring purity of view and excellent purity of view — the wisdom that abides in the dharma, and, resting on it, the wisdom of nirvāṇa. The wisdom that abides in the dharma knows the formations' own distinct particular marks and their shared mark of danger alike, discerning the three kinds of suffering across pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling in turn; the wisdom of nirvāṇa, resting on that same knowledge, first gives rise to the perception of suffering, then reflects that every suffering-laden formation whatsoever can be permanently, entirely extinguished without residue — down through, at length, this very knowing is called the wisdom of nirvāṇa.

215. Section: The Buddha's Own Skillful Means Begins — Leading to Complete Wisdom

It should be known that here, for the sake of those who have not yet attained complete wisdom regarding the formations, so that they might attain it, the Tathāgata, the great teacher, teaches the true dharma's own essential points.

Chinese Source

當知此中，為於諸行未得遍智者令得遍智故，如來大師說正法要。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that here, for the sake of those who have not yet attained complete wisdom regarding the formations, so that they might attain it, the Tathāgata, the great teacher, teaches the true dharma's own essential points.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

For disciples who have not yet reached the wisdom that abides in the dharma, unable to fully see the aggregates' own selfless nature, the Buddha teaches the true dharma's own essential core directly, so that inward, outward, and combined complete wisdom might genuinely be attained.

216. Section: Leading to Ultimate Cutting-Off — Closing Threefold Complete Wisdom and Cutting-Off, and Fascicle 85's Entire Available Content, in Its Complete Entirety

If complete wisdom regarding the formations has already been attained, yet its ultimate cutting-off has not, then, in order that the complete wisdom already gained might, through repeated habituation, reach its own ultimate cutting-off, the Buddha further offers exhortation and guidance.

Chinese Source

若於諸行已得遍智，而未永斷者，為令唯於如先所得遍智數習得永斷故，復加勸導。

Ru-Yi Translation

If complete wisdom regarding the formations has already been attained, yet its ultimate cutting-off has not, then, in order that the complete wisdom already gained might, through repeated habituation, reach its own ultimate cutting-off, the Buddha further offers exhortation and guidance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra directly

For disciples who already possess the wisdom that abides in the dharma but have not yet cut off affliction's own seed, the Buddha continues to encourage sustained habituation of that same wisdom until nirvāṇa's own wisdom is finally reached — recognizing that such disciples have already done the foundational scriptural work, and are now ready to move from study into genuine, sustained practice. A companion sūtra records a monk requesting brief instruction, resolving to practice alone in solitude toward arhatship; the Buddha teaches him directly to swiftly cut off whatever dharma is not genuinely his own, since doing so brings nirvāṇa's own benefit and lasting ease — the monk understanding at once that the five aggregates themselves are exactly what must be released, and, through diligent practice, swiftly realizing arhatship, departing with a joyful heart. With this, threefold complete wisdom and cutting-off — its classification, the causes of its attainment, and the Buddha's own skillful means leading first to complete wisdom and then to its ultimate cutting-off — is closed in its entirety, completing the fifth of brief teaching's eleven parts.
