Fascicle 85 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十五 · The Compendium of Bases Begins

Twenty-Four Kinds of Sūtra, and the Aggregates Section Opens

Fascicle 85 opens the Compendium of Bases (攝事分), the treatise's fifth and final major division, spanning sixteen fascicles in total (85 through 100). Where the Compendium of Synonyms sorted out the many names scattered through the sūtras, this final division grounds all of that in the actual textual sources themselves — the sūtras, the vinaya, and the treatises (摩怛理迦) that comment on them. Fascicle 85 begins with sūtra matters (契經事), the first and largest of the three, itself organized into four sections of analysis: the aggregates (行擇攝, fascicles 85–88), the sense-bases (處擇攝, fascicles 89–92), dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements (緣起食諦界擇攝, fascicles 93–96), and the factors of awakening (菩提分法擇攝, fascicles 97–98). This fascicle opens by cataloguing twenty-four distinct kinds of sūtra found across the canon, then begins unpacking the first four in detail, starting with the Prātimokṣa Sūtra, the code of monastic discipline itself.

1. Section: The Compendium of Bases Opens — Sūtra Matters, the Aggregates Section, Part One

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the aggregates section, the first, part one.

Chinese Source

攝事分中契經事行擇攝第一之一

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Compendium of Bases: sūtra matters, the aggregates section, the first, part one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise's five major divisions unfold in sequence: the Basic Section explains the seventeen stages directly; the Determinations return to resolve what remained unclear there; the Compendium of Explanations then teaches how a sūtra's own text and meaning should be handled; the Compendium of Synonyms, just closed, sorts out the many names a single reality can bear; and now, finally, the Compendium of Bases grounds everything in the actual textual sources themselves — the sūtras, the vinaya, and the treatises that comment on them — since even sorting out names presupposes some text those names are drawn from. Spanning sixteen fascicles, the Compendium of Bases treats the sūtras first (fascicles 85–98, fourteen fascicles), then the vinaya (fascicles 99 through the first half of 100, a fascicle and a half), and finally the treatises (the second half of fascicle 100). Its sūtra section draws overwhelmingly on the Saṃyukta Āgama, with some material from the Ekottara, Madhyama, and Dīrgha Āgamas as well — the modern scholar Lü Cheng first demonstrated, in a 1923 paper, that this section follows the Saṃyukta Āgama's own sequence exactly, functioning as its mātṛkā (topical outline) though without quoting the sūtra text directly; Yin Shun later combined the two into a single reference work, the Combined Edition of the Saṃyukta Āgama and Its Treatise, which this fascicle's own notes draw on throughout. A sūtra (契經, "linking text") is prose narration of the Buddha's own teaching, distinct from verse — its own fascicle 81 defines it as running prose whose primary aim is to gather up the Buddha's intent through direct, connected exposition, and the Abhidharma-hṛdaya-śāstra offers five further glosses: like a person's birth, it can give rise to boundless further teaching; like a spring, it flows without exhaustion; it discloses many kinds of meaning; like a carpenter's inked line, it distinguishes right from wrong; and like a garland strung from many flowers, it threads scattered teachings into a single continuous whole. Sūtra matters (素怛纜事) is itself organized into four further sections of analysis: the aggregates (fascicles 85–88), the sense-bases (89–92), dependent origination together with food, the truths, and the elements (93–96), and the factors of awakening (97–98) — and since sūtra matters comes first among the three textual sources, and the aggregates section comes first among its four analyses, this fascicle is headed "the first, part one." The aggregates section itself unpacks the Saṃyukta Āgama's own chapter on the five aggregates, teaching how contemplating them rightly leads to liberation.

3. Section: Three Textual Sources Named — Closing the Overview

It should be known that bases are comprised through three sources: first, sūtra matters; second, vinaya matters; third, mātṛkā matters.

Chinese Source

謂由三處，應知攝事。一者、素怛纜事，二者、毗奈耶事，三者、摩怛理迦事。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that bases are comprised through three sources: first, sūtra matters; second, vinaya matters; third, mātṛkā matters.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 100 and the Śrāvaka Stage

Sūtra matters treats the sūtras themselves; vinaya matters treats the monastic code; and mātṛkā matters treats the treatises that comment on both — fascicle 100 defines mātṛkā simply as any treatise commenting on the sūtras or the vinaya, gathered together under this single name. Sorted by the twelvefold division of the canon, the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 25, assigns sūtra, verse teaching, prophecy, verse, spontaneous utterance, parable, past-life account, birth-story, extended teaching, and unprecedented dharma all to the sūtra basket; causal narrative to the vinaya basket; and discursive treatise to the abhidharma basket. With this, the three textual sources this final division rests on are named in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a brief catalogue of the sūtras themselves, twenty-four kinds in all.

Ru-Yi Translation

What are sūtra matters? By twenty-four categories, every sūtra is briefly comprised. First, the Prātimokṣa Sūtra. Second, sūtras of narrative matter. Third, sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle. Fourth, sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle. Fifth, sūtras that render explicit a meaning not yet made explicit. Sixth, sūtras that further clarify and purify a meaning already made explicit. Seventh, sūtras spoken beforehand as preparation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Synonyms directly

Sūtra matters catalogues every sūtra found across the canon under twenty-four headings — the first four sorted by the sūtra's own textual category, the remaining twenty by its meaning, the first four serving as root and the latter twenty as their branches, each implying the other. The Prātimokṣa Sūtra is the vinaya's own textual portion, teaching that individually keeping each precept individually releases a corresponding affliction. Sūtras of narrative matter are the four āgamas themselves — Saṃyukta, Madhyama, Ekottara, and Dīrgha. Sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle teach the four noble truths and dependent origination for an audience of śrāvakas — the four āgamas again fit this description. Sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle address the great vehicle's own capacity directly, such as the Vimalakīrti, Lotus, Avataṃsaka, Ratnakūṭa, and Mahāsaṃnipāta sūtras, classed among the extended teachings of the twelvefold canon. Sūtras that render explicit a meaning not yet made explicit disclose what a prior teaching left concealed — the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, for instance, unpacks the Prajñāpāramitā's own profound, hidden meaning for those of duller faculties unable to enter emptiness through the Middle Way's reasoning alone, the Buddha delivering it in his third turning of the wheel precisely for this purpose. And sūtras spoken beforehand as preparation teach the causal link between generosity, discipline, and rebirth among gods and humans, laying the ground before the deeper teaching follows — exactly the "preamble" the Compendium of Synonyms itself already described: a skillful teacher first speaks of giving, discipline, and heavenly rebirth to draw out a listener's own genuine interest before turning to their full capacity and offering the deeper teaching earnestly and without reserve.

Eighth, sūtras of praise. Ninth, sūtras revealing the dark portion. Tenth, sūtras revealing the bright portion. Eleventh, sūtras of provisional meaning. Twelfth, sūtras of definitive meaning. Thirteenth, sūtras brief in meaning yet extensive in wording. Fourteenth, sūtras extensive in meaning yet brief in wording. Fifteenth, sūtras brief in both meaning and wording. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Sūtras of praise extol the three jewels' own virtue, such as Aśvaghoṣa's Acts of the Buddha. Sūtras revealing the dark portion expose affliction, karma, and suffering, or the ten unwholesome deeds and the three evil destinies, as the Kṣitigarbha Sūtra does. Sūtras revealing the bright portion teach wholesome dharmas instead, the form and formless heavens or the ten wholesome deeds, as the Sūtra on the Ten Wholesome Ways of Action does. Sūtras of provisional meaning leave their own deepest intent unstated, as texts aligned with the Middle Way's own reasoning leave the hidden meaning of essence-less dependent origination unspoken, such as the Great Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra; sūtras of definitive meaning instead spell out every characteristic of every dharma directly, as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra does. The following pairs — brief or extensive in meaning, brief or extensive in wording, profound or plain in either — show that meaning and wording can combine in every possible way: sometimes a simple point is unpacked at great length or compressed to a few words; sometimes a profound truth is delivered in plain language accessible to all, or matched with equally dense and demanding wording — the Buddha spoke sometimes with utmost economy, sometimes at great expository length, always according to his listener's own capacity. Sūtras that free one from a fault yet to come address some prophesied future danger, as the Sūtra of Ānanda's Seven Dreams does — the Buddha, nearing his own final nirvāṇa, lamented that the true dharma's ruin would come not from outside attack but from within, "worms born in the lion's own body devouring the lion's own flesh," a warning his disciples were meant to heed. Sūtras that free one from a fault already present instead counter affliction actively present right now — the two paired against each other, future versus present. Sūtras that dispel doubt as it arises resolve uncertainty about the three jewels, or about one's own possession of buddha-nature, as the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra teaches that every being without exception possesses it. And sūtras that cause the true dharma to abide long in the world include, beyond the vinaya itself, every teaching on the emptiness of self and of dharmas, on consciousness-only, or on the perfection of wisdom. With this, all twenty-four kinds of sūtra are named in their full enumeration. The treatise turns next to explaining the first four in detail, beginning with the Prātimokṣa Sūtra.

6. Section: Explaining the First Four Begins — the Prātimokṣa Sūtra

The Prātimokṣa Sūtra means that within it, resting on the five categories of offense and on release from the five categories of offense, more than two hundred and fifty training precepts are taught, so that good sons who genuinely love themselves might diligently train in them.

Chinese Source

別解脫契經者，謂於是中，依五犯聚及出五犯聚，說過二百五十學處，為令自愛諸善男子精勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Prātimokṣa Sūtra means that within it, resting on the five categories of offense and on release from the five categories of offense, more than two hundred and fifty training precepts are taught, so that good sons who genuinely love themselves might diligently train in them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Discipline Section and the Śrāvaka Stage

Having catalogued all twenty-four kinds, the treatise now unpacks the first four individually, beginning with the Prātimokṣa Sūtra, the monastic precept text itself, sorting every offense from heaviest to lightest into five categories, and teaching, alongside them, how each offense is properly released through confession. Though loosely reckoned at two hundred and fifty precepts, the actual number of subtle rules exceeds this round figure considerably. The Discipline Section names the five categories directly: defeat, the gravest category, in which affliction has fully overcome the practitioner; remainder, a lesser fault still recoverable, like a beheading that leaves the throat somehow intact, curable by a skilled physician; downfall, offenses serious enough to lead toward the evil destinies — thirty each for monks and nuns concerning material goods, ninety more for monks and one hundred seventy-eight more for nuns concerning other matters, though confession can still purify them; confession-to-another, a lighter fault cleared simply by confessing individually to a fellow monastic; and wrongdoing, covering minor training rules and careless speech, born of a lapse in mindfulness, cleared through simple confession to oneself or to another. The Discipline Section, fascicle 99, treats all five at length; grouped by severity, the first two count as heavy offenses, the third and fourth as intermediate, and the fifth alone as light. The precise historical count of two hundred fifty precepts remains debated among commentators; the Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 22, confirms that a monastic, having heard the fuller Prātimokṣa from a preceptor or teacher beyond the core precepts already known, vows in each case: "in all of this, I shall train."

7. Section: Sūtras of Narrative Matter Begin — the Four Āgamas Named

Sūtras of narrative matter mean the four āgamas: first, the Saṃyukta Āgama; second, the Madhyama Āgama; third, the Dīrgha Āgama; fourth, the Ekottara Āgama.

Chinese Source

事契經者，謂四阿笈摩。一者、雜阿笈摩，二者、中阿笈摩，三者、長阿笈摩，四者、增一阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sūtras of narrative matter mean the four āgamas: first, the Saṃyukta Āgama; second, the Madhyama Āgama; third, the Dīrgha Āgama; fourth, the Ekottara Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of the Mind Ground

Āgama means "transmission" — teachings passed down unaltered from the Buddha through his disciples in turn. The Stage of the Mind Ground, fascicle 3, explains that every word the buddhas speak is comprised within nine matters: the matter of sentient beings, namely the five grasping aggregates; the matter of enjoyment, namely the twelve bases; the matter of arising, namely the twelvefold links of dependent origination and what is dependently arisen; the matter of abiding, namely the four kinds of food; the matter of defilement and purity, namely the four noble truths; the matter of distinctions, namely the boundless elements; the matter of the speaker, namely the Buddha and his disciples; the matter of what is spoken, namely the factors of awakening beginning with the four foundations of mindfulness; and the matter of the assembly, namely the eight kinds of assembly — each of the four āgamas draws on this same ninefold framework throughout.

8. Section: The Saṃyukta Āgama Begins — Correspondence With Its Own Subject Matter

The Saṃyukta Āgama means that within it, the World-Honored One, according to whichever beings he was teaching, proclaimed correspondences with what the Tathāgata and his disciples themselves taught; correspondences with the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases; correspondences with dependent origination, food, and the truths; and correspondences with the foundations of mindfulness, the right efforts, the bases of power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of awakening, the path-factors, mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith.

Chinese Source

雜阿笈摩者，謂於是中，世尊觀待彼彼所化，宣說如來及諸弟子所說相應，蘊、界、處相應，緣起、食、諦相應，念住、正斷、神足、根、力、覺支、道支、入出息念、學、證淨等相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Saṃyukta Āgama means that within it, the World-Honored One, according to whichever beings he was teaching, proclaimed correspondences with what the Tathāgata and his disciples themselves taught; correspondences with the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases; correspondences with dependent origination, food, and the truths; and correspondences with the foundations of mindfulness, the right efforts, the bases of power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of awakening, the path-factors, mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Compendium of Explanations and Yin Shun's own reorganization

The Saṃyukta Āgama takes its very name from this principle of correspondence (相應) — teachings gathered by shared theme, whether the aggregates, the sense-bases, dependent origination, the noble truths, and so on. Yin Shun's Combined Edition sorts its contents into seven recitations comprising fifty-one such correspondences: the aggregates recitation (correspondence 1); the six sense-bases recitation (2); the mixed-causes recitation (3–6); the path-factors recitation (7–16); the eight-assemblies recitation (17–27); the disciples' own teachings recitation (28–33); and the Tathāgata's own teachings recitation (34–51, the largest, spanning eighteen correspondences alone, since eminent disciples such as Śāriputra, Maudgalyāyana, Aniruddha, Mahākātyāyana, Ānanda, and Citra each contributed their own recorded teachings as well). Sorted instead by content rather than speaker, this same material covers the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases (the aggregates and six-bases recitations); dependent origination and the food that sustains a being from moment to moment, itself absorbed within the mixed-causes recitation in Yin Shun's own reworking, alongside the noble truths (a hundred fifty sūtras) and the eighteen elements (thirty-seven sūtras); and the path-factors recitation's own further ten correspondences — mindfulness, right effort, the bases of power, the faculties, the powers, the factors of awakening, the path-factors, mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith — totaling four hundred five sūtras across widely varying lengths, from fifty-four sūtras on mindfulness alone to a mere handful surviving for the bases of power. The Compendium of Explanations, fascicle 81, already explained "what is taught" as whatever accords with the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, the possible and the impossible, and the foundations of mindfulness — the very same content named here as correspondence.

9. Section: Correspondence With the Eight Assemblies — Closing Correspondence's Twofold Account

And further, resting on the eight assemblies, correspondence with the assembly is spoken of.

Chinese Source

又依八眾說眾相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, resting on the eight assemblies, correspondence with the assembly is spoken of.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of the Mind Ground

Beyond who speaks and what is spoken, correspondence with the assembly names whom a teaching was spoken for — the Stage of the Mind Ground, fascicle 3, names eight such assemblies: khattiyas (the warrior-noble caste); brahmins (India's priestly caste, custodians of the Vedas); householders (wealthy or venerable laypeople, such as the elder Anāthapiṇḍada, or the aged Nakula, said to have reached one hundred twenty years); renunciants, who have given up worldly wealth for the homeless life; the four great heavenly kings, lowest of the six desire-realm heavens; the thirty-three gods, the second desire-realm heaven atop Mount Sumeru, ruled by Śakra from his central palace, surrounded by thirty-two further deva-kings for the thirty-three total; Yama's own heaven, the third desire-realm heaven, and the lowest heaven whose inhabitants no longer dwell on solid ground; and the Brahmā heavens, the first absorption's own form-realm gods, comprising Great Brahmā himself, his ministers, and his general retinue. Yin Shun's own Combined Edition further divides the eight-assemblies recitation into eleven correspondences: monks; Māra, including his own recorded exchanges with the Buddha; Śakra, the thirty-three gods' devoted protector, alongside Great Brahmā, in contrast to Māra's own frequent opposition; khattiyas; brahmins; the Brahmā heavens; nuns; the venerable Vaṅgīsa, famed among the Buddha's disciples both for his threefold knowledge and for composing spontaneous verses in praise of the three jewels; the various heavens; yakṣas, spirits dwelling on earth or in the air who may harass or protect the true dharma; and the forest, recording events that took place there, such as forest deities urging monks on toward diligent practice. With this, correspondence — with the Buddha's and his disciples' own teaching, with its content, and with the eight assemblies it was taught to — is closed in its full twofold account. The treatise turns next to the Saṃyukta Āgama's own compilation.

10. Section: Its Compilation Explained — the Summary Verses

Its later compilation, in order that the noble teaching might abide long, gathered summary verses, arranged in sequence according to what was fitting.

Chinese Source

後結集者，為令聖教久住，結嗢柁南頌，隨其所應次第安布。

Ru-Yi Translation

Its later compilation, in order that the noble teaching might abide long, gathered summary verses, arranged in sequence according to what was fitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 87 and Yin Shun directly

After the Buddha's parinirvāṇa, the venerable Mahākāśyapa proposed compiling the teachings, and five hundred arhats gathered to recite the three baskets together from memory, since no writing yet preserved them; to aid recitation and ensure the teaching's long survival, the elders composed a brief summary verse after every ten sūtras, gathering their essential content into a single memorable stanza. The first ten sūtras of the aggregates correspondence, for instance, are summarized: "impermanence, and suffering, and emptiness; not-self, and right reflection; ignorance and its like, four kinds; and joy taken in form" — each phrase capturing one sūtra's own central teaching in turn: the first taught impermanence, the second suffering, the third emptiness, the fourth not-self, the fifth right reflection, the sixth through ninth various kinds of ignorance, and the tenth delight taken in form. Fascicle 87 confirms: "the later compilers, within this teaching, are called the World-Honored One's own summary verses." Yin Shun's own Combined Edition cites a further source, the Treatise Distinguishing Merit: "having finished compiling the three baskets, they recorded ten sūtras as one verse, so that future reciters, fearing they might forget or err, could see the name and recall the source, reflecting and awakening to it themselves."

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that every correspondence of this kind rests, in brief, on three marks. What are the three? First, the one who speaks; second, what is spoken; third, whom it is spoken for.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Every correspondence throughout the Saṃyukta Āgama can be reduced to three simple marks: the Buddha or a noble disciple as the one who genuinely, correctly speaks; the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, food, the truths, and the path-factors as what is spoken; and the eight assemblies as whom it is spoken for.

12. Section: Matching Each Mark — the One Who Speaks

Whether the Tathāgata or a disciple of the Tathāgata, this is the one who speaks — as with what the disciples taught, and the portion the Buddha himself taught.

Chinese Source

若如來、若如來弟子，是能說。如弟子所說，佛所說分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether the Tathāgata or a disciple of the Tathāgata, this is the one who speaks — as with what the disciples taught, and the portion the Buddha himself taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond the Buddha himself, his noble disciples too count as genuine speakers of the teaching — exactly the disciples' own teachings and the Tathāgata's own teachings recorded across their respective recitations.

13. Section: What Is Spoken

Whether what is to be known or the very faculty that knows it, this is what is spoken — as with the five grasping aggregates, the six bases, the portion on dependent origination, and the portion on the path-factors.

Chinese Source

若所了知、若能了知，是所說。如五取蘊、六處、因緣相應分及道品分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether what is to be known or the very faculty that knows it, this is what is spoken — as with the five grasping aggregates, the six bases, the portion on dependent origination, and the portion on the path-factors.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

What is spoken divides in two: the object to be known, namely the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements taught across their own recitations, together with dependent origination taught in the mixed-causes recitation; and the knowing faculty itself, namely the wisdom cultivated through the path-factors recitation's own foundations of mindfulness, right efforts, and bases of power, since only by cultivating the noble path can a practitioner truly know the object contemplated. In short, what is to be known is the object of contemplation; what does the knowing is the noble path itself.

The Buddha taught according to each of the eight assemblies' own capacity — monks, Māra, Śakra, and the rest — exactly the eight-assemblies recitation's own many correspondences, each teaching pitched to its own particular audience. With this, the threefold essential meaning — the one who speaks, what is spoken, and whom it is spoken for — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement naming the Saṃyukta Āgama.

15. Section: Naming the Saṃyukta Āgama — Closing Its Full Account Entirely

All of this, broadly and briefly setting forth the one who speaks, what is spoken, and whom it is spoken for — this same teaching on correspondence with matters, interspersed in due sequence and gathered together — is therefore named the Saṃyukta Āgama.

Chinese Source

如是一切，麤略標舉能說、所說及所為說，即彼一切事相應教間廁鳩集，是故說名雜阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

All of this, broadly and briefly setting forth the one who speaks, what is spoken, and whom it is spoken for — this same teaching on correspondence with matters, interspersed in due sequence and gathered together — is therefore named the Saṃyukta Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing Yin Shun directly

"Interspersed in due sequence" describes how the Saṃyukta Āgama's own many teachings — those on who speaks, spoken by the Tathāgata's own recitation and his disciples' recitation, and those on what is spoken, sorted by aggregates, sense-bases, mixed causes, and path-factors in turn — were gathered by shared theme into an orderly sequence, sūtras on the aggregates grouped together, sūtras on dependent origination grouped together, and so on through the noble truths and the eighteen elements, everything interspersed yet ultimately unified into a single coherent whole. "Mixed" here does not mean disordered but abundant and interwoven — Yin Shun's own Combined Edition notes that in Chinese, this same character need not mean chaos at all; "interspersed" precisely captures its intended sense of orderly interweaving. With this, the Saṃyukta Āgama — its correspondences, its compilation, its threefold essential meaning, and this concluding statement of its own name — is closed in its entirety, completing the first of the four āgamas. The treatise turns next to the Madhyama Āgama.

16. Section: The Madhyama Āgama — Closing the Second Āgama

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken again through further marks at middling length, is therefore named the Madhyama Āgama.

Chinese Source

即彼相應教，復以餘相處中而說，是故說名中阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken again through further marks at middling length, is therefore named the Madhyama Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

All four āgamas ultimately teach the very same aggregates, sense-bases, elements, and dependent origination, corresponding to the same ultimate truth — yet since listeners' own capacities differ, the same content is delivered at different lengths. The Madhyama Āgama's own sūtras run neither especially brief nor especially long, a middling length, hence its name; in content, it dwells at particular length on meditative concentration and its methods of cultivation. With this, the Madhyama Āgama is closed, completing the second of the four āgamas. The treatise turns next to the Dīrgha Āgama.

17. Section: The Dīrgha Āgama — Closing the Third Āgama

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through further marks at great length, is therefore named the Dīrgha Āgama.

Chinese Source

即彼相應教，更以餘相廣長而說，是故說名長阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through further marks at great length, is therefore named the Dīrgha Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Certain portions of the same teaching required considerably more length to unpack — the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, for instance, recounts the Buddha's own life at length, from his birth through his teaching of disciples — and such extended treatments were gathered into the Dīrgha Āgama, named simply for its own greater length, its content thorough and detailed yet never exceeding the Saṃyukta Āgama's own foundational scope. With this, the Dīrgha Āgama is closed, completing the third of the four āgamas. The treatise turns next to the Ekottara Āgama.

18. Section: The Ekottara Āgama — Closing All Four Āgamas in Their Entirety

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through the reasoning of gradually increasing categories — one, two, three, and so on — is therefore named the Ekottara Āgama.

Chinese Source

即彼相應教，更以一二三等漸增分數道理而說，是故說名增一阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

That very same teaching on correspondence, spoken yet again through the reasoning of gradually increasing categories — one, two, three, and so on — is therefore named the Ekottara Āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Ekottara Āgama likewise rests on the Saṃyukta Āgama's own foundation, drawing out whatever content lends itself to numerical extension — wholesomeness itself, for instance, unpacked first as a single overall category, freedom from fault, then divided into two, then three, four, five, six, and onward through ten or eleven distinct kinds, the same underlying principle examined through gradually increasing enumeration; this same treatise itself often teaches in exactly this incremental style. Among the four āgamas, the Saṃyukta Āgama is root and origin, earliest of all, while the remaining three each further explain and extend it — content of middling length forming the Madhyama Āgama, more extensively worded content forming the Dīrgha Āgama, and content expressed through gradually increasing numerical categories forming the Ekottara Āgama, yet none departing from what the Saṃyukta Āgama itself already established. With this, the Ekottara Āgama is closed, completing all four āgamas — the Saṃyukta, Madhyama, Dīrgha, and Ekottara alike — in their entirety. The treatise turns next to explaining the term āgama itself.

19. Section: Explaining the Term Āgama — Closing Its Own Meaning

These four kinds, passed down from teacher to disciple in unbroken succession, have arrived down to the present day. Through this very principle, they are therefore called āgama.

Chinese Source

如是四種，師弟展轉傳來於今。由此道理，是故說名阿笈摩。

Ru-Yi Translation

These four kinds, passed down from teacher to disciple in unbroken succession, have arrived down to the present day. Through this very principle, they are therefore called āgama.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Āgama means "transmission," carrying the sense of unbroken succession, teacher instructing disciple across generations — precisely why these four collections, handed down from the Buddha and his noble disciples all the way to the present, bear this shared name. With this, the term āgama itself is closed, its own meaning fully explained. The treatise turns next to a concluding summary naming sūtras of narrative matter.

20. Section: A Concluding Summary — Closing Sūtras of Narrative Matter Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called sūtras of narrative matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As the treatise's own earlier catalogue named it, sūtras of narrative matter form the second of the twenty-four kinds of sūtra — namely the four āgamas just explained in full. With this, sūtras of narrative matter is closed in its entirety, completing the second of the four kinds explained in detail. The treatise turns next to sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle and the great vehicle together, the third and fourth kinds.

21. Section: Śrāvaka and Great Vehicle Sūtras Begin — What Each Comprises

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The canon's twelve textual genres sort here into two groups: the extended teachings (方廣, vaipulya) alone comprise sūtras corresponding to the great vehicle, while sūtra, verse teaching, prophecy, verse, spontaneous utterance, causal narrative, past-life account, birth-story, unprecedented dharma, parable, and discursive treatise together comprise sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka vehicle.

22. Section: A Reference to Fuller Treatment — Closing Śrāvaka and Great Vehicle Sūtras, and All Four Kinds Explained in Detail

This same distinction should be understood exactly as already explained before.

Chinese Source

此分別義，如前應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

This same distinction should be understood exactly as already explained before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing fascicle 38 and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Fascicle 38, in the Bodhisattva Stage, already taught this same division: the extended teachings alone belong to the bodhisattva basket, while every remaining genre belongs to the śrāvaka basket. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes record several further accounts of how the twelve genres divide between the two vehicles: the Nirvāṇa Sūtra, fascicle 3, assigns nine genres to the great vehicle, omitting only causal narrative, parable, and discursive treatise; the Lotus Sūtra, fascicle 1, assigns nine to the śrāvaka vehicle, omitting only extended teachings, spontaneous utterance, and prophecy; and the Compendium of Mixed Topics offers a fourfold sorting instead — sūtra, verse teaching, prophecy, verse, and spontaneous utterance belong to the śrāvaka basket's sūtra division; causal narrative, parable, past-life account, and birth-story belong to the vinaya division shared by both vehicles; extended teachings and unprecedented dharma belong to the bodhisattva basket's own sūtra division; and discursive treatise belongs to the abhidharma shared by both — noting, further, that in truth both the greater and lesser baskets each contain all twelve genres in full, these various groupings simply emphasizing different angles of classification. With this, śrāvaka and great vehicle sūtras is closed in its entirety, completing all four of the twenty-four kinds explained in detail — the Prātimokṣa Sūtra, sūtras of narrative matter, and sūtras corresponding to the śrāvaka and great vehicles alike. The treatise turns next to the remaining twenty kinds, left to the reader's own inference.

The treatise now turns to examine all three textual sources in careful detail, beginning with the mātṛkā, or root-treatise, that comments on the sūtras themselves — its purpose is precisely to determine and clarify the true intent of the sūtras the Buddha himself spoke and endorsed. The Śrāvaka Stage, fascicle 25, defines a sūtra broadly as the World-Honored One, in various places, teaching various beings he was training, according to their own particular conduct, proclaiming boundless discourse corresponding to the aggregates, the sense-bases, dependent origination, food, the truths, the elements, the śrāvaka vehicle, the pratyekabuddha vehicle, the buddha vehicle, the foundations of mindfulness through the path-factors, and mindfulness of breathing, the trainings, and confirmed faith. This sūtra-commentary undertakes to determine and discern exactly this discourse — hence the four major sections that follow: the aggregates section, the sense-bases section, the section on dependent origination together with food, the truths, and the elements, and the section on the factors of awakening — unpacking the four āgamas' own full content in turn.

25. Section: Why It Is Called Mātṛkā

Just as, without the root-mother of writing, meaning remains unclear, so too a sūtra not comprised within its own root-mother remains obscure, its meaning unclear. Where this is not so, meaning becomes clear — hence it is called mātṛkā.

Chinese Source

譬如無本母字，義不明了。如是本母所不攝經，其義隱昧，義不明了。與此相違，義即明了，是故說名摩怛理迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as, without the root-mother of writing, meaning remains unclear, so too a sūtra not comprised within its own root-mother remains obscure, its meaning unclear. Where this is not so, meaning becomes clear — hence it is called mātṛkā.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Mātṛkā means "root-mother," the mother of wisdom itself — without such a root-treatise to draw out its structure, a sūtra's own text and meaning are far from easy to grasp; portions of the Saṃyukta Āgama, read alone, often remain genuinely obscure. Only by studying this very fascicle alongside the Saṃyukta Āgama itself does its meaning become truly accessible — had Maitreya never composed this treatise to unlock it, disciples would be left to interpret the bare text unaided, its most profound, hidden meaning forever out of reach; with a treatise to illuminate it, that same meaning becomes clear and wisdom can genuinely arise in the reader — precisely why this kind of commentary earns the name mātṛkā, root-mother of the text it explains.

27. Section: Element Begins — Its Own Particular Summary Verse

Chinese Source

別嗢柁南曰：界說前行觀察果　愚相無常等定界

二種漸次應當知　非斷非常及染淨

Ru-Yi Translation

The particular summary verse says: element, teaching, preparation, observation, and result; delusion's own mark, the impermanence that fixes the boundary of element; the two kinds of gradual sequence should be known; neither cut off nor permanent, and defilement and purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's first topic, element, unfolds across eleven further parts, this verse naming each: element, teaching, preparation, observation, result, delusion's own mark, the impermanence that fixes element's boundary, element again, the two kinds of gradual sequence, neither cut off nor permanent, and defilement and purity. Maitreya explains the opening five sūtras of the Saṃyukta Āgama's own aggregates recitation through the first three of these alone — element, teaching, and preparation. Element carries the sense of a seed: the Buddha spoke these five sūtras because four kinds of being carry four kinds of wrong view, each needing right view — observing the five aggregates as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — to counter it. Teaching names the three features that distinguish the Buddha's own teaching from every non-Buddhist path: genuine liberation itself, the means to it, and the personal realization that follows once it is reached — contemplating the five aggregates through impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, both in their full extent and in their true nature, leads a practitioner all the way to genuine, ultimate liberation. And preparation names the preliminary practice leading up to the path of seeing, and the preliminary practice leading up to arhatship itself.

28. Section: The Prose Explanation Begins — Element's Wrong Kind — a Question Posed

There are four kinds of being trained who have, through long habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation. What are the four?

有四種所化有情先數習邪解脫見所集成界。

There are four kinds of being trained who have, through long habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation. What are the four?

The prose explanation unfolds across eleven sections matching the verse's own eleven parts, the first of which, element, first distinguishes its own wrong kind. Four kinds of being, prior to gaining right view, have habituated a wrong view of liberation in a past life or earlier in this one, forming four distinct seeds of wrong element — the treatise poses the question of what these four are before answering it in turn.

29. Section: The First Wrong View — Eternalism

The first: resting on a prior existence, a prior life, a prior body, or a prior attainment of one's own being, having heard a teaching dominated by the view of eternalism, and through the ascendant power of improper attention, now, through that very view as cause and that very attention as condition, one has, through repeated habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation.

Chinese Source

謂於先有、先世、先身、先所得自體中，聽聞常見增上不正法、不如理作意增上力故，於今由彼為因、由彼為緣，數習邪解脫見所集成界。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first: resting on a prior existence, a prior life, a prior body, or a prior attainment of one's own being, having heard a teaching dominated by the view of eternalism, and through the ascendant power of improper attention, now, through that very view as cause and that very attention as condition, one has, through repeated habituation, gathered together an element from a wrong view of liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first wrong view, eternalism, takes root when a being, in some prior existence, life, body, or resultant state, hears and absorbs a teaching dominated by the view that self and world are permanent, giving rise to improper attention that reinforces it — through the combined power of that view as cause and that attention as condition, repeated habituation gathers together a seed, an "element," of this same wrong view of liberation, carried forward into the present.

30. Section: The Remaining Three Wrong Views — Annihilationism, Present-Life Nirvāṇa, and the View of a Perishable Self — Closing Element's Wrong Kind Entirely

As is said of eternalism, so too, at length, should annihilationism, the view of nirvāṇa in this very life, and the view of a perishable self be understood.

Chinese Source

如說由常見，如是由斷見、由現法涅槃見、由薩迦耶見，廣說亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

As is said of eternalism, so too, at length, should annihilationism, the view of nirvāṇa in this very life, and the view of a perishable self be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Annihilationism clings to the opposite extreme, that self and world are cut off entirely at death or eventual cosmic dissolution, with nothing whatsoever continuing after. The view of nirvāṇa in this very life takes one of two forms: some non-Buddhist ascetics, materialists among them, take present enjoyment of the five sense pleasures itself to be nirvāṇa, and so indulge them without restraint; others mistake the joy, ease, or equanimity attained through meditative absorption for the noble fruit itself — traditionally enumerated as five distinct versions of this same wrong view, tied to enjoyment in the desire realm and to each of the four form-realm absorptions in turn. And the view of a perishable self is simply the view of self, root of the sixty-two speculative views tradition catalogues, here reduced to these same four broad categories: eternalism, annihilationism, the view of present-life nirvāṇa, and the view of a perishable self. With this, element's own wrong kind — all four views, eternalism and its three companions — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to how the Buddha subdues each of these four wrong elements in turn.

31. Section: Subduing These Views Begins — a General Statement

Here, the World-Honored One, through the ascendant power of his manifold wisdom regarding resolve and his manifold wisdom regarding elements, seeks out a being's own prior resolve and its resulting element, and, according to what is fitting, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element, for the most part turns the wheel of four kinds of teaching.

Chinese Source

此中世尊，由種種勝解智力、種種界智力增上力故，尋求彼先勝解及彼後界，如其所應，為調伏彼邪勝解界故，多分為轉四種法教。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the World-Honored One, through the ascendant power of his manifold wisdom regarding resolve and his manifold wisdom regarding elements, seeks out a being's own prior resolve and its resulting element, and, according to what is fitting, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element, for the most part turns the wheel of four kinds of teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Possessing the wisdom to know every being's own disposition and the wisdom to know every being's own underlying element, the Buddha, in meditative absorption, directly perceives a being's habituated wrong view from a past life together with its seed still present in the mind today, and, matching his teaching to what that being needs, proclaims four teachings — impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self — to counter eternalism, annihilationism, the view of present-life nirvāṇa, and the view of a perishable self in turn. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm these same four teachings are meant, applied respectively to subdue each of the four wrong views named above.

32. Section: Countering Eternalism — Teaching Impermanence Through the Cessation of Formations

For beings fallen into the first wrong element, it is taught that because the cause ceases, formations cease — through this approach of formations' own exhaustion, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為初邪界有情，說因滅故行滅，由行盡門說無常性，為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the first wrong element, it is taught that because the cause ceases, formations cease — through this approach of formations' own exhaustion, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

To subdue a being habituated to eternalism, the Buddha teaches impermanence through the angle of cessation: once affliction, the cause, ceases, the formations it produces cease as well — this ceasing, that ceases — directly dismantling the belief that self or world persists unchanged.

33. Section: Countering Annihilationism — Teaching Impermanence Through the Arising of Formations

For beings fallen into the second wrong element, it is taught that because the cause gathers, formations gather — through this approach of formations' own arising, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為墮第二邪界有情，說因集故行集，由行起門說無常性，為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the second wrong element, it is taught that because the cause gathers, formations gather — through this approach of formations' own arising, impermanence's own nature is taught, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Against the belief that death is like a lamp snuffed out, the Buddha teaches impermanence through the opposite angle, arising: this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises — so long as affliction's own cause remains, formations continue to form and the five aggregates continue to arise in succession, death settling nothing at all; where the first teaching stressed "this ceasing, that ceases" against eternalism, this one stresses "this arising, that arises" against annihilationism, each countering its own particular error.

34. Section: Countering the View of Present-Life Nirvāṇa — Teaching Formations as Suffering

For beings fallen into the third wrong element, the true dharma is turned through the approach of every formation's own suffering, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為墮第三邪界有情，由諸行苦門轉正法教，為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the third wrong element, the true dharma is turned through the approach of every formation's own suffering, in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Against the mistaken belief that pleasant feeling itself, or the joy and ease of meditative absorption, constitutes genuine nirvāṇa, the Buddha teaches that every formation, every feeling, is suffering — joy and pleasure are themselves the suffering of change, and even neutral feeling is the suffering inherent in all conditioned things; none of it is nirvāṇa at all.

35. Section: Countering the View of a Perishable Self — Teaching Emptiness or No-Self According to the Kind of Self-View — Closing the Four Teachings and Element Entirely

For beings fallen into the fourth wrong element: for those whose view of a perishable self arises apart from the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of the formations' own emptiness; for those whose view of a perishable self arises identified with the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of no-self — in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Chinese Source

為墮第四邪界有情，若離諸行起薩迦耶見行者，由諸行空門轉正法教；若即諸行起薩迦耶見行者，由無我門轉正法教；為調伏彼邪勝解界故。

Ru-Yi Translation

For beings fallen into the fourth wrong element: for those whose view of a perishable self arises apart from the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of the formations' own emptiness; for those whose view of a perishable self arises identified with the formations themselves, the true dharma is turned through the approach of no-self — in order to subdue that wrong resolve and its element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The view of self takes two shapes: one clings to a self existing apart from the five aggregates entirely, unrelated to them — for such a person, the Buddha teaches that formations beyond the five aggregates are likewise impermanent and empty, no fixed, unchanging self to be found anywhere; the other clings to the five aggregates themselves as self — for such a person, the Buddha teaches that the aggregates, arising from causes and conditions, are impermanent and never self-governing, and being impermanent, cannot be self, no unchanging self to be found within them either. With this, all four teachings matched to the four wrong views are closed in their entirety, completing element's own first section — both its wrong kind, fourfold, and its subduing, general and specific alike — in its complete entirety. The treatise turns next to teaching, the second of the eleven parts.

36. Section: Teaching Begins — Three Features Unique to the Buddha's Own Dharma and Discipline

Further, a well-taught dharma and discipline rests, in brief, on three features unique to it, not shared with any non-Buddhist path, and thereby falls within the category of what is well taught. First, it teaches genuine, ultimate liberation. Second, it teaches the very means to it. Third, it teaches personal realization, inwardly witnessed, of that same liberation.

Chinese Source

復次，善說法律略由三種不共支故，不共外道，墮善說數。一者、宣說真實究竟解脫故；二者、宣說即彼方便故；三者、宣說即彼自內所證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, a well-taught dharma and discipline rests, in brief, on three features unique to it, not shared with any non-Buddhist path, and thereby falls within the category of what is well taught. First, it teaches genuine, ultimate liberation. Second, it teaches the very means to it. Third, it teaches personal realization, inwardly witnessed, of that same liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The aggregates section's second part, teaching, names three marks distinguishing the Buddha's own teaching from every non-Buddhist path. First, genuine and ultimate liberation: stream-entry is genuine liberation, arhatship is ultimate liberation, and contemplating the five aggregates through impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, as the Buddha taught, leads a practitioner to both in turn. Second, the very means to it: right view and right reflection, correctly contemplating or reflecting on the five aggregates through these same four aspects, serve as the preliminary practice through which liberation is actually reached. And third, personal, inwardly-witnessed realization: a practitioner who genuinely practices in accordance with the dharma personally realizes the noble path directly — the Buddha, never speaking falsely, guarantees that whoever practices exactly as taught will certainly realize the path and attain liberation.

37. Section: Unpacking Each Feature Begins — What Is Genuine and Ultimate Liberation?

What is genuine and ultimate liberation?

云何真實究竟解脫？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is genuine and ultimate liberation?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having named the three unique features, the treatise now unpacks each individually across three further sections, beginning with a direct question: what, precisely, constitutes genuine and ultimate liberation?

38. Section: Two Kinds of Liberation Named

This means ultimate liberation and liberation in every respect — namely, the fruit of the path of seeing, and, following after it, the fruit of the path of cultivation, worldly and transcendent alike.

Chinese Source

謂畢竟解脫及一切解脫，即是見道果，及此後所得世出世修道果。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means ultimate liberation and liberation in every respect — namely, the fruit of the path of seeing, and, following after it, the fruit of the path of cultivation, worldly and transcendent alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Genuine liberation names ultimate liberation, the fruit of the path of seeing — an ordinary being's own affliction genuinely overcome, freedom from the three evil destinies' own suffering truly realized and never again lost, stream-entry itself. Yet this is not yet complete: a practitioner must continue cultivating insight through the path of cultivation, worldly and transcendent, advancing through the second and third fruits to arhatship, liberation in every respect, every affliction without exception finally released.

39. Section: A Further Distinction — Why Stream-Entry Is Genuine Yet Not Ultimate — Closing the Twofold Account of Liberation

Here, the fruit of the path of seeing, through being ultimate in its own respect, is named genuine — yet it is not ultimate in every respect, since there still remains something further to be done regarding liberation in every respect.

Chinese Source

此中見道果，由畢竟故得名真實，而非究竟，於一切解脫猶有所應作故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the fruit of the path of seeing, through being ultimate in its own respect, is named genuine — yet it is not ultimate in every respect, since there still remains something further to be done regarding liberation in every respect.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A stream-enterer, never again to fall back into an ordinary being, is genuinely liberated — the distinctly-arisen seeds of affliction are truly cut off, never to arise again. Yet sixteen innately-arisen afflictions of craving and view remain: six in the desire realm (the view of a perishable self, extreme views, craving, hatred, conceit, and ignorance — every distinctly-arisen affliction already removed), and five each in the form and formless realms (the same list minus hatred) — all sixteen still awaiting the path of cultivation's own further work. A stream-enterer must therefore continue cultivating calm and insight to remove what remains — genuine liberation, yet not the ultimate liberation only an arhat achieves. With this, the twofold account of liberation — ultimate liberation and liberation in every respect — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to a threefold account of liberation.

40. Section: A Threefold Account of Liberation Begins — Three Kinds Named

And further, liberation is of three kinds: first, worldly liberation; second, a trainee's liberation; third, liberation beyond training.

Chinese Source

又解脫有三種。一、世間解脫，二、有學解脫，三、無學解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, liberation is of three kinds: first, worldly liberation; second, a trainee's liberation; third, liberation beyond training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second, threefold sorting of liberation now follows the twofold account just closed, naming worldly liberation, a trainee's liberation, and liberation beyond training in turn.

41. Section: Distinguishing the Three — Only the One Beyond Training Possesses Both Genuine and Ultimate Liberation — Closing Genuine and Ultimate Liberation Entirely

Worldly liberation is not genuine, since it is subject to regression. A trainee's liberation, though genuine, is not ultimate, since something further still remains to be done. It should be known that what remains possesses both.

Chinese Source

世間解脫非是真實，有退轉故。有學解脫雖是真實，而非究竟，猶有所作故。當知所餘，具足二種。

Ru-Yi Translation

Worldly liberation is not genuine, since it is subject to regression. A trainee's liberation, though genuine, is not ultimate, since something further still remains to be done. It should be known that what remains possesses both.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Worldly liberation names meditative attainment — form-realm absorption frees one from the desire realm's own affliction, formless attainment frees one from both desire and form; yet this liberation only suppresses affliction's active manifestation, never uprooting its underlying seed, and once concentration itself is lost, the practitioner falls back to the desire realm once more — not genuine liberation at all. A trainee — the first through third fruits — genuinely cuts off the distinctly-arisen afflictions by their very root, like pulling a weed out whole, where worldly liberation merely mows the grass down to the soil, leaving the root to sprout again after the next rain; yet even this genuine cutting falls short of the ultimate, since the remaining innately-arisen afflictions still call for further diligent practice. Only what remains — liberation beyond training, arhatship itself — possesses both together, every affliction of the three realms, distinctly-arisen and innately-arisen alike, cut off entirely; only this liberation beyond training is truly ultimate. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm that "what remains" here refers specifically to liberation beyond training. With this, genuine and ultimate liberation, examined through both the twofold and threefold accounts, is closed in its entirety, completing the first of teaching's three unique features. The treatise turns next to the means that leads to it.

42. Section: The Means to Liberation Begins — a Question Posed

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the means?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Teaching's second unique feature poses directly what method actually leads a practitioner to genuine and ultimate liberation alike.

43. Section: The Means to Genuine Liberation — Contemplating Formations Through Their Full Extent and True Nature

This means that, among the formations, resting on their true nature and their full extent, one cultivates the perception of impermanence; resting on impermanence, one cultivates the perception of suffering; resting on suffering, one cultivates the perception of emptiness and no-self — through this, upon entering the direct realization of the truths, through correctly observing the object to be known, right view is attained.

Chinese Source

謂於諸行中，依如所有性及盡所有性，修無常想；依無常，修苦想；依苦，修空、無我想；因此得入諦現觀時，由正觀察所知境故，獲得正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that, among the formations, resting on their true nature and their full extent, one cultivates the perception of impermanence; resting on impermanence, one cultivates the perception of suffering; resting on suffering, one cultivates the perception of emptiness and no-self — through this, upon entering the direct realization of the truths, through correctly observing the object to be known, right view is attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations' Bodhisattva Stage

The means to genuine, stream-entry liberation rests on contemplating the five aggregates through both their true nature and their full extent. True nature contemplates the aggregates' own shared mark — impermanent, suffering, empty, without self — the Determinations, Bodhisattva Stage, fascicle 77, defining true nature as suchness itself, present in every defiled and pure dharma alike: reflecting on impermanence gives rise to suffering, genuinely felt; resting on suffering, emptiness follows — no self apart from the aggregates to be found (empty), and no governing, permanent function within the aggregates either (no-self). Full extent instead contemplates every possible instance of the aggregates without exception — past, present, or future, fine or coarse, superior or inferior, near or far, desire-realm or form- or formless-realm — every single one alike impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self. Cultivating these four perceptions in meditation leads a practitioner into direct realization of the four noble truths; this account specifically describes the truth of suffering's own contemplation at the level of forbearance and supreme worldly dharma, the final steps before the path of seeing itself — a practitioner still at the level of heat or summit must additionally contemplate the truth of origination through cause, arising, and condition, the truth of the path through path, suchness, accomplishment, and deliverance, and the truth of cessation through cessation, peace, excellence, and freedom, the complete preparatory contemplation leading up to the path of seeing.

44. Section: The Means to Ultimate Liberation Begins — Dwelling in the Perception of Aversion

Resting on this very right view as support, within the path of cultivation, one dwells pervasively in the perception of aversion toward every formation.

Chinese Source

由此正見為依止故，修道位中，遍於諸行住厭逆想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on this very right view as support, within the path of cultivation, one dwells pervasively in the perception of aversion toward every formation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The means to ultimate liberation, arhatship, unfolds across three further steps. Resting on the right view already gained through contemplating the aggregates' true nature and full extent, a trainee at the path of cultivation continues reflecting on the aggregates as sickness, an abscess, an arrow, and a source of harm, dwelling pervasively in aversion toward them — no longer delighting in the aggregates, turning against them, no longer wishing for the resultant fruit of further birth-and-death.

45. Section: Extinguishing the Active Manifestation of Joy and Craving Begins — While Dwelling in Stillness

While dwelling in that state, though feeling correspondent to it, recollecting, and reflecting on an object not currently present, brings it vividly before the mind, joy does not arise.

Chinese Source

彼於住時，雖由彼相應受，憶念思惟不現前境明了現前，而不生喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

While dwelling in that state, though feeling correspondent to it, recollecting, and reflecting on an object not currently present, brings it vividly before the mind, joy does not arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second step, extinguishing the active manifestation of joy and craving, unfolds first while dwelling in stillness: even deliberately calling to mind some pleasing object of the five aggregates during seated calm and insight, vividly present though not actually before the senses, no attachment or delight arises — the aversion cultivated through right view holds firm even under deliberate testing, no longer drawn toward the impure, unlovely body or its pleasures. This corresponds to the application of attention called examination, among the seven applications of attention.

46. Section: While in Motion — No Defiled Attachment Arises Even Toward What Is Genuinely Present

Through the ascendant power of no joy arising, while in motion, toward the very object that feeling takes as its support, no defiled attachment arises. Toward every object one might seek, having attained equanimity, one does not even long for it, let alone indulge it.

Chinese Source

由不生喜增上力故，彼於行時，即於彼受所緣境界不生染著。彼於一切所求境界，得處中故，尚不希求，何況耽著。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the ascendant power of no joy arising, while in motion, toward the very object that feeling takes as its support, no defiled attachment arises. Toward every object one might seek, having attained equanimity, one does not even long for it, let alone indulge it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Where the previous step concerned deliberately-imagined objects during seated stillness, this step concerns genuinely encountered objects during ordinary activity — walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, a practitioner may actually meet a pleasing circumstance, yet the same firmly-rooted aversion, strengthened by the absence of joy already cultivated, prevents any defiled attachment from arising toward it, self or other alike. Toward whatever the world prizes — lasting youth, a long life — a practitioner who knows it is impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, mastering neither it nor the desire for it, remains in equanimity, neither seeking nor rejecting, resting always in the highest equanimity — not even longing for such fine circumstances, let alone becoming attached to them. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes gloss equanimity as dwelling evenly within a neutral, unbiased state. Whether in motion or at rest, this practice extinguishes the active manifestation of joy and craving alike.

47. Section: A Summary Statement — Closing Extinguishing Joy and Craving's Active Manifestation Entirely

Through dwelling and moving in this way, one swiftly extinguishes the entanglement of joy and craving entirely, the mind abiding in purity.

Chinese Source

彼由如是若住、若行，於喜貪纏速能滅盡，心清淨住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through dwelling and moving in this way, one swiftly extinguishes the entanglement of joy and craving entirely, the mind abiding in purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Through this sustained training in both stillness and activity, the active manifestation of joy and craving is swiftly and thoroughly extinguished, the mind settling into lasting purity — mindfulness strengthened, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self genuinely realized, nothing left in the world worth chasing or being troubled by. With this, extinguishing the active manifestation of joy and craving — both while dwelling and while in motion — is closed in its entirety, completing the second of ultimate liberation's three steps. The treatise turns next to permanently uprooting the latent tendency itself.

48. Section: Permanently Uprooting the Latent Tendency — Closing the Means to Both Genuine and Ultimate Liberation Entirely

And, resting on that very path already attained, through the cause and condition of cultivating it most thoroughly, one permanently uproots the coarse latent tendency belonging to that class of affliction, attaining genuine and ultimate liberation — this should be known as the mind's own excellent liberation.

Chinese Source

又即於彼如所得道極多修習為因緣故，永拔彼品麤重隨眠，獲得真實究竟解脫，當知即是心善解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

And, resting on that very path already attained, through the cause and condition of cultivating it most thoroughly, one permanently uproots the coarse latent tendency belonging to that class of affliction, attaining genuine and ultimate liberation — this should be known as the mind's own excellent liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

As a practitioner's own path-power deepens through repeated cultivation of the same contemplation — the aggregates' true nature and full extent, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, applied again and again, not once or twice but countless times over — the coarse, incapable latent tendency of innate desire and craving is finally uprooted entirely, its very seed extinguished, genuine and ultimate liberation both attained at once: arhatship, the mind's own excellent liberation. The content contemplated never changes, only the depth of realization — reading a text for the first time and the tenth time differ profoundly, though the words themselves are identical; speaking a teaching once and speaking it a hundred times carry entirely different power. With this, the means to both genuine and ultimate liberation — contemplation's own true nature and full extent, dwelling in aversion, extinguishing joy and craving's active manifestation, and permanently uprooting its latent tendency — is closed in its entirety, completing teaching's second unique feature. The treatise turns next to personal, inwardly-witnessed realization, the third unique feature.

49. Section: Personal, Inwardly-Witnessed Realization Begins — a Question Posed

What is personal, inwardly-witnessed realization?

云何自內所證？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is personal, inwardly-witnessed realization?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Teaching's third and final unique feature poses the question of what a practitioner actually experiences, personally and directly, upon genuinely realizing the path.

50. Section: Four Marks Named

It should be known that there are four marks.

Chinese Source

當知有四種相。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be known that there are four marks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Realizing the path, from stream-entry through arhatship, carries four distinct marks, each to be examined by whether it belongs to a trainee or to one beyond training.

51. Section: The Trainee's Stage Begins — Two Marks Named

When turning within a trainee's own liberation, inward wisdom directly touches and confirms two marks.

Chinese Source

若於有學解脫轉時，由二種相，內慧觸證。

Ru-Yi Translation

When turning within a trainee's own liberation, inward wisdom directly touches and confirms two marks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The two positions distinguished here, trainee and one beyond training, are read two ways by the tradition: the Yogācārabhūmi Notes take "trainee" as the first fruit specifically, matching the surrounding contrast with arhatship; the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation instead reads it as spanning the first three fruits together. Since the passages before and after both concern the specific contrast between stream-entry and arhatship, the noble path divides here into two broad stages — stream-entry marking the pivotal shift from ordinary being to noble one, and arhatship marking the śrāvaka path's own complete fulfillment — following the Yogācārabhūmi Notes' own angle of reading.

52. Section: The Two Marks Explained — Freedom From the Evil Destinies, and an Unregressing Path

This means: I have exhausted every formation that would otherwise arise within the evil destinies. And I have exhausted every remaining formation of future existence apart from seven more births, two more births, or one more birth — and I now abide in an unregressing path that will bring this to its ultimate end.

Chinese Source

謂我已盡諸惡趣中所生諸行，又我已盡除其七生、二生、一生所餘後有所生諸行，又我已住能究竟盡無退轉道。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means: I have exhausted every formation that would otherwise arise within the evil destinies. And I have exhausted every remaining formation of future existence apart from seven more births, two more births, or one more birth — and I now abide in an unregressing path that will bring this to its ultimate end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Yogācārabhūmi Notes and the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation

A stream-enterer directly knows, through personal realization, that the three evil destinies are forever closed — not through deliberate wisdom cutting them off, but simply because the cause that would lead there no longer exists, the discriminated affliction that alone could produce such a rebirth already gone, even though innate affliction still remains. Commentators differ on how to read "seven births, two births, one birth, and the remaining formations of future existence." Read through the Yogācārabhūmi Elucidation's own account, a stream-enterer, not yet fully free of desire, knows their remaining innate affliction still permits at most seven further rounds of birth between the human and heavenly realms, or two, or one, never an eighth existence — certain, in every case, to reach arhatship, the noble path itself never again subject to regression. All three figures apply specifically to rebirth among humans and gods in the desire realm, corresponding to three traditional patterns: seven returns between the human and heavenly realms before arhatship (the "seven births" case); the "family-to-family" pattern, cutting off three of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction, six grades still remaining, needing at most three further human-heaven-human rounds (the pattern the "two births" phrase points to, in an equal reading — three heavenly and three human rebirths, or three human and three heavenly — or an unequal reading — three heavenly and two human, or three human and two heavenly); and a second family-to-family pattern, cutting off four of the nine grades, five grades remaining, needing at most two further rounds, again divided into an equal reading (two heavenly and two human, or two human and two heavenly, corresponding to "two births") and an unequal reading (two heavenly and one human, or one human and two heavenly, corresponding to "one birth"). The Yogācārabhūmi Notes offer a different, simpler correspondence instead: seven births belongs to the stream-enterer as a whole, two births to the once-returner, and one birth to the non-returner — a cleaner mapping across the first three fruits themselves rather than these more granular family-to-family sub-patterns. With this, the trainee's own stage — its two marks, freedom from the evil destinies and an unregressing path toward at most seven, two, or one further birth in all its detailed patterns — is closed in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the stage of the one beyond training.

53. Section: The Stage Beyond Training Begins — Two Marks Named

54. Section: The Two Marks Explained — What Was to Be Trained Is Complete, and Confirming It for Others — Closing the Stage Beyond Training, and Both Stages Entirely

This means: I have accomplished what was to be trained in order to cut off every remaining affliction; I now have not even one further birth remaining, let alone two, let alone seven. And, according to what is fitting, I am also able to confirm this directly and accurately for others.

Chinese Source

謂我已作為斷其餘一切煩惱所應學事，我今尚無餘一生在，況二、況七。又隨所樂，亦能為他如實記別。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means: I have accomplished what was to be trained in order to cut off every remaining affliction; I now have not even one further birth remaining, let alone two, let alone seven. And, according to what is fitting, I am also able to confirm this directly and accurately for others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

An arhat knows directly that every task is done, every affliction subject to the path of cultivation entirely cut off, the śrāvaka path's own training complete — birth, aging, sickness, and death across the three realms and five destinies ultimately exhausted, no further existence to come, not even a single rebirth, let alone two or seven. Beyond this self-knowledge, a noble one beyond training can also, according to another practitioner's own progress, genuinely confirm whether they have reached the first, second, or third fruit, or arhatship itself. These two marks, then: an arhat's own knowledge that every affliction a trainee still carries has now been fully removed, the task complete; and the capacity, beyond simply knowing one's own freedom from further existence, to confirm the same for others. With this, the stage beyond training is closed in its entirety, completing both stages, trainee and beyond training, together. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement.

55. Section: A Concluding Statement — Closing Personal Realization, and Teaching in Its Complete Entirety

Ru-Yi Translation

This is what is called personal, inwardly-witnessed realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A trainee at the first, second, or third fruit knows directly that the evil destinies are closed and the noble path secure against regression; an arhat, whose path-power runs deeper still, knows this same freedom and, further, can confirm it accurately in others as well. With this, personal, inwardly-witnessed realization — its four marks across both the trainee's and the one-beyond-training's own stage — is closed in its entirety, completing teaching, the aggregates section's second topic, across all three of its unique features in their complete entirety. The treatise turns next to preparation, the third topic.

56. Section: Preparation Begins — Two Kinds Named

Further, that very liberation carries two kinds of preparatory practice: first, the preparatory practice for seeing; second, the preparatory practice for the path's own fruit.

The aggregates section's third topic, preparation, names two stages of preliminary practice: the preparation leading to the path of seeing, stream-entry itself, and the preparation leading beyond it, all the way to arhatship's own fruit.

57. Section: The Preparation for Seeing Explained

The preparatory practice for seeing means that, through the ascendant power of liberation, its means, and personal realization, one gives rise, from hearing another's own voice, to the wondrous, skillful, proper attention accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation — and thereby, not yet having entered the true nature that transcends birth, one is able to enter it, gaining the genuine, transcendent right view that sees things as they truly are.

Chinese Source

見前行法者，謂由解脫及彼方便自內所證增上力故，從他言音，起聞思修所成妙善如理作意，未入正性離生能入正性離生，得如實見出世正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

The preparatory practice for seeing means that, through the ascendant power of liberation, its means, and personal realization, one gives rise, from hearing another's own voice, to the wondrous, skillful, proper attention accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation — and thereby, not yet having entered the true nature that transcends birth, one is able to enter it, gaining the genuine, transcendent right view that sees things as they truly are.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Determinations and the Yogācārabhūmi Notes

Hearing the Buddha or a noble disciple skillfully teach liberation, its means, and personal realization gives rise, through their combined power, to the wondrous proper attention accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation in turn — resting on calm, contemplating the aggregates' full extent (all eleven marks: past, present, future, near, far, and the rest) and true nature (impermanent, suffering, empty, without self). Diligently cultivated until conditions ripen, this attention carries a practitioner not yet free of birth into freedom from it, genuine undefiled insight and transcendent right view finally attained. "True nature that transcends birth" names the undefiled noble path or nirvāṇa itself as "true nature"; "birth" names either affliction or an immature wholesome root, and "transcending" it means cutting off the discriminated affliction born of wrong view, or leaving behind roots not yet mature enough to bear the noble path. The Determinations, Stage of the Wisdom Born of Hearing, fascicle 64, name five ways of entering this transcendence: not yet free of desire, halfway free of desire, already free of desire, the pratyekabuddha's own entry, and the bodhisattva's own entry. The Yogācārabhūmi Notes confirm that "ascendant power" here names precisely the three features unique to the Buddha's own teaching already explained — liberation, its means, and personal realization. Together, element, teaching, and preparation explain the aggregates recitation's own first five sūtras: the first teaches that observing form as impermanent is itself right view, leading through weariness, freedom from desire, and the mind's own liberation, to the declaration "birth is exhausted, the chaste life established, what was to be done is done, no further existence to be known" — "birth is exhausted" describing the stream-enterer, whose ordinary rebirth now has a final limit of at most seven returns; "the chaste life established" describing the non-returner, free of desire and no longer returning to the desire realm; and "what was to be done is done" describing the arhat, this present life the very last. The second through fourth sūtras repeat the same teaching applied to suffering, emptiness, and no-self in turn, each equally capable of leading to the very same realization. The fifth sūtra shifts from right view to right reflection — even a stream-enterer must continue reflecting properly on form's own impermanence to fully release craving for it, exactly as an ordinary being must, sustained proper reflection alone eventually freeing the mind from all five aggregates in turn.

58. Section: The Preparation for the Path's Own Fruit — Closing Preparation Entirely

The preparatory practice for the path's own fruit means that, having already gained this right view, one further gives rise to right reflection and the rest, whether arising together with it or arising afterward, as preparatory practice for the path, in order to cut off every remaining affliction.

Chinese Source

道果前行法者，謂得如是正見已，復起所餘正思惟等，或同時生、或後時生道前行法，為斷所餘諸煩惱故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The preparatory practice for the path's own fruit means that, having already gained this right view, one further gives rise to right reflection and the rest, whether arising together with it or arising afterward, as preparatory practice for the path, in order to cut off every remaining affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once the undefiled right view of stream-entry is gained, a practitioner continues generating right reflection, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration — reflecting on the aggregates as sickness, an abscess, and the rest, growing weary of the three realms' own aggregates, these further path-factors arising either together with right view or in its wake, all directed at cutting off whatever affliction remains until arhatship itself is finally reached. With this, preparation — the preparatory practice for seeing and the preparatory practice for the path's own fruit — is closed in its entirety, completing the aggregates section's third topic. The treatise turns next to observation, the fourth topic.

59. Section: Observation Begins — Eight Things to Be Observed

Further, in order to realize a liberation not yet attained, one should observe eight things: observing, among the formations, their relish, their danger, and escape from them; and observing hearing, reflection, the power of discernment, the path of seeing, and the path of cultivation.

Chinese Source

復次，為欲證得所未得解脫故，應觀察八事。謂於諸行中愛味、過患、出離觀察，及聞、思、思擇力、見道、修道觀察。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, in order to realize a liberation not yet attained, one should observe eight things: observing, among the formations, their relish, their danger, and escape from them; and observing hearing, reflection, the power of discernment, the path of seeing, and the path of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Stage of Cultivated Wisdom

The aggregates section's fourth topic names eight things a practitioner must observe in sequence, from ordinary being to noble one: relish taken in the aggregates, the danger that relish conceals, escape from that very relish, and then the wisdom of hearing, of reflection, of discernment (a further sharpening of reflective wisdom capable of examining even worldly cultivated wisdom itself), and finally the path of seeing and the path of cultivation themselves. The Stage of Cultivated Wisdom, fascicle 19, describes escape directly: a practitioner who is able to subdue, cut off, and transcend every craving toward form, and toward every further object of touch, is said to have achieved escape from desire.

60. Section: The First Three Things Begin — Observing Relish

When observing relish among the formations, one becomes able to skillfully penetrate the formations' own particular mark of relish.

Chinese Source

於諸行中觀察愛味時，能善通達諸行愛味所有自相。

Ru-Yi Translation

When observing relish among the formations, one becomes able to skillfully penetrate the formations' own particular mark of relish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Observing whether relish is present toward a pleasing body, pleasing sensory objects, pleasant feeling, endorsed thoughts, a seemingly resolute and self-governing will, and the consciousnesses that discern every object in turn — reflecting on this repeatedly gradually penetrates relish's own particular mark within each of the five aggregates; even the slightest trace of such relish still marks an ordinary being caught in birth-and-death.

61. Section: Observing Danger

When observing danger among those very formations, one becomes able to skillfully know the shared mark of danger present across the three feelings' own distinct conditions — namely, that relish found there is very little, while danger found there is manifold.

Chinese Source

即於諸行觀察過患時，能善了知三受分位過患共相。謂於是中甚少愛味，多諸過患。

Ru-Yi Translation

When observing danger among those very formations, one becomes able to skillfully know the shared mark of danger present across the three feelings' own distinct conditions — namely, that relish found there is very little, while danger found there is manifold.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Observing danger penetrates all three feelings' shared mark as suffering — painful feeling as the suffering of suffering, pleasant feeling as the suffering of change, neutral feeling as the suffering inherent in every conditioned formation; sustained observation of this kind reveals how little the aggregates genuinely offer and how much danger they actually conceal. Where there is a head, there is headache; where there is a heart, it can be wounded; where there is feeling, there is discomfort; where there is perception, grasping at marks and finer characteristics makes right mindfulness difficult to sustain; where there is volition, decisions often go wrong; and where there is consciousness, false discrimination follows, subject and object seemingly split apart, unaware that the object itself is nothing but consciousness's own manifestation — form, feeling, perception, volition, and consciousness alike offer very little worth wanting and a great deal of danger, pleasure fleeting and pain seemingly endless: truly, very little relish, manifold danger.

62. Section: Observing Escape Begins — Its Distinct Mark Named

Having thus understood relish, defiled clinging, and their manifold shared danger, whatever craving and greed then arises toward any formation once relished can, in that very moment, be dispelled, suppressed, and cut off and abandoned.

Chinese Source

如是了知愛味染著多諸過患共相應已，於所愛味一切行中，隨所生起欲貪煩惱，即能除遣、制伏、斷捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus understood relish, defiled clinging, and their manifold shared danger, whatever craving and greed then arises toward any formation once relished can, in that very moment, be dispelled, suppressed, and cut off and abandoned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once a practitioner sees clearly how relish and defiled clinging are bound up with manifold danger, the very craving that relish produces toward any formation can be dispelled, suppressed, and cut off — though it should be understood that this alone only suppresses affliction's active manifestation, not yet its underlying seed, since this is still an early stage of practice; only deeper path-power can genuinely uproot the seed itself.

63. Section: What "Cutting Off" Actually Means — Closing the First Three Things Observed

Because this craving and greed no longer manifests, it is called cut off — not because desire has been permanently left behind that it is called cut off. And, regarding that very matter, the mind is not yet liberated; only once the latent tendency itself is ultimately transcended is desire permanently left behind, the mind genuinely liberated.

Chinese Source

於此欲貪不現行故，說名為斷，非永離欲故名為斷。又於彼事心未解脫，若於隨眠究竟超越，乃永離欲，心得解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because this craving and greed no longer manifests, it is called cut off — not because desire has been permanently left behind that it is called cut off. And, regarding that very matter, the mind is not yet liberated; only once the latent tendency itself is ultimately transcended is desire permanently left behind, the mind genuinely liberated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise clarifies precisely what "cutting off" means at this preliminary stage: craving and greed merely fail to manifest, called cut off in only this limited sense — not that desire is permanently abandoned. Only once the latent seed itself is finally, completely transcended does genuine, permanent freedom from desire arrive, the mind truly liberated at last. With this, the first three of the eight things observed — relish, danger, and escape, together with a careful clarification of what "cutting off" truly means at each stage — are closed in their entirety. The treatise turns next to a concluding statement on these first three.
