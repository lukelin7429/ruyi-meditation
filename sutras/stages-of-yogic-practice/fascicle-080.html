Fascicle 80 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Establishing What a Bodhisattva Learns Through the Middle Way

This is the ninth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, continuing directly from fascicle 79's unfinished chapter on conduct — the sixteen gateways of teaching found within the bodhisattva canon, of which the first five (wrong conduct, right conduct, its excellent benefits, its thirty-two marks, and nineteen similes for pure faith) are already complete. This fascicle opens with the sixth gateway: how a bodhisattva establishes what is to be learned by holding the middle way between two kinds of scripture — provisional teachings addressed to the śrāvaka vehicle, resting on conventional truth, and definitive teachings addressed to the Great Vehicle, resting on both conventional and ultimate truth together. Neither is dismissed; both are studied, so that a bodhisattva's body, speech, and mind stay anchored in right view rather than drifting to one extreme or the other. The remaining ten gateways continue afterward, carrying the chapter on conduct toward its close and opening the treatise's Determination of the Grounds With and Without Residue.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 80 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Sixth Gateway Opens — Studying Both Provisional and Definitive Scripture Without Favoring Either

Furthermore, how does a bodhisattva establish what is to be learned within right conduct? It is that bodhisattvas, possessing Dharma-abiding conduct in full, having already relied upon and grown skillful in those non-definitive scriptures associated with the śrāvaka vehicle that are taught according to the principle of conventional truth and are not what a bodhisattva ultimately depends on, and who further go beyond these, diligently studying those definitive scriptures that may be relied upon, associated with the Great Vehicle and with profound emptiness, taught according to the principle of both conventional and ultimate truth — this is called studying diligently and correctly, according to reason.

Chinese Source

復次，云何菩薩於正行中安立所學？謂諸菩薩具足法住，於依世俗諦道理所說不了義非所依聲聞乘相應經典，已作依持、已作善巧，而復超度於大乘相應、甚深空性相應，依世俗、勝義諦道理所說了義可依經典勤修學時，名為如理正勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, how does a bodhisattva establish what is to be learned within right conduct?
It is that bodhisattvas, possessing Dharma-abiding conduct in full, having already relied upon and grown skillful in those non-definitive scriptures associated with the śrāvaka vehicle that are taught according to the principle of conventional truth and are not what a bodhisattva ultimately depends on, and who further go beyond these, diligently studying those definitive scriptures that may be relied upon, associated with the Great Vehicle and with profound emptiness, taught according to the principle of both conventional and ultimate truth — this is called studying diligently and correctly, according to reason.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Fascicle 80 opens exactly where fascicle 79 left off, continuing the chapter on conduct's sixteen gateways with the sixth: establishing what a bodhisattva actually studies. The Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on conduct (juan 49) names four broad areas of bodhisattva practice — the ten pāramitās, the thirty-seven factors of awakening, the six kinds of supernormal power, and the maturing of sentient beings — but only in outline; the fuller account of how these are actually studied and practiced runs from the end of fascicle 79 through this fascicle, organized as sixteen gateways of teaching drawn from across the entire canon. This sixth gateway addresses a genuine risk: without careful anchoring in right view, even sincere practice can drift to one extreme or another. The remedy is studying both kinds of scripture in their proper place — first relying on and growing skilled in the śrāvaka vehicle's own teaching (not a bodhisattva's final resting place, since it addresses only the emptiness of self, not of dharmas, but still genuinely useful and, in its own domain, correctly called "ultimate" when it leads to direct realization of selflessness), and then going beyond it to the Great Vehicle's own definitive teaching on profound emptiness, taught through both conventional and ultimate truth together. Neither is bypassed; the middle way here means passing properly through both rather than skipping either one.

2. Section: Thirteen Kinds of Middle-Way Conduct — The Complete List

Practicing diligently and correctly, according to reason, in this way, is called rightly practicing the excellent conduct of the middle way. Why is this? Because this true Dharma pierces through thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct. First, piercing through the emptiness of the person; second, piercing through the selflessness of the person; third, piercing through the emptiness of dharmas; fourth, piercing through the selflessness of dharmas; fifth, piercing through the extreme of superimposition; sixth, piercing through the extreme of denial; seventh, piercing through direct realization of dharmas; eighth, piercing through the dedication of that direct realization toward great awakening; ninth, piercing through the nature by which, for one practicing thus, affliction and manifold suffering no longer entangle the mind; tenth, piercing through the distinction between resolute understanding of the two kinds of selflessness; eleventh, piercing through how the prior selflessness serves as cause for the latter; twelfth, piercing through emptiness reaching its outermost limit; and thirteenth, piercing through the majestic power that this very realization brings.

Chinese Source

如是如理勤修學時，名正修行中道勝行。所以者何？由此正法貫穿十三中道行故。一者、貫穿補特伽羅空性；二者、貫穿補特伽羅無我性；三者、貫穿法空性；四者、貫穿法無我性；五者、貫穿增益邊；六者、貫穿損減邊；七者、貫穿法現觀；八者、貫穿法現觀迴向大菩提性；九者、貫穿如是行者煩惱眾苦不纏繞心性；十者、貫穿二無我勝解差別；十一者、貫穿前無我性是後因性；十二者、貫穿到邊際空性；十三者、貫穿即彼威德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Practicing diligently and correctly, according to reason, in this way, is called rightly practicing the excellent conduct of the middle way.
Why is this? Because this true Dharma pierces through thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct.
First, piercing through the emptiness of the person; second, piercing through the selflessness of the person; third, piercing through the emptiness of dharmas; fourth, piercing through the selflessness of dharmas; fifth, piercing through the extreme of superimposition; sixth, piercing through the extreme of denial; seventh, piercing through direct realization of dharmas; eighth, piercing through the dedication of that direct realization toward great awakening; ninth, piercing through the nature by which, for one practicing thus, affliction and manifold suffering no longer entangle the mind; tenth, piercing through the distinction between resolute understanding of the two kinds of selflessness; eleventh, piercing through how the prior selflessness serves as cause for the latter; twelfth, piercing through emptiness reaching its outermost limit; and thirteenth, piercing through the majestic power that this very realization brings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Piercing through" (貫穿) names a thread running continuously through and holding together thirteen aspects of realization, each avoiding one of two extremes. Master Cháng Bǎi's own brief preview of the thirteen, elaborated in the sections that follow: the emptiness of the person and dharmas are both approached through conventional truth (the merely verbal, conceptual self and dharmas ordinary beings impute are simply not found); the selflessness of the person and dharmas are both approached through ultimate truth (the suchness this absence itself reveals is genuinely realized). The extreme of superimposition names eight escalating layers of false construction, from taking permanence as real down to the pure and impure dharmas built upon a falsely substantialized mind; the extreme of denial names the opposite error of "nihilistic emptiness" (惡取空), wrongly concluding that because the imagined nature is unreal, the dependent and perfected natures must be unreal too. Direct realization of dharmas names the threefold process by which the imagined self-nature, its distinguishing features, and finally the practiced familiarity with both are correctly seen through; its dedication toward great awakening names cultivating this very realization out of compassion rather than for one's own escape alone. The ninth names freedom from affliction once emptiness is seen; the tenth distinguishes how self-emptiness and dharma-emptiness are each misapprehended in their own characteristic way; the eleventh explains how the earlier realization causes the later; the twelfth names emptiness carried to its absolute limit; and the thirteenth names the majestic power — likened, in the section ahead, to a single moment of light dispelling a room dark for a thousand years — that this whole realization confers.

3. Section: Piercing Through the Emptiness of the Person — No Real Self to Be Found in Three Kinds of Matter

What is piercing through the emptiness of the person? It is displayed through a single mark: what cannot be found. Within this, what cannot be found is with respect to three kinds of matter: first, the matter of a sentient being; second, the matter of its distinguishing features; and third, the matter of its enjoyment — whether inward, outward, or in between, the real self that the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿補特伽羅空性？謂由一種相，不可得所顯故。此中不可得者，謂於三種事。一者、有情事；二者、彼差別事；三者、彼受用事，若內、若外、若二中間，愚夫遍計所執實我都不可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the emptiness of the person? It is displayed through a single mark: what cannot be found.
Within this, what cannot be found is with respect to three kinds of matter: first, the matter of a sentient being; second, the matter of its distinguishing features; and third, the matter of its enjoyment — whether inward, outward, or in between, the real self that the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of the thirteen begins the sequence with the emptiness of the person — a conventional-truth realization that a substantial, imagined self simply cannot be located anywhere it is sought. Three kinds of matter are searched in turn: the matter of a being itself (the five aggregates of clinging — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — where the sense of "I" first arises); the matter of a being's distinguishing features (the eighteen elements, whose countless combinations produce the boundless variety among the sixty-two kinds of beings the treatise elsewhere catalogues); and the matter of enjoyment (the six inner faculties and six outer objects, and the six consciousnesses arising between them, through which pleasure and pain are actually experienced). Searched inwardly, outwardly, or in the meeting-point between faculty and object, no substantial self ever turns up — the way a car, taken apart into chassis, glass, engine, and seats, reveals no separate thing called "car" beyond its assembled parts. This is the classic argument for selflessness "apart from the aggregates" (離蘊無我): there is no self hiding somewhere among the five aggregates once every conventional label is stripped away.

4. Section: Piercing Through the Selflessness of the Person — What Actually Can Be Found Once the Imagined Self Is Gone

What is piercing through the selflessness of the person? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found. Within this, what can be found is that, within those same three kinds of matter, among the dependently arisen dharmas that the foolish conceive, no permanently abiding real nature can be found — and it is precisely this, the principle of a nature genuinely different from what the foolish conceive, that can be found.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿補特伽羅無我性？謂由唯一相，可得所顯故。此中可得者，謂即於彼三事，愚夫所遍計緣生諸法中，常住實性不可得故，愚夫所計我異相性道理可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the selflessness of the person? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found.
Within this, what can be found is that, within those same three kinds of matter, among the dependently arisen dharmas that the foolish conceive, no permanently abiding real nature can be found — and it is precisely this, the principle of a nature genuinely different from what the foolish conceive, that can be found.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the first kind of piercing-through establishes that no self can be found (an ultimate-truth realization stated in conventional terms), the second flips the emphasis: something genuinely can be found — not a self, but the very absence of one, realized as suchness itself. The distinction, as the Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi puts it concisely: "no self within the aggregates" is called emptiness; "the aggregates themselves are not a self" is called selflessness. The first stresses that a self is not to be found apart from or within the aggregates (離蘊無我); the second stresses that the aggregates, being merely dependently arisen and impermanent, were never in themselves a self to begin with (即蘊無我) — and this negative fact itself constitutes something real and directly realizable, the suchness a noble one's wisdom actually apprehends. Together the first two kinds of piercing-through cover the same ground from two angles: emptiness (what cannot be found) and selflessness (what, precisely because of that absence, can be found instead).

5. Section: Piercing Through the Emptiness of Dharmas — The Verbal Nature Ordinary Beings Impute Cannot Be Found Anywhere

What is piercing through the emptiness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what cannot be found. Within this, what cannot be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its very impermanence — whether taken inwardly, outwardly, or in between — the verbal nature the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿法空性？謂唯由一相，不可得所顯故。此中不可得者，即於彼事所取無常性，若內、若外、若二中間，愚夫遍計所執言說自性都不可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the emptiness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what cannot be found.
Within this, what cannot be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its very impermanence — whether taken inwardly, outwardly, or in between — the verbal nature the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the first pair of piercings addressed the person, this third addresses dharmas themselves — the very aggregates, elements, and sense-fields already searched for a self. Here the target is subtler: not whether a self is found among the aggregates, but whether the aggregates' own imagined verbal nature (the fixed, substantial "what-it-is" that ordinary language projects onto every named thing) can be found once the same three-way search — inward, outward, in between — is applied to their impermanence. It cannot: what conceptual thought fixes as a stable, nameable "form" or "consciousness" is, on examination, nothing but a name laid over a stream of dependently arisen, impermanent process, never the fixed entity the name suggests. This is emptiness of dharmas approached, like the emptiness of the person, through conventional truth: what is empty is specifically the imagined nature (遍計所執) language projects, not the dependent and perfected natures underlying it.

6. Section: Piercing Through the Selflessness of Dharmas — Realized Within, Never Fully Told to Another, and Known by Six Marks Beyond All Language

What is piercing through the selflessness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found. Within this, what can be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its principle can be found — the very domain in which noble wisdom operates. And this is realized within, through one's own inner attainment, and cannot be fully conveyed to another through words. It is known, with respect to the verbal nature the foolish and unlearned imagine and cling to, through six marks that reveal a nature genuinely different from it. What are the six? First, it cannot be reached through one's own discursive thought; second, it cannot be fully shown to another; third, it surpasses the domain of the physical sense-faculties; fourth, it surpasses every characteristic; fifth, it surpasses the domain of consciousness; and sixth, it surpasses the domain of affliction.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿法無我性？謂唯由一相，可得所顯故。此中可得者，謂即於彼事道理可得，聖智所行。又即於彼自內所證，不可以言為他宣說。彼由六相，於諸凡愚遍計所執言說自性、異相可得。何等六相？一者、不可自尋思，二者、不可說示他，三者、超過色根所行，四者、超過一切相，五者、超過識所行，六者、超過煩惱所行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the selflessness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found.
Within this, what can be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its principle can be found — the very domain in which noble wisdom operates. And this is realized within, through one's own inner attainment, and cannot be fully conveyed to another through words.
It is known, with respect to the verbal nature the foolish and unlearned imagine and cling to, through six marks that reveal a nature genuinely different from it. What are the six? First, it cannot be reached through one's own discursive thought; second, it cannot be fully shown to another; third, it surpasses the domain of the physical sense-faculties; fourth, it surpasses every characteristic; fifth, it surpasses the domain of consciousness; and sixth, it surpasses the domain of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth piercing mirrors the second: where dharma-emptiness (the third) found the imagined verbal nature absent, dharma-selflessness (the fourth) finds something genuinely present instead — suchness itself, the very domain noble wisdom directly operates within, here called "the selflessness of dharmas" to distinguish it from the mere emptiness of self already covered. Cháng Bǎi carefully distinguishes this from "emptiness of dharmas" in the strict sense (per the Cheng Weishi Lun): emptiness of dharmas negates only the imagined nature falsely projected onto the dependent and perfected natures, never those two natures themselves — to deny the dependent and perfected natures too would be the very "nihilistic emptiness" the treatise later warns against under the sixth kind of piercing-through. This suchness is realized, crucially, only through direct inner attainment — a teacher cannot hand it to a student through words alone, the way water's temperature is known only by drinking it, not by being told. And because it lies utterly outside conceptual construction, it is known only negatively, through six marks stating what it is not: unreachable by one's own ordinary discursive thought, not fully expressible to another, beyond the physical senses, beyond every conceptual characteristic, beyond consciousness's ordinary operation, and beyond affliction's reach entirely.

7. Section: The Fifth Piercing Through Opens — The Extreme of Superimposition, Where Nothing There Is Wrongly Taken to Be There

What is piercing through the extreme of superimposition? It is displayed through two kinds of mark. First, displayed through the superimposition of distinguishing features; and second, displayed through the superimposition of an own-nature.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿增益邊？謂由二種相。一者、差別增益所顯故；二者、自性增益所顯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the extreme of superimposition? It is displayed through two kinds of mark. First, displayed through the superimposition of distinguishing features; and second, displayed through the superimposition of an own-nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth piercing-through addresses one of the two classic extremes a middle-way understanding must avoid: superimposition (增益), wrongly asserting something exists where, in truth, nothing does — the opposite error from the sixth piercing (denial, wrongly asserting nothing exists where something genuinely does). As the Decisive-Discussion chapter puts it, superimposition means falsely clinging to an imagined own-nature within what is actually only the dependent or perfected nature — asserting a verbal, conceptual "thisness" belongs to something that, apart from that concept, was never really there. Two kinds are distinguished: the superimposition of an own-nature (the root error — treating a merely conventional, verbally-designated nature as though it were genuinely, substantially real) and the superimposition of distinguishing features built on that same false root (the elaborations of permanence, self, and the rest that follow once a false own-nature is already assumed) — the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's image of a silkworm spinning its own cocoon and dying within it captures exactly this: beings spin false constructions out of their own conceptual thread and then become trapped by what they themselves spun.

8. Section: Five More Layers of Superimposition — From Impermanence to Self, Then to a "Real" or "Unreal" Mind, Each Built on the One Before

Second, the superimposition of impermanence. Third, resting on the superimposition of permanence, the superimposition of a self. Fourth, resting on the superimposition of impermanence, the superimposition of selflessness. Fifth, resting on the superimposition of selflessness, the superimposition of a real mind. Sixth, resting on the superimposition of a self, the superimposition of an unreal mind.

Chinese Source

二者、執無常增益。三者、執常增益為所依止，執我增益。四者、執無常增益為所依止，執無我增益。五者、執無我增益為所依止，執真實心增益。六者、執我增益為所依止，執不真實心增益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the superimposition of impermanence.
Third, resting on the superimposition of permanence, the superimposition of a self.
Fourth, resting on the superimposition of impermanence, the superimposition of selflessness.
Fifth, resting on the superimposition of selflessness, the superimposition of a real mind.
Sixth, resting on the superimposition of a self, the superimposition of an unreal mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Eight layers of superimposition unfold in a single causal chain, each resting on the one before, splitting into two parallel branches from the first fork between permanence and impermanence. Taking the aggregates as permanent (already covered) leads, on this branch, to superimposing a self — since a permanently persisting "something" naturally invites the further false step of calling it "me." Taking the aggregates as impermanent instead leads to superimposing selflessness — but even the correct insight of selflessness, wrongly reified into a "real" attaining mind, becomes its own subtle superimposition (a fault the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* attributes specifically to śrāvakas and pre-ground bodhisattvas, who may mistake the merely conventional mark of the person's emptiness for suchness itself, rather than realizing suchness directly). And superimposing a self, on the other branch, leads to superimposing an "unreal mind" — the deluded, self-clinging mind that self-view itself generates, real as an operating delusion even though what it clings to is not.

9. Section: Five Faults of Clinging to an Unreal Mind, the Final Two Layers of Superimposition, and Why a Bodhisattva Holds to Neither — Closing Differentiated Superimposition Entirely

Within the defiled dharmas to be counteracted, there are five faults: the fault of distortion, the fault of idle proliferation, the fault of giving rise to unwholesome conduct, the fault of coarseness, and the fault of impermanence. And likewise within the pure dharmas that counteract them. Seventh, resting on the superimposition of a real mind, the superimposition of wholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of purity itself. Eighth, resting on the superimposition of an unreal mind, the superimposition of unwholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of defilement itself. These are called the eight kinds of differentiated superimposition. Within these, a bodhisattva clings to none of these superimpositions, neither urging others to cling to them nor praising them.

Chinese Source

於所對治雜染法中，由五過失，謂顛倒過失、戲論過失、發起惡行過失、麤重過失、無常性過失。及於彼能對治清淨法中。七者、執真實心增益為所依止，執善等增益，乃至執清淨增益。八者、執不真實心增益為所依止，執不善等增益，乃至執雜染增益。是名八種差別增益。此中菩薩，於彼增益都不執著，不勸他執，亦不讚美。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the defiled dharmas to be counteracted, there are five faults: the fault of distortion, the fault of idle proliferation, the fault of giving rise to unwholesome conduct, the fault of coarseness, and the fault of impermanence. And likewise within the pure dharmas that counteract them.
Seventh, resting on the superimposition of a real mind, the superimposition of wholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of purity itself.
Eighth, resting on the superimposition of an unreal mind, the superimposition of unwholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of defilement itself.
These are called the eight kinds of differentiated superimposition.
Within these, a bodhisattva clings to none of these superimpositions, neither urging others to cling to them nor praising them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The unreal, self-clinging mind already superimposed carries five faults into everything it touches: distortion (mistaking the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, the impure for pure, the selfless for self); idle proliferation (empty, meaningless verbal and mental elaboration — the Ratnakūṭa's own gloss calls this the fault of "slander," since clinging to an unreal mind eventually slanders the Three Jewels themselves); giving rise to unwholesome conduct (the ten unwholesome courses of action, generated once self-view takes hold); coarseness (the affliction's own seeds deepening, further obstructing practice — a scripture elsewhere lists twenty specific harms this proliferation brings, from present suffering to rebirth in unfavorable conditions); and impermanence's own fault (compounding suffering, since clinging to permanence only makes impermanence's actual arrival hurt worse). The same five faults recur even in the "pure" dharmas meant to counteract defilement — even grasping at nirvāṇa or the noble path as a real, substantial attainment is its own subtle superimposition. The seventh and eighth layers then extend outward from the real and unreal minds already superimposed: a "real" mind superimposes wholesome dharmas, neutral dharmas, and finally purity itself as substantially existing; an "unreal" mind superimposes unwholesome dharmas, obscured-neutral dharmas, and finally defilement itself the same way. A bodhisattva, avoiding all eight layers at once, neither clings to any of them, encourages others toward them, nor praises them as though they were something to be sought. With this, differentiated superimposition — all eight layers, from permanence and impermanence through self, selflessness, a real or unreal mind, and finally purity or defilement — is fully explained.

10. Section: Superimposition of an Own-Nature — The Root Beneath All Eight Layers of Differentiated Superimposition

What is called superimposition of an own-nature? It is that, resting on differentiated superimposition, the foolish, through their own imagined and clung-to verbal own-nature, superimpose existence onto that same matter.

Chinese Source

何等名為自性增益？謂差別增益為所依止，由諸愚夫遍計所執所有言說自性增益，即於彼事增益為有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called superimposition of an own-nature? It is that, resting on differentiated superimposition, the foolish, through their own imagined and clung-to verbal own-nature, superimpose existence onto that same matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second and final kind of superimposition names the deeper root the eight preceding layers all quietly depend on: not any one of the eight specific misconceptions (permanence, self, purity, and so forth) but the more basic act of treating any verbally-designated "own-nature" at all as though it substantially existed. Cháng Bǎi notes an apparent puzzle in the text's own phrasing — differentiated superimposition is said to rest on superimposition of an own-nature, yet here own-nature superimposition is introduced as resting on differentiated superimposition — resolved by recognizing that the coarser, more obvious differentiated superimpositions are noticed first in practice, even though the subtler root, own-nature superimposition itself, is what actually makes them possible in the first place; the treatise simply follows the order of what is easier to observe before addressing what underlies it. The Bodhisattva Ground's own distinction is helpful here: "own-nature" discrimination asks what a thing fundamentally is (form as such); "differentiated" discrimination asks how that thing further divides (with form or without, past or present, and so on) — own-nature superimposition is the more basic error of the two, since without first assuming some fixed "what-it-is," none of the further differentiated superimpositions could even get started.

11. Section: Piercing Through the Extreme of Denial — Nihilistic Emptiness, Where Even the Dependent and Perfected Natures Are Wrongly Denied

What is piercing through the extreme of denial? It is displayed through a single mark: the denial of what is genuinely real. Within this, denial of what is genuinely real is: forming resolute understanding based on a false view of the selflessness of dharmas, and clinging to the belief that every kind and every characteristic of dharmas whatsoever does not exist at all.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿損減邊？謂由一相，損減實事所顯故。此中損減實事者，謂即於彼邪法無我性起於勝解，執著一切種、一切法相都無所有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the extreme of denial? It is displayed through a single mark: the denial of what is genuinely real.
Within this, denial of what is genuinely real is: forming resolute understanding based on a false view of the selflessness of dharmas, and clinging to the belief that every kind and every characteristic of dharmas whatsoever does not exist at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The sixth piercing-through names the mirror-image error of the fifth: where superimposition wrongly asserts something exists that does not, denial wrongly asserts that nothing exists at all — even the dependent and perfected natures, which genuinely are real apart from what is merely imagined. The Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on true meaning describes exactly this failure mode: someone hears a profound Great Vehicle teaching on emptiness, whose hidden intent is not yet fully grasped, and concludes wrongly that "everything is merely conventional, and this alone is the truth" — a conclusion the treatise calls slandering the bodhisattva canon itself, since it denies both the genuinely real and, in effect, the merely false along with it. This "nihilistic emptiness" (惡取空) breeds heedlessness and abandons diligent practice, since if literally nothing is real, there is nothing left worth striving for either — exactly the trap a bodhisattva must avoid on this side of the middle way, just as superimposition must be avoided on the other.

12. Section: Piercing Through Direct Realization of Dharmas Opens — Three Marks, and How the Imagined Own-Nature Itself Cannot Be Found

What is piercing through direct realization of dharmas? It is displayed through three kinds of mark. First, displayed through the fact that, within that same matter and the fourth matter of arising, and within what is to be counteracted, what counteracts it, and the conditioned and unconditioned as they are conventionally established, no own-nature can be found; second, displayed through the fact that no distinguishing feature of these can be found; and third, displayed through the wisdom that, through sustained familiarity with this very realization, comes to understand it exactly as it is. Within this, what is meant by no own-nature being found is: the own-nature that the foolish imagine and cling to.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿法現觀？謂由三種相。一者、即於彼事及第四生事，所治、能治、有為、無為安立中，自性不可得所顯故；二者、彼差別不可得所顯故；三者、即彼串習故，如實通達智所顯故。此中自性不可得者，謂諸愚夫遍計所執自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh piercing-through moves from avoiding the two extremes to actually realizing dharmas directly. It adds a "fourth matter" to the three already searched for a self — the twelve links of dependent origination (ignorance conditioning formations, all the way to birth conditioning aging and death), where what is to be counteracted (affliction, karma, and suffering) and what counteracts it (the wisdom of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, or the three gates of liberation) are both examined the same way: their own conventionally-designated nature simply cannot be found, whether one is searching among conditioned or unconditioned dharmas. This matters because even the "medicine" of counteracting wisdom can itself become a new sickness if clung to as though it were a real, substantial thing — the antidote to affliction is not meant to become a fresh object of grasping in its own right, which is exactly why its own imagined nature, too, must be seen as unfindable.

13. Section: No Distinguishing Feature to Be Found, and Wisdom That Needs No More Deliberate Effort — Closing Direct Realization of Dharmas Entirely

Within this, what is meant by no distinguishing feature being found is: the very cessation, arising, accumulation, and accomplishment of that same own-nature, taken in their two divisions, cannot be found. Within this, understanding through wisdom is: understanding, through wisdom directly realized within oneself, of that same characteristic of own-nature, without further deliberate attention or discursive effort.

Chinese Source

此中差別不可得者，謂即彼自性滅、生、集、成二分不可得。此中智通達者，謂即彼自性相不作意、不思擇加行自內所證智通達。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, what is meant by no distinguishing feature being found is: the very cessation, arising, accumulation, and accomplishment of that same own-nature, taken in their two divisions, cannot be found.
Within this, understanding through wisdom is: understanding, through wisdom directly realized within oneself, of that same characteristic of own-nature, without further deliberate attention or discursive effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second mark shows that even the paired categories used to describe the path — cessation of what is counteracted and arising of what counteracts it, the accumulation of conditioned dharmas and the accomplishment of unconditioned ones — are themselves only relative, mutually-established labels, never realities with their own fixed nature; affliction, karma, and suffering to be counteracted, and the wisdom that counteracts them, are spoken of only provisionally, for the sake of guiding practitioners toward what ultimately cannot be put into words at all. The third mark then describes how this entire three-stage process (first, no own-nature to be found; second, no distinguishing feature to be found) matures through sustained familiarity into direct realization — a wisdom that no longer needs deliberate discursive effort to see the point, having become the practitioner's own directly-realized understanding. With this, the seventh piercing through — direct realization of dharmas, its three marks of no own-nature, no distinguishing feature, and wisdom made direct through sustained practice — is fully explained.

14. Section: Dedicating Realization Toward Great Awakening — Why a Bodhisattva, Able to End Rebirth, Deliberately Does Not

What is piercing through the dedication of direct realization of dharmas toward great awakening? It is displayed through a single mark: that, having attained what counteracts it through discursive reflection, what is to be counteracted is not brought to an end. Within this, what counteracts is emptiness as the counteragent of affliction, wishlessness as the counteragent of aspiration for existence, and signlessness as the counteragent of every characteristic — all of this is called freedom from fabrication. This is further the counteragent of karma leading to future existence, and the counteragent of the transmigration of the reborn body and of momentary transmigration alike — called the practice that extinguishes and leads to nirvāṇa, without own-nature. This, further, takes the transmigration of birth-and-death as what is to be counteracted. If bodhisattvas, possessing this counteragent, deliberately reflect and do not bring what is to be counteracted to an end, it is because, out of compassion for sentient beings, they seek great awakening.

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth piercing-through addresses a genuine puzzle: having realized the three gates of liberation — emptiness (countering affliction), wishlessness (countering craving for future rebirth), and signlessness (countering every conceptual mark) — a bodhisattva is fully capable of ending saṃsāra for good, yet deliberately does not. Each gate counters a different layer of transmigration at once: emptiness counters karma that would generate future existence, wishlessness counters the reborn body's own continued transmigration, and signlessness counters even the moment-to-moment transmigration of arising and passing thought. All three together are called "freedom from fabrication" precisely because they see through the imagined own-nature that would otherwise drive continued rebirth. And yet a bodhisattva, holding this very capacity to end rebirth, deliberately chooses not to exercise it — not from confusion, but out of compassion for beings still caught in saṃsāra, choosing to remain within birth-and-death for their sake while dedicating the merit of this very realization toward the far greater goal of complete awakening, rather than personal escape alone. Master Yìn Shùn notes both the Madhyamaka and Yogācāra schools converge exactly here: whatever their differences elsewhere, both traditions equally exhort bodhisattvas to return to saṃsāra rather than exit it prematurely.

15. Section: Piercing Through So That Affliction and Suffering No Longer Entangle the Mind — Why a Bodhisattva Can Remain in Saṃsāra Without Being Overwhelmed

What is piercing through so that, for one who practices thus, affliction and manifold suffering no longer entangle the mind? It is displayed through a single mark: that, although what is to be counteracted is not permanently ended, one is nonetheless able to understand it exactly as it is. Within this, understanding exactly as it is means: with respect to that same dharma, through the effortful application of the selflessness of dharmas, observing that its own-nature is without defilement and without suffering.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿如是行者煩惱眾苦不纏繞心？謂由一種相，雖不永斷所對治法，而能如實通達故。此中如實通達者，謂即於彼法，由法無我加行，觀彼自性無染無苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through so that, for one who practices thus, affliction and manifold suffering no longer entangle the mind? It is displayed through a single mark: that, although what is to be counteracted is not permanently ended, one is nonetheless able to understand it exactly as it is.
Within this, understanding exactly as it is means: with respect to that same dharma, through the effortful application of the selflessness of dharmas, observing that its own-nature is without defilement and without suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The ninth piercing-through explains why the eighth's deliberate retention of affliction does not simply overwhelm a bodhisattva with the very suffering they chose not to fully end. The key is that affliction and suffering, examined through the wisdom of the selflessness of dharmas, are themselves found to have no substantial own-nature — no real defilement, no real suffering to be found once their imagined verbal nature is seen through. An ordinary being caught in saṃsāra suffers acutely because affliction and pain are taken as solidly real; a bodhisattva, whose compassion remains undiminished precisely because this same wisdom keeps affliction from taking genuine hold, can remain within birth-and-death indefinitely without being crushed by it — the strength of realized emptiness is exactly what keeps compassion from ever burning out.

16. Section: Piercing Through Distinction Opens — Four Ways Even a Genuine Insight Into Selflessness Can Go Astray

What is piercing through distinction? It is displayed through four kinds of mark. First, displayed through a distinction of view; second, displayed through this same distinction taken to the far extreme of denial; third, displayed through a distinction of confusion regarding cessation; and fourth, displayed through a distinction of confusion regarding the mind.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿差別？謂由四種相。一者、見差別所顯故；二者、即此極遠損減差別所顯故；三者、於斷迷失差別所顯故；四者、於心迷失差別所顯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through distinction? It is displayed through four kinds of mark. First, displayed through a distinction of view; second, displayed through this same distinction taken to the far extreme of denial; third, displayed through a distinction of confusion regarding cessation; and fourth, displayed through a distinction of confusion regarding the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The tenth piercing-through distinguishes the two kinds of selflessness (of person and of dharmas) from four specific ways even a sincere practitioner can misapprehend them. A distinction of view names the conceit of mistaking mere suppression for genuine purification, or clinging to dharmas as substantially real and thereby missing dharma-emptiness. This same error, pushed further, becomes the far extreme of denial — the nihilistic emptiness already covered under the sixth piercing-through, now revisited as one of the four possible ways distinction itself can go astray. A distinction of confusion regarding cessation names the fear that arises upon hearing that all dharmas lack an own-nature, mistaking this teaching for a call to mere annihilation and, out of that fear, abandoning the willingness to return to saṃsāra for beings' sake. And a distinction of confusion regarding the mind names how this same fear compounds: a distorted perception gives rise to a distorted view, which in turn gives rise to distorted thinking, the practitioner's mind becoming lost in exactly the three-fold distortion the Heart Sūtra's own closing lines describe overcoming — freedom from inverted dream-thinking, ultimate nirvāṇa.

17. Section: The Conceit of Premature Attainment, and the Subtle Clinging Even a Genuine Practitioner of Emptiness Can Still Fall Into

Within this, a distinction of view is: abiding in the selflessness of the person and in nirvāṇa, giving rise to the conceit of a future extinction of the body. And further, it is: having examined what is grasped, yet, with respect to that which grasps, not examining that its verbal own-nature is ultimately to be transcended, comprised within emptiness — this is called failing to skillfully examine the object of one's own knowledge. Because of clinging to dharmas in this way, one seeks only to end affliction. Bodhisattvas, however, are not like this.

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Two faces of the first distinction, both drawn from śrāvaka practice specifically. The first is a subtle conceit: a practitioner who has genuinely realized the selflessness of the person, not yet at the path of seeing, mistakes what is still only a suppression of affliction for its actual purification, generating pride over an attainment ("the body will no longer arise") not yet genuinely secured. The second is more structural: such a practitioner correctly examines the grasped object (the five aggregates) as without self, yet never turns the same scrutiny on the grasping mind that observes them — the very wisdom of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness is itself left unexamined, still treated as a real, substantial thing rather than seen as equally empty. Clinging to dharmas in this residual way, such a practitioner seeks only personal liberation from affliction, unable to bear returning to saṃsāra for others' sake. A bodhisattva, by contrast, examines both the grasped and the grasping together from the outset, missing neither self-emptiness nor dharma-emptiness.

18. Section: Worse Than a Mountain of Self-View — Nihilistic Denial, Fear of Extinction, and the Mind Lost in Threefold Distortion — Closing Piercing Through Distinction Entirely

Within this, the distinction taken to the far extreme of denial is: abiding in the selflessness of the person, yet, among the lower and even lower classes of ordinary beings who hold a view of self, being lower still — through two causes and conditions, namely, not being liberated from suffering, and abiding in suffering, one's earlier and later graspings both being ruined. Bodhisattvas, however, are not like this. Within this, the distinction of confusion regarding cessation is: abiding in the selflessness of the person, and, clinging to the selflessness of dharmas as lacking any own-nature at all, thereby giving rise to terror, taking the absence of verbal own-nature to mean pursuing utter extinction. Bodhisattvas, however, are not like this. Within this, the distinction of confusion regarding the mind is: in just this way, being confused regarding cessation while abiding in the selflessness of the person, and, amid the very objects one's own conceptual construction has given rise to, being overturned by distorted perception and the rest. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second mark names the treatise's own severe verdict on nihilistic emptiness: someone who has genuinely realized the selflessness of the person, yet takes this teaching to mean nothing whatsoever exists, ranks even lower than an ordinary being who still clings to a self — the Buddha's own words, cited here, say he would rather someone hold self-view as vast as Mount Sumeru than hold a view of emptiness as small as a mustard seed, because self-view can still be corrected through emptiness, while a view that denies emptiness itself leaves no remaining foothold for correction at all. Such a person suffers doubly: unliberated from suffering (having abandoned the very practices that would counteract it, on the mistaken belief nothing needs counteracting) and abiding in suffering (having ruined both their own practice and, by leading others astray, the practice of any followers too). The third mark names sheer terror at hearing that dharmas lack any own-nature — a fear the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* likens to a feverish patient so identified with the fever that recovery itself feels like annihilation — driving a practitioner to flee prematurely into a one-sided nirvāṇa rather than remain to help others. And the fourth names how that same terror compounds into full derangement: a distorted perception breeding a distorted view, and a distorted view hardening into a genuinely distorted state of mind, exactly the "not-sunk," "secure," and "tranquil" notions already identified back in fascicle 79 as the śrāvaka's own characteristic (and, from a bodhisattva's vantage, inverted) way of relating to the dharma-realm. With this, the tenth piercing through — distinction's four ways even a genuine insight into selflessness can go astray, none of which touch a bodhisattva — is fully explained.

19. Section: Piercing Through Causality — Even the Grasping Wisdom Itself Must Finally Be Released

Within this, observing what grasps means: observing that this very wisdom of selflessness is itself free of verbal own-nature, free of the conceptual construction attached to it, to be released as a characteristic, and momentary. Within this, understanding it exactly as it is means: through observing both what is grasped and what grasps, taking correct reflective attention as the cause, a decisive wisdom, individually realized within, arises.

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eleventh piercing-through closes a loop the earlier piercings left open: having realized that the grasped object (the five aggregates, dharmas generally) is empty, a subtle danger remains — treating the very wisdom that realized this emptiness as itself something real and permanent, a fixed possession rather than a momentary, dependently-arisen event like everything else. This mark corrects that: the wisdom of selflessness is itself observed to be free of verbal own-nature, free of the conceptual overlay that would fix it as a "thing," and merely momentary — arising and passing like any other conditioned dharma, even the Tathāgata's own stainless consciousness. Once both what is grasped and what grasps are seen through together, and this releases both, "correct reflective attention" as the cause finally yields the decisive wisdom directly and individually realized within each practitioner — exactly the teaching of the Verses Distinguishing the Middle from the Extremes: "herein there is only emptiness; within that, this too [is empty]" — first the object is seen as empty, then the very seeing.

20. Section: Emptiness Reaching Its Outermost Limit, and the Majestic Power That Burns Away a Thousand Years of Darkness in a Single Moment — All Thirteen Kinds of Middle-Way Conduct Complete

What is piercing through the majestic power of that very emptiness? It is displayed through a single mark: that karma and affliction are cut off through this very counteragent. Within this, cutting off means: that, the very instant the notion of radiance arises, it is able to cut off every karma and affliction accumulated since beginningless time.

Within this, manifesting exactly as it is means: manifesting the resembling mark of karma and affliction, yet as an inexpressible dharma, free of verbal own-nature, and, being thus, without attachment, and momentary.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The final two piercings bring the whole sequence to its climax. Emptiness reaching its outermost limit describes a bodhisattva's wisdom so thoroughly realized that it now operates spontaneously within saṃsāra itself — appearing, to outward view, to undergo karma and affliction just as an ordinary being does, yet without ever actually being caught by them, since the whole display is understood as inexpressible, free of any fixed own-nature, and momentary; saṃsāra and nirvāṇa here are neither the same nor different, a bodhisattva living within both without attachment to either. The majestic power this confers, closing the set, is compared to a single lamp lit in a room that has been dark for a thousand years: the darkness needs no laborious removal — light itself, the moment it appears, simply leaves no room for it, without the light having to "think" about dispelling anything. In just this way, one moment of the "notion of radiance" — genuine realization of selflessness — burns away karma and affliction accumulated since beginningless time, not through effortful battle but simply because realized wisdom and unreality cannot coexist any more than a lit room can remain dark. With this, all thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct — the emptiness and selflessness of the person and of dharmas, the extremes of superimposition and denial, direct realization of dharmas and its dedication toward great awakening, freedom from affliction's grip, the four ways distinction goes astray, causality's final release, emptiness at its limit, and its majestic power — are now complete in full, closing the sixth gateway, establishing what is to be learned, entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With the sixth gateway's thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct now fully explained — by far the longest and most technical of the sixteen gateways in the chapter on conduct — the treatise moves to the seventh: a direct comparison between what śrāvakas study and what bodhisattvas study. The question opens by first classifying śrāvakas into four kinds (traditionally: those newly entered into the vehicle, those bent solely on quiescence, those who have turned back from that quiescence toward the Great Vehicle, and those whose entire course is naturally suited to the Great Vehicle from the outset), before turning to name thirteen specific differences between the two paths of study — a far shorter gateway than the sixth, taken up in the sections that follow.

22. Section: Four Kinds of Śrāvaka — the Transformation-Body Śrāvaka and the Śrāvaka of Overweening Conceit

What are called the four kinds of śrāvaka? First, the transformation-body śrāvaka; second, the śrāvaka of overweening conceit; third, the śrāvaka turned toward awakening; and fourth, the śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence. The transformation-body śrāvaka is: because of wishing to convert and liberate the sentient beings to be converted by that means, either bodhisattvas or Tathāgatas transform themselves and appear as śrāvakas. The śrāvaka of overweening conceit is: one who, merely through the wisdom of the selflessness of the person, together with clinging to a mistaken wisdom of the selflessness of dharmas, reckons this to be purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh gateway now unfolds in detail. Vasubandhu's commentary on the Lotus Sūtra names a closely parallel fourfold division — transformation śrāvakas, śrāvakas of overweening conceit, śrāvakas who retreat from the bodhi-mind, and fixed-lineage śrāvakas — three of which match here directly; the third differs subtly, since "retreating from the bodhi-mind" names someone who once held it and lost it, while "turned toward awakening" below names an indeterminate-lineage śrāvaka who eventually turns small toward great. The transformation-body śrāvaka is not an ordinary practitioner at all: it is a buddha or bodhisattva who, inwardly holding the true bodhisattva practice concealed, outwardly manifests the form of a śrāvaka in order to draw the two indeterminate-lineage types above toward the Great Vehicle and to teach beings broadly. The śrāvaka of overweening conceit may be an ordinary being who has not yet attained any fruit yet believes he has, or — per Vasubandhu and the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra* — even a learner-stage noble one who, having gained only the wisdom of personal selflessness, clings to a mistaken understanding of the selflessness of dharmas and reckons it pure; only the fully liberated arhat, having perfected personal selflessness in full, is free of this conceit. Master Yuance's commentary on the *Sūtra Explaining the Profound Secrets* records three positions on how broadly this conceit applies — to ordinary beings alone, to ordinary beings and learner-level nobles together, or even to arhats themselves (since though free of afflictive conceit, a subtler conceit rooted in the cognitive obstruction can remain) — reconciling the apparent tension with the Lotus Sūtra's own statement that a true arhat could not fail to believe its teaching, by distinguishing conceit rooted in affliction from conceit rooted in what remains unknown. Such a practitioner, lacking the wisdom of dharma-emptiness, clings to dharmas as truly existent, regards the five aggregates as sheer suffering, and seeks only to end affliction and enter nirvāṇa as swiftly as possible — mistaking a partial, one-sided realization for the genuine article.

23. Section: The Śrāvaka Turned Toward Awakening — Renewed Cultivation, Awakening Through the Buddhas, and a Sluggish Diligence

The śrāvaka turned toward awakening is: one who, originally of an extremely weak compassionate lineage, through drawing near to and abiding with the Tathāgata, gives rise, within the vast Buddha-dharma, to the notion of great merit, and cultivates it continuously. Though such a one has arrived at the ultimate abiding in the uncontaminated realm, yet, met with the buddhas' awakening, guidance, and skillful opening, by this cause is then able to set forth toward vast awakening. Though able to set forth toward such vast awakening, because of a fondness for quiescence, this one's diligence toward it is exceedingly sluggish, not like one who, from the very beginning of arousing the resolve, already possesses the buddha-lineage.

Chinese Source

迴向菩提聲聞者，謂從本來是極微劣慈悲種姓，由親近如來住故，於廣大佛法中，起大功德想，熏修相續。雖到究竟住無漏界，而蒙諸佛覺悟引入方便開導，由此因故，便能發趣廣大菩提。彼於如是廣大菩提雖能發趣，由樂寂故，於此加行極成遲鈍，不如初始發心有佛種性者。

Though such a one has arrived at the ultimate abiding in the uncontaminated realm, yet, met with the buddhas' awakening, guidance, and skillful opening, by this cause is then able to set forth toward vast awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This third kind of śrāvaka unfolds in three stages. First, renewed cultivation: originally of a lineage too weak in compassion to have any interest in liberating others, this practitioner, simply through proximity to a living Buddha and exposure to the Great Vehicle's vast teaching, comes to admire it and begins cultivating it alongside the śrāvaka path — differing from Vasubandhu's "retreating" śrāvaka, who once held and then lost the bodhi-mind, since this one never held it to begin with. Second, awakening: even after such a person has reached the ultimate uncontaminated fruit — arhatship itself — the buddhas, honoring the Lotus Sūtra's teaching that there is finally only one vehicle and the other two are skillful expedients, do not leave the matter there, but awaken and guide even the arhat toward unsurpassed awakening. Third, diligence: the habituation of favoring quietude built up over a whole path of small-vehicle practice does not vanish merely because the resolve for great awakening has newly arisen; such a person moves toward benefiting beings only sluggishly, still preferring solitude to the crowded, effortful work bodhisattvas take on willingly — a marked contrast with one who possessed the buddha-lineage and its ease with beings from the very first arousal of the resolve.

24. Section: The Śrāvaka Bent Solely on Quiescence — the Two Princes, Alike in the King's Happiness Yet Unlike in Statecraft — Closing the Four Kinds of Śrāvaka Entirely

The śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence is: one who, from the very beginning, is of the most extremely weak compassionate lineage, who thereby turns wholly away from and abandons the matter of benefiting sentient beings, who, because of an intense dread of the suffering of birth-and-death, has only the resolve to abide in nirvāṇa, and who, in the end, is utterly unable to set forth toward great awakening. It is as with two princes born in a mutually resembling place, receiving, equally and evenly, the king's happiness — one of whom is thoroughly skilled in all matters of royal governance and its arts, while the second prince is not so — the two differing only by this portion, not by their enjoyment of the king's happiness. Just so, within the uncontaminated realm, know that there is a distinction between the multitude of bodhisattvas and the śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence.

Chinese Source

一向趣寂聲聞者，謂從本來是最極微劣慈悲種性故，一向棄背利益眾生事故，於生死苦極怖畏故，唯有安住涅槃意樂，畢竟不能趣大菩提。如二王子相似處生，平等平等受王快樂，一於王政討論工巧處等皆悉善知，第二王子則不如是。彼二但由此分差別，非由受用王之快樂。如是於無漏界中，諸菩薩眾與一向趣寂聲聞，當知差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence is: one who, from the very beginning, is of the most extremely weak compassionate lineage, who thereby turns wholly away from and abandons the matter of benefiting sentient beings, who, because of an intense dread of the suffering of birth-and-death, has only the resolve to abide in nirvāṇa, and who, in the end, is utterly unable to set forth toward great awakening.
It is as with two princes born in a mutually resembling place, receiving, equally and evenly, the king's happiness — one of whom is thoroughly skilled in all matters of royal governance and its arts, while the second prince is not so — the two differing only by this portion, not by their enjoyment of the king's happiness.
Just so, within the uncontaminated realm, know that there is a distinction between the multitude of bodhisattvas and the śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This fourth and final kind of śrāvaka is the "fixed lineage" of Vasubandhu's fourfold scheme — a practitioner who, on hearing the four noble truths, enters directly into a small, residue-free extinction and is ultimately unable to turn back toward the Great Vehicle at all. The parable of the two princes then clarifies exactly where the bodhisattva's superiority to such a śrāvaka lies, and where it does not. Both princes are born into the same palace and receive, in equal measure, "the king's happiness" — a stand-in for nirvāṇa, the liberation itself, which the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* by Masters Jing and Tai glosses as the palace, the Buddha as king, the three-basket teaching as statecraft, and beneficial activity for others as the princely arts. Only one prince has also mastered governance and the arts of benefiting the realm; the second enjoys the same royal comfort but nothing more. So too here: on the score of liberation from affliction itself, the emptiness a bodhisattva realizes and the emptiness a fixed-lineage śrāvaka realizes are genuinely equal — one cannot be graded superior to the other in this respect alone. What distinguishes them is entirely the follow-on wisdom devoted to benefiting others, which the śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence never develops at all. Because this passage lists no analogous fifth category for the śrāvaka of overweening conceit, the commentary notes this confirms that figure was an ordinary being all along, outside the fourfold division of realized śrāvakas being compared here to bodhisattvas. With this, the explanation of the four kinds of śrāvaka is complete, closing the first half of the seventh gateway entirely; the treatise turns next to the thirteen differences between what a śrāvaka learns and what a bodhisattva learns.
