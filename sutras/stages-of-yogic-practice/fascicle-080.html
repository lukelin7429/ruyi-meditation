Fascicle 80 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Establishing What a Bodhisattva Learns Through the Middle Way

This is the ninth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, continuing directly from fascicle 79's unfinished chapter on conduct — the sixteen gateways of teaching found within the bodhisattva canon, of which the first five (wrong conduct, right conduct, its excellent benefits, its thirty-two marks, and nineteen similes for pure faith) are already complete. This fascicle opens with the sixth gateway: how a bodhisattva establishes what is to be learned by holding the middle way between two kinds of scripture — provisional teachings addressed to the śrāvaka vehicle, resting on conventional truth, and definitive teachings addressed to the Great Vehicle, resting on both conventional and ultimate truth together. Neither is dismissed; both are studied, so that a bodhisattva's body, speech, and mind stay anchored in right view rather than drifting to one extreme or the other. The remaining ten gateways continue afterward, carrying the chapter on conduct toward its close and opening the treatise's Determination of the Grounds With and Without Residue.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 80 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Sixth Gateway Opens — Studying Both Provisional and Definitive Scripture Without Favoring Either

Furthermore, how does a bodhisattva establish what is to be learned within right conduct? It is that bodhisattvas, possessing Dharma-abiding conduct in full, having already relied upon and grown skillful in those non-definitive scriptures associated with the śrāvaka vehicle that are taught according to the principle of conventional truth and are not what a bodhisattva ultimately depends on, and who further go beyond these, diligently studying those definitive scriptures that may be relied upon, associated with the Great Vehicle and with profound emptiness, taught according to the principle of both conventional and ultimate truth — this is called studying diligently and correctly, according to reason.

Chinese Source

復次，云何菩薩於正行中安立所學？謂諸菩薩具足法住，於依世俗諦道理所說不了義非所依聲聞乘相應經典，已作依持、已作善巧，而復超度於大乘相應、甚深空性相應，依世俗、勝義諦道理所說了義可依經典勤修學時，名為如理正勤修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, how does a bodhisattva establish what is to be learned within right conduct?
It is that bodhisattvas, possessing Dharma-abiding conduct in full, having already relied upon and grown skillful in those non-definitive scriptures associated with the śrāvaka vehicle that are taught according to the principle of conventional truth and are not what a bodhisattva ultimately depends on, and who further go beyond these, diligently studying those definitive scriptures that may be relied upon, associated with the Great Vehicle and with profound emptiness, taught according to the principle of both conventional and ultimate truth — this is called studying diligently and correctly, according to reason.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Fascicle 80 opens exactly where fascicle 79 left off, continuing the chapter on conduct's sixteen gateways with the sixth: establishing what a bodhisattva actually studies. The Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on conduct (juan 49) names four broad areas of bodhisattva practice — the ten pāramitās, the thirty-seven factors of awakening, the six kinds of supernormal power, and the maturing of sentient beings — but only in outline; the fuller account of how these are actually studied and practiced runs from the end of fascicle 79 through this fascicle, organized as sixteen gateways of teaching drawn from across the entire canon. This sixth gateway addresses a genuine risk: without careful anchoring in right view, even sincere practice can drift to one extreme or another. The remedy is studying both kinds of scripture in their proper place — first relying on and growing skilled in the śrāvaka vehicle's own teaching (not a bodhisattva's final resting place, since it addresses only the emptiness of self, not of dharmas, but still genuinely useful and, in its own domain, correctly called "ultimate" when it leads to direct realization of selflessness), and then going beyond it to the Great Vehicle's own definitive teaching on profound emptiness, taught through both conventional and ultimate truth together. Neither is bypassed; the middle way here means passing properly through both rather than skipping either one.

2. Section: Thirteen Kinds of Middle-Way Conduct — The Complete List

Practicing diligently and correctly, according to reason, in this way, is called rightly practicing the excellent conduct of the middle way. Why is this? Because this true Dharma pierces through thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct. First, piercing through the emptiness of the person; second, piercing through the selflessness of the person; third, piercing through the emptiness of dharmas; fourth, piercing through the selflessness of dharmas; fifth, piercing through the extreme of superimposition; sixth, piercing through the extreme of denial; seventh, piercing through direct realization of dharmas; eighth, piercing through the dedication of that direct realization toward great awakening; ninth, piercing through the nature by which, for one practicing thus, affliction and manifold suffering no longer entangle the mind; tenth, piercing through the distinction between resolute understanding of the two kinds of selflessness; eleventh, piercing through how the prior selflessness serves as cause for the latter; twelfth, piercing through emptiness reaching its outermost limit; and thirteenth, piercing through the majestic power that this very realization brings.

Chinese Source

如是如理勤修學時，名正修行中道勝行。所以者何？由此正法貫穿十三中道行故。一者、貫穿補特伽羅空性；二者、貫穿補特伽羅無我性；三者、貫穿法空性；四者、貫穿法無我性；五者、貫穿增益邊；六者、貫穿損減邊；七者、貫穿法現觀；八者、貫穿法現觀迴向大菩提性；九者、貫穿如是行者煩惱眾苦不纏繞心性；十者、貫穿二無我勝解差別；十一者、貫穿前無我性是後因性；十二者、貫穿到邊際空性；十三者、貫穿即彼威德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Practicing diligently and correctly, according to reason, in this way, is called rightly practicing the excellent conduct of the middle way.
Why is this? Because this true Dharma pierces through thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct.
First, piercing through the emptiness of the person; second, piercing through the selflessness of the person; third, piercing through the emptiness of dharmas; fourth, piercing through the selflessness of dharmas; fifth, piercing through the extreme of superimposition; sixth, piercing through the extreme of denial; seventh, piercing through direct realization of dharmas; eighth, piercing through the dedication of that direct realization toward great awakening; ninth, piercing through the nature by which, for one practicing thus, affliction and manifold suffering no longer entangle the mind; tenth, piercing through the distinction between resolute understanding of the two kinds of selflessness; eleventh, piercing through how the prior selflessness serves as cause for the latter; twelfth, piercing through emptiness reaching its outermost limit; and thirteenth, piercing through the majestic power that this very realization brings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Piercing through" (貫穿) names a thread running continuously through and holding together thirteen aspects of realization, each avoiding one of two extremes. Master Cháng Bǎi's own brief preview of the thirteen, elaborated in the sections that follow: the emptiness of the person and dharmas are both approached through conventional truth (the merely verbal, conceptual self and dharmas ordinary beings impute are simply not found); the selflessness of the person and dharmas are both approached through ultimate truth (the suchness this absence itself reveals is genuinely realized). The extreme of superimposition names eight escalating layers of false construction, from taking permanence as real down to the pure and impure dharmas built upon a falsely substantialized mind; the extreme of denial names the opposite error of "nihilistic emptiness" (惡取空), wrongly concluding that because the imagined nature is unreal, the dependent and perfected natures must be unreal too. Direct realization of dharmas names the threefold process by which the imagined self-nature, its distinguishing features, and finally the practiced familiarity with both are correctly seen through; its dedication toward great awakening names cultivating this very realization out of compassion rather than for one's own escape alone. The ninth names freedom from affliction once emptiness is seen; the tenth distinguishes how self-emptiness and dharma-emptiness are each misapprehended in their own characteristic way; the eleventh explains how the earlier realization causes the later; the twelfth names emptiness carried to its absolute limit; and the thirteenth names the majestic power — likened, in the section ahead, to a single moment of light dispelling a room dark for a thousand years — that this whole realization confers.

3. Section: Piercing Through the Emptiness of the Person — No Real Self to Be Found in Three Kinds of Matter

What is piercing through the emptiness of the person? It is displayed through a single mark: what cannot be found. Within this, what cannot be found is with respect to three kinds of matter: first, the matter of a sentient being; second, the matter of its distinguishing features; and third, the matter of its enjoyment — whether inward, outward, or in between, the real self that the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿補特伽羅空性？謂由一種相，不可得所顯故。此中不可得者，謂於三種事。一者、有情事；二者、彼差別事；三者、彼受用事，若內、若外、若二中間，愚夫遍計所執實我都不可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the emptiness of the person? It is displayed through a single mark: what cannot be found.
Within this, what cannot be found is with respect to three kinds of matter: first, the matter of a sentient being; second, the matter of its distinguishing features; and third, the matter of its enjoyment — whether inward, outward, or in between, the real self that the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of the thirteen begins the sequence with the emptiness of the person — a conventional-truth realization that a substantial, imagined self simply cannot be located anywhere it is sought. Three kinds of matter are searched in turn: the matter of a being itself (the five aggregates of clinging — form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness — where the sense of "I" first arises); the matter of a being's distinguishing features (the eighteen elements, whose countless combinations produce the boundless variety among the sixty-two kinds of beings the treatise elsewhere catalogues); and the matter of enjoyment (the six inner faculties and six outer objects, and the six consciousnesses arising between them, through which pleasure and pain are actually experienced). Searched inwardly, outwardly, or in the meeting-point between faculty and object, no substantial self ever turns up — the way a car, taken apart into chassis, glass, engine, and seats, reveals no separate thing called "car" beyond its assembled parts. This is the classic argument for selflessness "apart from the aggregates" (離蘊無我): there is no self hiding somewhere among the five aggregates once every conventional label is stripped away.

4. Section: Piercing Through the Selflessness of the Person — What Actually Can Be Found Once the Imagined Self Is Gone

What is piercing through the selflessness of the person? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found. Within this, what can be found is that, within those same three kinds of matter, among the dependently arisen dharmas that the foolish conceive, no permanently abiding real nature can be found — and it is precisely this, the principle of a nature genuinely different from what the foolish conceive, that can be found.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿補特伽羅無我性？謂由唯一相，可得所顯故。此中可得者，謂即於彼三事，愚夫所遍計緣生諸法中，常住實性不可得故，愚夫所計我異相性道理可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the selflessness of the person? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found.
Within this, what can be found is that, within those same three kinds of matter, among the dependently arisen dharmas that the foolish conceive, no permanently abiding real nature can be found — and it is precisely this, the principle of a nature genuinely different from what the foolish conceive, that can be found.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the first kind of piercing-through establishes that no self can be found (an ultimate-truth realization stated in conventional terms), the second flips the emphasis: something genuinely can be found — not a self, but the very absence of one, realized as suchness itself. The distinction, as the Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi puts it concisely: "no self within the aggregates" is called emptiness; "the aggregates themselves are not a self" is called selflessness. The first stresses that a self is not to be found apart from or within the aggregates (離蘊無我); the second stresses that the aggregates, being merely dependently arisen and impermanent, were never in themselves a self to begin with (即蘊無我) — and this negative fact itself constitutes something real and directly realizable, the suchness a noble one's wisdom actually apprehends. Together the first two kinds of piercing-through cover the same ground from two angles: emptiness (what cannot be found) and selflessness (what, precisely because of that absence, can be found instead).

5. Section: Piercing Through the Emptiness of Dharmas — The Verbal Nature Ordinary Beings Impute Cannot Be Found Anywhere

What is piercing through the emptiness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what cannot be found. Within this, what cannot be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its very impermanence — whether taken inwardly, outwardly, or in between — the verbal nature the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿法空性？謂唯由一相，不可得所顯故。此中不可得者，即於彼事所取無常性，若內、若外、若二中間，愚夫遍計所執言說自性都不可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the emptiness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what cannot be found.
Within this, what cannot be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its very impermanence — whether taken inwardly, outwardly, or in between — the verbal nature the foolish imagine and cling to cannot, in any of these, be found at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the first pair of piercings addressed the person, this third addresses dharmas themselves — the very aggregates, elements, and sense-fields already searched for a self. Here the target is subtler: not whether a self is found among the aggregates, but whether the aggregates' own imagined verbal nature (the fixed, substantial "what-it-is" that ordinary language projects onto every named thing) can be found once the same three-way search — inward, outward, in between — is applied to their impermanence. It cannot: what conceptual thought fixes as a stable, nameable "form" or "consciousness" is, on examination, nothing but a name laid over a stream of dependently arisen, impermanent process, never the fixed entity the name suggests. This is emptiness of dharmas approached, like the emptiness of the person, through conventional truth: what is empty is specifically the imagined nature (遍計所執) language projects, not the dependent and perfected natures underlying it.

6. Section: Piercing Through the Selflessness of Dharmas — Realized Within, Never Fully Told to Another, and Known by Six Marks Beyond All Language

What is piercing through the selflessness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found. Within this, what can be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its principle can be found — the very domain in which noble wisdom operates. And this is realized within, through one's own inner attainment, and cannot be fully conveyed to another through words. It is known, with respect to the verbal nature the foolish and unlearned imagine and cling to, through six marks that reveal a nature genuinely different from it. What are the six? First, it cannot be reached through one's own discursive thought; second, it cannot be fully shown to another; third, it surpasses the domain of the physical sense-faculties; fourth, it surpasses every characteristic; fifth, it surpasses the domain of consciousness; and sixth, it surpasses the domain of affliction.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿法無我性？謂唯由一相，可得所顯故。此中可得者，謂即於彼事道理可得，聖智所行。又即於彼自內所證，不可以言為他宣說。彼由六相，於諸凡愚遍計所執言說自性、異相可得。何等六相？一者、不可自尋思，二者、不可說示他，三者、超過色根所行，四者、超過一切相，五者、超過識所行，六者、超過煩惱所行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the selflessness of dharmas? It is displayed through only a single mark: what can be found.
Within this, what can be found is that, with respect to that same matter, its principle can be found — the very domain in which noble wisdom operates. And this is realized within, through one's own inner attainment, and cannot be fully conveyed to another through words.
It is known, with respect to the verbal nature the foolish and unlearned imagine and cling to, through six marks that reveal a nature genuinely different from it. What are the six? First, it cannot be reached through one's own discursive thought; second, it cannot be fully shown to another; third, it surpasses the domain of the physical sense-faculties; fourth, it surpasses every characteristic; fifth, it surpasses the domain of consciousness; and sixth, it surpasses the domain of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth piercing mirrors the second: where dharma-emptiness (the third) found the imagined verbal nature absent, dharma-selflessness (the fourth) finds something genuinely present instead — suchness itself, the very domain noble wisdom directly operates within, here called "the selflessness of dharmas" to distinguish it from the mere emptiness of self already covered. Cháng Bǎi carefully distinguishes this from "emptiness of dharmas" in the strict sense (per the Cheng Weishi Lun): emptiness of dharmas negates only the imagined nature falsely projected onto the dependent and perfected natures, never those two natures themselves — to deny the dependent and perfected natures too would be the very "nihilistic emptiness" the treatise later warns against under the sixth kind of piercing-through. This suchness is realized, crucially, only through direct inner attainment — a teacher cannot hand it to a student through words alone, the way water's temperature is known only by drinking it, not by being told. And because it lies utterly outside conceptual construction, it is known only negatively, through six marks stating what it is not: unreachable by one's own ordinary discursive thought, not fully expressible to another, beyond the physical senses, beyond every conceptual characteristic, beyond consciousness's ordinary operation, and beyond affliction's reach entirely.

7. Section: The Fifth Piercing Through Opens — The Extreme of Superimposition, Where Nothing There Is Wrongly Taken to Be There

What is piercing through the extreme of superimposition? It is displayed through two kinds of mark. First, displayed through the superimposition of distinguishing features; and second, displayed through the superimposition of an own-nature.

Chinese Source

云何貫穿增益邊？謂由二種相。一者、差別增益所顯故；二者、自性增益所顯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is piercing through the extreme of superimposition? It is displayed through two kinds of mark. First, displayed through the superimposition of distinguishing features; and second, displayed through the superimposition of an own-nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth piercing-through addresses one of the two classic extremes a middle-way understanding must avoid: superimposition (增益), wrongly asserting something exists where, in truth, nothing does — the opposite error from the sixth piercing (denial, wrongly asserting nothing exists where something genuinely does). As the Decisive-Discussion chapter puts it, superimposition means falsely clinging to an imagined own-nature within what is actually only the dependent or perfected nature — asserting a verbal, conceptual "thisness" belongs to something that, apart from that concept, was never really there. Two kinds are distinguished: the superimposition of an own-nature (the root error — treating a merely conventional, verbally-designated nature as though it were genuinely, substantially real) and the superimposition of distinguishing features built on that same false root (the elaborations of permanence, self, and the rest that follow once a false own-nature is already assumed) — the Nirvāṇa Sūtra's image of a silkworm spinning its own cocoon and dying within it captures exactly this: beings spin false constructions out of their own conceptual thread and then become trapped by what they themselves spun.

8. Section: Five More Layers of Superimposition — From Impermanence to Self, Then to a "Real" or "Unreal" Mind, Each Built on the One Before

Second, the superimposition of impermanence. Third, resting on the superimposition of permanence, the superimposition of a self. Fourth, resting on the superimposition of impermanence, the superimposition of selflessness. Fifth, resting on the superimposition of selflessness, the superimposition of a real mind. Sixth, resting on the superimposition of a self, the superimposition of an unreal mind.

Chinese Source

二者、執無常增益。三者、執常增益為所依止，執我增益。四者、執無常增益為所依止，執無我增益。五者、執無我增益為所依止，執真實心增益。六者、執我增益為所依止，執不真實心增益。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the superimposition of impermanence.
Third, resting on the superimposition of permanence, the superimposition of a self.
Fourth, resting on the superimposition of impermanence, the superimposition of selflessness.
Fifth, resting on the superimposition of selflessness, the superimposition of a real mind.
Sixth, resting on the superimposition of a self, the superimposition of an unreal mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Eight layers of superimposition unfold in a single causal chain, each resting on the one before, splitting into two parallel branches from the first fork between permanence and impermanence. Taking the aggregates as permanent (already covered) leads, on this branch, to superimposing a self — since a permanently persisting "something" naturally invites the further false step of calling it "me." Taking the aggregates as impermanent instead leads to superimposing selflessness — but even the correct insight of selflessness, wrongly reified into a "real" attaining mind, becomes its own subtle superimposition (a fault the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* attributes specifically to śrāvakas and pre-ground bodhisattvas, who may mistake the merely conventional mark of the person's emptiness for suchness itself, rather than realizing suchness directly). And superimposing a self, on the other branch, leads to superimposing an "unreal mind" — the deluded, self-clinging mind that self-view itself generates, real as an operating delusion even though what it clings to is not.

9. Section: Five Faults of Clinging to an Unreal Mind, the Final Two Layers of Superimposition, and Why a Bodhisattva Holds to Neither — Closing Differentiated Superimposition Entirely

Within the defiled dharmas to be counteracted, there are five faults: the fault of distortion, the fault of idle proliferation, the fault of giving rise to unwholesome conduct, the fault of coarseness, and the fault of impermanence. And likewise within the pure dharmas that counteract them. Seventh, resting on the superimposition of a real mind, the superimposition of wholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of purity itself. Eighth, resting on the superimposition of an unreal mind, the superimposition of unwholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of defilement itself. These are called the eight kinds of differentiated superimposition. Within these, a bodhisattva clings to none of these superimpositions, neither urging others to cling to them nor praising them.

Chinese Source

於所對治雜染法中，由五過失，謂顛倒過失、戲論過失、發起惡行過失、麤重過失、無常性過失。及於彼能對治清淨法中。七者、執真實心增益為所依止，執善等增益，乃至執清淨增益。八者、執不真實心增益為所依止，執不善等增益，乃至執雜染增益。是名八種差別增益。此中菩薩，於彼增益都不執著，不勸他執，亦不讚美。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the defiled dharmas to be counteracted, there are five faults: the fault of distortion, the fault of idle proliferation, the fault of giving rise to unwholesome conduct, the fault of coarseness, and the fault of impermanence. And likewise within the pure dharmas that counteract them.
Seventh, resting on the superimposition of a real mind, the superimposition of wholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of purity itself.
Eighth, resting on the superimposition of an unreal mind, the superimposition of unwholesome dharmas and the rest, extending to the superimposition of defilement itself.
These are called the eight kinds of differentiated superimposition.
Within these, a bodhisattva clings to none of these superimpositions, neither urging others to cling to them nor praising them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The unreal, self-clinging mind already superimposed carries five faults into everything it touches: distortion (mistaking the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, the impure for pure, the selfless for self); idle proliferation (empty, meaningless verbal and mental elaboration — the Ratnakūṭa's own gloss calls this the fault of "slander," since clinging to an unreal mind eventually slanders the Three Jewels themselves); giving rise to unwholesome conduct (the ten unwholesome courses of action, generated once self-view takes hold); coarseness (the affliction's own seeds deepening, further obstructing practice — a scripture elsewhere lists twenty specific harms this proliferation brings, from present suffering to rebirth in unfavorable conditions); and impermanence's own fault (compounding suffering, since clinging to permanence only makes impermanence's actual arrival hurt worse). The same five faults recur even in the "pure" dharmas meant to counteract defilement — even grasping at nirvāṇa or the noble path as a real, substantial attainment is its own subtle superimposition. The seventh and eighth layers then extend outward from the real and unreal minds already superimposed: a "real" mind superimposes wholesome dharmas, neutral dharmas, and finally purity itself as substantially existing; an "unreal" mind superimposes unwholesome dharmas, obscured-neutral dharmas, and finally defilement itself the same way. A bodhisattva, avoiding all eight layers at once, neither clings to any of them, encourages others toward them, nor praises them as though they were something to be sought. With this, differentiated superimposition — all eight layers, from permanence and impermanence through self, selflessness, a real or unreal mind, and finally purity or defilement — is fully explained.

10. Section: Superimposition of an Own-Nature — The Root Beneath All Eight Layers of Differentiated Superimposition

What is called superimposition of an own-nature? It is that, resting on differentiated superimposition, the foolish, through their own imagined and clung-to verbal own-nature, superimpose existence onto that same matter.

Chinese Source

何等名為自性增益？謂差別增益為所依止，由諸愚夫遍計所執所有言說自性增益，即於彼事增益為有。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called superimposition of an own-nature? It is that, resting on differentiated superimposition, the foolish, through their own imagined and clung-to verbal own-nature, superimpose existence onto that same matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second and final kind of superimposition names the deeper root the eight preceding layers all quietly depend on: not any one of the eight specific misconceptions (permanence, self, purity, and so forth) but the more basic act of treating any verbally-designated "own-nature" at all as though it substantially existed. Cháng Bǎi notes an apparent puzzle in the text's own phrasing — differentiated superimposition is said to rest on superimposition of an own-nature, yet here own-nature superimposition is introduced as resting on differentiated superimposition — resolved by recognizing that the coarser, more obvious differentiated superimpositions are noticed first in practice, even though the subtler root, own-nature superimposition itself, is what actually makes them possible in the first place; the treatise simply follows the order of what is easier to observe before addressing what underlies it. The Bodhisattva Ground's own distinction is helpful here: "own-nature" discrimination asks what a thing fundamentally is (form as such); "differentiated" discrimination asks how that thing further divides (with form or without, past or present, and so on) — own-nature superimposition is the more basic error of the two, since without first assuming some fixed "what-it-is," none of the further differentiated superimpositions could even get started.

11. Section: Piercing Through the Extreme of Denial — Nihilistic Emptiness, Where Even the Dependent and Perfected Natures Are Wrongly Denied

What is piercing through the extreme of denial? It is displayed through a single mark: the denial of what is genuinely real. Within this, denial of what is genuinely real is: forming resolute understanding based on a false view of the selflessness of dharmas, and clinging to the belief that every kind and every characteristic of dharmas whatsoever does not exist at all.

Chinese Source

The sixth piercing-through names the mirror-image error of the fifth: where superimposition wrongly asserts something exists that does not, denial wrongly asserts that nothing exists at all — even the dependent and perfected natures, which genuinely are real apart from what is merely imagined. The Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on true meaning describes exactly this failure mode: someone hears a profound Great Vehicle teaching on emptiness, whose hidden intent is not yet fully grasped, and concludes wrongly that "everything is merely conventional, and this alone is the truth" — a conclusion the treatise calls slandering the bodhisattva canon itself, since it denies both the genuinely real and, in effect, the merely false along with it. This "nihilistic emptiness" (惡取空) breeds heedlessness and abandons diligent practice, since if literally nothing is real, there is nothing left worth striving for either — exactly the trap a bodhisattva must avoid on this side of the middle way, just as superimposition must be avoided on the other.

The seventh piercing-through moves from avoiding the two extremes to actually realizing dharmas directly. It adds a "fourth matter" to the three already searched for a self — the twelve links of dependent origination (ignorance conditioning formations, all the way to birth conditioning aging and death), where what is to be counteracted (affliction, karma, and suffering) and what counteracts it (the wisdom of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, or the three gates of liberation) are both examined the same way: their own conventionally-designated nature simply cannot be found, whether one is searching among conditioned or unconditioned dharmas. This matters because even the "medicine" of counteracting wisdom can itself become a new sickness if clung to as though it were a real, substantial thing — the antidote to affliction is not meant to become a fresh object of grasping in its own right, which is exactly why its own imagined nature, too, must be seen as unfindable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second mark shows that even the paired categories used to describe the path — cessation of what is counteracted and arising of what counteracts it, the accumulation of conditioned dharmas and the accomplishment of unconditioned ones — are themselves only relative, mutually-established labels, never realities with their own fixed nature; affliction, karma, and suffering to be counteracted, and the wisdom that counteracts them, are spoken of only provisionally, for the sake of guiding practitioners toward what ultimately cannot be put into words at all. The third mark then describes how this entire three-stage process (first, no own-nature to be found; second, no distinguishing feature to be found) matures through sustained familiarity into direct realization — a wisdom that no longer needs deliberate discursive effort to see the point, having become the practitioner's own directly-realized understanding. With this, the seventh piercing through — direct realization of dharmas, its three marks of no own-nature, no distinguishing feature, and wisdom made direct through sustained practice — is fully explained.

14. Section: Dedicating Realization Toward Great Awakening — Why a Bodhisattva, Able to End Rebirth, Deliberately Does Not

This is further the counteragent of karma leading to future existence, and the counteragent of the transmigration of the reborn body and of momentary transmigration alike — called the practice that extinguishes and leads to nirvāṇa, without own-nature. This, further, takes the transmigration of birth-and-death as what is to be counteracted. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth piercing-through addresses a genuine puzzle: having realized the three gates of liberation — emptiness (countering affliction), wishlessness (countering craving for future rebirth), and signlessness (countering every conceptual mark) — a bodhisattva is fully capable of ending saṃsāra for good, yet deliberately does not. Each gate counters a different layer of transmigration at once: emptiness counters karma that would generate future existence, wishlessness counters the reborn body's own continued transmigration, and signlessness counters even the moment-to-moment transmigration of arising and passing thought. All three together are called "freedom from fabrication" precisely because they see through the imagined own-nature that would otherwise drive continued rebirth. And yet a bodhisattva, holding this very capacity to end rebirth, deliberately chooses not to exercise it — not from confusion, but out of compassion for beings still caught in saṃsāra, choosing to remain within birth-and-death for their sake while dedicating the merit of this very realization toward the far greater goal of complete awakening, rather than personal escape alone. Master Yìn Shùn notes both the Madhyamaka and Yogācāra schools converge exactly here: whatever their differences elsewhere, both traditions equally exhort bodhisattvas to return to saṃsāra rather than exit it prematurely.

The ninth piercing-through explains why the eighth's deliberate retention of affliction does not simply overwhelm a bodhisattva with the very suffering they chose not to fully end. The key is that affliction and suffering, examined through the wisdom of the selflessness of dharmas, are themselves found to have no substantial own-nature — no real defilement, no real suffering to be found once their imagined verbal nature is seen through. An ordinary being caught in saṃsāra suffers acutely because affliction and pain are taken as solidly real; a bodhisattva, whose compassion remains undiminished precisely because this same wisdom keeps affliction from taking genuine hold, can remain within birth-and-death indefinitely without being crushed by it — the strength of realized emptiness is exactly what keeps compassion from ever burning out.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The tenth piercing-through distinguishes the two kinds of selflessness (of person and of dharmas) from four specific ways even a sincere practitioner can misapprehend them. A distinction of view names the conceit of mistaking mere suppression for genuine purification, or clinging to dharmas as substantially real and thereby missing dharma-emptiness. This same error, pushed further, becomes the far extreme of denial — the nihilistic emptiness already covered under the sixth piercing-through, now revisited as one of the four possible ways distinction itself can go astray. A distinction of confusion regarding cessation names the fear that arises upon hearing that all dharmas lack an own-nature, mistaking this teaching for a call to mere annihilation and, out of that fear, abandoning the willingness to return to saṃsāra for beings' sake. And a distinction of confusion regarding the mind names how this same fear compounds: a distorted perception gives rise to a distorted view, which in turn gives rise to distorted thinking, the practitioner's mind becoming lost in exactly the three-fold distortion the Heart Sūtra's own closing lines describe overcoming — freedom from inverted dream-thinking, ultimate nirvāṇa.

Two faces of the first distinction, both drawn from śrāvaka practice specifically. The first is a subtle conceit: a practitioner who has genuinely realized the selflessness of the person, not yet at the path of seeing, mistakes what is still only a suppression of affliction for its actual purification, generating pride over an attainment ("the body will no longer arise") not yet genuinely secured. The second is more structural: such a practitioner correctly examines the grasped object (the five aggregates) as without self, yet never turns the same scrutiny on the grasping mind that observes them — the very wisdom of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness is itself left unexamined, still treated as a real, substantial thing rather than seen as equally empty. Clinging to dharmas in this residual way, such a practitioner seeks only personal liberation from affliction, unable to bear returning to saṃsāra for others' sake. A bodhisattva, by contrast, examines both the grasped and the grasping together from the outset, missing neither self-emptiness nor dharma-emptiness.

The second mark names the treatise's own severe verdict on nihilistic emptiness: someone who has genuinely realized the selflessness of the person, yet takes this teaching to mean nothing whatsoever exists, ranks even lower than an ordinary being who still clings to a self — the Buddha's own words, cited here, say he would rather someone hold self-view as vast as Mount Sumeru than hold a view of emptiness as small as a mustard seed, because self-view can still be corrected through emptiness, while a view that denies emptiness itself leaves no remaining foothold for correction at all. The final two piercings bring the whole sequence to its climax. Emptiness reaching its outermost limit describes a bodhisattva's wisdom so thoroughly realized that it now operates spontaneously within saṃsāra itself — appearing, to outward view, to undergo karma and affliction just as an ordinary being does, yet without ever actually being caught by them, since the whole display is understood as inexpressible, free of any fixed own-nature, and momentary; saṃsāra and nirvāṇa here are neither the same nor different, a bodhisattva living within both without attachment to either. With this, all thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct — the emptiness and selflessness of the person and of dharmas, the extremes of superimposition and denial, direct realization of dharmas and its dedication toward great awakening, freedom from affliction's grip, the four ways distinction goes astray, causality's final release, emptiness at its limit, and its majestic power — are now complete in full, closing the sixth gateway, establishing what is to be learned, entirely.

22. Section: Four Kinds of Śrāvaka — the Transformation-Body Śrāvaka and the Śrāvaka of Overweening Conceit

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh gateway now unfolds in detail. Vasubandhu's commentary on the Lotus Sūtra names a closely parallel fourfold division — transformation śrāvakas, śrāvakas of overweening conceit, śrāvakas who retreat from the bodhi-mind, and fixed-lineage śrāvakas — three of which match here directly; the third differs subtly, since "retreating from the bodhi-mind" names someone who once held it and lost it, while "turned toward awakening" below names an indeterminate-lineage śrāvaka who eventually turns small toward great. The transformation-body śrāvaka is not an ordinary practitioner at all: it is a buddha or bodhisattva who, inwardly holding the true bodhisattva practice concealed, outwardly manifests the form of a śrāvaka in order to draw the two indeterminate-lineage types above toward the Great Vehicle and to teach beings broadly. The śrāvaka of overweening conceit may be an ordinary being who has not yet attained any fruit yet believes he has, or — per Vasubandhu and the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra* — even a learner-stage noble one who, having gained only the wisdom of personal selflessness, clings to a mistaken understanding of the selflessness of dharmas and reckons it pure; only the fully liberated arhat, having perfected personal selflessness in full, is free of this conceit. Master Yuance's commentary on the *Sūtra Explaining the Profound Secrets* records three positions on how broadly this conceit applies — to ordinary beings alone, to ordinary beings and learner-level nobles together, or even to arhats themselves (since though free of afflictive conceit, a subtler conceit rooted in the cognitive obstruction can remain) — reconciling the apparent tension with the Lotus Sūtra's own statement that a true arhat could not fail to believe its teaching, by distinguishing conceit rooted in affliction from conceit rooted in what remains unknown. Such a practitioner, lacking the wisdom of dharma-emptiness, clings to dharmas as truly existent, regards the five aggregates as sheer suffering, and seeks only to end affliction and enter nirvāṇa as swiftly as possible — mistaking a partial, one-sided realization for the genuine article.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This third kind of śrāvaka unfolds in three stages. First, renewed cultivation: originally of a lineage too weak in compassion to have any interest in liberating others, this practitioner, simply through proximity to a living Buddha and exposure to the Great Vehicle's vast teaching, comes to admire it and begins cultivating it alongside the śrāvaka path — differing from Vasubandhu's "retreating" śrāvaka, who once held and then lost the bodhi-mind, since this one never held it to begin with. Second, awakening: even after such a person has reached the ultimate uncontaminated fruit — arhatship itself — the buddhas, honoring the Lotus Sūtra's teaching that there is finally only one vehicle and the other two are skillful expedients, do not leave the matter there, but awaken and guide even the arhat toward unsurpassed awakening. Third, diligence: the habituation of favoring quietude built up over a whole path of small-vehicle practice does not vanish merely because the resolve for great awakening has newly arisen; such a person moves toward benefiting beings only sluggishly, still preferring solitude to the crowded, effortful work bodhisattvas take on willingly — a marked contrast with one who possessed the buddha-lineage and its ease with beings from the very first arousal of the resolve.

24. Section: The Śrāvaka Bent Solely on Quiescence — the Two Princes, Alike in the King's Happiness Yet Unlike in Statecraft — Closing the Four Kinds of Śrāvaka Entirely

The śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence is: one who, from the very beginning, is of the most extremely weak compassionate lineage, who thereby turns wholly away from and abandons the matter of benefiting sentient beings, who, because of an intense dread of the suffering of birth-and-death, has only the resolve to abide in nirvāṇa, and who, in the end, is utterly unable to set forth toward great awakening. It is as with two princes born in a mutually resembling place, receiving, equally and evenly, the king's happiness — one of whom is thoroughly skilled in all matters of royal governance and its arts, while the second prince is not so — the two differing only by this portion, not by their enjoyment of the king's happiness. Just so, within the uncontaminated realm, know that there is a distinction between the multitude of bodhisattvas and the śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This fourth and final kind of śrāvaka is the "fixed lineage" of Vasubandhu's fourfold scheme — a practitioner who, on hearing the four noble truths, enters directly into a small, residue-free extinction and is ultimately unable to turn back toward the Great Vehicle at all. The parable of the two princes then clarifies exactly where the bodhisattva's superiority to such a śrāvaka lies, and where it does not. Both princes are born into the same palace and receive, in equal measure, "the king's happiness" — a stand-in for nirvāṇa, the liberation itself, which the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* by Masters Jing and Tai glosses as the palace, the Buddha as king, the three-basket teaching as statecraft, and beneficial activity for others as the princely arts. Only one prince has also mastered governance and the arts of benefiting the realm; the second enjoys the same royal comfort but nothing more. So too here: on the score of liberation from affliction itself, the emptiness a bodhisattva realizes and the emptiness a fixed-lineage śrāvaka realizes are genuinely equal — one cannot be graded superior to the other in this respect alone. What distinguishes them is entirely the follow-on wisdom devoted to benefiting others, which the śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence never develops at all. Because this passage lists no analogous fifth category for the śrāvaka of overweening conceit, the commentary notes this confirms that figure was an ordinary being all along, outside the fourfold division of realized śrāvakas being compared here to bodhisattvas. With this, the explanation of the four kinds of śrāvaka is complete, closing the first half of the seventh gateway entirely; the treatise turns next to the thirteen differences between what a śrāvaka learns and what a bodhisattva learns.

Having distinguished the four kinds of śrāvaka from the bodhisattva by way of realization itself — where, as the two princes showed, they stand equal — the treatise turns now to a second and more searching comparison: thirteen ways a śrāvaka's whole path of learning differs from a bodhisattva's, even granting the same liberation from affliction. Twelve headings are listed here; "bearing the seed" and "undertaking" are counted as two separate items rather than one compound item, yielding the thirteen promised at the gateway's opening. In brief outline, ahead of the detailed treatment to come: in disposition, the śrāvaka turns away from the afflicted activity of benefiting beings while the bodhisattva remains within saṃsāra to benefit them; in accumulated white dharmas, the śrāvaka's are narrow while the bodhisattva's are boundless; in accumulated wisdom, the śrāvaka's uncontaminated wisdom serves only to remove personal affliction while the bodhisattva's serves all beings everywhere; in kind, the śrāvaka is not a child of the Buddha while the bodhisattva is; in lineage, the śrāvaka does not accomplish the mark of the buddha-lineage while the bodhisattva does; in bearing the seed and undertaking, the śrāvaka's faculties, though ripened, cannot yet perform a buddha's deeds in future lives, while a bodhisattva performs them from the very first instant of the resolve; in majestic power, the śrāvaka receives no such reverence from gods and humans as even a beginning bodhisattva does; in correct conduct, the śrāvaka's medicine for affliction, though perfected, cannot cure every being's disease while the bodhisattva's can; in being a field of merit, the śrāvaka is not a true field for the offerings of gods and the world while the bodhisattva is; in surpassing excellence, the bodhisattva outstrips even the Tathāgata as cause outstrips fruit; in cause and fruit, the śrāvaka's pure cause cannot draw forth great awakening while the bodhisattva's does; and in what one's own arising depends upon, śrāvakas arise dependent on bodhisattvas, never the reverse. Each of these thirteen now receives its own detailed treatment.

26. Section: Disposition, the Accumulation of White Dharmas, and the Accumulation of Wisdom

The śrāvaka bent solely on quiescence, because he turns wholly away from and abandons the afflicted activity of benefiting sentient beings amid all conditioned formations, has solely the disposition to abide in quiescence; the bodhisattva, though possessed of defilement, is the reverse of this. Further, that śrāvaka, seeking only his own increase, has white dharmas that are narrow and small; the bodhisattva, wishing to increase the happiness of all sentient beings, has white dharmas that are boundless. Further, that śrāvaka, through his uncontaminated wisdom, seeks only to remove his own affliction; the bodhisattva is, universally, for the sake of every sentient being throughout the ten directions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first difference, disposition: the fixed-lineage śrāvaka's motivation is purely a wish for release from formations that are themselves afflicted, cultivating the eightfold path and the four truths toward "attaining one's own benefit"; a defiled bodhisattva, by contrast, has aroused the bodhi-mind and great compassion, and through that mind, compassion, and the six perfections joined to the understanding of emptiness, works to "adorn lands and ripen beings." The second, the accumulation of white dharmas: a śrāvaka's wholesome practice, being largely self-directed with only incidental benefit to others, remains narrow no matter how sincerely pursued, while a bodhisattva's benefits every being without limit — moving those without the three-vehicle lineage toward the human and heavenly happiness suited to them, and moving those who do possess it all the way to the fruit of their own vehicle. In sum, the śrāvaka's path of liberation emphasizes self-benefit, helping others only as circumstances allow and easily narrowing, if the joys of this present life are neglected, into a passive avoidance of harm; the bodhisattva's path emphasizes benefiting others first, achieving self-benefit precisely by giving everything to beings, and values both their ultimate liberation and their well-being here and now. The third, the accumulation of wisdom: an uncontaminated wisdom conditioned on the selflessness of the person and of dharmas and the true suchness they reveal is called uncontaminated wisdom; the śrāvaka's, built from personal selflessness alone, exists only to remove his own affliction, while the bodhisattva's, built from both selflessnesses together, is universal — the wisdom of personal selflessness preventing self-view, the wisdom of dharma-selflessness making it possible to bear suffering without fleeing it, to realize saṃsāra and nirvāṇa as one and the same emptiness, and so to labor for beings from within birth-and-death rather than seeking one's own swift escape from it. A śrāvaka's wisdom is purified wisdom only; a buddha's, once perfected, is purified wisdom, wisdom of the paths, and wisdom of all modes together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth difference, kind: "the supreme object of liberation" names nirvāṇa itself, the fixed-lineage śrāvaka's constant object of attention, while "the inferior object of sentient beings amid conditioned formations" names birth-and-death, the bodhisattva's constant object — inferior only in the sense of being lower and more entangled, not in value. Precisely by dwelling attentively on suffering beings rather than fleeing toward liberation, cultivating loving-kindness, exchanging self for other, and aspiring to benefit others and attain awakening for their sake, the bodhisattva carries forward the Buddha's own intention, perpetuating the lineage without allowing it to be severed, and so alone earns the name "child of the Buddha." The fifth, lineage: even a śrāvaka diligent in effort, thoroughly skilled in the four noble truths, and settled in mind through having ended affliction, accomplishes no mark or substance of the buddha-lineage, since he has no wish to return life after life to liberate beings — for the fixed-lineage śrāvaka alone, not for the one turned toward awakening, even the buddhas cannot draw him in. A parable found elsewhere in the canon makes the same point: a son born to the queen and a son born to a maidservant to the same king are not equal — only the queen's son is a true prince, however the palace as a whole may regard them. The bodhisattva alone is born into the Tathāgata's own family and is his genuine heir.

The sixth difference, bearing the seed and undertaking: even once a śrāvaka's faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, together with the three uncontaminated roots, have fully ripened into arhatship, entry into final nirvāṇa still leaves him unable to perform a buddha's deeds — though even after that entry a buddha's own unabiding great nirvāṇa continues, through the power of prior vows, to respond whenever beings sincerely call. A bodhisattva, by contrast, begins performing buddha-deeds — not only reciting the teaching but propagating, educating, and liberating beings in every form — from the very first instant the resolve arises; Elder Miao Jing often urged his students to take the bodhisattva vow precisely on these grounds, that so great a benefit begins at the very first thought. The seventh, majestic power: an arhat, having sought only the ending of his own affliction, carries little majestic power despite his ultimate attainment, and receives no reverence from gods and humans; even an ordinary bodhisattva only newly embarked on the path, though still thoroughly afflicted, already possesses majestic power and wisdom that outshines every śrāvaka and pratyekabuddha who seeks self-benefit alone — Maitreya's own praise of the bodhisattva path's greatness. The eighth, correct conduct: a śrāvaka's wisdom-medicine, personal selflessness, is genuinely perfected, yet its power is confined and cannot treat every being's affliction; the bodhisattva, possessing personal selflessness together with dharma-selflessness — the latter especially emphasized — is able to cultivate conduct benefiting others in the ultimate sense, and where a fortunate encounter allows, even a śrāvaka can still help a particular being toward liberation, but only the bodhisattva's medicine reaches the full range of beings' afflictions.

The ninth difference, being a field of merit: a fixed-lineage arhat's wisdom genuinely illumines beings, yet because he will exhaust his life and enter nirvāṇa without returning, he cannot serve indefinitely as anyone's field of merit; a bodhisattva, though still carrying affliction, vows to labor for beings until every last one is liberated, and so remains a genuine field of merit for gods and the whole world without end. The tenth, surpassing excellence, unfolds in two parts. First, the standing of the three is compared directly: among śrāvakas the Buddha is supreme, yet the bodhisattva — the cause from which that very Buddha was accomplished — is more supreme still, since fruit presupposes cause; this is why the treatise everywhere praises bodhisattva conduct as the more excellent cause, in the same sense that beings dread the fruit of their deeds while bodhisattvas are careful chiefly about the cause, knowing a crooked cause yields a crooked fruit. Second, this excellence is explained through two conditions: a bodhisattva ripens beings through the six perfections and four means of attraction, and, in that very labor, also ripens his own buddha-dharmas, enduring hardship and advancing diligently even as he gives to others — self-benefit and benefiting others accomplished together from beginning to end — and by this twofold cause draws forth the fruit of awakening while bringing about the liberation of whatever beings are thereby ripened. The closing parable makes plain where the bodhisattva's excellence over even a buddha, and over the two vehicles, finally lies: one person who can both prepare a rare and wondrous feast and serve it to others is not the same as one who merely sits down and eats alone — the śrāvaka, having attained the fruit, partakes of nirvāṇa by himself, while the bodhisattva, skillfully accomplished in both self-benefit and benefiting others, does not stop at partaking alone but first invites the many to share the feast; as the perfection of giving teaches elsewhere in this treatise, whatever a bodhisattva has, of dharma or of wealth, he regards as already belonging to the buddhas and bodhisattvas rather than to himself, freely given the moment any being asks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eleventh difference, cause and fruit: a fixed-lineage śrāvaka's whole training in precepts, concentration, and wisdom, and personal selflessness, is genuine and often supported by countless good spiritual friends who share the same aspiration toward quietude — yet because none of this was undertaken with unsurpassed awakening as its aim, it cannot draw forth that fruit; a bodhisattva, sharing the same purity of cause and the same support of good friends, aims at unsurpassed awakening from the very start of cultivation, and so draws it forth. The śrāvaka's highest attainable fruit remains arhatship; the bodhisattva's is buddhahood itself. The twelfth, what one's own arising depends upon, settles the question of ultimate priority: both a buddha's awakening and an arhat's awakening occur only through the Buddha's own teaching, yet it is specifically the bodhisattva path that the Buddha opens as the root capacity by which beings of every kind — of whatever vehicle — become able to be liberated; without the Buddha there would be no Buddha-dharma at all, and the Buddha himself only arises through completing the bodhisattva path, so that śrāvakas arise in dependence on bodhisattvas, and never the reverse. With this, all twelve headings — together making the thirteen differences promised at the outset, disposition being counted as the first — are fully explained, closing the seventh gateway, comparing what a śrāvaka learns with what a bodhisattva learns, entirely. The treatise turns next to the eighth gateway: how a bodhisattva, through worldly and supramundane wisdom together, is able to perform the work of benefiting others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth gateway names three wisdoms at once, though the opening question names only two: worldly wisdom, which addresses gross and middling defilement through suppression and subjugation; supramundane wisdom, also called root wisdom or true wisdom, which uproots the subtle latent seeds of defilement themselves and, per the *Compendium of the Great Vehicle*, neither depends on nor conditions any external object — like eyes closed, admitting no discrimination at all; and worldly-and-supramundane wisdom, the subsequently-attained wisdom of noble bodhisattvas above the ground of realization, which — per fascicle 72 — arises after the root wisdom has, through initial correct wisdom, penetrated true suchness, and thereafter, within the domain of conventionally-established truths, generates revulsion toward the three realms' faults and relish for their quiescence, hastening the pure ending of the afflictive obstruction; being an unprecedented realization it is called supramundane, yet, taking verbal-conventional characteristics as its object, it is also called worldly — hence "worldly-and-supramundane." Per the *Compendium*, this wisdom does depend on mind and does condition an external object, discriminating in various ways — like eyes open upon a world of appearing colors — arising after the root wisdom as its subsequently-attained companion. The two lines of this opening passage divide cleanly along these lines: becoming "a great physician" names benefit performed through worldly wisdom, addressing the gross and middling defilements to be treated below; proclaiming a path "unsurpassed" names benefit performed through supramundane wisdom, addressing the subtle latent-seed defilements no worldly remedy can reach. A bodhisattva able to roam every world-system, every land, and even every one of the four modes of birth — appearing, as the Buddha himself once did in his prior lives as elephant-king or hare-king, in whatever form of life still needs saving — brings both remedies to bear wherever beings can be reached.

32. Section: What Is Worldly Wisdom, and What Is the Gross Grade of Defilement

What is worldly wisdom? It is that, with respect to the gross grade of all defilement, one is able to bring about its cessation and counteraction; and with respect to the middling grade, one is able to bring about its subjugation and counteraction. What is called the gross grade of defilement? It is: for householders, the disposition of greed, hatred, and delusion in action; for renunciants, the disposition depending on views, and the disposition of incorrect reflective attention on which that in turn depends, and the disposition of craving for future existence; and, through the four inverted views, whether taken together or individually, the disposition of clinging to what is not liberation as though it were liberation.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the gross grade of defilement? It is: for householders, the disposition of greed, hatred, and delusion in action; for renunciants, the disposition depending on views, and the disposition of incorrect reflective attention on which that in turn depends, and the disposition of craving for future existence; and, through the four inverted views, whether taken together or individually, the disposition of clinging to what is not liberation as though it were liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Worldly wisdom operates at two grades: the hearing-and-reflection wisdom of a bodhisattva still at the stage of accumulation suppresses the gross grade, and the meditative, cultivation-born worldly wisdom of a bodhisattva already possessed of concentration at the stage of application subjugates the middling grade — neither uproots defilement at the root, but both bring real relief. The gross grade differs by householder and renunciant: householders, immersed in a life of desire, are marked chiefly by blatant greed, hatred, and delusion, which surface easily; renunciants, freed from that life of desire, instead struggle with views — the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* lists the false view of self, the extremist view, the view of holding inferior views as supreme, attachment to wrong precepts and vows, and outright wrong view as the "disposition depending on views," with incorrect reflective attention as their common root-condition and the craving for pleasant future rebirths (rather than genuine liberation) as a further gross affliction alongside them — and, compounding all of this, the four inverted views regarding what is impermanent, suffering, impure, and selfless, taken either as one composite inversion applied to the five aggregates as a whole or as four individual inversions applied severally to body, feeling, mind, and dharmas, which lead a practitioner to mistake what is not liberation — an increase in future rebirth's pleasures — for liberation itself. Views, incorrect attention, craving for future existence, and the four inversions all reinforce one another in a single tangled knot; it is precisely to correct these gross views that a renunciant must study the teaching without pause, since uncorrected views make affliction coarser and more visible than even a householder's undisciplined desire.

33. Section: The Middling Grade of Defilement, and Its Gradual Counteraction Through Mindfulness and the Factors of Awakening

What is the middling grade of defilement? It is that, the gross grade of defilement having already been brought to cessation, taking as one's support the individual counteragent for each, one has the disposition, with respect to various objects, of being bound by the entanglement of greed, hatred, and delusion. Through correctly fixing mindfulness upon its object, one causes the unsettled mind to attain settling, and, diligently cultivating the factors of awakening, one is thereby able to subjugate it. Not relying on this cultivation, one instead becomes self-satisfied and complacent — therefore, binding the mind upon its object so as to make it abide, and advancing with courageous diligence, from this one is able correctly to sustain that abiding; abiding being sustained, then, within the mass of aggregation, the very instant a single grasping affliction stirs within thought, one is thereby able to subjugate it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once the gross grade — householders' blatant greed and hatred, renunciants' wrong views, incorrect attention, craving for future existence, and the four inversions — has each been individually counteracted by worldly right view, a subtler layer remains: even a well-behaved, view-corrected practitioner still finds greed, hatred, and delusion binding the mind whenever it meets an agreeable or disagreeable object, since the roots of concentration remain undisturbed. The remedy is the four foundations of mindfulness: fixing correct attention on whatever object triggers the entanglement — contemplating the impure where lust for a pleasant object arises, for instance — steadies a mind that has been unsettled, and diligent cultivation of the thirty-seven factors of awakening built upon this steadying is what finally subjugates it. The treatise then warns against a specific pitfall on this path: a practitioner who gains even a modicum of concentration, perhaps proximate absorption itself, and a little inferior insight, can become complacent and self-satisfied, mistaking the absence of gross affliction for real attainment and ceasing to press further — a satisfaction that itself obstructs the cultivation of insight. The corrective is to keep binding the mind to its object with courageous, unrelenting diligence, so that the four foundations correctly sustain the mind's abiding in contemplating the body as impure, feeling as suffering, mind as impermanent, and dharmas as selfless; once that abiding is genuinely sustained, a practitioner maintains constant mindful awareness within the aggregates — "a mass of nothing but great suffering" — such that the very instant a grasping affliction of greed, hatred, or delusion stirs in a single thought, it is caught and subjugated before it can continue, the mark of a practitioner whose meditative power has reached at least the level of proximate absorption.

34. Section: The Gradual Counteraction of the Counteragent, and the Definition of Worldly Wisdom — Closing World-Wisdom Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Because the seeds of the middling-grade afflictions are not yet uprooted, only suppressed, the treatise closes worldly wisdom's account with one further step: taking the counteragent already produced through the four foundations and the factors of awakening, one now makes it firmly abide without regressing, continuing to press forward with unrelenting diligence toward the uncontaminated wisdom of no discrimination still to come. From this firmly-abiding counteragent, a practitioner is able to subjugate the delusion built into dependent origination itself — the ignorance driving craving and grasping in the twelve-linked chain — together with the delusion of a self in the person and the delusion of a self in dharmas, and thereby gains a decisive, settled understanding of what is the wrong path and what is the eightfold correct path, no longer in any doubt on this score. With this, the definition of worldly wisdom is complete: the counteracting wisdom that brings the gross and middling grades of defilement to cessation and to subjugation, still contaminated cultivated wisdom rather than uncontaminated insight, closing the first of the eighth gateway's two wisdoms entirely. The treatise turns next to supramundane wisdom, which alone can uproot the subtle latent seeds worldly wisdom can only suppress.

35. Section: What Is Supramundane Wisdom, and Its Threefold Skillful Support

It is that, having in this way already subjugated the defilements of the entanglement of greed, hatred, and delusion, one is further able to counteract the defilement belonging to the subtle latent seeds; this true and real wisdom is called supramundane wisdom. And what, further, is this? It is that one relies precisely upon skillfulness in three domains that subjugate and counteract, namely: skillfulness in dependent origination, skillfulness in the resolute understanding of the selflessness of the person, and skillfulness in the resolute understanding of the selflessness of dharmas.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"True and real wisdom" is the non-discriminating root wisdom itself, attained at the path of seeing when the direct realization of dharmas is accomplished. Where worldly wisdom, through the four foundations of mindfulness, could only suppress the entangling activity of greed, hatred, and delusion — because the mind still met its objects with some residual defilement — this wisdom goes further, reaching and counteracting the subtle seeds that worldly wisdom could never touch, since only at the path of seeing does the truly selfless nature of persons and dharmas come to be directly, non-conceptually experienced, at which point the seeds of affliction can finally be uprooted rather than merely managed. This wisdom is not produced from nothing: it depends precisely on the three skillful masteries already subjugated and cultivated at the middling grade — thorough grounding in dependent origination (the working of affliction, karma, and suffering), genuine resolute understanding of the selflessness of the person (whether self is absent from the aggregates or absent within them), and genuine resolute understanding of the selflessness of dharmas — and it is by relying on this threefold prior skillfulness as an expedient practice that entry into the path of seeing finally becomes possible.

The four kinds of non-wisdom are: first, non-wisdom regarding the common characteristic; second, non-wisdom regarding the own characteristic; third, non-wisdom regarding the defiled characteristic; and fourth, non-wisdom regarding the pure characteristic.

Ru-Yi Translation

Wishing to pass utterly beyond defilement with nothing left over, in order to counteract the four kinds of non-wisdom, one need not wait upon another's teaching, but within, diligently, observes one's own mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having reached the path of seeing and directly realized true wisdom, a practitioner has cut off the coarser, conceptually-constructed ignorance, but the subtler, innate ignorance acquired since beginningless time still remains to be worked through on the path of cultivation, by a further advancing path beyond the one already traveled — one that, having reached this point, no longer needs an outside teacher, but proceeds through diligent, direct observation of one's own mind. Four kinds of not-yet-known ignorance are named as its object: not knowing the mind's common characteristic (the marks the mind shares with every dharma at every time), not knowing its own particular characteristic, not knowing its defiled characteristic, and not knowing its pure characteristic — each now to be individually explained and, in the explaining, counteracted.

37. Section: The Mind's Common Characteristic and Own Characteristic — Neither Findable Nor Ever Truly Absent

Through three kinds of mark, know the mind's common characteristic. First, because what arises from conditions has no function when not present before one; second, because what is present has no function, being but a single instant; and third, because greed and the rest, arising from their own conditions, are not made by the mind. Through three kinds of mark, know the mind's own characteristic. First, as before, because its verbal own-nature cannot be found; second, as before, because through six kinds of mark it can genuinely be found; and third, because it is what is attained only by the non-differentiating wisdom of every noble one.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three kinds of mark, know the mind's common characteristic. First, because what arises from conditions has no function when not present before one; second, because what is present has no function, being but a single instant; and third, because greed and the rest, arising from their own conditions, are not made by the mind.
Through three kinds of mark, know the mind's own characteristic. First, as before, because its verbal own-nature cannot be found; second, as before, because through six kinds of mark it can genuinely be found; and third, because it is what is attained only by the non-differentiating wisdom of every noble one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The mind's common characteristic — what it shares with every conditioned dharma, not a special property of mind alone — is drawn out in three marks. First, a mind dependent on causal condition, the condition of immediate succession, the condition of the object, and the condition of dominance is never simply given; when not yet arisen or already ceased, it has no function at all — past mind cannot be found, future mind cannot be found. Second, even the present mind, vividly active as it seems, is only a single instant with no truly abiding function — arisen, it perishes at once, with barely even a moment of "remaining" in which to act. Third, even greed, hatred, and delusion, though they seem to be the mind's own doing, actually arise from their own separate conditions; the mind is not their independent author or agent. The mind's own particular characteristic, meanwhile, draws directly on the earlier piercing-through of dharma-selflessness: first, spoken of in verbal, conventional terms, no fixed own-nature can ever be found in it; second — an echo of the sixth piercing-through's own six marks — it can genuinely be found by the same six marks already explained there (unable to be pondered by oneself, unable to be shown to another, transcending the domain of the physical faculties, transcending every characteristic, transcending the domain of consciousness, and transcending the domain of affliction); and third, this own-nature is finally attained only by the non-differentiating wisdom of noble ones directly, never merely by inference or description. The mind, in short, is neither simply findable as a fixed thing nor simply absent — a genuine middle way running through the whole discussion.

38. Section: The Mind's Defiled Characteristic — Arising Into Rebirth, and Turning Under Craving's Sway

Because, among the various destinies, one's own particular embodiment arises in various kinds, this is called defiled arising. And within this very process, because what has arisen naturally turns instant by instant, because it turns with difficulty subjugated with respect to every object, and because it turns under the sway of craving's force, this is called defiled turning.

Ru-Yi Translation

Because, among the various destinies, one's own particular embodiment arises in various kinds, this is called defiled arising.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The mind's defilement shows itself in three ways, of which the first two are explained here. Defiled arising: the three realms are mind-only, the ten thousand dharmas are consciousness-only — that a being's own embodiment keeps arising in one or another of the five destinies, again and again, is itself the clearest sign that the mind driving that rebirth remains defiled, its lingering affliction for the desire realm, form realm, or formless realm each drawing its own corresponding rebirth. Defiled turning: once a being has taken up a particular embodiment through beginningless afflicted causes, consciousness itself never stops turning instant to instant — the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra* likens its speed to a spinning firebrand or a wild horse in the wind, never still — and meeting every object of the six senses, a mind that does not see how objects are themselves projections of consciousness clings to them as external and real, becomes entangled with them, and, driven by the overwhelming force of self-love and object-love, turns and turns under craving's sway, extremely difficult to subjugate. Defiled arising shows itself across lifetimes; defiled turning shows itself within a single moment-to-moment stream of consciousness — together the first two of the mind's three defiled characteristics, with defiled activity, the third and most elaborate, still to come.

39. Section: Defiled Activity — the Mind at Work in the Wholesome and Unwholesome, Undecided, and Swayed by Feeling

If activity occurs in that, or if activity occurs in this way, it is called defiled activity.
It is that, at one time, activity occurs within the wholesome; or, at one time, activity occurs within the unwholesome; or, at one time, activity occurs within an object; or, at one time, activity occurs within the performance of karma; or, at one time, activity occurs within affliction.
Further, with respect to activity in affliction — greed, hatred, and the rest — there is no fixed, decided activity: it is not that, having engaged in this activity possessed of greed, one then again engages in activity free of greed, and, having engaged in activity free of greed, again engages in activity possessed of greed, and so on.
Further, within dharmas conforming to pleasure and the rest, one attains dominant manifest activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Defiled activity, the mind's third and richest defiled characteristic, is explained in two parts — "activity occurring in that" (where the mind is active) and "activity occurring in this way" (the manner of that activity) — the first of which unfolds here in three further points. First, the mind is sometimes active within wholesome dharmas, sometimes within unwholesome ones, sometimes absorbed in an object, sometimes driving the performance of bodily or verbal karma, and sometimes simply churning in affliction without any outward act at all — wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral by turns, never fixed. Second, this very shifting is itself proof the mind has no fixed, substantial nature: because a mind genuinely, permanently possessed of greed could never also engage in activity free of greed, and vice versa, the plain fact that greed and its absence alternate freely shows the mind lacks any real, unchanging "following" nature bound to affliction — a point elsewhere drawn out at length in the *Discussion of the Stage of Thought-Made [Wisdom]*, where the mind's inconsistent association with karmic formations similarly demonstrates it is not truly, substantially defiled. Third, feeling itself amplifies whatever activity is already underway: whenever the six sense-faculties meet an object productive of pleasant, painful, or neutral feeling, that feeling becomes a dominant condition intensifying the mind's current activity — greed surging toward an agreeable object, aversion toward a disagreeable one, and confusion toward a neutral one. With this threefold account of "activity occurring in that" complete, the treatise turns next to "activity occurring in this way," beginning with the specific manner in which distorted views compound suffering upon suffering.

40. Section: Distortion That Only Deepens Suffering — the Ascetic Who Mistakes Pain Itself for the Path

Further, generating suffering for oneself and cutting off manifold pleasures — this is not through clinging, but through distortion alone. By this, one draws forth manifold suffering upon oneself, with never any satisfaction.

Chinese Source

又生自苦斷壞眾樂，不由執著故，但由顛倒故。由此引發自身眾苦，無有厭足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, generating suffering for oneself and cutting off manifold pleasures — this is not through clinging, but through distortion alone. By this, one draws forth manifold suffering upon oneself, with never any satisfaction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of four ways the mind's activity manifests distortedly names the ascetic who mistakes self-inflicted pain for the path itself — pulling out one's own hair, scorching the body with fire, and similar practices undertaken in the belief that suffering now purchases constant pleasure later and thereby liberation. The *Exegesis on the Meaning of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* and the earlier *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi*'s account of the Nirgrantha ascetics (who held that all present suffering is the ripening of past evil karma, and that ascetic practice exhausts it) both illuminate the specific error the treatise names here: not clinging to any doctrine that pleasant, painful, or neutral karma straightforwardly yields a matching pleasant, painful, or neutral result — a view the Buddha explicitly rejected, since a future result is determined by the karma itself and not simply mirrored from the felt quality of its cause — but distorted view alone, a plain failure to see how karmic result actually works. The Bodhisattva Ground's fuller account of the four kinds of karmic feeling clarifies the stakes: wholesome or unwholesome karma performed with joy or with sorrow can each ripen, in a later life, into either pleasant or painful result, with no fixed correspondence between the felt tone of the deed and the felt tone of its fruit; failing to see this, the mistaken ascetic keeps inflicting suffering on the belief it secures a happy result, endlessly renewing the very pain he hopes to end, with never any satisfaction — the treatise's own vivid case of how a merely distorted view of the mind's activity, left uncorrected, becomes a self-perpetuating source of anguish.

41. Section: Picking Flaws in the Wholesome, Ruin Through Heedlessness, and Attachment to Form — Closing Defiled Activity, and the Mind's Defiled Characteristic, Entirely

Or, when settled within the wholesome, one then abandons it, seeking out some flaw, so as to let the unwholesome become manifest and active; amid that flaw, in both its waning and its flourishing, one is injured and vexed by craving and hatred. Further, following the force of heedlessness, every root of wholesome activity one has performed is thereby caused to be damaged and ruined. Further, because of intense delight in and attachment to form and the rest as objects, though one reflects again and again within the realm of the deathless, of the utmost benefit, it remains difficult to establish oneself firmly there. Within this meaning, the similes set forth to display what is but a temporary aggregation should be understood accordingly.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, when settled within the wholesome, one then abandons it, seeking out some flaw, so as to let the unwholesome become manifest and active; amid that flaw, in both its waning and its flourishing, one is injured and vexed by craving and hatred.
Further, following the force of heedlessness, every root of wholesome activity one has performed is thereby caused to be damaged and ruined.
Further, because of intense delight in and attachment to form and the rest as objects, though one reflects again and again within the realm of the deathless, of the utmost benefit, it remains difficult to establish oneself firmly there.
Within this meaning, the similes set forth to display what is but a temporary aggregation should be understood accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three further ways defiled activity manifests, completing the account of "activity occurring in this way." Second, picking flaws in the wholesome: the mind, even while genuinely settled in wholesome practice, cannot bear sustained effort and looks for an excuse to rest — and in that gap, where wholesome activity has waned, the unwholesome slips in and craving or hatred take hold; a donor who scrutinizes a recipient's worthiness until no suitable field of merit can be found, and so withholds the gift after all, exemplifies exactly this pattern. Third, ruin through heedlessness: indulging unreflectively in the five sensory pleasures without seeing their danger or seeking a way out, a mind steeped in greed, hatred, and delusion fails to press forward in cutting off evil and generating good, and thereby damages the very roots of merit and wisdom painstakingly built up before. Fourth, attachment to form and the rest: even one who fully recognizes nirvāṇa's supreme benefit and returns to contemplate it again and again finds correct mindfulness will not stay established, so long as craving for the five sense-objects remains this strong — the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi*'s own account of the mind consorting-body says such a mind, unable to abide in freedom from craving nor find any delight there, only rushes back, again and again, into its accustomed craving. The old teaching that practice means making "the familiar unfamiliar, and the unfamiliar familiar" names exactly the remedy: what is habitually unwholesome must be worn thin through disuse, and what is habitually unfamiliar — wholesome practice — must be worn smooth through repetition. The Buddha's own similes close this account of the mind's defiled characteristic: form is like foam gathered on water, feeling like a bubble, perception like a mirage, volitional formations like the hollow trunk of a plantain tree, and consciousness like a magician's illusion — each teaching that the five aggregates, however vividly they appear, are merely a temporary aggregation of conditions, without any real substance behind the appearance. With this, defiled activity is fully explained, closing the mind's defiled characteristic — arising, turning, and activity together — entirely. The treatise turns next to the fourth and final non-wisdom: the mind's pure characteristic.

42. Section: The Mind's Pure Characteristic Opens — Three Marks: What Cannot Be Apprehended, the Unconditioned, and Lineage

Through three kinds of mark, know the mind's pure characteristic. First, because of the mark of what cannot be apprehended; second, because of the mark of the unconditioned; and third, because of the mark of lineage.

Chinese Source

由三種相，應知心清淨相。一者、不得相故；二者、無為相故；三者、種性相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three kinds of mark, know the mind's pure characteristic. First, because of the mark of what cannot be apprehended; second, because of the mark of the unconditioned; and third, because of the mark of lineage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained the mind's common, own, and defiled characteristics, the treatise arrives at the fourth and final non-wisdom to be counteracted: not knowing the mind's pure characteristic. Three marks are named, each to be unfolded individually in what follows. The mark of what cannot be apprehended points to seeing true suchness itself, where verbal own-nature is nowhere to be found. The mark of the unconditioned is explained through nine further marks distinguishing the unconditioned from every conditioned dharma. And the mark of lineage concerns the capacity — spoken of in four stages and nine marks — that makes purification and the fruit of no-more-learning possible at all. Together these three marks give the complete picture of what it means for the mind to be genuinely purified, the positive counterpart to everything said above about its defilement.

43. Section: What Cannot Be Apprehended — Seeing True Suchness by Failing to See Any Verbal Own-Nature

If, through some other and different practice, one diligently cultivates as is correct, seeking purity of mind, it cannot be attained; if, through cultivating diligently and correctly in just this way, one is then able to attain it. And it is that one does not observe any verbal own-nature, and thereby sees the mark of true suchness.

Chinese Source

若由別異如理勤修，求心清淨不能證得。若由如是如理勤修，便能證得。又不觀見言說自性，見真如相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, through some other and different practice, one diligently cultivates as is correct, seeking purity of mind, it cannot be attained; if, through cultivating diligently and correctly in just this way, one is then able to attain it.
And it is that one does not observe any verbal own-nature, and thereby sees the mark of true suchness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The mind's purity cannot be reached by any diligent practice whatsoever — only by the one practice genuinely suited to it, namely cultivating the true suchness of dharma-emptiness "in just this way." This directly recalls the treatise's earlier account, back at the third of the thirteen kinds of middle-way conduct, of piercing through the emptiness of dharmas: what cannot be apprehended there is the imagined verbal own-nature ordinary beings project onto impermanence itself, whether internal, external, or between; failing to observe any such fixed own-nature in what is merely spoken of conventionally is not a deficiency but precisely how true suchness — the mark of the unconditioned, free of the imagined nature laid over the dependent — comes to be genuinely seen. Purity, in other words, is reached not by adding some special attainment to the mind, but by the mind's coming, through correct practice, not to find what was never really there to begin with.

44. Section: The Unconditioned Mark — Nine Ways the Purified Mind Transcends Every Conditioned Dharma

By nine kinds of mark, the unconditioned mark should be observed. First, because it does not proceed through time. Second, because — unlike the state within the attainment of cessation — its verbal own-nature cannot be found, yet the mark of true suchness can be found, so it is a mark of neither-of-the-two. Third, because it is not a mark of a born body. Fourth, because it transcends the own-nature that is the cause of a born body. Fifth, because it transcends future birth. Sixth, because it transcends death. Seventh, because it transcends the succession of instant upon instant without departure. Eighth, because it transcends the changing course of the destinies. Ninth, because it transcends the activity of karma and affliction.

Chinese Source

此由九種相，當觀無為相。一者、不行世故。二者、非如在滅盡定，言說自性不可得故，真如相可得故，是無二相。三者、非生身相故。四者、超過生身因自性相故。五者、超過當來生故。六者、超過死沒故。七者、超過剎那展轉不遠離故。八者、超過趣轉易故。九者、超過業煩惱行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

By nine kinds of mark, the unconditioned mark should be observed.
First, because it does not proceed through time.
Second, because — unlike the state within the attainment of cessation — its verbal own-nature cannot be found, yet the mark of true suchness can be found, so it is a mark of neither-of-the-two.
Third, because it is not a mark of a born body.
Fourth, because it transcends the own-nature that is the cause of a born body.
Fifth, because it transcends future birth.
Sixth, because it transcends death.
Seventh, because it transcends the succession of instant upon instant without departure.
Eighth, because it transcends the changing course of the destinies.
Ninth, because it transcends the activity of karma and affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Nine marks distinguish the unconditioned from every conditioned dharma. First, conditioned dharmas alone bear the three-fold distinction of past, present, and future — a distinction posited only relative to a dharma's arising, presence, and cessation — while the unconditioned, having no arising or ceasing, does not proceed through time at all. Second, this mark should not be confused with the attainment of cessation, where the six unstable consciousnesses (and part of the seventh) simply cease and "cessation of feeling-and-perception" is provisionally posited on that basis; true suchness is subtler than mere absence — its verbal own-nature cannot be found, so it is not simply "existent," yet the mark of true suchness genuinely can be found, so it is not simply "non-existent" either — a mark of neither-of-the-two beyond both being and non-being, where thought and language alike fall silent, as the *Establishment of Consciousness-Only* teaches at length regarding how every kind of unconditioned dharma is only provisionally posited relative to conditioned ones, never independently, substantially real apart from mind. The remaining seven marks trace how thoroughly the unconditioned stands outside the entire causal chain of rebirth: it bears no mark of a born retribution-body (three), causes none of the affliction and karma that would produce one (four), draws forth no future birth (five), knows no death (six, since it is joined with emptiness, in which there is no aging-and-death), exceeds the instant-by-instant continuity characteristic of every conditioned dharma (seven), exceeds the changing course through the five destinies (eight), and exceeds the very activity of karma and affliction altogether (nine) — nine ways, taken together, of showing exactly how far a genuinely purified mind stands outside the wheel of birth-and-death just described at such length under the mind's defiled characteristic.

45. Section: Five Things Transcended in This Very Life, and Six Things Transcended in Lives to Come — Closing the Unlearner's Realm Entirely

First, transcending what is to be done; second, transcending what is not to be done; third, transcending application to what is to be done; fourth, transcending non-application to what is to be done; and fifth, transcending application to what is not to be done. In lives to come, six things are transcended. First, transcending the activity able to give rise to future existence; second, transcending that activity itself; third, transcending the birth of its fruit; fourth, transcending the flourishing and decline dependent on that; fifth, transcending, within all the wavering, karmically-neutral states dependent on it, the cultivation, aspiration, and enjoyment thereof; and sixth, transcending the very distinction of embodiment dependent on that.

Chinese Source

於現法中，超過五事。一者、超過所作；二者、超過非所作；三者、超過所作加行；四者、超過所作非加行；五者、超過非所作加行。於後法中，超過六事。一者、超過能發起後有行；二者、超過彼行；三者、超過彼果生；四者、超過依彼衰盛；五者、超過於彼所依一切無記動搖中，修學期願受用；六者、超過彼所依自體差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this very life, five things are transcended. First, transcending what is to be done; second, transcending what is not to be done; third, transcending application to what is to be done; fourth, transcending non-application to what is to be done; and fifth, transcending application to what is not to be done.
In lives to come, six things are transcended. First, transcending the activity able to give rise to future existence; second, transcending that activity itself; third, transcending the birth of its fruit; fourth, transcending the flourishing and decline dependent on that; fifth, transcending, within all the wavering, karmically-neutral states dependent on it, the cultivation, aspiration, and enjoyment thereof; and sixth, transcending the very distinction of embodiment dependent on that.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The mark of lineage, the third pure mark, is here explained as the unlearner's realm — the causal condition for the fruit of no-more-learning — through two sets of transcendences, one for this present life and one for lives to come. In this life, an arhat: has finished what needed doing (precepts, concentration, wisdom, the foundations of mindfulness, the factors of awakening, all brought to completion, like a meal already cooked needing no further cooking); no longer touches what should never be done (the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* names five specific things an arhat with outflows forever exhausted simply cannot do — deliberately take life, take what is not given, engage in sexual misconduct, knowingly speak falsehood, or hoard provisions for the morrow); no longer strains with effort at what is already accomplished; yet does not lapse into idleness either, continuing to observe body, feeling, mind, and dharmas as selfless even without the earlier grasping urgency; and never deliberately applies effort toward the unwholesome or the neutral. In lives to come, an arhat transcends: the ignorance that gives rise to karmic formations productive of further existence, having uprooted self-grasping entirely; the meritorious, demeritorious, and unmoving karma that ignorance would otherwise produce; the arising of consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-bases, contact, and feeling that would constitute a new birth; the rise and fall of clan, appearance, strength, lifespan, and fortune that a new birth would bring; the mistaken aspiration — held by certain non-Buddhist ascetics who take the attainment of the concentration without perception, or of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, for nirvāṇa itself — to cultivate toward and enjoy rebirth in such a heaven, not realizing its support still wavers and obstructs the noble path; and the very distinction of embodiment across the desire, form, and formless realms, since no future rebirth into any of the three realms remains at all. With this, the unlearner's realm — the first of the two sub-marks of lineage — is fully explained, closing it entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second account of the lineage-mark maps it onto the five paths of practice through four stages carrying nine marks between them. The impure stage, corresponding to the stage of accumulation, has one mark: a mind not yet purified runs outward toward every object, following after form, sound, smell, taste, touch, greed, hatred, delusion, and the notions of male and female alike, exactly as empty space takes on the shape of whatever clouds happen to pass through it — scattered and unsettled by whatever characteristic currently presents itself. The purified stage, corresponding to the stage of application, has two marks: even flavor, meaning the mind has attained concentration and now continues spontaneously, evenly, without the scattering of the impure stage; and seclusion of body and mind — bodily seclusion meaning one no longer dwells intermixed with householders or renunciants of differing aspiration, "alone, without companion" even amid a crowded monastery where everyone is quietly absorbed in their own practice, and mental seclusion meaning the mind has become free of every defiled or neutral attention. Both marks reflect real, if not yet final, subjugation of the middling grade of affliction discussed earlier under worldly wisdom. The treatise turns next to the stage of penetration and the ultimate stage, where lineage finally comes to fruition.

47. Section: Penetration and the Ultimate Stage — Transcending Even the View of Self, and the Three Kinds of Change Nirvana Forever Transcends — Closing the Mind's Pure Characteristic, and the Eighth Gateway, Entirely

Chinese Source

The ultimate stage is: happiness brought to its fullness, and the transcending of the three kinds of change.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The stage of penetration, corresponding to the path of seeing (and, in its ongoing advance, the path of cultivation), has one mark: having entered the noble path and cut off the conceptually-constructed afflictions of craving and view, a practitioner is now freed from the three realms' bondage and stain, transcending the false view of self in the five aggregates together with every evil view rooted in it — false view of self, attachment to wrong precepts, holding an inferior view as supreme, extremist views, and wrong view generally — and thereby conforms and advances, stage by stage, toward the ultimate. The ultimate stage, corresponding to the fruit of no-more-learning, has four marks: happiness brought to its fullness, since nirvāṇa is itself great bliss now perfectly attained, together with transcending three kinds of change that would otherwise still threaten it. First, the change of aging and death — an unlearner has no future rebirth and so no future aging or death to come, though the residual body of this present life still ages within the nirvāṇa-with-residue. Second, the change of the ground of inverted views — the four inversions regarding permanence, pleasure, self, and purity, discussed at length above as the mind's very defilement, no longer arise at all. And third, the change of falling away from purity — because both the conceptually-constructed and the innate afflictions of craving and view have been utterly and finally cut off, the noble path attained can never regress, not even by a single stage back toward the fruits still short of arhatship. With this, the mark of lineage is fully explained through both its accounts — the unlearner's realm, and the four stages with nine marks — closing the mark of lineage, and with it the entire mind's pure characteristic (what cannot be apprehended, the unconditioned, and lineage together), entirely. This in turn closes the four kinds of non-wisdom to be counteracted, closes supramundane wisdom, and closes the eighth gateway of teaching — worldly and supramundane wisdom performing the work of benefiting others — in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the ninth gateway: what, within the Bodhisattva's own teaching, a śrāvaka learns.

48. Section: The Ninth Gateway Opens — What a Śrāvaka Learns Within the Bodhisattva's Own Teaching, Cast as the Three Trainings

It is that, because of the discipline of craving and of anxious sorrow, this is application to the training in higher discipline; because of the reflective attention of revulsion, this is application to the training in higher mind; and because of the selflessness of the person, or because of the selflessness of dharmas, this is application to the training in higher wisdom. Within this, craving and anxious sorrow are what give rise to every transgression.

Chinese Source

復次，云何於菩薩教授中聲聞所學？謂諸貪、憂毗奈耶故，是增上戒學加行；厭患作意故，是增上心學加行；補特伽羅無我性故，或法無我性故，是增上慧學加行。此中貪、憂，是能發起所有毀犯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, what, within the Bodhisattva's own teaching, does a śrāvaka learn? It is that, because of the discipline of craving and of anxious sorrow, this is application to the training in higher discipline; because of the reflective attention of revulsion, this is application to the training in higher mind; and because of the selflessness of the person, or because of the selflessness of dharmas, this is application to the training in higher wisdom.
Within this, craving and anxious sorrow are what give rise to every transgression.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The ninth gateway asks what a śrāvaka, specifically as taught within the great vehicle's own bodhisattva-teaching, actually studies — and answers by mapping that study onto the three trainings. Craving is desire for pleasant objects, whether presently enjoyed or anxiously hoped for; anxious sorrow is the grief of desire thwarted, or the fear of losing what has been gained; disciplining both through precepts that restrain bodily and verbal conduct is the training in higher discipline. Cultivating a reflective attention that sees the objects of desire as genuinely repugnant — not merely abstractly undesirable but felt as such — is what makes concentration possible, and so constitutes the training in higher mind. And cultivating the wisdom that sees the selflessness of the person — with especially sharp-facultied śrāvakas, per the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi*'s twin readings, also cultivating the emptiness of dharmas, or, on a second reading, "selflessness of dharmas" simply naming the selflessness of what belongs to the person — constitutes the training in higher wisdom. Because craving and anxious sorrow, taken together, are the root cause of every transgression a practitioner commits, the discussion that follows takes up in detail exactly how a practitioner comes to be free, or fails to come free, of these two roots.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having named craving and anxious sorrow as the root of transgression, the treatise now traces the mind's whole relationship to affliction — craving, self-view, and hatred alike — through four stages, from first arising to final removal. The first, not presently discarding, names the moment affliction first stirs: encountering an agreeable or disagreeable object, or coming under a companion's influence through bad company and habituated indulgence, false conceptual construction simply arises, with incorrect reflective attention present but no counteraction yet attempted at all. The second, having not discarded, names affliction already settled in and left unaddressed for some time: whether triggered by an especially compelling object or by another's genuinely harmful conduct, the practitioner is carried off and seized by it, the affliction now established rather than merely arising. Both stages describe a mind still governed by incorrect attention; the two stages of active removal — presently discarding, through correct reflection, and having discarded, through the discipline of precepts — are still to come.

50. Section: One Undischarged, All Undischarged — One Discharged, All Discharged — Closing the Fourfold Discipline of the Mind Entirely

Because of not discharging even a single one, know that not even a single one is discharged. Because of discharging even a single one, know that even a single one is indeed discharged.

Chinese Source

由隨一不除遣故，當知隨一亦不除遣。由隨一除遣故，當知隨一亦復除遣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of not discharging even a single one, know that not even a single one is discharged.
Because of discharging even a single one, know that even a single one is indeed discharged.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third and fourth marks complete the fourfold account of a mind's relation to craving, self-view, and hatred by naming their mutual entanglement: because every dharma conditions every other, leaving even one of the three afflictions unaddressed allows the others to keep arising in its train, so failing to discard any single one effectively means none are truly discarded; conversely, once genuine counteraction is undertaken and one affliction is actually removed, the interlinked others loosen along with it — without craving, hatred too finds no further foothold. With this, the fourfold discipline of the mind's own affliction — not presently discarding, having not discarded, presently discarding, and having discarded — is fully explained, closing this account entirely. The treatise turns next to naming the specific faults that follow when this discipline is neglected.

51. Section: The Faults That Follow Neglect — Undermining Discipline, Concentration, and the Wisdom of Both Selflessnesses

Further, if not discarded, then, even while abiding within monastic rule, this already amounts, with respect to higher discipline, to transgression — how much more so for one abiding outside monastic rule altogether. Further, higher mind, with respect to its object, becomes scattered and erroneous, which is itself an obstruction. For one cultivating higher wisdom in dependence on the selflessness of the person, the false view of self is the obstruction. For one cultivating higher wisdom in dependence on the selflessness of dharmas, the bondage of conceptual construction regarding own-nature and regarding distinctions is the obstruction.

Chinese Source

又若不除遣，雖住律儀，於增上戒尚名毀犯，何況安住不律儀者。又增上心學，於所緣境散亂錯誤，是能障礙。依補特伽羅無我修增上慧者，薩迦耶見是能障礙。依法無我修增上慧者，自性、差別分別計縛是能障礙。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, if not discarded, then, even while abiding within monastic rule, this already amounts, with respect to higher discipline, to transgression — how much more so for one abiding outside monastic rule altogether.
Further, higher mind, with respect to its object, becomes scattered and erroneous, which is itself an obstruction.
For one cultivating higher wisdom in dependence on the selflessness of the person, the false view of self is the obstruction.
For one cultivating higher wisdom in dependence on the selflessness of dharmas, the bondage of conceptual construction regarding own-nature and regarding distinctions is the obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Each of the three trainings has its own characteristic fault when the discipline just described is neglected. For discipline: purity was never really a matter of outward monastic form alone — a practitioner who outwardly keeps the precepts, yet inwardly harbors undischarged affliction, has already transgressed against the higher training, and one who neither keeps the outward form nor addresses the inward affliction transgresses all the more obviously. For concentration: a mind still carrying craving, sorrow, or hatred meets the objects of the five senses in a scattered, error-prone way, which directly obstructs the settling concentration requires. For wisdom, in its two modes: cultivating the selflessness of the person is obstructed specifically by the false view of self, since that view is precisely what the cultivation is meant to dissolve; and cultivating the selflessness of dharmas is obstructed specifically by conceptual construction and its resulting bondage — clinging to a fixed own-nature in verbal designations, and to the many distinctions drawn among them — since exactly this construction is what dharma-emptiness cuts through. Each training, in other words, fails for its own distinct reason when the underlying affliction is left unaddressed.

52. Section: Eight Ways to Go Astray From the Three Trainings — Wasted Indulgence Spiraling Into an Affliction Too Entrenched to Cure — Closing the Ninth Gateway Entirely

Within correctly cultivating and discharging these three trainings, there are eight dharmas that go against the training, and eight dharmas that conform to the training. What are the eight? First, wasted indulgence; second, because of indulgence, bondage; third, because of bondage, obstruction; fourth, because of obstruction, defilement; fifth, because of defilement, calamity like hail; sixth, because of that hail, sores and blisters; seventh, because of sores and blisters, feverish distress; and eighth, because of feverish distress, the disease of affliction becomes difficult to cure. The reverse of this, know, is precisely the eight dharmas that conform to the training.

Chinese Source

於此三學正修遣中，有八種違逆學法，有八種隨順學法。何等為八？一者、唐捐耽著；二者、耽著故縛；三者、縛故障礙；四者、障礙故垢；五者、垢故災雹；六者、雹故瘡皰；七者、瘡皰故熱惱；八者、熱惱故諸煩惱病難可療治。與此相違，當知即是八種隨順學法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within correctly cultivating and discharging these three trainings, there are eight dharmas that go against the training, and eight dharmas that conform to the training. What are the eight? First, wasted indulgence; second, because of indulgence, bondage; third, because of bondage, obstruction; fourth, because of obstruction, defilement; fifth, because of defilement, calamity like hail; sixth, because of that hail, sores and blisters; seventh, because of sores and blisters, feverish distress; and eighth, because of feverish distress, the disease of affliction becomes difficult to cure.
The reverse of this, know, is precisely the eight dharmas that conform to the training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A chain of eight causally-linked faults traces how indulging the five sensory pleasures snowballs, unaddressed, into an affliction that finally resists any cure at all — the *Compendium of Determinations* elsewhere reduces the same teaching to five faults and five remedies, but this account keeps the full eightfold escalation. First, wasted indulgence: time spent enjoying present, agreeable objects is simply squandered, contributing nothing to the path. Second, indulgence becomes bondage: the practitioner, per the *Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi*, no longer acts freely according to wholesome intention but is bound by the pull of what is enjoyed. Third, bondage becomes obstruction: mind fixed on sensory objects now actively obstructs cultivation — the *Śrāvaka Ground* names three such obstructions, to application, to withdrawal, and to quietude. Fourth, obstruction becomes defilement: the three trainings themselves acquire a stain, one of the seven kinds of defilement (desire, view, doubt, conceit, arrogance, latent tendency, and stinginess) named in the *Determinations on the Bodhisattva Ground*. Fifth, defilement becomes calamity like hail: just as sudden hail destroys young seedlings, this stain damages the very roots of discipline, concentration, and wisdom already planted. Sixth, that calamity becomes sores and blisters: damaged roots of the three trainings erupt into impure bodily, verbal, and mental karma, like a body breaking out in infected wounds. Seventh, the sores become feverish distress: transgression, or cultivation gone astray into wrong concentration and wrong wisdom, brings grief and anguish of mind, craving intensifying round upon round. And eighth, that distress renders the resulting affliction so deeply entrenched it becomes genuinely difficult to cure — repeated error compounding until the disease resists treatment altogether. Reversed, these same eight links become the eight dharmas that conform to and support the three trainings; the whole chain exists chiefly to warn a renunciant against any indulgence in the five sensory pleasures whatsoever. With this, the discipline that discharges craving, self-view, and hatred, the faults of neglecting it, and the eight ways of going astray or conforming are all fully explained, closing the ninth gateway — what a śrāvaka learns within the Bodhisattva's own teaching — entirely. The treatise turns next to the tenth gateway, distinguishing the śramaṇa who learns poorly from the śramaṇa who learns well.

53. Section: The Tenth Gateway Opens — Three Kinds of Śramaṇa Who Learn Poorly

Furthermore, what is a śramaṇa who learns poorly? Know that there are three kinds. First, the disregarding śramaṇa; second, the śramaṇa alike only in outward form; and third, the śramaṇa unalike in conduct, in right livelihood, in enjoyment, in application, in discipline and view, and in disposition altogether.

Chinese Source

復次，云何不善學沙門？謂三種應知。一者、不顧沙門；二者、形相同分；三者、軌則、正命、受用、加行、戒見、意樂皆不同分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, what is a śramaṇa who learns poorly? Know that there are three kinds.
First, the disregarding śramaṇa; second, the śramaṇa alike only in outward form; and third, the śramaṇa unalike in conduct, in right livelihood, in enjoyment, in application, in discipline and view, and in disposition altogether.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A śramaṇa, in the *Madhyama-āgama*'s own definition, is one who brings every evil and unwholesome dharma, every outflow and defilement, and the causes of future rebirth's affliction and suffering to rest — yet not every renunciant genuinely learns this well. Three kinds of poor learner are named. The disregarding śramaṇa has, per the *Determinations on the Ground of Concentrated and Unconcentrated States*, already abandoned the precepts and taken readily to backsliding, or transgressed morality and practiced evil, inwardly rotten though still calling himself, falsely, one of pure conduct — still wearing the robes and claiming the title while having genuinely forfeited both. The śramaṇa alike only in outward form appears, by contrast, to practice the three trainings quite convincingly, yet the underlying disposition is wrong: not genuinely seeking liberation or nirvāṇa as a śrāvaka should, nor genuinely seeking unsurpassed awakening as a bodhisattva should, but privately seeking material support or reputation instead — indistinguishable from a real practitioner to outward appearance, yet lacking any actual practice within. And the third kind, unalike in every respect — conduct, livelihood (defined in the *Śrāvaka Ground* as rightly seeking robes and food, free of every wrong means of livelihood), enjoyment of requisites, application to the three trainings, discipline and view, and inward disposition alike — resembles what elsewhere is called a heretic disguised within the Buddhist order: unlike the first kind, who erred after ordaining sincerely, this one entered with no real intention of practicing anything the robes represent.

54. Section: Alike in Outward Form Alone — Seeking Requisites, and Seeking Renown — Closing the Poor Learner Entirely

If one is oriented toward requisites, this is alike in outward form to higher discipline, and alike in outward form to higher mind and higher wisdom, yet unalike in the disposition of that conduct. If one is oriented toward renown, this is alike in the branch of calming, alike in the branch of insight, alike in the branch of their joint cultivation, alike in the branch of their joint requisite accumulation, yet unalike in disposition.

Chinese Source

若迴向資具，是增上戒形相同分，是增上心及增上慧形相同分，是彼行意樂不同分。若迴向聲譽，是奢摩他支同分，是毗鉢舍那支同分，是俱修支同分，是俱資糧支同分，是意樂不同分。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one is oriented toward requisites, this is alike in outward form to higher discipline, and alike in outward form to higher mind and higher wisdom, yet unalike in the disposition of that conduct.
If one is oriented toward renown, this is alike in the branch of calming, alike in the branch of insight, alike in the branch of their joint cultivation, alike in the branch of their joint requisite accumulation, yet unalike in disposition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Two specific motivations illustrate the second kind, "alike in outward form alone." One oriented toward requisites practices all three trainings convincingly enough in outward form — the postures, the precepts, the meditative bearing — but is really after better material support, matching the *Great Treasures Sūtra*'s own "śramaṇa in outward garb," who dresses and shaves like a monk while harboring impure bodily, verbal, and mental karma, stingy, jealous, indolent, and morally lax beneath the robe. One oriented toward renown appears, more subtly still, alike in calming, alike in insight, alike in their joint cultivation, and alike in accumulating the requisites for both — outwardly dignified, deliberately unhurried, refined in speech, seemingly indifferent to worldly assembly — matching the *Great Treasures Sūtra*'s "deceptive-deportment śramaṇa," who cultivates every outward mark of practice not from renunciation or genuine aspiration to the path but purely to be seen doing so, even regarding a monk who genuinely teaches emptiness with suspicion, as though facing an enemy or looking into an abyss. A third kind named there, the "śramaṇa of renown" proper, seeks recognition even more directly — keeping precepts to be known as disciplined, studying scripture to be known as learned, dwelling alone to be known as a forest-ascetic, wanting only to be seen as content and secluded, never for the sake of renunciation, quietude, the path, or nirvāṇa at all. With this, the poor learner is fully explained through all three kinds, closing this half of the tenth gateway entirely. The treatise turns next to the śramaṇa who learns well.

55. Section: The Śramaṇa Who Learns Well — Application Perfected, and Disposition Perfected Through Not Clinging to What Is Attained

In this present life, through the application of revulsion; and in a former life, through the application of continuous ripening — know that application is thereby perfected. Through the resolute disposition of the selflessness of dharmas; because, with respect to both what is to be attained and what is capable of attaining it, one has no attachment to the verbal own-nature of either.

Chinese Source

於現法中，由厭患加行故；於前生中，由相續成熟加行故；當知加行圓滿。由法無我勝解意樂故；若所應得、若能應得，於此二言說自性無執著故；於意趣義正尋求故；不但隨順言辭故；當知意樂圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this present life, through the application of revulsion; and in a former life, through the application of continuous ripening — know that application is thereby perfected.
Through the resolute disposition of the selflessness of dharmas; because, with respect to both what is to be attained and what is capable of attaining it, one has no attachment to the verbal own-nature of either; because one correctly seeks the intended meaning; and because one does not merely follow the wording — know that disposition is thereby perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having named the four marks of a good learner — application, disposition, penetration, and progress toward the ultimate — the treatise unfolds each in turn, beginning with the first two. Application is perfected two ways at once: diligent hearing and reflection in this very life cultivates revulsion toward the three realms' faults, while good roots already ripened continuously from a past life of similar renunciation lend that present effort real momentum — present diligence and past cultivation reinforcing each other. Disposition is perfected through the resolute understanding proper to dharma-emptiness: great awakening as what is to be attained (the fruit) and the factors of awakening as what is capable of attaining it (the path) are both held without clinging to any fixed verbal own-nature in either — one seeks the intended meaning behind sixteen traditional modes of scriptural intent rather than resting content with the words themselves, since relying on meaning rather than mere language, and genuinely practicing rather than merely thinking about emptiness once it has been described, is what it means for aspiration toward the teaching to be genuinely, not superficially, complete.

56. Section: Penetration and Progress Toward the Ultimate — Realizing True Suchness Without an Outside Teacher, and Subduing Every Distorted Mark — Closing the Good Learner's Four Marks Entirely

Because one has already well cultivated the counteragent to every defilement; and because, with respect to true suchness, one has no interruption or ruin, and is able to subdue every distorted mark — know that progress toward the ultimate is thereby perfected.

Chinese Source

若於法真如，以不緣他智通達自性無雜染故；於世俗寶，及世俗生死、涅槃、解脫、繫縛自性無所得故；當知通達圓滿。已善修習一切雜染對治故，又於真如無斷壞故，及能勝伏故，當知趣究竟圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, with respect to the true suchness of dharmas, through wisdom not dependent on another, one penetrates its own-nature as free of defilement; and, with respect to the conventional treasure, and to conventional saṃsāra, nirvāṇa, liberation, and bondage, one has no attainment with respect to their own-nature — know that penetration is thereby perfected.
Because one has already well cultivated the counteragent to every defilement; and because, with respect to true suchness, one has no interruption or ruin, and is able to subdue every distorted mark — know that progress toward the ultimate is thereby perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Penetration is realized not through another's instruction but through wisdom awakened from within — directly seeing that the true suchness of dharmas, dharma-emptiness itself, is free of defiled own-nature — and, precisely because of this, no longer taking any attainment in the "Three Treasures" as spoken of in words, nor in the conventional teaching of saṃsāra, nirvāṇa, liberation, and bondage: these remain useful, provisional language, yet clinging to their verbal own-nature would itself be a subtle bondage, so a genuinely penetrating wisdom finds saṃsāra to be nirvāṇa, with neither bondage nor liberation to grasp as ultimately real. Progress toward the ultimate then names what continues after penetration: having already well cultivated the counteragent to defilement of affliction, of rebirth, and of karma alike, a practitioner attains an unbroken, unruined mastery of true suchness — free rein within it rather than mere fleeting access — and is thereby able to subdue every remaining mark of distorted, dualistic conceptual construction, an inconceivable accomplishment marking the perfection of non-discriminating wisdom. For a bodhisattva-śramaṇa this ultimate is buddhahood, where both obstructions are entirely removed; for a śrāvaka-śramaṇa it is arhatship, where the seeds of the three realms' afflicted craving and view are entirely exhausted. With this, all four marks of the śramaṇa who learns well — application, disposition, penetration, and progress toward the ultimate — are fully explained, closing this definition entirely. The treatise turns next to distinguishing this good learner more sharply against three further varieties of the poor learner.

57. Section: A Name Without the Reality — Three Ways Disposition and Application Fall Short of Each Other

Furthermore, the śramaṇa who learns poorly, by three kinds of mark, should be known as one whose name does not match its meaning. First, disposition impaired, application fulfilled; second, disposition fulfilled, application impaired; and third, both disposition and application impaired. Within this, disposition impaired, application fulfilled, is itself further of three kinds. First, being satisfied merely with being able to listen; second, being satisfied merely with being able to teach; and third, being satisfied merely with being able to attain worldly concentration and taking relish in it.

Chinese Source

復次，不善學沙門，由三種相，當知彼名不如其義。一者、意樂衰損，加行具足；二者、意樂具足，加行衰損；三者、意樂衰損、加行衰損。此中意樂衰損，加行具足，復有三種。一者、能聽唯此喜足；二者、能說唯此喜足；三者、能證世間三摩地而生愛味唯此喜足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the śramaṇa who learns poorly, by three kinds of mark, should be known as one whose name does not match its meaning. First, disposition impaired, application fulfilled; second, disposition fulfilled, application impaired; and third, both disposition and application impaired.
Within this, disposition impaired, application fulfilled, is itself further of three kinds. First, being satisfied merely with being able to listen; second, being satisfied merely with being able to teach; and third, being satisfied merely with being able to attain worldly concentration and taking relish in it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A second, sharper account of the poor learner returns to the vocabulary of application and disposition just used to define the good learner, showing exactly how a "śramaṇa" in name can fail to be one in substance. The three basic patterns: application intact but disposition impaired (outwardly capable, inwardly aimless); disposition intact but application impaired (rightly aspiring, yet not diligent); and both impaired at once (the counterfeit practitioner encountered earlier, with neither genuine aim nor genuine effort). The first pattern, application without disposition, is unpacked into three familiar failures: contentment with mere listening, never advancing to practice; contentment with being able to teach others, mistaking the ability to explain for the attainment explained; and contentment with worldly concentration itself, savoring its pleasant states without pressing on toward the uncontaminated wisdom of personal or dharma selflessness that alone constitutes real disposition — able to listen, able to teach, able to enter concentration, each a genuine application, yet none oriented toward nirvāṇa or awakening as its true aim.

58. Section: A Single Mark That Fulfills Both — Closing the Tenth Gateway, Poor and Good Learning Together, Entirely

If one is a śramaṇa who learns well, then, through a single mark alone, know that disposition is fulfilled and application is fulfilled.

Chinese Source

若善學沙門，唯由一相，當知意樂具足、加行具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one is a śramaṇa who learns well, then, through a single mark alone, know that disposition is fulfilled and application is fulfilled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the poor learner splinters into three mismatched patterns of disposition and application, the good learner needs only a single mark to fulfill both at once: taking nirvāṇa, or unsurpassed awakening, genuinely as the guiding aim — not merely being satisfied with hearing, teaching, or worldly concentration as ends in themselves — automatically brings disposition and application into alignment, since a rightly-aimed disposition naturally directs application toward its proper object, and diligent application in turn matures that disposition. With this, the tenth gateway — poor learning and good learning among śramaṇas, first defined through the good learner's own four marks and then sharpened by contrast with the poor learner's three — is fully explained, closing it entirely. The treatise turns next to the eleventh gateway: what it means to abide in conventional monastic discipline.

59. Section: The Eleventh Gateway Opens — Abiding in Conventional Discipline, and Its First Two Marks: the Lingering View of Self, and Concentration Not Yet Won

Furthermore, what is it to abide in conventional discipline? Know that there are four kinds of mark. It is that, though one has accomplished the six branches of moral discipline, one is yet harmed by two kinds of injury, injuring that discipline — namely, because of the entanglement of the false view of self, and because one does not understand transgression and its release. Though free of these two faults, one has not yet attained the pure worldly discipline, and so is unable to subdue the false view of self.

Chinese Source

復次，云何住世俗律儀？當知有四種相。謂雖成就六支尸羅，而為二種損害損害尸羅。謂由薩迦耶見纏故，及於毀犯出離不了知故。雖遠離此二種過失，而未得世間清淨律儀，不能制伏薩迦耶見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, what is it to abide in conventional discipline? Know that there are four kinds of mark.
It is that, though one has accomplished the six branches of moral discipline, one is yet harmed by two kinds of injury, injuring that discipline — namely, because of the entanglement of the false view of self, and because one does not understand transgression and its release.
Though free of these two faults, one has not yet attained the pure worldly discipline, and so is unable to subdue the false view of self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eleventh gateway takes up the practitioner who abides in discipline still conventional rather than ultimate — holding the precepts of individual liberation and concentration-bound discipline, but not yet the uncontaminated path-discipline of a noble one — traced through four progressively refined marks. The first mark: even a monastic who has genuinely accomplished the six branches of moral discipline (the *Discussion of the Stage of Thought-Made Wisdom* lists them as abiding in pure precepts, well guarding the vows of individual liberation, perfected deportment, perfected conduct, great dread even of minor faults, and continued training in the rules of discipline) can still have that very discipline injured in two ways: first, by the lingering entanglement of the false view of self, seen for instance in a monastic who keeps pure precepts yet inwardly thinks "I keep them, you do not" — self-view still actively operating even amid outwardly excellent conduct, marking the lowest grade of purity; and second, by simply not understanding transgression and the proper means of release from it — keeping the precepts scrupulously in form while lacking real skill in how to confess and restore purity once a fault occurs, remaining bound to the outward mark of discipline alone. The second mark: even once free of both these faults, a monastic who has not yet attained genuine meditative concentration still cannot subdue the false view of self at its root — no longer disturbing others outwardly, as in the first mark, but still stirring quietly, unaddressed, deep within, for lack of the settling power concentration alone can bring.

60. Section: Dread of Dharma-Selflessness, and Overweening Conceit — the Last Two Marks — Closing the Eleventh Gateway Entirely

Though one has already attained pure worldly discipline, and has already subdued the false view of self, yet, not having diminished the habituated dread of the selflessness of dharmas, one's discipline is thereby impaired. Though free of every other remaining fault, yet, through a mistaken resolute understanding of the selflessness of dharmas together with overweening conceit, one's discipline is thereby impaired.

Chinese Source

雖已得世間清淨律儀，已制伏薩迦耶見，而不損減串習法無我性怖畏，損壞尸羅。雖遠離一切所餘過失，而為邪法無我勝解及增上慢損壞尸羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Though one has already attained pure worldly discipline, and has already subdued the false view of self, yet, not having diminished the habituated dread of the selflessness of dharmas, one's discipline is thereby impaired.
Though free of every other remaining fault, yet, through a mistaken resolute understanding of the selflessness of dharmas together with overweening conceit, one's discipline is thereby impaired.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The last two marks show that even genuine attainment in discipline and concentration is not yet ultimate discipline. The third mark: a practitioner who has attained pure worldly discipline and genuinely subdued the manifest activity of self-view — its seed not yet cut off, but no longer stirring — may still recoil in dread on hearing the teaching of dharma-selflessness, alarmed that emptiness seems to leave nothing at all, and wondering what purpose pure discipline could then serve; this dread itself, the *Notes on the Discussion of the Yogācārabhūmi* explains, still impairs discipline, since it betrays an unresolved coarse grasping at self not yet released through genuine dharma-emptiness. The fourth mark: even a practitioner free of this dread can still fall into the opposite error, a mistaken, nihilistic grasp at emptiness that denies not just the imagined own-nature of ordinary conceptual construction but the dependent and perfectly-accomplished natures as well — this treatise's own earlier warning, in the account of piercing through distinction, against denying causality itself — compounded by overweening conceit, mistaking mere concentration attained for the fruit actually realized; both errors, though quite different in flavor, equally impair discipline by substituting a false understanding of emptiness for the genuine article. With this, all four marks of abiding in conventional discipline — the entangled view of self, concentration not yet won, dread of dharma-selflessness, and a mistaken grasp at emptiness compounded by conceit — are fully explained, closing the eleventh gateway entirely. The treatise turns next to the twelfth gateway: what it means to abide in ultimate-truth discipline.

61. Section: The Twelfth Gateway Opens — Ultimate Discipline as Freedom From Every Elaboration That Still Troubled the Four Conventional Marks

Furthermore, what is it to abide in ultimate discipline? It is that what one has accomplished is discipline dissociated from every affliction, of the supramundane, able to counteract the discipline of the three realms. Further, within the four kinds of abiding in discipline, dharmas of elaboration are presently to be found; if one is able to still those marks, know that this is called uncontaminated discipline. What is called the dharmas of elaboration?

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where conventional discipline, however genuinely accomplished, was shown across four marks to remain vulnerable — to the entangled view of self, to concentration not yet won, to dread of dharma-selflessness, and to a mistaken grasp at emptiness compounded by conceit — ultimate discipline is defined in a single stroke: discipline wholly dissociated from every affliction, supramundane, able to counteract the discipline of all three realms at its very root, an attainment only a fully accomplished arhat perfects. The treatise then reframes the whole discussion through a single unifying lens: each of the four conventional marks just explained is now shown to harbor its own "dharmas of elaboration" — habits of conceptual proliferation and dualistic mental chatter that persist even within outwardly correct discipline — and it is precisely the stilling of these elaborations, not any change in outward conduct, that distinguishes uncontaminated discipline from its conventional counterpart. What follows enumerates, mark by mark, exactly what these elaborations consist of.

62. Section: Ten Elaborations at the First Abiding — Self-Grasping, Transgression, and the Presence or Absence of Correct Knowing

It is that, within the first abiding in discipline, self-grasping can be found; or grasping at what belongs to self; or committing transgression; or not committing it; or deliberate, intentional application; or application that is not so intentional; or acting with correct knowing; or not so acting; or acting with lapsed mindfulness; or not so acting.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

It is that, within the first abiding in discipline, self-grasping can be found; or grasping at what belongs to self; or committing transgression; or not committing it; or deliberate, intentional application; or application that is not so intentional; or acting with correct knowing; or not so acting; or acting with lapsed mindfulness; or not so acting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first conventional abiding — impaired, recall, by the entangled view of self and by not understanding transgression and its release — is now shown to harbor ten specific elaborations, in five contrasting pairs. The first pair is the coarse self-view itself: grasping at "I" and grasping at "what belongs to me" as truly existent in the five aggregates. The remaining four pairs concern transgression and its avoidance: whether a fault is actually committed or not; whether wrongdoing is undertaken deliberately or arises without such intent; whether one acts with correct knowing, vigilantly guarding body and speech at every moment, or fails to; and whether an actual transgression occurs through lapsed mindfulness, or mindfulness holds and no transgression occurs. Every one of these ten remains a case of dualistic mental proliferation — the mind still busy classifying self and other, fault and non-fault, deliberate and accidental — even when the outward precepts are kept immaculately; it is exactly this proliferating activity, not any lapse in conduct, that must finally be stilled for discipline to become genuinely uncontaminated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second abiding corresponds to the second conventional mark — free of manifest self-view, yet without concentration to subdue it at the root. Its elaborations unfold in three movements. First, the latent seed itself persists: though self-view no longer disturbs others outwardly, its coarse latent tendency still clings to the five aggregates, "name" and "form" both still findable as seeds capable of producing a future embodiment — a mark not yet manifest, since the fruit has not yet ripened, yet already present as potential; even when entanglement appears momentarily stilled through the discipline of precepts alone, the unremoved seed means this stilling is not genuine, liable to stir again at any time. Second, a subtler grasping persists even in one who has heard and accepted the teaching of selflessness: not yet having genuinely penetrated personal selflessness, such a person may cling instead to "selflessness" itself as a fixed thing to be grasped — the very teaching meant to release grasping becoming a new object of grasping — distinguishing what is genuinely grasped as real from what is understood, through hearing and reflection, as merely conventionally designated and not to be grasped at all. Third, this residual grasping colors even reverence for the noble ones: admiring a stream-enterer or an arhat as genuinely, substantially existing "selves" who have transformed from ordinary being to sage is itself a subtle grasping at a sentient being; praising these attainments as conventionally real, and further distinguishing the many different grades of nobility (stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arhat, and the twenty-seven kinds of noble ones named in the Śrāvaka Ground) through mind and speech as though each grade possessed its own fixed, substantial reality, compounds one increase in superimposed reality upon another. So long as any of this remains — self-view's own root, or grasping at selflessness itself, or reverence for nobility taken as substantially real — genuine ultimate discipline has not yet been reached.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the third abiding in discipline: there is being born above, and so being of the world; there is discarding what is below, and so not being of the world; there is dependence on meditative absorption; there is dependence on desires; there is being conceited and elated over one's own discipline; and there is scorning and belittling another's discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third abiding corresponds to the third conventional mark, where genuine meditative concentration has now been attained, yet dread of dharma-selflessness remains — and its elaborations are correspondingly refined. First, a genuine paradox: concentration powerful enough to secure rebirth in the form or formless realms does carry one, by aspiration, beyond the desire realm — no longer "of this world" in that narrow sense — yet rebirth in any of the three realms, however exalted, still falls within saṃsāra and so remains "of the world" in the larger sense; self-grasping has not actually been uprooted, merely relocated to a higher plane. Second, a lingering unevenness in that very concentration: sometimes resting genuinely on meditative absorption, sometimes still colored by residual desire not fully purified. Third, a specifically moral pride: taking satisfaction and even conceit in the purity of one's own discipline while looking down on another's — the concentration attained lending false confidence to a comparison that discipline itself should have dissolved rather than fueled.

65. Section: Elaborations at the Fourth Abiding — Reckoning One's Own Discipline Pure — Closing All the Elaborations, and Stilling Ultimate Discipline, Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the fourth abiding in discipline: there is reckoning that one's own discipline is pure; and there is discriminating discipline through the conceptual construction of own-nature and of distinctions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth abiding, corresponding to the fourth conventional mark, harbors the subtlest elaboration of all: even the very thought "my discipline is pure" is itself a conceptual construction, discriminating discipline through an imagined own-nature and imagined distinctions rather than seeing it as it truly is, empty of any such fixed self-nature — the mistaken resolute understanding of dharma-selflessness and overweening conceit named earlier now traced to this specific residual habit of mind. With this, all the elaborations found across the four abidings in conventional discipline — ten at the first, thirteen at the second, six at the third, and two at the fourth — are fully explained, and the treatise confirms that every one of them is entirely stilled within genuinely uncontaminated discipline, closing this whole account of elaboration and its stilling entirely. The treatise turns next to how ultimate discipline furnishes the very accumulations of merit and wisdom the path requires.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ultimate discipline, once every elaboration is stilled, is shown to furnish both of the path's two great accumulations at once. The *Discussion of the Stage of Thought-Made Wisdom* records the very verse referenced here on the purified three trainings: "first, firmly abiding in the root; next, delighting in the mind's quiescence; finally, the noble seeing what is a wrong view and what is not" — first the conventional root-discipline of the six branches, then concentration's quiescence, and finally uncontaminated discipline in full accord with noble insight. Discipline and concentration together furnish the accumulation of merit, making the practitioner's residual affliction — manifest dust and latent stain alike — increasingly slight, drawing in good spiritual friends without any falling to ruin; wisdom furnishes the accumulation of wisdom, giving rise to genuine resolute understanding within the profound domain of dharma-emptiness. Two further causes then explain how a practitioner is admitted into the Buddha's own teaching at all: the wisdom that abides in dharmas, gained through correctly understanding the Buddha's own verbal teaching on dependent origination through hearing, reflection, and cultivation, which discerns the causal workings of affliction, karma, and suffering in depth; and true and real wisdom, the non-discriminating wisdom of a noble one who has directly realized selflessness, which decisively settles the truth of dependent origination beyond any further doubt. With this, ultimate discipline is fully explained — its counteraction of contaminated discipline, the stilling of every elaboration across the four abidings, and its furnishing of both accumulations together — closing the twelfth gateway entirely. The treatise turns next to the thirteenth gateway: the Tathāgata's own skillful means of taming beings.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The thirteenth gateway asks how the Tathāgata actually reaches beings — and answers with two shared portions. A shared portion of embodiment means the Buddha or a bodhisattva appears in the same realm, destiny, and kind of birth as the beings to be taught — appearing human among humans, exactly as a being to be liberated might expect, per the definition given in the *Determinations on the Ground of the Fivefold Body Together With Mind*, where "shared portion" names the mutual resemblance among beings born into the same realm, destiny, birth, or class, extending even to shared understanding of merit and fault, so that stream-enterers share a portion with stream-enterers, bodhisattvas with bodhisattvas, and buddhas with buddhas. A shared portion of resolute understanding means the teaching offered matches the level of wisdom the audience can actually receive, conveyed through language they can follow. Both serve one purpose: had the Buddha appeared too exalted or too different in form, beings would find the teaching intimidating or simply beyond them; had he appeared too far below their own understanding, they would find nothing worth respecting or following. Appearing genuinely alongside beings, sharing their embodiment and pitching the teaching at their own level of resolute understanding, is what first secures a receptive mind, and it is by relying on that same teaching, once received, that beings are then able to set out toward liberation from the three realms.

First, instruction regarding dread at the transformed dependence between defilement and purity; second, instruction on the causal conditions for freedom from defilement; third, instruction on the causal conditions for freedom from dread at purity; and fourth, instruction on the application to the foremost dwelling in present-life bliss.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the correctly pure application of guiding instruction should further be known as of four kinds.
First, instruction regarding dread at the transformed dependence between defilement and purity; second, instruction on the causal conditions for freedom from defilement; third, instruction on the causal conditions for freedom from dread at purity; and fourth, instruction on the application to the foremost dwelling in present-life bliss.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond merely appearing among beings, the Tathāgata offers four specific kinds of instruction addressing where practitioners actually get stuck. First: for beings governed by self-love and love of their own experience, unable to see saṃsāra's real danger, the prospect of transformed dependence — abandoning the defiled basis and gaining the pure — provokes genuine dread, a fear of falling into sheer annihilation; the Buddha teaches such beings the true nature of dependent origination and its transformed result, freeing them from that dread and thereby enabling realization of the qualities exclusive to a buddha. Second: to free beings from the very causal conditions of the three defilements — affliction, karma, and rebirth — the Buddha teaches that clinging to a fixed, distinct own-nature in conditioned formations is itself what produces them, and that once the verbal own-nature and distinctions projected onto formations are seen as ultimately unfindable, the imagined nature that generates defilement is cut off at the root. Third: to free beings specifically from dread of the purified path and of nirvāṇa itself, the Buddha teaches the true character of that transformed purity — that conditioned formations are impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, their danger vast, while renunciation of craving for them is peaceful and unsurpassed, bringing final security, from which the qualities exclusive to a buddha are likewise realized. And fourth, to lead beings toward the very foremost dwelling in present-life bliss — cessation itself, most excellent among every meditative attainment, as the *Notes* here clarify, though the term implicitly covers the eight lesser absorptions building toward it as well — the Buddha teaches the nine progressively deepening states of concentration in sequence. Each of these four addresses a distinct obstacle standing between a being and liberation, tailored precisely to where that being currently stands.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first two of the four kinds of guiding instruction are now unpacked in detail. The causal conditions of defilement trace back to a single root: because beings, communicating chiefly through language, do not see that the verbal own-nature of every conventionally-designated dharma is itself empty, they cling to what language merely designates as though it possessed real substance — a superimposed own-nature that is the very origin of the twin graspings at self and dharmas. Built upon this same root, a second conceptual construction then discriminates further, distinguishing "virtue" from "fault" among dharmas so construed, generating the eight kinds of superimposition already explained earlier under piercing through the extreme of superimposition (permanence, impermanence, self, selflessness, a real mind, an unreal mind, and their downstream wholesome or unwholesome, pure or defiled elaborations) — a chain that, left unaddressed, makes unsurpassed awakening impossible to attain. Dread at the path of purity, meanwhile, arises from two different failures of understanding: first, not correctly grasping the actual sequence by which the path of purity unfolds — discipline supporting concentration, concentration supporting wisdom — a practitioner who cultivates for a long while without visible result becoming anxious that the path yields nothing; and second, simply failing to recognize genuine danger in the defiled state currently occupied, finding present enjoyment of the five senses agreeable enough that discipline, concentration, and wisdom come to seem an unnecessary, even unwelcome, imposition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Dread specifically at nirvāṇa compounds the general dread at purity into something more acute. First, nirvāṇa's true nature — free of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, free of the six sense-bases, free of the five aggregates, free of ignorance and the ending of ignorance, beyond every conditioned and unconditioned dharma alike — is not something language can capture, yet an ordinary being, not knowing that language has no substance, clings to what "nirvāṇa" merely designates as though it named literal nothingness, and, gripped by a nihilistic reading of self-view, comes to dread nirvāṇa as sheer annihilation. Second, "nirvāṇa" is itself only a verbal designation superimposed on what, in ultimate truth, admits no such distinction at all; not knowing this, a being speculates endlessly within that mere designation — whether nirvāṇa is the same as or different from the five aggregates, whether anything at all continues after entering it — and the more such speculation proliferates, the more dread it produces. The remedy, and the fourth kind of instruction, is thoroughgoing knowledge of exactly this: fully understanding every notion that arises even within a quiescent, concentrated mind, and every merely-designated name applied to meditative attainments (the many named absorptions and forms of concentration catalogued elsewhere in the treatise), as themselves without fixed verbal own-nature or fixed distinction — precisely the same insight, now applied reflexively to concentration's own terminology. Only when both causal conditions of dread — grasping at defilement's own-nature, and dread at purity's own sequence and terminology — are together departed from does a practitioner become fit for the foremost dwelling in present-life bliss. With this, the Tathāgata's skillful means of taming beings — a shared portion of embodiment and understanding, and four kinds of guiding instruction now unpacked in full — is completely explained, closing the thirteenth gateway entirely. The treatise turns next to the fourteenth gateway: the Buddha's own words of hidden intent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourteenth gateway addresses words of "hidden intent" — teachings, chiefly given to śrāvakas, whose surface meaning conceals a deeper one the Buddha does not spell out directly, which a bodhisattva must learn to penetrate even while studying material nominally pitched at the lesser vehicle. Only one instrument suffices to unlock such language: the wisdom of the mark of non-duality, the middle way that clings neither to existence nor to non-existence. As the *Bodhisattva Ground* explains, what is genuinely non-dual departs from both existence and non-existence together, comprising only the true reality embraced by the character of dharmas — precisely this freedom from both extremes at once is called the middle way, transcending both, and for that reason also called unsurpassed. The chapters that follow will show, case by case, that whenever the Buddha's words seem to affirm or deny something outright, penetrating what is genuinely meant requires exactly this refusal to fall into either "it is" or "it is not."

72. Section: Non-Duality's First Meaning — the Dependent Nature's Own-Nature Neither Existent Through Naming Nor Non-Existent Apart From It

It is that, with respect to every basis upon which verbal designation rests, because its own-nature has no existence whatsoever, and because it is not the case that the own-nature of what the impression habituated by verbal designation ranges over exists — it is said to be non-dual. Within this non-duality, if the two graspings arise, this is called defilement; if there is no grasping at the two, this is called purity.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

It is that, with respect to every basis upon which verbal designation rests, because its own-nature has no existence whatsoever, and because it is not the case that the own-nature of what the impression habituated by verbal designation ranges over exists — it is said to be non-dual.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of two meanings of non-duality concerns the dependent nature specifically. Every dharma that language designates presupposes some dependently-arisen basis — a conditioned event — on which the designation rests; the designation's own verbal own-nature, however, is mere name with no genuine substance behind it, so, on one side, it is simply "non-existent." Yet this does not mean the underlying dependently-arisen basis itself is nothing at all — it is not the case that this basis exists only as an artifact of habituated naming, the way the *Composition on the Establishment of Illumination*'s verse on the dependent nature puts it: neither decidedly existent as posited nor decidedly non-existent in every respect, since all such natures may still be spoken of provisionally, whether as existent, non-existent, both, or neither — so, on the other side, it is also not simply "non-existent." Freed from both "it exists" and "it does not exist" together, this is what non-duality names here: a dream itself is unfindable, yet the mind dreaming cannot be said not to exist either — genuinely knowing dependently-arisen phenomena as existing in just this qualified way is what allows emptiness to be taught without ever severing it from dependent origination itself. Falling into either extreme grasping — clinging to real existence, or denying the dependent basis outright — constitutes defilement; remaining free of both graspings is purity, the direct application of this teaching's non-dual wisdom to daily practice.

73. Section: Non-Duality's Second Meaning — the Perfected Nature Neither Existent to Ordinary Cognition Nor Non-Existent to a Noble One's Wisdom

Further, it is not the case that every basis upon which verbal designation rests, through the impression habituated by conventional, verbal designation ranging over it, has no own-nature whatsoever; and it is not the case that the own-nature ranged over by wisdom free of that habituation exists — it is said to be non-dual. Within this non-duality, if the two graspings arise, this is called defilement; if there is no grasping at the two, this is called purity.

Chinese Source

又非一切名言安足處事，由彼世俗言說熏習想所行自性無所有故，非彼熏習智所行自性有故，說為無二。於此無二若起二執，名為雜染；若無二執，名為清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, it is not the case that every basis upon which verbal designation rests, through the impression habituated by conventional, verbal designation ranging over it, has no own-nature whatsoever; and it is not the case that the own-nature ranged over by wisdom free of that habituation exists — it is said to be non-dual.
Within this non-duality, if the two graspings arise, this is called defilement; if there is no grasping at the two, this is called purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second meaning of non-duality shifts from the dependent nature to the perfected nature — true suchness itself, defined elsewhere as what is disclosed by the selflessness of dharmas, the domain of noble wisdom, not a basis for any verbal designation whatsoever. Ordinary cognition, ranging only over what habituated conventional language has constructed, finds no such thing there — hence "non-existent" in that sense — yet this does not mean the underlying reality on which even mistaken designation ultimately depends is simply absent either. Conversely, the "own-nature ranged over by wisdom free of habituation" — the noble one's direct, wordless realization — is not something that could be said flatly to "exist" in the way conditioned things exist, since it transcends existence and non-existence as ordinarily conceived. Freed from both extremes, this is the second sense of non-duality, focused on the perfected rather than the dependent nature: superimposing "existence" on it, or superimposing "non-existence" by denying the very ground designation depends on, are both defilement, while holding neither extreme is purity — the same structure as the first meaning, now applied one level deeper. With this, both meanings of non-duality, through the dependent and the perfected natures respectively, are fully explained.

74. Section: Entering Every Word of Hidden Intent Through This Very Non-Dual Wisdom — Closing How Hidden Intent Is Penetrated, Entirely

Through this mark of non-duality, know that one thereby penetrates every word of hidden intent spoken by the Tathāgata.

Chinese Source

由此無二相，應知悟入如來一切密意語言。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through this mark of non-duality, know that one thereby penetrates every word of hidden intent spoken by the Tathāgata.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This single line closes the account of how hidden intent is penetrated: whatever teaching the Buddha gives — including teachings pitched, on their surface, only to the śrāvaka path — a bodhisattva equipped with the non-dual wisdom just explained, refusing to fall into either grasping at real existence or grasping at nihilistic non-existence, is able to see through to what is genuinely meant beneath the literal words. With this, the explanation of how the mark of non-duality enables entry into hidden intent is complete, closing this whole account entirely. The treatise turns next to what this same non-dual wisdom serves as the support for — the perfection, in the treatise itself, of its own composition, its fruit, and the benefit of that fruit.

75. Section: The Threefold Perfection Resting on Hidden Intent — Composition Perfected Begins, Five Marks of a Truly Complete Teaching

Within this, by five kinds of mark, composition is named perfected; and within the guiding instruction, by five kinds of mark, fruit is named perfected, and by five kinds of mark, the benefit of that fruit is named perfected. Know that all of these rest upon words of hidden intent. By what five kinds of mark is composition named perfected? It is: if proclaimed through this mark; if it is a proclamation; if it is what is proclaimed; if proclaimed in just this way; and if it is that proclamation — perfection is thus.

Chinese Source

此中由五種相名論圓滿；即於教授中，由五種相名果圓滿，由五種相名果勝利圓滿；當知皆依密意語言。云何由五種相名論圓滿？謂若由此相宣說、若是宣說、若所宣說、若如是宣說、若彼宣說，如是圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, by five kinds of mark, composition is named perfected; and within the guiding instruction, by five kinds of mark, fruit is named perfected, and by five kinds of mark, the benefit of that fruit is named perfected. Know that all of these rest upon words of hidden intent.
By what five kinds of mark is composition named perfected? It is: if proclaimed through this mark; if it is a proclamation; if it is what is proclaimed; if proclaimed in just this way; and if it is that proclamation — perfection is thus.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Non-dual wisdom, having enabled entry into hidden intent, now serves as the ground for a threefold perfection: the composition of the teaching itself, the fruit that teaching yields, and the benefit that fruit confers — each explained through five marks, all of them ultimately dependent on words of hidden intent for their genuine completion. Composition's five marks, terse in the root text, unfold richly in the commentarial tradition: the cause that occasions the proclamation (typically a listener's earnest request, spanning some twenty-seven distinguishable kinds of person); the speech itself, possessing the eight qualities of excellent teaching (leading toward nirvāṇa as its aim, sweet-sounding, clear, easily understood, delightful to hear, free of any wish for personal renown, neither too much nor too little, and boundlessly resourceful) together with eight further marks of perfected verbal expression; the dharma actually proclaimed — the twelve divisions of scripture together with their various modes of address (the aggregates, the elements, dependent origination, and so on); the proper manner of exposition, unfolding in five steps from brief summary through occasion, meaning, resolving objections, and finally sequence; and the qualified speaker — śrāvaka, bodhisattva, or Tathāgata alike. Crucially, "proclaimed in just this way" names specifically the definitive teaching, the deep, emptiness-linked scriptures a bodhisattva may rely upon directly, while the merely provisional teaching suited to the śrāvaka's personal-selflessness alone belongs to the fifth mark instead — the very distinction between plain and hidden meaning this whole gateway has been tracing.

76. Section: Fruit Perfected — Nirvana With and Without Residue, the Perfected Path, and Victory Over the Inner and Outer Foe

By what five kinds of mark is fruit named perfected? It is: the realm of nirvāṇa without residue; or the realm of nirvāṇa with residue; or the perfected noble path; or victory over the inner foe; or victory over the outer foe — perfection is thus.

Chinese Source

云何由五種相名果圓滿？謂無餘依涅槃界、若有餘依涅槃界、若聖道圓滿、若勝內怨、若勝外怨，如是圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

By what five kinds of mark is fruit named perfected? It is: the realm of nirvāṇa without residue; or the realm of nirvāṇa with residue; or the perfected noble path; or victory over the inner foe; or victory over the outer foe — perfection is thus.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fruit that a perfectly composed teaching yields is likewise marked by five signs. Nirvāṇa with residue and without residue together cover both stages of an arhat's or bodhisattva's liberation — respectively, the ending of the three realms' afflicted craving and view while the present body still remains, and the final passing beyond even that residual embodiment. The perfected noble path names the wisdom of both personal and dharma selflessness brought to completion. And "victory over the inner foe" and "victory over the outer foe" locate the real battlefield of practice: every affliction, without remainder, finally and permanently cut off is victory over the inner foe — the true enemy is one's own deluded mind, never another being — while subduing the great armies of Māra (the aggregates, affliction, death, and the god of the sixth heaven) is victory over the outer foe, no longer troubled by demonic obstruction from without because nothing within remains for it to exploit. Together these five marks constitute genuine, complete fruition of the path.

77. Section: The Fruit's Benefit Perfected — Becoming a True Child of the Tathagata Through the Very Dharma That Gave Rise to One — Closing the Fourteenth Gateway Entirely

By what five kinds of mark is the benefit of the fruit named perfected? It is precisely this: making offering to the Great Teacher; requiting the kindness of faith and of giving; transcending the suffering of birth-and-death; never regressing from the nature of a field of merit; and, born of the transformation of dharma, being called a child of the Tathāgata, taking the Tathāgata as one's support.

Chinese Source

云何由五種相名果勝利圓滿？謂即是供養大師；報信施恩；越生死苦；於福田性無有退轉；從法化生名如來子，依止如來。

Ru-Yi Translation

By what five kinds of mark is the benefit of the fruit named perfected? It is precisely this: making offering to the Great Teacher; requiting the kindness of faith and of giving; transcending the suffering of birth-and-death; never regressing from the nature of a field of merit; and, born of the transformation of dharma, being called a child of the Tathāgata, taking the Tathāgata as one's support.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond fruition itself lies its further benefit, again fivefold. Genuinely realizing the truth taught, through cultivating the calming and insight the composition made possible, is itself the truest offering a practitioner can make the Great Teacher — practicing the Dharma outstrips any material gift. Attaining the noble fruit is also how the fourfold debt is genuinely requited: to the Three Treasures, to donors whose faith and generosity sustained the practitioner materially, and to one's parents. Attaining fruition transcends the suffering of saṃsāra's endless cycle. It also secures, once and for all, an irreversible standing as a genuine field of merit for others, never again liable to regress into an ordinary being. And finally, because this attainment is born entirely from the Buddha's own teaching — every thought now moving in accord with the Dharma just as instructed — such a practitioner is truly called a child of the Tathāgata, dependent on him as an heir depends on a father. With this, the threefold perfection resting on words of hidden intent — composition, fruit, and the fruit's benefit, each unfolded through five marks — is fully explained, closing the fourteenth gateway, words of hidden intent, entirely. The treatise turns next to the fifteenth gateway: the benefit of resolute understanding within the Bodhisattva Basket's own teaching.

78. Section: The Fifteenth Gateway Opens — Five Benefits of Resolute Understanding Within the Bodhisattva Basket's Teaching

Furthermore, what is the benefit of resolute understanding within the guiding instruction of the Bodhisattva Basket? Know that it is of five kinds. First, because, in establishing the cause, it is thereby able to eclipse the dominant cause that draws forth great wealth and eminence; second, because of the transformation of dependence; third, because it thereby becomes a vessel for expounding at that very place; fourth, because it becomes a vessel for being a teacher; and fifth, because, at the time of relinquishing the body, one attains the purity of visible karma.

Chinese Source

復次，云何於菩薩藏教授中，勝解勝利？當知由五種相。一者、建立因時即能映蔽感大富貴增上因故；二者、由轉依故；三者、即於是處作說器故；四者、作說者器故；五者、於捨身時，得見業清淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, what is the benefit of resolute understanding within the guiding instruction of the Bodhisattva Basket? Know that it is of five kinds.
First, because, in establishing the cause, it is thereby able to eclipse the dominant cause that draws forth great wealth and eminence; second, because of the transformation of dependence; third, because it thereby becomes a vessel for expounding at that very place; fourth, because it becomes a vessel for being a teacher; and fifth, because, at the time of relinquishing the body, one attains the purity of visible karma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifteenth gateway names five benefits that follow from a firm, unwavering resolute understanding of the deep teaching found specifically within the Bodhisattva Basket — encouraging every practitioner to take up study of the great vehicle's own scriptures with real conviction. First, establishing the cause of personal and dharma selflessness at the causal stage outshines even the karmic cause of great wealth and eminence in five specific ways: wealth's cause yields a bounded fruit while resolute understanding yields boundless fruit; wealth's cause is exhaustible while this cause is inexhaustible; wealth's cause draws only limited happiness while this draws vast happiness; wealth's cause belongs to the accumulation of merit alone while this cause is the very own-nature of wisdom, itself furnishing both accumulations; and this resolute understanding alone is able to draw forth the wisdom of unsurpassed awakening. Second, the transformation of dependence: the wisdom of dharma-emptiness genuinely transforms the practitioner's own body and mind, moving toward the Tathāgata's own stainless consciousness. Third, becoming a vessel for expounding the Dharma at the very place one stands, since genuine resolute understanding of discipline, concentration, and wisdom qualifies one to actually teach it. Fourth, becoming a vessel for being a teacher altogether, one's own practice now bearing witness to what is taught. And fifth, at the moment of relinquishing this body, attaining purity in both view and karma — free of any of the five failings that might otherwise mar the moment of death — such a practitioner need not fear the three lower destinies, and may even choose, retaining a trace of affliction on purpose, to return among gods and humans for the sake of continuing to benefit beings.
