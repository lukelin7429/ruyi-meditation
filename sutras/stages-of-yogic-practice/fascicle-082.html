Fascicle 82 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十二 · The Listener, and Praising the Buddha

How to Settle a Listener's Mind in Respectful, Undistorted Attention

Fascicle 81 closed the sūtra's own body, its five aspects of explanation, and the ten marks and eightfold speech of a genuine teacher. Fascicle 82, the second half of the Compendium of Explanations (攝釋分之下), turns to the listener: how a teacher settles the listener's mind in respectful, undistorted attention, unpacked through as many as ten reasons for listening with respect, and continuing on to the practice of praising the Buddha before teaching begins.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 82 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Compendium of Explanations Continues — the Listener Begins, with as Many as Ten Reasons to Listen Respectfully

The Compendium of Explanations, part two. The listener: this means that when a teacher of dharma such as this teaches the true dharma, he should settle others so that they abide in respectful, undistorted listening. What, then, is meant by "settle"? This is by way of one reason, or as many as ten.

Chinese Source

攝釋分之下。

聽者，謂如是說法者說正法時，應安處他，令住恭敬無倒聽聞。

云何安處？

謂或由一因，或乃至十。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Compendium of Explanations, part two.

The listener: this means that when a teacher of dharma such as this teaches the true dharma, he should settle others so that they abide in respectful, undistorted listening.

What, then, is meant by "settle"?

This is by way of one reason, or as many as ten.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second half of the Compendium of Explanations opens directly from the first: having explained how a teacher perfects his own marks and speech, the treatise turns to the other side of the encounter — how that teacher settles the listener's mind. A teacher equipped with the ten marks and eight kinds of speech, addressing any of the five assemblies, should bring each listener to genuinely respectful, error-free attention — not merely five assemblies reached, but each mind within them rightly settled. This settling is accomplished through as many as ten reasons, which the treatise now unfolds one by one, echoing the Śrāvaka Ground's own account of undistorted instruction: teaching dharma without distortion so that hearers retain it, recite it, and practice it, truly gaining release and reaching the very limit of suffering.

2. Section: The First Reason — Present Benefit and Ease, Through the Higher Trainings

The first reason: this means that respectful listening presently and directly realizes benefit and ease. "Thus what makes you benefited is spoken by way of the higher discipline; thus what makes you at ease is spoken by way of not relying on wretched, difficult, unfree conduct."

Chinese Source

一因者，謂恭敬聽法，現前能證利益安樂故。

如是能令汝利益者，依增上戒說；如是能令汝安樂者，謂不依止弊苦、艱難、不自在行。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first reason: this means that respectful listening presently and directly realizes benefit and ease.

"Thus what makes you benefited is spoken by way of the higher discipline; thus what makes you at ease is spoken by way of not relying on wretched, difficult, unfree conduct."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

A disciple at the stage of accumulation or preparation who respectfully receives and retains the twelve divisions of scripture, reflects on their meaning, holds them in mind, recites them aloud, and takes them into quiet contemplation — the first four of these belong to the wisdom born of hearing, the last to the wisdom born of reflection — realizes real benefit and ease of body and mind in this very life. The 披尋記 further ties benefit to the higher trainings in mind and wisdom, and ease to freedom from renunciation's harsher austerities; elsewhere it notes that wherever the Bhagavān praises ascetic virtues, that is benefit, and wherever he permits the enjoyment of fine food and rich robes, that is ease — and that wherever the three higher trainings in discipline, mind, and wisdom are established, both benefit and ease are established together, since the Tathāgata, by wisdom, teaches whichever of the three a given listener most needs.

3. Section: Four Ways Benefit and Ease Combine — Closing the First Reason

Here there are the four propositions — benefit without ease, and so on — as already explained in the Bodhisattva Ground's account of experiencing dharma.

Chinese Source

此中或有利益非安樂等四句，如菩薩地法受中已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here there are the four propositions — benefit without ease, and so on — as already explained in the Bodhisattva Ground's account of experiencing dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Bodhisattva Ground distinguishes benefit (concerning the next life) from ease (concerning this one) across four cases: ease without benefit — present pleasure ripening into future suffering, as when the ten evil deeds are committed gladly; benefit without ease — present hardship ripening into future pleasure, as when the ten wholesome deeds are practiced reluctantly; both benefit and ease — present pleasure ripening into future pleasure, as when good is practiced gladly; and neither — present suffering ripening into future suffering, as when the ten evil deeds are committed with anguish. With this fourfold analysis, the first of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

4. Section: The Second Reason Begins — Rightly Establishing All Dharmas

The second reason: this means rightly establishing all dharmas. Rightly establishing means being free of faults, and possessing great meaning.

Chinese Source

二因者，謂善建立一切法故。善建立者，離諸過故，具大義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second reason: this means rightly establishing all dharmas. Rightly establishing means being free of faults, and possessing great meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Ground of What Is Heard names four kinds of inner seed underlying all dharmas: worldly seeds (afflictions bound to the three realms), transcendent seeds (the noble paths and fruits of the three vehicles), impure seeds (the aggregates and activities bound to the desire realm), and pure seeds — themselves worldly-pure (activities bound to the form and formless realms) and transcendent-pure (the paths, fruits, and eightfold noble path of the three vehicles). Rightly establishing means the listener's mind is led to cultivate both kinds of pure seed: worldly purity through freeing oneself of the faults of affliction and evil karma — trusting good companions, not mixing carelessly with householders and renunciants alike, and inwardly applying right attention to awaken to the truth of what is known — and transcendent purity through fully entering the profound meaning of conditioned and unconditioned dharmas, grasping both their nature as they truly are and the whole extent of what they are, and so realizing nirvāṇa's vast, transcendent benefit and ease.

5. Section: A Surpassing Result for Both Speaker and Listener — Closing the Second Reason

And further, because the toil undertaken by both speaker and listener has a surpassing result. Were it not so, the one who speaks and the one who listens would only waste their own proper work, expend effort in vain, and have no result at all.

Chinese Source

又為說者、聽者所設劬勞有勝果故。若不爾者，能說、能聽徒廢己業，虛設功勞，應無有果。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because the toil undertaken by both speaker and listener has a surpassing result. Were it not so, the one who speaks and the one who listens would only waste their own proper work, expend effort in vain, and have no result at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Both the effort of teaching and the effort of listening bear real fruit, worldly and transcendent — which is exactly why a teacher must settle listeners in respectful attention, and why listeners in turn must listen respectfully and without distortion; otherwise the labor on both sides would be spent for nothing. With this, the second of the ten reasons is closed.

6. Section: The Third Reason Begins — Abandoning Evil Destinies, Gaining Good Ones, Swiftly Drawing In the Cause of Nirvāṇa

The third reason: respectful listening enables sentient beings to abandon evil destinies, to attain good destinies, and swiftly to draw in the cause of nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

三因者，恭敬聽法，能令眾生捨惡趣故，得善趣故，速能引攝涅槃因故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third reason: respectful listening enables sentient beings to abandon evil destinies, to attain good destinies, and swiftly to draw in the cause of nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Respectful listening deepens faith in karmic cause and effect — that every result comes from action, that action once done is never lost and never ripens unearned, and that any evil committed, whether premeditated or already carried out, calls for swift confession and remedy before it grows — which alone lets a listener abandon the ten evil deeds and the evil destinies they lead to, and gain a good destiny instead. Beyond this, respectful listening enables cultivation of the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest of the noble eightfold path, the very path leading out of the three realms' cycle of suffering toward nirvāṇa — suffering and its origin being the dark portion's result and cause, cessation and the path being the bright portion's result and cause. The Ground of Neither-With-nor-Without-Investigation names the causes of nirvāṇa as twofold: superior conditions — the true dharma's own supporting voice, chiefly the four noble truths, together with inward right reflection on what is heard — and lesser conditions, a longer list including one's own and others' perfections, wholesome aspiration, right going-forth, restraint by precepts and by the senses, moderation in eating, diligent wakeful yoga through the watches of the night, mindful abiding, delight in solitude, purifying the hindrances, and resting in concentration.

7. Section: All Three Attainments Come Only Through Respectful Listening — Closing the Third Reason

These three matters are attained only through respectful listening.

Chinese Source

如是三事，要由恭敬聽聞方得。

Ru-Yi Translation

These three matters are attained only through respectful listening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Abandoning evil destinies and gaining good ones together fulfill what the Ground of Thought Cultivation calls the fulfillment of higher rebirth — going toward a good destiny — while swiftly drawing in the cause of nirvāṇa fulfills the fulfillment of decisive excellence — craving exhausted, desire abandoned, cessation and nirvāṇa reached. With this, the third of the ten reasons is closed.

8. Section: The Fourth Reason — Understanding the Teaching, Taking Up Good and Abandoning Evil, and Swiftly Realizing Nirvāṇa

The fourth reason: first, respectful listening enables one to skillfully penetrate the sūtras and other such dharmas. Second, this true dharma enables sentient beings to abandon what is unwholesome and take up what is wholesome; one who listens well can then diligently abandon the one and take up the other. Third, through this taking-up and abandoning, one abandons the future suffering brought on by evil causes. Fourth, through taking up good causes and abandoning evil ones in this way, one swiftly realizes nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

四因者，一、恭敬聽法，能善了達契經等法。二、如是正法，能令眾生捨諸不善，攝受諸善；若善聽者，則能精勤若捨、若受。三、由捨受故，捨離惡因所招後苦。四、由此受捨善惡因故，速證涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth reason: first, respectful listening enables one to skillfully penetrate the sūtras and other such dharmas. Second, this true dharma enables sentient beings to abandon what is unwholesome and take up what is wholesome; one who listens well can then diligently abandon the one and take up the other. Third, through this taking-up and abandoning, one abandons the future suffering brought on by evil causes. Fourth, through taking up good causes and abandoning evil ones in this way, one swiftly realizes nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These four links form a single chain: understanding the twelve divisions of scripture makes it possible to abandon greed, hatred, and delusion and take up wholesome dharmas both worldly and transcendent; abandoning unwholesome causes means no further evil karma is created, and so no further suffering in the three evil destinies is incurred; and abandoning evil causes while taking up good ones in this way leads swiftly to nirvāṇa itself. With this, the fourth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

9. Section: The Fifth Reason Begins, First Meaning — Five Excellences of the Buddha's True Dharma

The fifth reason: this means that the true dharma spoken by the Bhagavān has cause and occasion, has leading-out, has what is relied upon, has boldness, and has miraculous transformation.

Chinese Source

五因者，謂佛世尊所說正法，有因緣、有出離、有依趣、有勇猛、有神變。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fifth reason: this means that the true dharma spoken by the Bhagavān has cause and occasion, has leading-out, has what is relied upon, has boldness, and has miraculous transformation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Speaking generally of the twelve divisions of scripture, these five excellences unfold as follows. Has cause and occasion: the Buddha speaks only when asked, as bodhisattvas and disciples request teaching on renunciation, concentration, and wisdom; he speaks the precepts only in response to an actual occasion of misconduct, since the Vinaya is always occasioned by some monk's specific failing; and every teaching is fitted to the particular cause, matter, and listener before him. Has leading-out: the dharma leads a practitioner out of every defiled state into every pure one — worldly concentration freeing one from the afflictions of desire, the transcendent path freeing one from the three realms' suffering entirely, up through the fruits of stream-entry and beyond to unsurpassed awakening. Has what is relied upon: in brief, four things — relying on the dharma rather than the person; relying on the meaning rather than the letter; relying on sūtras of fully drawn-out meaning rather than those not fully drawn out; and relying on wisdom rather than ordinary consciousness, since only wisdom, not the sixth consciousness's own discriminations, can be trusted before the truth is directly realized. Has boldness: the dharma that brings a being to unsurpassed awakening is vast in content and extremely lofty, demanding bold, unbroken diligence sustained across three great incalculable eons to reach completion — the very same boldness already described in fascicle 81's account of the eighth mark of a good teacher: constant delight in unheard teachings and ever-clearer mastery of what is already heard, without abandoning yoga, attentive application, or inward calm-abiding. Has miraculous transformation: the Śrāvaka Ground names three — miraculous transformation of spiritual power, evoking such reverence in the listener that he attends to yogic instruction with utmost respect; miraculous transformation of mind-reading, discerning the particular state of another's mind; and miraculous transformation of instruction, teaching each disciple exactly as his own faculties and conduct allow him to enter the path — together comprising every perfection of instruction the Buddha permits, so as to draw beings joyfully into the sacred teaching.

10. Section: Referred to the Compendium of Synonyms — Closing the First Meaning of the Fifth Reason

Statements such as these should be distinguished at length as in the Compendium of Synonyms.

Chinese Source

如是諸句，如攝異門分當廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Statements such as these should be distinguished at length as in the Compendium of Synonyms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Compendium of Synonyms restates these five in terms closer to monastic discipline: having cause and occasion, since every precept is established only upon an actual occasion of misconduct; having leading-out, since a proper method of confession is established so a violator may return to purity; having what is relied upon, since the four reliances — on robes, food, dwelling, and medicine, each kept simple — let a practitioner rise above every corrupted precept; having what transcends, since the precepts steer a practitioner in the middle way, away from both indulgence in desire and fruitless austerity; and having miraculous transformation, the same three already explained, established so that practitioners swiftly gain spiritual mastery. Though the wording differs slightly from the account just given, the meaning is the same. With this, the first of the two meanings under the fifth reason is closed. The true dharma's five excellences give disciples every reason to listen and study it well.

11. Section: A Second Meaning of the Fifth Reason — the Threefold Wisdom of Hearing, Reflection, and Cultivation — Closing the Fifth Reason Entirely

There is further a fivefold reason: I shall hear what has not yet been heard; I shall, having heard, investigate it; I shall cut off the net of doubt; I shall cast aside all views; I shall, through wisdom, penetrate all profound words and meanings. All buddha bhagavāns speak these five so as to reveal the pure means of the threefold wisdom accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation. The first two reveal the wisdom accomplished through hearing; the next two reveal the wisdom accomplished through reflection; the last reveals the wisdom accomplished through cultivation.

Chinese Source

復有五因。謂我當聞所未聞，我當聞已研究，我當除斷疑網，我當棄背諸見，我當以慧通達一切甚深句義。

諸佛世尊說此五種，顯聞思修三所成慧清淨方便。謂初二種顯聞所成慧，次二種顯思所成慧，後一種顯修所成慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is further a fivefold reason: I shall hear what has not yet been heard; I shall, having heard, investigate it; I shall cut off the net of doubt; I shall cast aside all views; I shall, through wisdom, penetrate all profound words and meanings.

All buddha bhagavāns speak these five so as to reveal the pure means of the threefold wisdom accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation. The first two reveal the wisdom accomplished through hearing; the next two reveal the wisdom accomplished through reflection; the last reveals the wisdom accomplished through cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Hearing what has not yet been heard, and investigating what has been heard, together purify the mind through the wisdom born of hearing; cutting off the net of doubt and casting aside every view together purify it through the wisdom born of reflection; and penetrating all profound words and meanings — the empty nature of all dharmas — purifies it through the wisdom born of cultivation, corresponding to the Grounds of What Is Heard, What Is Thought, and What Is Cultivated among the treatise's seventeen grounds: the three vehicles all rely on hearing, reflection, and cultivation as the means by which surpassing virtue is established. With this second meaning, and with both meanings of the fifth reason together, the fifth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

12. Section: The Sixth Reason — Repaying the Teacher's Kindness, and Five Further Excellences of the True Dharma

The sixth reason: first, in order to repay the great teacher's kindness with reverence — since the Bhagavān, for our sake, practiced measureless difficult and painful austerities to seek out this dharma, how could one now fail to listen to it? Second, contemplating one's own benefit — for the Buddha's true dharma has present benefit. Third, it can ultimately free one from every consuming affliction. Fourth, it accords well with true deportment. Fifth, it is easily seen and understood. Sixth, it is what the wise know through their own inner realization.

Chinese Source

六因者，一、為欲敬報大師恩德，謂佛世尊為我等故，行於無量難行苦行，求得此法，云何今者而不聽聞。二、觀自義利，謂佛正法有現義利。三、究竟能離一切熱惱。四、善順正儀。五、易可了見。六、諸聰慧者內證所知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth reason: first, in order to repay the great teacher's kindness with reverence — since the Bhagavān, for our sake, practiced measureless difficult and painful austerities to seek out this dharma, how could one now fail to listen to it? Second, contemplating one's own benefit — for the Buddha's true dharma has present benefit. Third, it can ultimately free one from every consuming affliction. Fourth, it accords well with true deportment. Fifth, it is easily seen and understood. Sixth, it is what the wise know through their own inner realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Buddha labored across three great incalculable eons to win this dharma for our sake — gratitude alone should move a listener to attend. Present benefit means every precept observed and every training undertaken now already elevates one's lifespan, appearance, and fortune, ripening toward nirvāṇa itself. Freedom from consuming affliction follows from rightly establishing all dharmas, worldly and transcendent, as already explained under the second reason. According with true deportment means the Buddha's own moral code, unlike the brahmins' — which the 披尋記 contrasts point by point: permitting harm where the brahmin code allows it, versus prohibiting it entirely; encouraging accumulation of land, house, and wealth, wife and servants, versus renouncing all such possessions but the means to end suffering; indulging adornment, song, dance, and sensual pleasure, versus enjoying only the blameless wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation — the monastic code proving superior in precept, in what is embraced, and in what is enjoyed, at every point. Easily seen and understood follows the same contrast: brahminical teaching is hollow and deliberately obscured in wording, while the Buddha's teaching is genuinely meaningful and stated in plain, undisguised language. And what the wise know through inner realization means suchness itself, tasted directly, not merely reasoned about. With this, the sixth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

13. Section: The Seventh Reason — Cultivating Seven True Dharmas, from Knowing the Teaching to Knowing Persons

The seventh reason: this means, I shall cultivate seven true dharmas — I shall know the teaching and know its meaning, and so on, up to skillfully knowing the distinctions of noble and lowly among persons.

Chinese Source

七因者，謂我當修集七種正法，我當知法知義，乃至善知補特伽羅尊卑差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The seventh reason: this means, I shall cultivate seven true dharmas — I shall know the teaching and know its meaning, and so on, up to skillfully knowing the distinctions of noble and lowly among persons.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, drawing on the Vinaya and the Śrāvaka Ground

Traditional Vinaya sources unpack this list as seven things a renunciant should know daily. Knowing the teaching: grasping the twelve divisions of scripture — their provisional names, how their terms interrelate, their proper sequence of practice, who among the noble ones spoke them, and the self- or other-benefiting intent behind each. Knowing the meaning: grasping the circumstances that occasioned a teaching, the different angles from which its words are framed, the sequence by which it is explained and established, the four principles of reasoning behind it, and its essential gist in brief. Knowing the time: recognizing, moment to moment in meditation practice, when dullness or agitation is present, approaching, subsiding, or already resolved, and applying the right corrective — exertion, calming, or equanimity — accordingly. Knowing the measure: moderation in food and in exertion alike, so that the body stays balanced and healthy and effort neither slackens into dullness nor strains into agitation. Knowing oneself: an honest, daily accounting of one's own present measure of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. Knowing the assembly: recognizing who is present — noble or common, monastic or lay, elder or junior, scholar of sūtra, treatise, or yogic practice. And knowing persons: discerning each listener's own capacity, sharp or dull, faithful or not, without attachment yet with real clarity, so as to give each the teaching actually suited to them. A teacher equipped with all seven forms of knowing is thereby equipped to settle any listener in respectful, undistorted attention.

14. Section: The Eighth Reason — Eight Marks of Excellence in the Dharma Itself

The eighth reason: first, the Buddha's dharma is easily obtained, taught even to outcasts. Second, it is easily practiced, practicable in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down. Third, it draws in benefit, able to draw in both the fruit of higher rebirth and the fruit of decisive excellence. Fourth, it is good in the beginning. Fifth, it is good in the middle. Sixth, it is good in the end. Seventh, it brings about a pleasant result in the present. Eighth, it draws in a pleasant result hereafter.

Chinese Source

八因者，一、佛法易得，乃至為旃荼羅等而開示故。二、易可修學，行住坐臥皆得修故。三、引發義利，謂能引發增上生果、決定勝果故。四、初善故。五、中善故。六、後善故。七、感現樂果故。八、引後樂果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eighth reason: first, the Buddha's dharma is easily obtained, taught even to outcasts. Second, it is easily practiced, practicable in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down. Third, it draws in benefit, able to draw in both the fruit of higher rebirth and the fruit of decisive excellence. Fourth, it is good in the beginning. Fifth, it is good in the middle. Sixth, it is good in the end. Seventh, it brings about a pleasant result in the present. Eighth, it draws in a pleasant result hereafter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Buddha teaches every being alike regardless of caste or station, even the lowliest outcaste, in whatever way — provisional or ultimate, direct or skillful — fits the listener; his teaching is coherent and clear enough to practice in any posture at any time; and it draws out both worldly and transcendent fruit at once. The Compendium of Synonyms explains the three "good"s concretely: good in the beginning, since simply hearing it gives rise to joy; good in the middle, since practicing it involves no real hardship, keeping to the middle way between indulgence and austerity; and good in the end, since carried through it ultimately uproots every defilement and brings freedom from the three realms entirely. Present pleasant result and future pleasant result then round out the eight, teaching bearing fruit both now and in lives to come. With this, the eighth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

15. Section: The Ninth Reason — Freed from Nine Kinds of Worldly Oppression, the Prison of Birth-and-Death Foremost Among Them

The ninth reason: this means being freed from nine kinds of worldly oppression. First, it enables one to escape the great prison of birth-and-death. Second, it permanently severs the firm bonds of craving and the rest. Third, it casts off the poverty of the seven kinds of wealth and establishes their riches. Fourth, it delivers one from scarcity of good conduct and right dharma once heard, and establishes their abundance. Fifth, it extinguishes the darkness of ignorance and gives rise to the light of wisdom. Sixth, it crosses the four torrents and ascends to nirvāṇa's shore. Seventh, it ultimately cures the sickness of affliction. Eighth, it frees one from every snare of craving. Ninth, it carries one across the trackless wilderness and dense forest of beginningless birth-and-death. Among all prisons, the prison of birth-and-death is foremost — hence it is named first.

Chinese Source

九因者，謂能解脫九種世間逼迫事故。一、能出生死大牢獄故。二、永斷貪等堅牢縛故。三、棄捨七財貧，建立七財富故。四、超度善行聞正法儉，建立善行聞正法豐故。五、滅無明闇，起智慧明故。六、度四暴流，昇涅槃岸故。七、究竟能療煩惱病故。八、解脫一切貪愛羂故。九、能度無始生死曠野稠林行故。諸牢獄中，生死牢獄最為第一，是故先說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ninth reason: this means being freed from nine kinds of worldly oppression. First, it enables one to escape the great prison of birth-and-death. Second, it permanently severs the firm bonds of craving and the rest. Third, it casts off the poverty of the seven kinds of wealth and establishes their riches. Fourth, it delivers one from scarcity of good conduct and right dharma once heard, and establishes their abundance. Fifth, it extinguishes the darkness of ignorance and gives rise to the light of wisdom. Sixth, it crosses the four torrents and ascends to nirvāṇa's shore. Seventh, it ultimately cures the sickness of affliction. Eighth, it frees one from every snare of craving. Ninth, it carries one across the trackless wilderness and dense forest of beginningless birth-and-death. Among all prisons, the prison of birth-and-death is foremost — hence it is named first.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Nine images, one after another, for what the dharma frees a listener from. The prison of birth-and-death holds every being fast in craving, aversion, and the fetters of desire, shrouded in ignorance's dense thicket, unable to escape the three realms unaided — the dharma alone offers a way out. The seven kinds of noble wealth it establishes in place of poverty are faith, discipline, a sense of shame before oneself, a sense of shame before others, learning, generosity, and wisdom — each the remedy for a corresponding deficiency the 披尋記 names as a kind of poverty. Scarcity of good conduct gives way to abundance the more one hears and practices; ignorance's darkness gives way to wisdom's light; the four torrents — of desire, of becoming, of views, and of ignorance — are crossed only by hearing the true dharma, carrying one to nirvāṇa's far shore; affliction's sickness is cured, since the Buddha alone is the great physician; the net of craving, woven from endless attachment, is escaped; and the trackless wilderness of beginningless birth-and-death, thick with every conditioned activity, is finally crossed. With this, the ninth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

16. Section: The Tenth Reason Begins — Wealth Rightly Sought, Loss Rightly Borne, Desire Rightly Seen Through, and Dependent Origination Rightly Penetrated

The tenth reason: first, respectful listening gains the power of discernment, and through this one receives the benefit of hearing the dharma — seeking wealth rightly, not wrongly, and, seeing its faults clearly, making right use of it. Second, one comes to know release well: losing one's wealth, one feels no grief and utters no complaint, and so on, at length; losing one's family, or facing illness, one does not grieve deeply or fall into sorrow, and so on, at length. Third, seeing clearly the faults in the objects of desire, and the surpassing virtue of release from them, one goes forth in purity, abandoning attachment to fine bedding, up to realizing the wondrous states of meditative absorption. Fourth, respectful listening swiftly brings understanding in accord with the vast and profound, and the seemingly profound, dharmas of dependent origination, and draws forth the vast joy of wholesome roots and of release. As the Bhagavān said: my noble disciples, attending with the ear single-mindedly to the true dharma, can cut off five dharmas and cultivate seven dharmas, swiftly reaching completeness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The tenth and final reason unfolds in ten parts of its own. The first four appear here: discernment lets one earn and use wealth rightly, aware of its dangers; equanimity in loss — of wealth, of family, of health — comes from truly understanding cause and effect, so grief need not follow misfortune; seeing through the objects of desire clears the way to a genuinely pure renunciation and, eventually, meditative absorption; and swift insight into dependent origination, both in its vast profundity (emptiness itself, neither definitely existent nor definitely non-existent) and its seeming profundity (conditioned phenomena that, though truly impermanent and without self, appear constant), draws forth wholesome roots and real joy in release. The 披尋記 glosses the "five dharmas cut off" as the distorted views that mistake the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, and the selfless for a self, together with seeking personal gain and craving long life; and the "seven dharmas cultivated" as the seven factors of awakening — investigation of dharmas, energy, and joy belonging to insight; tranquility, concentration, and equanimity belonging to calm-abiding; and mindfulness common to both — the very state of unified calm and insight that brings the four noble truths to direct realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Affliction and karma are dharmas of accumulation, belonging to the truth of origin; once craving and views are fully cut off, present and future suffering are stilled, becoming dharmas of cessation, belonging to the truth of cessation — respectful listening turns one into the other. "Far from dust, free of defilement" the Compendium of Synonyms glosses two ways: dust as the conceit still arising, intermittently or continuously, before direct realization is complete, and defilement as its residual latent tendencies together with those cut off at the path of seeing — both now permanently absent; or, more broadly, dust as self-conceit and every view-based affliction cut off at the path of seeing, defilement as the coarse latent residue of both. With this tenth reason complete in all its ten parts, all ten reasons for respectful listening — presently realized benefit and ease, right establishment of all dharmas, escape from evil destinies, understanding what is taught, the dharma's five excellences and threefold wisdom, repaying the teacher's kindness, the sevenfold knowing, the dharma's eight further excellences, freedom from ninefold worldly oppression, and this tenfold final reason — are now fully set forth, closing the account of the listener entirely. The treatise turns next to praising the Buddha before teaching begins.

Master Kuiji's own praise captures it: mind still, bearing dignified and wondrous, every faculty utterly at rest, having reached the very peak of self-mastery and calm.

Together, as the Bodhisattva Ground likewise teaches, the six perfections and the four means of gathering beings perfect both at once.

23. Section: Majestic Virtue — the Unhindered Play of Spiritual Power — Closing Brief Praise by Five Marks

Ru-Yi Translation

Majestic virtue means the unhindered play of spiritual powers possessed by all tathāgatas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise

The Buddha's six perfected spiritual powers, wielded with great wisdom and great compassion, transform freely and rescue beings effortlessly, mind utterly pure and unstained — hence "play." The Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise explains the word directly: a magician conjures endless transformations at will and calls it play; a bodhisattva's spiritual powers transform just as freely, and so are likewise called play — most essential of all in maturing beings and purifying buddha-lands together. Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary citing the Determinations, fascicle 74

Complete in merit means every worldly and transcendent virtue the Buddha has achieved, surpassing what any word or song could capture. Free of every defilement means free of the seven kinds of taint — no taint of desiring others' recognition, no taint of clinging to views, no taint of doubt, no taint of comparing himself to others, no taint of intoxicated self-satisfaction, no taint of any latent tendency of affliction whatsoever, and no taint of stinginess. Without turbidity or impurity means unshakable in seven further ways: no outsider can shake it; no army of Māra — the aggregates, afflictions, death, or the god of the sixth heaven — can shake it; no thief can seize the noble wealth of faith, discipline, learning, shame before self, shame before others, generosity, and wisdom he has perfected, since it is formless and beyond every calamity; no kinsman can destroy it; no king's decree can remove it; no elemental disaster of fire, water, or wind can alter it; and even when his lifespan ends and he enters cessation, his merit never diminishes, enduring to the very end of future time. Without equal means his conditioned and unconditioned virtues are so vast, so exalted, so many, and so powerfully great that neither pure-minded śrāvakas nor bodhisattvas already on the grounds can match him, let alone beings still cycling through the six destinies — his merit stands foremost in all three realms. Whose only work is benefiting sentient beings means that, rather than resting in the vast, faultless ease of the nirvāṇa he has attained, he skillfully manifests himself again and again for others' benefit. And fully capable in this work means he accomplishes this benefiting effortlessly and spontaneously, without needing to form any intention, sustaining it without interruption to the very end of future time. The 披尋記 traces all six back to the Determinations' fourfold account of great awakening's own nature — as the ground on which arising and its cessation both rest, as what rightly discerns the fruit of all that is known, and as the pure dharma-realm itself — together with its ten masteries (over lifespan, mind, requisites, karma, birth, resolve, aspiration, spiritual power, wisdom, and dharma, detailed in the Bodhisattva Ground's chapter on abiding) and its skillful means for teaching beings through four kinds of transformative activity: ripening the unripe, liberating the ripened across countless world-systems at once, and appearing as a śrāvaka or a solitary buddha as each occasion calls for. With this sixfold account, brief praise of the Buddha — whether by the five marks or by these six — is now fully closed. The treatise turns next to extensive praise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Twelve further titles. No being equals the Buddha's wisdom, merit, compassion, and power; yet every buddha equals every other buddha across all three times — "the unequaled equal." He is foremost among beings, having realized unsurpassed awakening in full. He is the great sage-king, an elder venerable in discipline through three great incalculable eons of accumulated pure conduct, who has realized the very ancient path — the noble eightfold path — that every noble one before him has walked. He prevails over every outsider and every demonic hindrance — the afflictions, the aggregates, death, and the sixth-heaven god alike. He is the great muni ("muni" meaning stillness): body, speech, and mind all stilled, free of restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — the very three stillnesses just named. No being's arising and no rival philosophy can shift his understanding. He is "well bathed," forever free of every affliction and evil karma, having washed himself clean of dust. And he has reached the far shore, having gone beyond every view of a self, wisdom encompassing the whole dharma-realm, both forms of self-clinging extinguished, unmoving in his own true nature. With this, the seventh of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

32. Section: The Ten Epithets — Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Enlightened, and the Rest, Ending in Bhagavān

Thus he is Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Enlightened, and so on, at length, ending with Bhagavān.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he is Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Enlightened, and so on, at length, ending with Bhagavān.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, drawing on the Bodhisattva Ground's chapter on awakening

The traditional ten epithets, each explained here in turn. Tathāgata — his every word, from the day of his awakening to his final nirvāṇa, was never once false. Worthy One — in the threefold sense of having attained every attainment to be attained, being fit to serve as the unsurpassed field of merit for the world, and being worthy of all reverence and offering. Perfectly and Fully Enlightened — awakened to all dharmas exactly as their ultimate nature truly is, both obstructions cut off, wisdom unobstructed toward everything knowable. Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct — complete in the three knowledges (of the heavenly eye, of past lives, and of the exhaustion of contaminants) together with the practice of calm-abiding and insight. Well-Gone — risen to the very highest, never to return. Knower of the World — skillfully understanding every realm of beings and their vessel-world alike, defiled and pure. Unsurpassed Tamer — the one true great being of the world, master of his own mind, guided by wisdom alone. Teacher of Gods and Humans — explained through four aspects (the true eye, true wisdom, true meaning, and true dharma he offers as refuge and guide) that together let him rightly instruct gods and humans out of every suffering. Buddha — awakened effortlessly and spontaneously, without needing to reflect, to all three gatherings of teaching: what leads to benefit, what leads away from it, and what is neither. Bhagavān — victorious over the aggregates, afflictions, death, and the sixth-heaven god, and, by another accounting, possessed of sovereignty, blazing glory, dignity, renown, auspiciousness, and honor all at once. With this, the eighth of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

Thus he is complete in white dharmas; the omniscient one; the lord of all dharmas; possessed of the dharma of never forgetting; firmest and most excellent among all sentient beings; his mind undisturbed by any pleasure or pain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The ninth of extensive praise's fourteen sections is the longest, gathering some twenty-two virtues; these are the first six. Complete in white dharmas: the six perfections and four means of gathering beings and every other wholesome dharma perfected in one who is complete in merit and wisdom alike. The omniscient one: wisdom moving without obstruction across every realm, every matter, every category, every time — marked, the Determinations add, by being universally renowned, by the thirty-two marks, by the ten powers able to resolve every doubt, by the four fearlessnesses that overturn every rival doctrine, and by the eightfold path and four kinds of renunciant all being genuinely realized in his own well-taught discipline. Lord of all dharmas: the very root and master from whom the dharma issues, as the āgamas themselves declare. Never forgetting: mindfully recollecting every matter, place, occasion, and time without exception, always. Firmest and most excellent among beings: fearless as one entering battle, prevailing however great the opposing host, auspicious in every posture. And undisturbed by pleasure or pain: a mind vast beyond measure, equanimous and pure, seeing through the nature of both pleasure and pain so that neither can shake it.

34. Section: Well Tamed, Truly Still, and Truly at Peace — Three Virtues

Chinese Source

是善調者，密護根門善圓滿故；是寂靜者，受持尸羅善圓滿故；是安隱者，已入決定地故。

Ru-Yi Translation

He is well tamed, having perfected the guarding of the sense gateways; he is truly still, having perfected upholding moral discipline; he is truly at peace, having entered the ground of certainty.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Well tamed: guarding every sense gateway to complete perfection — not merely holding the vows of individual liberation but fully mastering conduct down to the smallest matter, seeing even minor faults with real dread. Truly still: discipline held so completely pure that none of the ten conditions that undermine it — from a corrupted initial ordination to a failure in livelihood — remain. Truly at peace: having reached the eighth ground and beyond, where effort and mental marks alike fall away and no arising affliction can any longer disturb — a mind as still as calm water, resting undisturbed however form is seen or sound is heard.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with citations from across the treatise and the āgamas

Thirteen final images close this longest of the fourteen sections. He has attained nirvāṇa, both obstructions cut off; drawn out the arrow of craving that pierces every being's own sense of self and its objects; tamed and stilled both himself and others, echoing the "well tamed" and "truly still" just explained, now extended to benefiting others as well; comforted ordinary beings into the first two fruits and carried those already there on to non-returning and arhatship; he bears no shackles of greed, hatred, or delusion; has escaped the desires of the three realms, pictured as a pit of fire from which the treatise elsewhere says no one escapes unburned; has crossed the deep trench of ignorance that the āgamas name explicitly; has mastered the three kinds of craving — for pleasure, for continued existence, and for a renunciant's own liberation; remains unmoved by any arising pride, idle elaboration, or craving born of aspiration, as one settled in signless concentration; has toppled the rigid banner of self-conceit, having realized the selflessness of both person and dharma; and abides as the great, unconditioned reality of nirvāṇa itself — beyond arising, abiding, change, or cessation, neither large by contrast with something small nor first small and later become large, but simply the true nature of mind itself, without location, without measure, without before or after. With this, the ninth and longest of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

36. Section: An Arhat, All Contaminants Forever Exhausted — Closing the Tenth Section of Extensive Praise

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

The Śrāvaka Ground spells this out: what was to be done is done, nothing further remains, one's own goal — nirvāṇa — is realized, and every fetter of existence is exhausted, the five lower fetters (self-view, clinging to rules and rituals, and doubt, together with sensual desire and ill will) and the five higher fetters (craving and conceit and ignorance tied to the form and formless realms) alike. Whatever an arhat has accomplished, the Buddha has accomplished in full. With this, the tenth of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Ground of Thought Cultivation and the Śrāvaka Ground

The five branches forever cut off: never again abandoning the training and turning back; never again hoarding possessions or indulging in sense pleasures; never again lying for the sake of wealth or life; never again taking what is not given; and never again departing from solitude to engage in unchaste conduct. The six branches accomplished: seeing a form, or any object, with neither elation nor distress, abiding in equanimity with mindfulness and clear comprehension throughout. And the treatise goes on, at length, to describe one always guarding the four requisites rightly, utterly withdrawn, singular and truthful, free of craving, free of impure thought, body at ease, mind well liberated, wisdom well liberated, without peer, right conduct established — one who has drawn near to the unsurpassed great being. Whatever is said here of an arhat applies to the Buddha without exception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the Compendium's own gloss

Seven kinds of knowing, in brief: knowing the teaching, the twelve divisions of scripture; knowing its meaning, the principle it conveys; knowing the time, what practice suits which moment; knowing the measure, right conduct through every posture, meal, and word; knowing himself, his own awakening and its perfection of merit and wisdom fully realized; knowing the assembly, the particulars of whoever is present; and knowing persons, discerning the noble and the lowly among them by their own wholesome roots. This same sevenfold knowing already grounds the seven true dharmas explained earlier in this fascicle as the seventh reason for respectful listening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourteenth and final section gathers eight further excellences. Pure in every kind, no dharma of any category remaining impure in him. Adorned with the marks, both the thirty-two general marks and the eighty finer excellences, down to the smallest feature. The ten powers of wisdom, honored by every god and human for them. The four fearlessnesses — declaring himself fully awakened, contaminants exhausted, the path of release rightly taught, and every obstruction to the path rightly named — proclaimed openly and without fear, since only truth is claimed, never boast. Great compassion, treated separately just below. The three foundations of mindfulness — equanimous, unshaken mindfulness held toward beings who only obey, beings who never obey, and beings who sometimes do and sometimes don't, without partiality toward any of the three. The three kinds of unguarded conduct — body, speech, and mind so completely pure that nothing need ever be concealed, unlike even an arhat, whose habituation may still surface in small, harmless ways. And the dharma of never forgetting, together with affliction's habituation permanently destroyed and all-knowing wisdom achieved — memory so complete that nothing said or done, however long past, is ever lost, and wisdom penetrating the wholesome, the unwholesome, and the indeterminate alike without exception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary and the Bodhisattva Ground's fourfold account

Four qualities together make this compassion "great," matching the Bodhisattva Ground's own list: an object profound and vast (all beings, and all their suffering — the Bodhisattva Ground itemizes a full 110 kinds); intense application (never shaken by beings' own harmful, changeable, or ungrateful conduct); and perfect constancy (turning equally toward every being without exception) — together with the long accumulation just named. The Compendium of the Mahāyāna further sorts beings into ten kinds this compassion embraces: those blazing with desire, those beset by hostile obstacles, those crushed by the suffering of the three evil destinies, those shrouded in the darkness of unknowing wrongdoing, those content to drift in saṃsāra, those bound tightly by wrong views, those intoxicated by the very pleasure of meditative absorption, those who mistake attainment for true realization, those of indeterminate lineage with no path to buddhahood at all, and bodhisattvas still thin in merit and wisdom — and pictures great compassion as a tree: compassion its root, patience its trunk, the intention to benefit others its branches, the vow for an excellent rebirth its leaves, the resulting excellent birth its flowers, and the ripening of all beings its fruit. With this, great compassion is fully explained, closing the fourteenth and final section of extensive praise — and with it, extensive praise entire, both brief and extensive praise of the Buddha together, and all four aspects of explaining well — the teacher, the speaker's eightfold speech to five assemblies, the listener's tenfold reasons, and praising the Buddha — are now closed in full. The treatise turns next to a summary in brief of everything explained.

42. Section: A Summary in Brief — Five Steps for Any Teacher, Together like the Five Notes of Music

And one should likewise settle oneself in the marks of a teacher already explained — being complete in the ten perfections such as skill in the dharma's meaning. Having settled oneself in this way, one should give rise to speech of this kind — teaching the great assembly amid the five assemblies through the eight kinds of speech such as delightful speech already explained. And one should settle others, so they abide in respectful, undistorted listening. And one should first praise the great teacher's own virtues. Whoever teaches the true dharma complete with these five parts should be known to be like the five notes of music, able to give rise to great joy in self and other alike, and able to draw forth benefit for self and other alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained every part in full across fascicles 81 and 82, the treatise now draws the whole method together into five steps, likened to the five notes of the classical Chinese pentatonic scale, sounding together in harmony: first, grasp the sutra's own text and meaning, and unfold it through the five aspects of explanation — the dharma itself, its arising, its meaning, resolving objections, and sequence; second, perfect oneself in the ten marks of a genuine teacher, from skill in the dharma's meaning to the power of patience; third, address the five assemblies through the eight kinds of skillful speech; fourth, settle the listener in respectful, undistorted attention through the ten reasons already given; and fifth, praise the Buddha's own virtues, briefly or at length, before teaching begins. A teacher complete in all five brings joy and benefit to speaker and listener alike, exactly as the five notes of music, sounded together, bring joy no single note could bring alone.

43. Section: Five Surpassing Benefits of This Training — Closing the Summary in Brief, and Explaining Well Entire

If one has trained well in this way, one should know that five surpassing benefits are thereby complete. First, the Buddha's own words and their meaning become no longer difficult to understand. Second, one can skillfully and completely explain the marks of all dharmas. Third, one can skillfully arouse vast joy in the continuum of both oneself and others. Fourth, one can draw beings toward wholesome release, up to praise filling both heaven and the human world. Fifth, one gives rise to measureless merit.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, one can skillfully and completely explain the marks of all dharmas. Third, one can skillfully arouse vast joy in the continuum of both oneself and others. Fourth, one can draw beings toward wholesome release, up to praise filling both heaven and the human world. Fifth, one gives rise to measureless merit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Five benefits, each tracing back to one part of the whole method just summarized. Grasping a sutra's text and meaning first makes the Buddha's own words no longer hard to understand. Mastering the five aspects of explanation lets one give a complete account of any dharma's marks. Cultivating the ten marks of a teacher and the eight kinds of speech across the five assemblies arouses real joy in both speaker and listener. Settling listeners in respectful, undistorted attention draws them toward wholesome release, their praise eventually filling heaven and the human world alike. And praising the Buddha, briefly or at length, gives rise to measureless merit in its own right. With this, the summary in brief — both the five-step sequence and its five benefits — is closed, and with it, explaining well, the second of the four topics under the sūtra's body and explanation, is closed entirely. The treatise turns next to a worked demonstration, applying the whole method to an actual sūtra passage.

Having explained the whole method in the abstract, the treatise now demonstrates it directly on an actual sūtra passage — of the kind found in the Sūtra of This Was Said, on abiding in pure discipline as the ground of concentration, wisdom, and release from birth-and-death. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Meaning unfolds in seven kinds; this section demonstrates the first, ground, matched line by line against the whole path a monastic disciple travels. The second of the ten kinds of meaning applied to the same four phrases. Discipline itself is what "the benefit of training" names directly. Wisdom's own mark is its own faculty of discernment; but wisdom never operates alone — the mind it depends on, its supporting conditions, its accompanying mental factors, and its object are all bound up with it, and these shared factors form its "shared characteristic" as distinct from wisdom's own essential nature. Liberation's mark is simply this: every seed of affliction permanently uprooted, "coarse residue" here naming the latent seed itself. And mindfulness's mark is holding a familiar object clearly in mind without forgetting, thereby advancing liberation further still. With this, the meaning of characteristic is closed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Discipline itself is not one of the seven attentive applications, but the very ground on which they are built — without it, calm-abiding and insight have nowhere to stand. Wisdom as foremost belongs to the two applications that begin the path of preparation, grasping the object's mark and forming resolve about it. Firm liberation spans the four remaining applications: withdrawal cuts off the coarsest afflictions, gathering joy the middling ones, the culmination of effort the subtlest, and the fruit of that culmination is liberation itself realized — the first three belonging to the uninterrupted path, the last to the path of liberation proper. And governing mindfulness names the application of observation, the careful checking that confirms affliction is genuinely, completely cut off, belonging to the path of distinction that follows. The treatise then extends this same method outward: matched against the ten kinds of wisdom (of dharmas, of their kind, of convention, of others' minds, and of each of the four truths, together with wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising), the six consciousnesses, the nine kinds of comprehension that mark affliction's cutting-off stage by stage, and the three gates of liberation (emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness) — training in discipline belongs to none of these, being still at the very ground stage; wisdom made foremost belongs to conventional wisdom and the mind consciousness; firm liberation, spanning the stages of seeing, cultivation, and the ultimate, draws in six or all ten kinds of wisdom according to stage, the earlier comprehensions and, at the ultimate stage, all nine, together with all three gates of liberation; and governing mindfulness belongs again to the mind consciousness alone. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The fourth kind of meaning, basis, unfolds through three lenses — the matter at hand, the person addressed, and the time — beginning here with the matter. Among the six root bases (good destiny, evil destiny, backsliding, advancement, birth-and-death, and nirvāṇa) and the five ways a compassionate teacher may guide (freeing from desire, demonstrating, instructing, encouraging, and rejoicing with), this sentence belongs to nirvāṇa and to pure conduct specifically, met with whichever form of guidance fits the listener. Yet strictly speaking, freedom from desire, wholesome conduct, non-painful conduct, conduct favoring advancement, and conduct serving one's own good all likewise lead toward nirvāṇa — pure conduct is simply named here as the most excellent among them, standing in for the rest.

Among the twenty-seven kinds of listener, this sūtra's own audience is the renunciant, of whatever faculty — dull, sharp, or in between, the Śrāvaka Ground distinguishing them by how readily each grasps the dharma's meaning and swiftly realizes the truth. As for the meaning of benefit: cultivating the three trainings, one swiftly attains perfection — this is the meaning of benefit.

The fifth and sixth kinds of meaning read the same sentence two ways at once: what benefit follows from heeding it, and what fault follows from ignoring it. Reading a passage for both its benefit and its fault together uncovers meaning that a single reading would miss. With this, the meanings of benefit and fault are closed.

61. Section: "Wisdom Is Foremost" Frees One from Craving Empty Renown — Three Kinds of Poorly Trained Renunciant

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Great Treasures Collection Sūtra

The Great Treasures Collection Sūtra names three poorly trained renunciants this phrase corrects: the "robe renunciant," outwardly in robes with head shaved and bowl in hand, yet inwardly impure in body, speech, and mind, stingy, envious, and lax in discipline; the "deceptive-deportment renunciant," whose flawless posture and gentle speech are performed purely to win better offerings, secretly fearful of and hostile toward teachings of true emptiness; and the "fame-seeking renunciant," who keeps discipline, studies, and practices solitude only to be seen doing so, never out of real renunciation or the wish for the path itself. The first two echo the previous phrase's warning against mere lineage and performance; this phrase in particular targets the third — chasing reputation rather than wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Ground of Thought Cultivation

A verse from the Ground of Thought Cultivation pictures the true monk: no cunning livelihood, valuing himself lightly, delighting in what is superior, every faculty fully released, homeless, possessionless, without expectation, desire cut off, walking alone — the true renunciant. Five virtues mark him: a livelihood free of scheming; contentment with little; a taste for superior discipline; the three roots of greed, hatred, and delusion exhausted; and liberation, every seed of affliction gone. And five things are eliminated: no home to return to; no sense of "mine" to cling to or hoard; no false claims made to sustain himself; sensual desire cut off entirely; and a mind that keeps company with no clinging to any object whatsoever. With this, all four kinds of monk this single sentence distinguishes — free of mere lineage or feigned form, free of craving for renown, and genuinely practicing toward firm liberation — are now fully shown.

63. Section: Why Wisdom Must Come First — Craving Renown Blocks Wisdom's Growth Even When the Teaching Is Heard — Closing This First Occasion

Why is this so? If one delights in and craves renown, then even if one exerts oneself to listen to the true dharma, wisdom does not grow. But if one is free of the fault just described, one becomes fit to be guided toward genuine right conduct and the taking hold of true liberation.

Chinese Source

所以者何？若有愛樂聲譽等者，雖自勉勵聽受正法，慧不增長。若有遠離前所說過，便於真實正行攝受正解脫中，堪任勸導。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this so? If one delights in and craves renown, then even if one exerts oneself to listen to the true dharma, wisdom does not grow. But if one is free of the fault just described, one becomes fit to be guided toward genuine right conduct and the taking hold of true liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Listening to the dharma in order to appear learned, rather than to remedy one's own afflictions, leaves wisdom unmoved no matter how diligently one attends — the motive itself blocks the very growth the practice is meant to produce. Only once that craving is set aside does a monk become someone the Buddha can actually guide toward genuine practice and real liberation. With this, the occasion for freeing beings from desire is closed.

64. Section: Encouragement Begins — Four Kinds of Monk Content with Too Little, Each Addressed by One Phrase — Closing the Whole Account of This Sūtra's Arising

And further, for those content with a lowly attainment, it encourages them to gradually train and advance. For those who delight in pursuing worldly literature and incantations, lax in discipline, it speaks of the benefit of training. For those who keep discipline but abandon broad learning, it speaks of wisdom as foremost. For those content merely with hearing and reflection, it speaks of liberation being firm. For those who fall into overweening conceit about discipline, wisdom, or liberation, it speaks of mindfulness as what governs.

Chinese Source

又為於下劣生喜足者，勸令漸漸修學增進。

為樂追求隨順世間文章呪術，於戒慢緩者，說學勝利；為守尸羅捨多聞者，說慧為上首；為唯於聞思生喜足者，說解脫堅固；為於戒慧解脫起增上慢者，說念為增上。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, for those content with a lowly attainment, it encourages them to gradually train and advance.

For those who fall into overweening conceit about discipline, wisdom, or liberation, it speaks of mindfulness as what governs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four further kinds of monk, each gently corrected by one phrase: one absorbed in worldly writing and magical arts rather than discipline is called back to the benefit of training itself; one who keeps discipline but neglects broad learning, mistaking discipline alone for the whole path, is reminded that wisdom, built on concentration built on discipline, must come foremost; one satisfied with mere intellectual understanding through hearing and reflection, never taking up sustained meditative practice, is urged toward firm liberation, the actual uprooting of affliction's seeds; and one who has practiced discipline, concentration, and wisdom yet mistakes some attainment along the way for realization itself is called back to mindfulness as governor — a constant, honest check on whether desire has truly been left behind. With this, every occasion behind the sūtra's four phrases — freeing from desire, and encouraging toward advancement — is now shown in full, closing the whole account of its arising. The treatise turns next to demonstrating its meaning, the third of the five aspects of explanation.

65. Section: Meaning Begins — the General Meaning: This Sūtra Teaches Both Right Conduct and Its Fruit

As for meaning: within the general meaning, one should know that this sūtra teaches both right conduct and the fruit of right conduct.

Chinese Source

義者，謂總義中，當知此經宣說正行及正行果。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for meaning: within the general meaning, one should know that this sūtra teaches both right conduct and the fruit of right conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having demonstrated the dharma itself and its arising, the treatise now demonstrates the third aspect, meaning, first in general and then in particular — beginning with the observation that the sentence teaches both the practice itself, the three trainings, and the fruit that practice bears.

66. Section: The Three Trainings Are the Very Limit of Training, and "Abiding" Names the Four Yogas of Right Effort

Thus discipline and the other two trainings should be known as the very limit of training. And the word "abiding" here reveals right effort, comprised within the four kinds of yoga. And further, "abiding in the three trainings" reveals the fruit of right conduct.

Chinese Source

如是戒等三學，當知是名學之邊際。又言如是住者，此顯正方便，四種瑜伽所攝。

又言如是住三學者，此顯正行果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus discipline and the other two trainings should be known as the very limit of training. And the word "abiding" here reveals right effort, comprised within the four kinds of yoga.

And further, "abiding in the three trainings" reveals the fruit of right conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the Śrāvaka Ground's account of the four yogas

Discipline, concentration, and wisdom mark the outer boundary of what training itself means — nothing more is asked of a trainee than to perfect these three. The single word "abiding" then packs in the four yogas that make such training actually work, each unfolding into four further aspects of its own: faith (two aspects of attitude and two grounds it rests on), aspiration (to realize, to inquire, to gather the necessary conditions, and to accord with yogic practice), effort (applied to hearing, to reflection, to cultivation, and to purifying obstacles), and skillful means (grounded in discipline, guarding one's own mindfulness, guarding the mind through vigilance, and cultivating calm-abiding and insight) — sixteen aspects in all, packed into that one small word. And read again, "abiding in the three trainings" names not the practice but its fruit: the very benefit of training, wisdom made foremost, and liberation made firm are themselves what steady abiding in the three trainings actually produces.

67. Section: Faith and Aspiration Come First, Then Discipline, Learning, Effort, and Wisdom as the Means — Closing the General Meaning

Here, faith and aspiration come first, followed by taking up discipline, hearing the teaching, effort, and wisdom, as the means.

Chinese Source

此中信欲為先，攝受尸羅、聽受、精進、慧等方便。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, faith and aspiration come first, followed by taking up discipline, hearing the teaching, effort, and wisdom, as the means.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Faith in the reality of nirvāṇa comes first, giving rise to genuine aspiration toward it; aspiration in turn drives two kinds of effort — abiding purely in discipline, and hearing the Buddha's own teaching — and two kinds of skillful means — insight and calm-abiding, the causes that draw forth concentration and the cutting-off of affliction. With this, the general meaning — that the sentence teaches both right conduct, practiced through these four yogas, and its fruit, purified discipline, concentration, and wisdom — is closed. The treatise turns next to the particular meaning of each phrase.

68. Section: The Particular Meaning Begins — "Training" Itself: Its Names, and Its Own Nature

Within the particular meaning, the word "training" means diligent effort practiced in accordance with the sacred teaching; "practice" and "cultivation" are its synonyms. Pure conduct of body and speech, and a right livelihood actually carried out, is training's own nature.

Chinese Source

於別義中，所言學者，是勤精進如聖教行，若習、若修名之差別。

清淨身語正命現行，是學自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the particular meaning, the word "training" means diligent effort practiced in accordance with the sacred teaching; "practice" and "cultivation" are its synonyms.

Pure conduct of body and speech, and a right livelihood actually carried out, is training's own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The particular meaning now takes each of the four phrases in turn, beginning with "training" itself. As a verb, training simply means diligent effort exercised according to the teaching, equally well called practice (repeated cultivation) or cultivation (formal practice). Its own nature — what makes discipline discipline, unshared with any other dharma — is pure bodily conduct (no killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct), pure speech (no lying, divisive, harsh, or idle talk), and a livelihood rightly obtained.

69. Section: Why It Is Called "Training" — Two Explanations, Both Tracing Cause to Fruit

Because this right conduct is brought forth through the cultivation of discipline, patience, and the rest, it is called training. And further, because it is for the sake of stillness and coolness, advancing what is wholesome and eliminating what is not, it is called training.

Chinese Source

由此正行，尸羅、忍辱等修顯發，故名為學。

又為寂靜及為清涼進習除滅，故名為學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because this right conduct is brought forth through the cultivation of discipline, patience, and the rest, it is called training.

And further, because it is for the sake of stillness and coolness, advancing what is wholesome and eliminating what is not, it is called training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Two explanations, each tracing training from cause to fruit. First: right conduct — pure body, speech, and livelihood — is itself a fruit, brought forth only through the causal practice of discipline, patience, and the rest; "training" as a verb names the causal practice, as a noun names the resulting fruit, one word carrying both. Second: discipline stills the mind's turbulence and cools its distress, but this stillness and coolness are themselves won only by diligently advancing every wholesome dharma not yet arisen and already arisen, while eliminating every unwholesome dharma before it arises and once it has — training, again, spanning cause and fruit together in a single vivid word.

70. Section: Five Further Ways to Gloss the Word — Mark, Nature, Function, Teaching, and Cause and Fruit — Closing the Account of "Training" Itself

Explanations of this kind, and terms like them, should further be understood, as before, through mark, through own-nature, through function, through the teaching, and through cause and fruit.

Chinese Source

如是等類訓釋名言，又應如前說相故、自性故、業故、法故，及因果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Explanations of this kind, and terms like them, should further be understood, as before, through mark, through own-nature, through function, through the teaching, and through cause and fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary referring to fascicle 81's own five-fold method of glossing terms

The same five-fold method for glossing any term, already given in fascicle 81, applies here to "training" itself: its mark, threefold — unbroken discipline actually upheld, discipline's own nature well guarded, and the further marks of flawless deportment before others and genuine dread of even the smallest fault; its own-nature, the pure bodily conduct, speech, and livelihood already named; its function, subduing affliction, the very basis on which calm-abiding and insight are established; the teaching, the body of names, phrases, and syllables that convey it; and cause and fruit, discipline kept purely being the near cause of a heavenly rebirth and the distant cause of nirvāṇa itself, with rebirth and nirvāṇa its respective fruits.

71. Section: Distinctions of Meaning Begin — Discipline by Its Own Nature: Seven Categories, Two Hundred Fifty Precepts and More

Within distinctions of meaning, as for the distinction of own-nature: "the benefit of training" reveals the seven categories of discipline, or more than two hundred fifty training-rules.

Chinese Source

義門差別中，自性差別者，謂學勝利是所顯示七品尸羅，或過二百五十學處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within distinctions of meaning, as for the distinction of own-nature: "the benefit of training" reveals the seven categories of discipline, or more than two hundred fifty training-rules.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

"The benefit of training" names discipline in general, but discipline itself differs by who holds it — the seven categories of individual liberation vows held respectively by monks, nuns, female probationers, novice monks, novice nuns, laymen, and laywomen. Counted in detail, a monk holds some two hundred fifty precepts, a nun some three hundred forty-eight, each community's own precepts differing in number and content — this is the distinction by own-nature.

72. Section: Discipline by Realm — Individual-Liberation Vows in the Desire Realm, Meditative Vows in Form and Formlessness, Contaminant-Free Vows Bound to No Realm at All

As for the distinction of realm: in conduct of the desire realm there are individual-liberation vows; in conduct of the form and formless realms there are meditative vows; contaminant-free vows are bound to no realm at all.

Chinese Source

界差別者，謂欲行中有別解脫律儀，色無色行中有靜慮律儀，無漏律儀非界所繫。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the distinction of realm: in conduct of the desire realm there are individual-liberation vows; in conduct of the form and formless realms there are meditative vows; contaminant-free vows are bound to no realm at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beings of the desire realm hold individual-liberation vows, each precept its own separate release from a corresponding affliction; beings of the form and formless realms, absorbed in meditative concentration, hold instead the discipline that arises together with concentration itself; and the noble ones, learners and adepts alike, who have attained contaminant-free concentration, hold a discipline arising with the path itself, bound to no realm of the three at all. With this, the distinction of realm is closed.

73. Section: Discipline Across Past, Present, and Future — Equally Excellent in All Three Times

As for the distinction of time: the benefit of training was trained in the past, will be trained in the future, and is being trained right now. One should know that this benefit of training is equal and undifferentiated across the past, future, and present.

Chinese Source

時差別者，謂學勝利過去已學，未來當學，現在正學。此學勝利，當知於去來今平等無異。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the distinction of time: the benefit of training was trained in the past, will be trained in the future, and is being trained right now. One should know that this benefit of training is equal and undifferentiated across the past, future, and present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline's own excellence never changes with the calendar — training already completed, training yet to come, and training happening this very moment are all equally worthy, so a practitioner has no excuse to relax simply because much time has already been spent in practice, nor to delay simply because the moment feels inconvenient. With this, the distinction of time is closed.

74. Section: Discipline by Stage of Ripening — Lower, Middle, and Upper, and by Feeling — Painful, Pleasant, and Neutral

As for the distinction of stage: among persons who have already entered the true dharma, the benefit of training in one not yet ripened is the lower stage; in one presently ripening, the middle stage; in one already ripened, the upper stage. If the mind takes no delight in it and one must force oneself to practice the pure conduct, this benefit of training is the painful stage. If the mind delights in it, practicing the pure conduct without needing to force oneself, this benefit of training is the pleasant stage. If one is neither delighted nor undelighted by the pure conduct, this benefit of training is the neither-painful-nor-pleasant stage.

Chinese Source

位差別者，謂已入正法補特伽羅諸學勝利，未成熟者是下位，正成熟者是中位，已成熟者是上位。

若心不喜樂勉勵修行諸梵行者，此學勝利是苦位；若心喜樂不自勉勵修梵行者，此學勝利是樂位；若於梵行非喜樂非不喜樂者，此學勝利是不苦不樂位。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the distinction of stage: among persons who have already entered the true dharma, the benefit of training in one not yet ripened is the lower stage; in one presently ripening, the middle stage; in one already ripened, the upper stage.

If the mind takes no delight in it and one must force oneself to practice the pure conduct, this benefit of training is the painful stage. If the mind delights in it, practicing the pure conduct without needing to force oneself, this benefit of training is the pleasant stage. If one is neither delighted nor undelighted by the pure conduct, this benefit of training is the neither-painful-nor-pleasant stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Stage differs first by how mature one's discipline has become — unripened, ripening, or fully ripened — and second by how it feels to practice it. Some must grit their teeth through pure conduct, disinclined but pressing on regardless — a painful stage, still genuine practice for all its reluctance. Others find pure conduct effortlessly delightful, temperamentally suited to it, tasting real benefit at every turn in discipline, concentration, and wisdom alike — the most excellent stage of the three. And others feel neither strong reluctance nor strong delight, simply following their companions in practice without complaint, faith present but joy not yet arisen — the neutral stage in between.

75. Section: Discipline Is Always Wholesome, Never Unwholesome or Indeterminate

And further, the benefit of training is entirely of the wholesome stage — never of the unwholesome stage, nor of the indeterminate stage.

Chinese Source

又學勝利皆是善位，非不善位、非無記位。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, the benefit of training is entirely of the wholesome stage — never of the unwholesome stage, nor of the indeterminate stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever the stage of ripening or the accompanying feeling, discipline that truly accords with the Buddha's teaching is by nature wholesome through and through — never unwholesome, and never merely neutral. With this, the distinctions by stage — of ripening, of feeling, and of moral quality — are closed.

76. Section: Discipline Through Hearing, Reflection, and Cultivation, and Through the Three Higher Trainings — Closing the Distinction of Stage

If one has not yet realized the higher mind or the higher wisdom, one stands only at the stage of higher discipline; once realized, one stands also at the stages of higher mind and higher wisdom. Distinctions such as these are called the distinction of stage.

Chinese Source

若聽受者是名聞位，若思惟者是名思位，若修習者是名修位。

若未證得增上心、慧，唯是增上戒位；若證得者，亦是增上心、慧二位。

如是等類，是位差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one has heard it taught, this is the stage of hearing; if one has reflected on it, this is the stage of reflection; if one has cultivated it, this is the stage of cultivation.

If one has not yet realized the higher mind or the higher wisdom, one stands only at the stage of higher discipline; once realized, one stands also at the stages of higher mind and higher wisdom.

Distinctions such as these are called the distinction of stage.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Two final lenses on stage. First, the threefold wisdom: simply hearing the benefits of discipline and forming the wish to receive it is the stage of hearing; reflecting afterward on what is permitted and what is not is the stage of reflection; and actually cultivating calm-abiding and insight once access concentration is reached, gaining discipline that arises with concentration, is the stage of cultivation. Second, the three higher trainings themselves stand in a nesting order: the higher wisdom already contains the higher mind and higher discipline beneath it, and the higher mind already contains higher discipline — so anyone still short of the higher mind and higher wisdom stands only at the stage of higher discipline, the foundation everything else builds on. With this, the distinction of stage — by maturity, by feeling, by moral quality, by wisdom, and by the three higher trainings, fifteen ways of viewing it in all — is closed.

77. Section: The Distinction of Person Begins — This Teaching Is Meant for Renunciants

As for the distinction of person: here the intended meaning is the renunciant person. Whether of dull faculty or of sharp faculty; whether given to greed and the like, or of balanced temperament, or of thin residue.

Chinese Source

補特伽羅差別者，此中意說出家補特伽羅。

或是鈍根、或是利根、或貪等行、或等分行、或薄塵行。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for the distinction of person: here the intended meaning is the renunciant person.

Whether of dull faculty or of sharp faculty; whether given to greed and the like, or of balanced temperament, or of thin residue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth and final distinction of meaning looks at the person addressed. Since the sūtra opens "you monks," its intended audience is renunciants specifically. Among them, faculties differ — dull or sharp in faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — as does temperament: some lean toward greed, hatred, or conceit, some carry all three afflictions in roughly equal measure ("balanced"), and some, through past cultivation of their remedies, meet even attractive objects without strong or sustained affliction, or if affliction arises it is mild and quickly let go ("thin residue"). Whatever the faculty or temperament, the Buddha instructs each renunciant skillfully according to their own capacity.

78. Section: Śrāvakas Alone, Not Solitary Buddhas or Bodhisattvas

It is śrāvakas alone, not solitary buddhas, and not bodhisattvas. Because solitary buddhas awaken separately, each on their own, and because bodhisattvas' liberation is already firm, communal training is not taught to them.

Chinese Source

唯是聲聞，非諸獨覺、非諸菩薩。

由彼獨覺別覺悟故，菩薩解脫為堅固故，不說共住修學勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is śrāvakas alone, not solitary buddhas, and not bodhisattvas.

Because solitary buddhas awaken separately, each on their own, and because bodhisattvas' liberation is already firm, communal training is not taught to them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Elucidation of the Sacred Teaching Treatise

A solitary buddha awakens alone, without a teacher and without a community to train alongside, so teaching on communal training simply does not apply. A bodhisattva of real attainment has already gone beyond the coarse afflictions and achieved a firmness surpassing a śrāvaka's own liberation, so the Tathāgata likewise has no need to teach communal training to bodhisattvas. This sentence, then, is addressed to śrāvaka monks specifically.

Distinctions such as these are called the distinction of person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Several more exclusions narrow the audience precisely: only those actually of the lineage capable of nirvāṇa, already inside the true dharma, and free of any obstructing view such as self-view; both ordinary beings still fully bound by affliction and learners at the first three fruits already partly free — but not arhats, already entirely unbound, for whom this teaching no longer applies; and human beings specifically, not the gods. With this, the distinction of person is closed, and with it, all five distinctions — own-nature, realm, time, stage, and person — as applied to "the benefit of training," the sentence's first phrase, are now fully demonstrated. The treatise turns next to the remaining three phrases.

80. Section: The Same Five Distinctions Apply to the Remaining Three Phrases — Closing the Entire Analysis of "Training"

Own-nature, realm, time, stage, and person — the same five-fold lens just applied at length to "the benefit of training" applies equally to wisdom, liberation, and mindfulness, though the treatise does not spell each one out again in full, leaving the reader to extend the same method. With this, the entire analysis of "training" — its name, its nature, why it is so called, the five ways of glossing any term, and these five distinctions of meaning — is closed. The treatise turns next to "benefit" itself, the second element of the phrase.

81. Section: "Benefit" Begins — Its Names, and Its Own Nature: Ten Benefits of Discipline

Here, "benefit" is a synonym for merit, advancement, and perfection. As it is said, one should contemplate the ten benefits — this is its own nature.

Chinese Source

此中勝利者，是功德、增進、圓滿名之差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "benefit" is a synonym for merit, advancement, and perfection.

As it is said, one should contemplate the ten benefits — this is its own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Benefit" may equally be called merit, advancement, or perfection — all naming the same reality from slightly different angles. Its own nature is spelled out as ten specific benefits discipline brings: gathering the monastic community together; keeping the community earnest; bringing the community ease; bringing faith to the faithless; deepening the faith of the faithful; taming the difficult to tame; bringing ease to those with a conscience; guarding against contaminants in this very life; destroying contaminants in lives to come; and causing the pure conduct to endure long for the benefit of many.

82. Section: Why It Is Called "Benefit" — Three Explanations

This dharma brings real advantage and is worthy of praise, hence it is called benefit. And further, this dharma is bound to follow any sentient being it arises in, hence it is called benefit. And further, this dharma is what praise itself follows, hence it is called benefit.

Chinese Source

此法能有饒益，應可稱讚，故名勝利。又復此法隨生有情定應隨逐，故名勝利。又復此法稱讚所隨，故名勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

This dharma brings real advantage and is worthy of praise, hence it is called benefit. And further, this dharma is bound to follow any sentient being it arises in, hence it is called benefit. And further, this dharma is what praise itself follows, hence it is called benefit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three reasons converge on the same name. Discipline genuinely helps both oneself and others, and so deserves to be called praiseworthy benefit. The pure seed it plants stays bound to the being who cultivated it through every future rebirth across the three realms, following wherever that being goes — benefit that never simply disappears. And discipline kept purely draws genuine praise that follows the practitioner as naturally as a shadow — benefit in the sense of a good name earned and kept. With this, the account of why the word "benefit" is used is closed.

As for distinctions of meaning, one should know there are ten: gathering the community together, keeping the community earnest, and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The ten distinctions here are simply the ten benefits already named as discipline's own nature — the Buddha, seeing these ten results clearly, taught discipline as what gathers the community together, in each of its further nine effects as well. With this, the account of "benefit" — its names, own nature, why it is so called, and its distinctions — is closed. The treatise turns next to "monk," the third element of the phrase.

84. Section: "Monk" Begins — Renunciant Names, and the Discipline Shared in Common as Its Own Nature

Here, "monk" is a synonym for renunciant, for one who has abandoned the household dharma and gone forth to homelessness, and the like. Being complete with the individual-liberation vows shared in common by the community is its own nature.

Chinese Source

此中苾芻者，是沙門、捨離家法趣非家等名之差別。

具足別解脫律儀眾同分，是其自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "monk" is a synonym for renunciant, for one who has abandoned the household dharma and gone forth to homelessness, and the like.

Being complete with the individual-liberation vows shared in common by the community is its own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise

Household life traps a being in the very cage where affliction breeds, blocking escape from the three realms — hence a monk is one who has abandoned that household dharma for homelessness, also called a mendicant, a breaker of afflictions, a renunciant, one who purely upholds discipline, and one who strikes fear into Māra. The two hundred fifty precepts shared in common by the monastic community make up a monk's own nature.

85. Section: Why It Is Called "Monk" — Diligence in Bearing, Guarding Against Evil Destinies, Free of Harm

Because of diligent effort even in one's outward bearing; because of guarding oneself out of fear of evil destinies; because of gathering without causing harm — hence it is called monk.

Chinese Source

於其形色勤精進故，怖畏惡趣自防守故，攝無損故，名為苾芻。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of diligent effort even in one's outward bearing; because of guarding oneself out of fear of evil destinies; because of gathering without causing harm — hence it is called monk.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Three marks explain the name. A monk's very bearing radiates diligent effort toward extinguishing greed, hatred, and delusion; fear of falling into an evil destiny keeps a monk guarding body, speech, and mind at every turn; and freedom from affliction and karmic fault means a monk gathers merit without harming self, other, or both, incurring no fault in this life, the next, or both together.

86. Section: Distinctions Among Monks — by Caste, by Family Standing, by Age — Closing the Account of "Monk"

As for distinctions of meaning: distinguished by kṣatriya and the other castes; distinguished by high or low family standing; distinguished by youth, middle age, and old age — these should be known as distinctions of meaning.

Chinese Source

門差別者，謂剎帝利等差別故，上族下族差別故，少中老年差別故，當知是門差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for distinctions of meaning: distinguished by kṣatriya and the other castes; distinguished by high or low family standing; distinguished by youth, middle age, and old age — these should be known as distinctions of meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Monks come from every caste of ancient India — Venerable Upāli from the lowest caste, Venerable Śāriputra from the priestly caste among them — and from every family standing, wealthy or humble, and every age, from under thirty to over fifty and beyond. With this, the account of "monk" — its names, nature, reason for the name, and distinctions — is closed. The treatise turns next to "abiding," the fourth and final element of the phrase.

87. Section: "Abiding" Begins — Yielding to the Occasion, Diligent Practice, and Discipline Itself as Its Nature

Here, "abiding" is a synonym for yielding to the occasion and diligently cultivating. This abiding's own nature is nothing apart from the training already explained.

Chinese Source

此中住者，是俯就於時精勤修習名之差別。

此住自性，離所說學，無有別法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "abiding" is a synonym for yielding to the occasion and diligently cultivating.

This abiding's own nature is nothing apart from the training already explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

"Yielding to the occasion" means setting aside personal preference to follow the community's own schedule, whatever the hour; "diligent" means never slacking while following it; "cultivating" means actually practicing discipline, concentration, and wisdom throughout. Its own nature is nothing other than the very discipline already explained under "training" — abiding purely in it, with no separate substance of its own.

88. Section: Why It Is Called "Abiding," and Its Distinctions by Deportment, Time of Day, and Day and Night — Closing "Abiding," and the Entire First Phrase

Gathering up every moment through one's various deportments — this is called abiding; this is its explanation. As for distinctions of meaning: distinguished by deportment; distinguished by morning, midday, and afternoon; distinguished by day and night — one should know these are called abiding's distinctions of meaning.

Chinese Source

種種威儀攝受時分，故名為住，此是訓詞。

門差別者，謂威儀差別故，朝中後分差別故，日夜差別故，當知是名住門差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Gathering up every moment through one's various deportments — this is called abiding; this is its explanation.

As for distinctions of meaning: distinguished by deportment; distinguished by morning, midday, and afternoon; distinguished by day and night — one should know these are called abiding's distinctions of meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever the moment — five-robe worn at labor, nine-robe worn while teaching, lying on the right side to sleep — discipline holds throughout every deportment, hence "abiding." A monk's day itself illustrates the distinctions: walking meditation and sitting before dawn, alms-round through late morning, more walking and sitting after the midday meal, dharma talks and self-examination in the afternoon — walking like the wind, sitting like a bell, lying like a bow, standing like a pine, day and night alike given over to purifying the mind and cutting off affliction. With this, "abiding" — its names, nature, reason for the name, and distinctions — is closed, and with it, the analysis of all four elements of the sūtra's first phrase — training, benefit, monk, and abiding — is complete. The treatise turns next to the second phrase, "wisdom is foremost."

89. Section: "Wisdom" Begins — Its Names, and Discernment as Its Own Nature

Here, "wisdom" is a synonym for knowledge, seeing, clarity, and direct realization. Discernment of the marks of dharmas, a mental factor, is its own nature.

Chinese Source

此中慧者，是智、見、明、現觀等名之差別。

簡擇法相心所有法，為其自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "wisdom" is a synonym for knowledge, seeing, clarity, and direct realization.

Discernment of the marks of dharmas, a mental factor, is its own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary and the Abhidharma Compendium's own gloss

The Great Compendium of Abhidharma explains: all these names point to a single reality, described differently depending on what each word remedies — "knowledge" remedies not-knowing, "seeing" remedies wrong views, "clarity" remedies ignorance, "realization" remedies wrong understanding directly confronted. Wisdom may equally be called patient acceptance. Its own nature, as a mental factor accompanying the mind, is simply the capacity to discern the particular and shared marks of dharmas — this or that action right or wrong, wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral.

90. Section: Why It Is Called "Wisdom" — Three Reasons, and Its Distinctions Following the Pattern Already Given

As for its explanation: because of its nature of discernment, and because it remedies not-knowing, it is called wisdom. And further, because it can distinguish and understand every category of difference, it is called wisdom. And further, because it can reveal that the wise are themselves of a wise nature, it is called wisdom. As for its distinctions of meaning, these should be established as is fitting, exactly as established before.

Chinese Source

訓詞者，簡擇性故，治無智故，名之為慧。又各品別能了知故，名之為慧。又能顯了諸聰慧者是聰慧性，故名為慧。

門差別者，隨其所應，如前安立。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for its explanation: because of its nature of discernment, and because it remedies not-knowing, it is called wisdom. And further, because it can distinguish and understand every category of difference, it is called wisdom. And further, because it can reveal that the wise are themselves of a wise nature, it is called wisdom.

As for its distinctions of meaning, these should be established as is fitting, exactly as established before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground

Three reasons for the name: positively, wisdom discerns; negatively, it remedies ignorance; further, it distinguishes every kind of dharma without exception; and further still, it shows that a genuinely wise person's body, speech, and mind are all themselves marked by that same wise nature — as the Śrāvaka Ground puts it, the wise think well what they think, say well what they say, and do well what they do. With this, the account of "wisdom" — its names, nature, and reason for the name — is closed, its distinctions of meaning left to follow the same five-fold pattern (own-nature, realm, time, stage, and person) already demonstrated in full for "training." The treatise turns next to "liberation."

91. Section: "Liberation" Begins — Permanent Severance, Freedom from Bondage, Purity, Extinction, Freedom from Desire, and the Two Kinds of Nirvāṇa

Here, "liberation" is a synonym for permanent severance, freedom from bondage, purity, extinction, freedom from desire, and the like.

Chinese Source

此中解脫者，是永斷、離繫、清淨、滅盡、離欲等名之差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "liberation" is a synonym for permanent severance, freedom from bondage, purity, extinction, freedom from desire, and the like.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Permanent severance names the seeds of craving and views cut off for good; freedom from bondage names release from the three realms' grip; purity names a mind free of defilement; extinction names two aspects at once — cessation, since a residue of the ripened body still remains, and exhaustion, since every craving tied to any contaminated dharma is entirely gone; and freedom from desire names release from the desire realm's own craving specifically. "And the like" points further to the two nirvāṇas: nirvāṇa with residue, an arhat's contaminants exhausted while the body that ripened from past karma still remains, and nirvāṇa without residue, when even that ripened result is finally relinquished and all suffering stilled for good — both themselves further names for liberation.

92. Section: Liberation's Own Nature — the Permanent Destruction of Coarse Residue, Affliction Permanently Severed

Its own nature is the permanent destruction of coarse residue, affliction permanently severed.

Chinese Source

自性者，謂麤重永害，煩惱永斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

Its own nature is the permanent destruction of coarse residue, affliction permanently severed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whether latent seed or active manifestation, affliction's every trace is permanently gone — this alone is what liberation's own nature actually consists in.

93. Section: Why It Is Called "Liberation" — Freed from Every Bond, and the Very Nature of Stillness Itself for Every Kind of Sage

As for its explanation: because it can free one from the bonds of craving and the rest, it is called liberation. And further, because the Bhagavān taught this to every kind of sage as the very nature of sagely stillness, it is called liberation.

Chinese Source

訓詞者，謂能脫種種貪等繫縛，故名解脫。又復世尊，為種種牟尼說此以為牟尼體性，故名解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for its explanation: because it can free one from the bonds of craving and the rest, it is called liberation. And further, because the Bhagavān taught this to every kind of sage as the very nature of sagely stillness, it is called liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Craving, hatred, and delusion bind beings as tightly as rope; freedom from that binding is liberation in the plainest sense. "Muni," the word for sage, itself means stillness — not the empty silence of one simply unaware, but the genuine quiet of one whose wisdom, understanding, and right view are all complete. The 披尋記 glosses "every kind of sage" as the three stillnesses of body, speech, and mind: bodily stillness, no restless movement; verbal stillness, no idle speech; mental stillness, discrimination itself finally at rest — three sagely stillnesses that liberation alone makes possible, which is why the Buddha taught liberation as stillness's own true nature.

94. Section: Seven Kinds of Liberation — Closing the Account of Liberation Entirely

As for distinctions of meaning: liberation dependent on occasion, unshakable liberation, liberation cut off at the path of seeing, liberation cut off at the path of cultivation, liberation from the conduct of the desire realm, liberation from the conduct of the form realm, and liberation from the conduct of the formless realm. Distinctions of meaning such as these should be known as before.

Chinese Source

門差別者，謂待時解脫、不動解脫、見所斷解脫、修所斷解脫、欲行解脫、色行解脫、無色行解脫。

如是等類義門差別，如前應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for distinctions of meaning: liberation dependent on occasion, unshakable liberation, liberation cut off at the path of seeing, liberation cut off at the path of cultivation, liberation from the conduct of the desire realm, liberation from the conduct of the form realm, and liberation from the conduct of the formless realm.

Distinctions of meaning such as these should be known as before.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with citations from the Abhidharma treatises

Seven kinds of liberation, ranging from dull to sharp faculty and from partial to complete: liberation dependent on occasion belongs to a dull-faculty arhat, whose peace still depends on suitable robes, food, lodging, and company; unshakable liberation belongs to a sharp-faculty arhat, undisturbed whatever the outer circumstance; liberation cut off at the path of seeing belongs to the stream-enterer, who has severed the discriminated afflictions; liberation cut off at the path of cultivation belongs to the once-returner and beyond, gradually severing the innate afflictions that remain; liberation from the conduct of the desire realm belongs to one approaching the third fruit; liberation from the conduct of the form realm always implies the previous; and liberation from the conduct of the formless realm, an arhat's freedom even from attachment to formless absorption, always implies both the previous two. With this, liberation — its names, own nature, reason for the name, and sevenfold distinction — is closed, its finer distinctions again following the same five-fold pattern already given. The treatise turns next to "mindfulness," the final element of "mindfulness is what governs."

95. Section: "Mindfulness" Begins — Not Forgetting, and Clear Recollection as Its Own Nature

Here, "mindfulness" is a synonym for not forgetting, and for the mind's own clear recollection. Its own nature is a mental factor.

Chinese Source

此中念者，是不忘失、心明記憶名之差別。

自性者，是心所有法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "mindfulness" is a synonym for not forgetting, and for the mind's own clear recollection.

Its own nature is a mental factor.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Extended Treatise on the Five Aggregates

"Not forgetting" states it negatively, "clear recollection" positively — both naming the same mental factor, defined as not losing a familiar object from mind, holding it clearly, and thereby supporting freedom from distraction.

96. Section: Why It Is Called "Mindfulness" — Recollecting Dharmas, and Clear Memory Wherever Attention Has Gone

As for its explanation: because it recollects dharmas, it is called mindfulness. And further, because it follows wherever an experience has gone and whatever attention was applied, and thereby brings about clear recollection, it is called mindfulness.

Chinese Source

訓詞者，追憶諸法，故名為念。又隨所經事，隨其作意，由此能令明了記憶，故名為念。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for its explanation: because it recollects dharmas, it is called mindfulness. And further, because it follows wherever an experience has gone and whatever attention was applied, and thereby brings about clear recollection, it is called mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Mindfulness recollects dharmas already encountered through the sixth consciousness, and follows wherever the mind's attention has actually gone, keeping what was experienced clearly and accurately in memory.

97. Section: Six Recollections and the Four Foundations of Mindfulness — Closing "Mindfulness," and the Entire Meaning of the Sūtra

As for distinctions of meaning: recollection of the Buddha, recollection of the dharma, and so on, at length, through six kinds of recollection. So too the distinctions of the foundations of mindfulness should be understood as elaborated at length. And further, as before, these should be known as differing according to what is fitting.

Chinese Source

門差別者，謂佛隨念、法隨念等，乃至廣說六種隨念。如是如念住差別，當知廣說差別。

As for distinctions of meaning: recollection of the Buddha, recollection of the dharma, and so on, at length, through six kinds of recollection. So too the distinctions of the foundations of mindfulness should be understood as elaborated at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Six recollections — of the Buddha, the dharma, the saṅgha, generosity, discipline, and the heavens — each unfold mindfulness in their own direction, just as the four foundations of mindfulness (of body, feeling, mind, and dharmas) each unfold it in theirs, contemplating the body as impure, feeling as suffering, mind as impermanent, and dharmas as without self. With this, "mindfulness" — its names, nature, reason for the name, and distinctions — is closed, closing the entire particular meaning of all four phrases (training, wisdom, liberation, and mindfulness), and with it, the whole demonstration of meaning, the third of the five aspects of explanation. The treatise turns next to demonstrating the fourth aspect: resolving objections.

98. Section: Resolving Objections Begins — What Does "the Benefit of Training" Actually Mean?

Further, within resolving objections: Question — what is the meaning of "the benefit of training"? Answer — this expression reveals abiding in the sight of surpassing merit within the higher training of discipline.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, within resolving objections: Question — what is the meaning of "the benefit of training"? Answer — this expression reveals abiding in the sight of surpassing merit within the higher training of discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having demonstrated the dharma, its arising, and its meaning, the treatise now demonstrates the fourth aspect of explanation, resolving objections, through a question-and-answer format applied to the same sentence. Every word is, strictly speaking, only a conventional label laid over reality for the sake of communication — "the benefit of training" is exactly such a label, and what it points to is simply this: abiding in pure discipline reveals surpassing merit directly seen, not merely believed.

99. Section: What Does "Wisdom Is Foremost" Mean? — Wisdom, First Among the Five Faculties

Question — what is the meaning of "wisdom is foremost"? Answer — this expression reveals that, among the faculties, the faculty of wisdom is foremost.

Chinese Source

問：慧為上首者，義何謂耶？答：此增語顯示，於諸根中慧根第一。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — what is the meaning of "wisdom is foremost"? Answer — this expression reveals that, among the faculties, the faculty of wisdom is foremost.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Among the five faculties — faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — wisdom is what the phrase singles out as foremost, the one faculty that finally cuts through affliction where the other four alone cannot.

100. Section: What Does "Liberation Is Firm" Mean, and What Does "Mindfulness Is What Governs" Mean? — Closing Resolving the Non-Definitive-Meaning Objection

Question — what is the meaning of "liberation is firm"? Answer — this expression reveals the permanent severance of afflictions cut off at both the path of seeing and the path of cultivation. Question — what is the meaning of "mindfulness is what governs"? Answer — this expression reveals never being satisfied with a small or inferior attainment.

Chinese Source

問：解脫堅固者，義何謂耶？答：此增語顯示，見、修所斷煩惱永斷。

問：念為增上者，義何謂耶？答：此增語顯示，於少下劣不生喜足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — what is the meaning of "liberation is firm"? Answer — this expression reveals the permanent severance of afflictions cut off at both the path of seeing and the path of cultivation.

Question — what is the meaning of "mindfulness is what governs"? Answer — this expression reveals never being satisfied with a small or inferior attainment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Liberation counts as firm only once every affliction cut off at both the path of seeing and the path of cultivation is permanently gone. And mindfulness governs precisely by refusing to settle for whatever small progress has already been made — the same observation-application already explained, now made explicit: a practitioner should constantly check, reflect on, and measure their own present attainment, pressing always toward what remains incomplete. With this, all four phrases have now been questioned and answered in turn, closing the demonstration of resolving the non-definitive-meaning objection. The treatise turns next to resolving the objection of apparent contradiction between differing accounts.

101. Section: Why Discipline Is Followed by Wisdom Here, Not by Concentration as Elsewhere

Question — in other sūtras, the Bhagavān teaches the sequence of the three trainings differently. Why here, after the higher discipline, does he speak of the higher wisdom, rather than the higher mind? Answer — here it reveals abiding in the benefit of training. By this statement, it reveals that the wisdom accomplished through hearing and the rest gathers in freedom from regret and the rest; through this, concentration is gradually attained — this itself reveals the higher training of mind.

Chinese Source

問：於餘經中，三學次第世尊異說。何故此中，增上戒後說增上慧，非增上心？

答：此中顯示住學勝利。由此言說，顯聞等所成慧，攝受無悔等；由此漸次得三摩地，即是顯示增上心學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — in other sūtras, the Bhagavān teaches the sequence of the three trainings differently. Why here, after the higher discipline, does he speak of the higher wisdom, rather than the higher mind?

Answer — here it reveals abiding in the benefit of training. By this statement, it reveals that the wisdom accomplished through hearing and the rest gathers in freedom from regret and the rest; through this, concentration is gradually attained — this itself reveals the higher training of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Most sūtras teach discipline, then concentration, then wisdom in order; this one moves straight from discipline to wisdom, skipping mind. The answer: "wisdom is foremost" already implicitly carries concentration within it, since the wisdom born of hearing and reflection makes pure discipline possible, and the freedom from regret and quiet joy that pure discipline brings then leads, step by step, to concentration itself and the wisdom born of cultivation — so concentration, though unnamed, is already folded into the single phrase.

102. Section: The Faculty of Wisdom Governs All Five Faculties — Closing the Sequence Objection's First Half

As the Bhagavān said: among these five faculties, the one that most gathers in what is to be gathered is the faculty of wisdom. Because monks who accomplish the faculty of wisdom are thereby able to cultivate the faculty of concentration, and so on — one should know that accomplishing the faculty of concentration is entirely due to the power of the faculty of wisdom. Now in this sūtra, the Bhagavān reveals that the faculty of wisdom is the cause that draws forth concentration, and the cause that draws forth the severance of affliction — the higher training of mind and the higher training of wisdom are spoken of together, at once.

Chinese Source

如世尊說：於是五根，最能攝受所攝受者，所謂慧根。由諸苾芻成就慧根，乃至能修定根；如是乃至成就定根，當知皆是慧根之力。今此經中，世尊顯示慧根是三摩地引因，及煩惱斷引因，增上心學與增上慧學俱時而說。

Ru-Yi Translation

As the Bhagavān said: among these five faculties, the one that most gathers in what is to be gathered is the faculty of wisdom. Because monks who accomplish the faculty of wisdom are thereby able to cultivate the faculty of concentration, and so on — one should know that accomplishing the faculty of concentration is entirely due to the power of the faculty of wisdom. Now in this sūtra, the Bhagavān reveals that the faculty of wisdom is the cause that draws forth concentration, and the cause that draws forth the severance of affliction — the higher training of mind and the higher training of wisdom are spoken of together, at once.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wisdom is like an eye — the sharper it grows, the more clearly a practitioner sees which afflictions are present and how to remove them, which is exactly why the other four faculties (faith, effort, mindfulness, and concentration) all mature under wisdom's own guidance: greater wisdom yields firmer faith, more accurate effort, steadier mindfulness, and more easily attained concentration. Along the eightfold noble path this same primacy holds — right view and right intention are wisdom, right speech, action, and livelihood are discipline, right effort and right mindfulness and right concentration are concentration — and wisdom runs through the whole path start to finish. With this, the objection of sequence — why wisdom follows discipline directly, without naming mind — is fully resolved.

103. Section: Why This Sūtra Does Not Speak of the Higher Mind's Own Perfection — Closing the Sequence Objection Entirely

Question — other sūtras teach that cultivating the three trainings leads to their perfection. Why does this sūtra not speak of the perfection of the higher training of mind? Answer — as already explained, one should know the same principle applies here as well.

Chinese Source

問：餘經中說三學修習，進趣圓滿。何故不說增上心學修習圓滿耶？答：如前所說，當知此中道理亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — other sūtras teach that cultivating the three trainings leads to their perfection. Why does this sūtra not speak of the perfection of the higher training of mind? Answer — as already explained, one should know the same principle applies here as well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The same answer as before applies again: since "wisdom is foremost" already reveals wisdom as the very cause that draws forth both concentration and the severance of affliction, the higher training of mind is already implicitly included, with no need to name its perfection separately. With this, the objection of apparent contradiction over sequence is closed in full. The treatise turns next to resolving the objection of apparent contradiction in reasoning.

104. Section: Resolving the Reasoning Objection Begins — Why "Benefit" Names Only Discipline, Not Wisdom or Liberation

And further, the ten benefits — gathering the community, keeping the community earnest, and the rest — are plainly evident and easily grasped; this is why only the benefit of training is named.

答：於下劣中勸取勝利，當知亦令所化有情，於勝妙中攝受勝利。

又攝受於僧、令僧精懇等十種勝利，分明易了，易可悟入，是故但說住學勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — why does this sūtra speak only of the benefit of training, and not the benefit of wisdom, or the benefit of liberation?

Answer — encouraging one to take up benefit even in what is lesser thereby also leads the beings taught to gather in benefit within what is excellent and sublime.

And further, the ten benefits — gathering the community, keeping the community earnest, and the rest — are plainly evident and easily grasped; this is why only the benefit of training is named.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline is, relatively speaking, the humblest of the three trainings, the foundation the other two are built on — praising its benefit encourages a beginner to secure that foundation first, while implicitly pointing toward the more excellent benefits of concentration and wisdom still to come. And its ten concrete benefits, from gathering the community to sustaining the pure conduct long into the future, are simply the easiest of the three trainings' rewards to see and understand directly — reason enough to single discipline out here.

105. Section: Why "Foremost" Names Wisdom, Not Liberation Itself — Closing the Reasoning Objection Entirely

Question — liberation is the most excellent of all dharmas. For what reason, then, does the sūtra speak only of wisdom as foremost, not liberation as foremost? Answer — encouraging one to take up foremost status even in what is lesser thereby also leads the beings taught to gather in foremost status within what is excellent and sublime. And further, it reveals a distinct, uncommon merit belonging to liberation. What is this uncommon, distinct merit? Amid impermanent, unsurpassed wisdom, liberation, being permanent, is the most firm of all.

Chinese Source

問：夫解脫者，於諸法中最為殊勝。何因緣故，但說住慧上首，不說住解脫上首？

答：於下劣中勸取上首性，當知亦令所化有情，於勝妙中攝受上首性。

又於解脫，顯示不共差別功德故。何等名為不共差別功德？謂於無常無上慧邊，解脫常故，最為堅固。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — liberation is the most excellent of all dharmas. For what reason, then, does the sūtra speak only of wisdom as foremost, not liberation as foremost?

Answer — encouraging one to take up foremost status even in what is lesser thereby also leads the beings taught to gather in foremost status within what is excellent and sublime.

And further, it reveals a distinct, uncommon merit belonging to liberation. What is this uncommon, distinct merit? Amid impermanent, unsurpassed wisdom, liberation, being permanent, is the most firm of all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wisdom is, relatively speaking, lesser than liberation, being only its preparatory cause — naming wisdom as foremost implicitly points toward liberation as more excellent still, exactly as discipline pointed toward wisdom and liberation before it. But there is a deeper reason too: wisdom, however unsurpassed, is still a conditioned dharma, itself impermanent, arising through the very contemplation of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self that eventually yields liberation; liberation itself, by contrast, is an unconditioned dharma, permanent, blissful, possessed of true self, and pure — which is exactly why the sūtra pairs "liberation" with "firm" rather than with "foremost," reserving "foremost" for wisdom's conditioned excellence and "firm" for liberation's unconditioned constancy. With this, the objection of apparent contradiction in reasoning is closed in full. The treatise turns next to resolving the objection of uncertain, non-definitive demonstration.

106. Section: Resolving the Uncertain-Demonstration Objection Begins — What, Precisely, Counts as "the Benefit of Training"?

Question — what precisely is called "the benefit of training"? Answer — within the training-grounds so laid down, contemplating the ten benefits, constantly guarding discipline, firmly guarding discipline, always practicing it and always turning it over in mind — this is what is called "the benefit of training." Question — what meaning do phrases such as "gathering the community" and the rest carry?

Chinese Source

問：何等名為住學勝利？答：如所施設諸學處中，觀十勝利，常守尸羅，堅守尸羅，常作常轉，如是名為住學勝利。

問：攝受於僧等諸句有何義耶？

答：攝受於僧者，是總句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question — what precisely is called "the benefit of training"? Answer — within the training-grounds so laid down, contemplating the ten benefits, constantly guarding discipline, firmly guarding discipline, always practicing it and always turning it over in mind — this is what is called "the benefit of training."

Question — what meaning do phrases such as "gathering the community" and the rest carry? Answer — "gathering the community" is the general statement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whatever precepts a practitioner holds — the five precepts, the ten, or the fuller vows of a monk, nun, or novice — genuinely abiding in the benefit of training means contemplating the ten benefits, keeping discipline both constantly and firmly, and sustaining two further qualities: unbroken practice, and returning to purity through confession whenever a lapse occurs. A second question then opens the ten benefits themselves for individual treatment, beginning with the first: "gathering the community" stands as the general statement under which the remaining nine will each be shown to fall.

107. Section: Keeping the Community Earnest, and Bringing the Community Ease

"Keeping the community earnest" means freeing it from the extreme of indulging in sensual pleasure. "Bringing the community ease" means freeing it from the extreme of self-mortification.

Chinese Source

令僧精懇者，令離受用欲樂邊故。

令僧安樂者，令離受用自苦邊故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Keeping the community earnest" means freeing it from the extreme of indulging in sensual pleasure.

"Bringing the community ease" means freeing it from the extreme of self-mortification.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Precepts governing robes, food, and dwelling steer a monk away from indulgent attachment to comfort, keeping practice earnest and diligent; and precepts permitting reasonable exceptions — medicine when ill, for instance — steer a monk away from the opposite extreme of needless austerity, unlike outside ascetic paths built on rigid self-denial. The middle way runs through discipline itself.

108. Section: Bringing Faith to the Faithless, and Deepening the Faith of the Faithful

"Bringing faith to the faithless" means causing those without pure faith to give rise to pure faith, and those who have not entered the true dharma to enter it. "Deepening the faith of the faithful" means causing those with pure faith to grow further, and those who have entered the true dharma to ripen.

Chinese Source

未淨信者令生淨信者，未入正法者令入正法故。

已淨信者令增長者，已入正法者令成熟故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Bringing faith to the faithless" means causing those without pure faith to give rise to pure faith, and those who have not entered the true dharma to enter it.

"Deepening the faith of the faithful" means causing those with pure faith to grow further, and those who have entered the true dharma to ripen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A community visibly upholding pure discipline, body and speech dignified by it, naturally draws faith from those who had none, and draws the already faithful further still — the outward dignity of discipline kept doing quiet missionary work of its own.

109. Section: Taming the Difficult to Tame, and Bringing Ease to Those Who Feel Shame

"Taming the difficult to tame" means those who violate discipline are rightly expelled. "Bringing ease to those who feel shame" means one who purely keeps discipline is freed of regret.

Chinese Source

難調伏者令調伏者，犯尸羅者善驅擯故。

令慚愧者安樂住者，淨持戒者令無悔故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Taming the difficult to tame" means those who violate discipline are rightly expelled.

"Bringing ease to those who feel shame" means one who purely keeps discipline is freed of regret.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Skillfully expelling one who gravely violates discipline protects the wider community from disruption to their own practice; and a monk with a genuine conscience, quick to confess any lapse rather than conceal it, finds real ease and freedom from regret precisely through that confession and the purity it restores.

110. Section: Guarding Against Contaminants in This Very Life, and Destroying Them in Lives to Come

"Guarding against contaminants in this very life" means according with the subduing of affliction's active manifestation. "Destroying contaminants in lives to come" means stilling the false aspiration for continued existence through pure conduct, according with the permanent severance of affliction's latent tendency.

Chinese Source

防現法漏者，隨順摧伏煩惱纏故。

害後法漏者，止息邪願修梵行故，隨順永斷惑隨眠故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Guarding against contaminants in this very life" means according with the subduing of affliction's active manifestation.

"Destroying contaminants in lives to come" means stilling the false aspiration for continued existence through pure conduct, according with the permanent severance of affliction's latent tendency.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline alone cannot uproot affliction's seeds, but it does subdue affliction's active manifestation in the present, and by supporting calm-abiding and insight, it prepares the higher trainings of mind and wisdom that alone can sever those seeds for good — not to secure a heavenly rebirth, which was never the Buddha's aim in setting forth the precepts, but to prepare the noble path itself.

111. Section: Causing the Pure Conduct to Endure Long for Many — Closing All Ten Benefits Individually Explained

"Causing the pure conduct to endure long for many, spreading and increasing further still, up to being rightly and skillfully proclaimed for gods and humans alike" means causing the sacred teaching to continue unbroken across a long span of time.

Chinese Source

為令多人梵行久住，轉得增廣，乃至為諸天人正善開示者，為令聖教長時相續無斷絕故。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Causing the pure conduct to endure long for many, spreading and increasing further still, up to being rightly and skillfully proclaimed for gods and humans alike" means causing the sacred teaching to continue unbroken across a long span of time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The tenth and final benefit looks beyond any single practitioner to the dharma's own survival — discipline preserved lets pure conduct spread among many, be taught rightly to gods and humans alike, and keep the sacred teaching itself alive, unbroken, across generations. With this, all ten benefits of discipline are now individually explained in full. The treatise turns next to gathering them into a threefold summary.

112. Section: The Ten Benefits Gathered into Three — an Undefiled Community, an Ease-Filled Community, and the Sacred Teaching's Long Continuance

These ten benefits may be gathered in brief into three, and these same three may be opened out at length into ten. What are the three? First, keeping the community free of defilement; second, bringing the community ease; third, causing the Buddha's sacred teaching to continue for a long time.

Chinese Source

如是十種勝利略攝為三，即此三種廣開為十。

何等為三？

一者、令僧無染汙住，二者、令僧得安樂住，三者、令佛聖教長時隨轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

These ten benefits may be gathered in brief into three, and these same three may be opened out at length into ten.

What are the three?

First, keeping the community free of defilement; second, bringing the community ease; third, causing the Buddha's sacred teaching to continue for a long time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first nine benefits already explained all serve either an undefiled or an ease-filled community; the tenth alone serves the sacred teaching's own long continuance. The treatise now turns to explaining how the first nine accomplish their shared twofold purpose.

113. Section: Seven Kinds of Protective Guarding Behind the First Nine Benefits

Among these, seven kinds of protective guarding reveal freedom from defilement and the bringing of ease. The seven kinds of protective guarding are: first, guarding against reverence and support; second, guarding against self-mortification; third, guarding against scarcity of provisions; fourth, guarding against mutual friction; fifth, guarding against regretful change of mind; sixth, guarding against affliction's active manifestation; seventh, guarding against false aspiration.

Chinese Source

此中由七種隨護，顯示無染汙住及安樂住。

七種隨護者，一、敬養隨護，二、自苦行隨護，三、資財乏少隨護，四、展轉相觸隨護，五、心追變隨護，六、煩惱纏隨護，七、邪願隨護。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, seven kinds of protective guarding reveal freedom from defilement and the bringing of ease.

The seven kinds of protective guarding are: first, guarding against reverence and support; second, guarding against self-mortification; third, guarding against scarcity of provisions; fourth, guarding against mutual friction; fifth, guarding against regretful change of mind; sixth, guarding against affliction's active manifestation; seventh, guarding against false aspiration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline guards a practitioner from seven distinct dangers, each mapping back onto the nine benefits already explained: guarding against the craving that reverence and material support can breed, so the community stays free of desire and earnestly engaged; guarding against the harm of needless austerity, keeping to the middle way rather than an outsider's fruitless self-denial; guarding against the harm scarcity itself might otherwise cause, since a monk content with little inspires faith in the faithless and confidence in the faithful precisely by that visible contentment; guarding against mutual friction, since expelling the incorrigible protects the rest of the community's peace to practice; guarding against regretful change of mind, since confession restores real ease once shame is honestly faced; guarding against affliction's active manifestation, subduing it before it disrupts practice; and guarding against the false aspiration for a heavenly rebirth, keeping the aim fixed on nirvāṇa instead. Together these seven protections are what actually deliver the freedom from defilement and the ease named among the first nine benefits.

114. Section: The Tenth Benefit, and How All Seven Protections Map onto the First Nine — Closing the Whole Account of the Ten Benefits

The last statement reveals the sacred teaching's long continuance.

Chinese Source

最後一句，顯示聖教長時隨轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

The last statement reveals the sacred teaching's long continuance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The 披尋記 maps each of the seven protections onto specific benefits among the first nine: guarding against reverence and support maps to the first two (gathering the community, keeping it earnest); guarding against self-mortification maps to the third (bringing ease); guarding against scarcity maps to the fourth and fifth (bringing and deepening faith); guarding against mutual friction maps to the sixth (taming the difficult); guarding against regretful change of mind maps to the seventh (ease for the ashamed); guarding against affliction's manifestation maps to the eighth (guarding against present contaminants); and guarding against false aspiration maps to the ninth (destroying future contaminants) — while the tenth and final benefit, the sacred teaching's own long continuance, stands apart, served by all nine together rather than mapped to any single protection. With this, the ten benefits of discipline, both individually and gathered into three, and the seven protections beneath them, are fully explained. Having seen these benefits, a monk naturally comes to delight in keeping discipline — the treatise turns next to five concrete marks of what that discipline-keeping actually looks like.

115. Section: What Is Constant Guarding of Discipline? — Never Abandoning the Training-Ground

What is constant guarding of discipline? It means not abandoning the training-ground.

Chinese Source

云何常守尸羅？謂不棄捨學處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is constant guarding of discipline? It means not abandoning the training-ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Ground of Thought Cultivation

A verse from the Ground of Thought Cultivation captures all five marks in four lines: never abandoning it, unshaken even facing death, always dwelling in right conduct, ever turning in accord with the discipline. "Never abandoning it" names this first mark — never once returning to lay life, holding the full precepts, monastic or bodhisattva, without interruption to the very end.

116. Section: What Is Firm Guarding of Discipline? — Never Violating the Training-Ground, Even Facing Death

What is firm guarding of discipline? It means not violating the training-ground.

Chinese Source

云何堅守尸羅？謂不毀犯學處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is firm guarding of discipline? It means not violating the training-ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Trusting discipline as the very root of unsurpassed awakening and truly understanding its benefits, a practitioner holds it firmly enough to never violate it — the same verse's second line, "unshaken even facing death": willing to die keeping discipline rather than live having broken it.

117. Section: What Is Constant Practice? — No Breach in the Training-Ground

What is constant practice? It means no breach in the training-ground.

Chinese Source

云何常作？謂於學處無穿穴故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is constant practice? It means no breach in the training-ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Discipline is likened to a raft crossing the sea — a single breach, like a hole in that raft, risks sinking into the ocean of birth-and-death. "Constant practice" means actively practicing the positive side of discipline, not merely avoiding wrong but actively doing right — releasing life as well as not killing, giving as well as not stealing, honoring others as well as avoiding misconduct, speaking truthfully as well as avoiding falsehood — matching the same verse's third line, always dwelling in right conduct.

118. Section: What Is Constant Turning? — Restored to Purity Once a Breach Has Occurred

What is constant turning? It means, once a breach has occurred, being restored to purity.

Chinese Source

云何常轉？謂穿穴已，復還淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is constant turning? It means, once a breach has occurred, being restored to purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the Yogācārabhūmi Commentary

Should a lapse occur despite one's best effort, swift confession restores purity — this is constant turning, matching the same verse's fourth line, ever turning in accord with the discipline: the invisible, ever-continuing commitment that, once generated at ordination, persists moment to moment beneath every outward action, always oriented back toward the discipline received.

119. Section: What Is Training in the Training-Grounds? — Learning Every Precept in Full — Closing the Five Marks of Discipline-Keeping

What is training in the training-grounds? It means being complete in training after every training-ground.

Chinese Source

云何受學學處？謂具隨學諸學處故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is training in the training-grounds? It means being complete in training after every training-ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Śrāvaka Ground at length

Without actually learning the precepts, one cannot know what is permitted and what is not, what is light and what is grave — ignorance itself becomes its own kind of fault. The Śrāvaka Ground describes this training in full: hearing a portion of the precepts' own nature from the preceptor at full ordination, then hearing the remaining individual-liberation teaching from one's instructing and supervising teachers — more than two hundred fifty training-grounds in all, each vowed to be learned; then hearing further from senior companions and at the fortnightly recitation of the code, and vowing again to train in all of it. Learning every training-ground in this way is what constitutes receiving the individual-liberation vows properly at all. With this, all five marks of genuinely keeping discipline — constant guarding, firm guarding, constant practice, constant turning, and training in the training-grounds — are now fully explained, closing this account of "the benefit of training." The treatise turns next to briefly demonstrate the remaining three phrases in the same manner.

120. Section: "Wisdom Is Foremost" Demonstrated — Solitary Reflection After Guarding Discipline and Hearing the True Dharma

Thus a practitioner who constantly and firmly guards discipline, having heard the true dharma, dwells alone in a quiet place, holding the object in mind, reflecting, weighing, and observing, seeking to advance toward the higher mind and higher wisdom.

Chinese Source

如是行者常守尸羅、堅守尸羅、聞正法已，獨居靜處，繫念思惟、籌量、觀察，為欲趣求增上心慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus a practitioner who constantly and firmly guards discipline, having heard the true dharma, dwells alone in a quiet place, holding the object in mind, reflecting, weighing, and observing, seeking to advance toward the higher mind and higher wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Reflecting, weighing, and observing name three depths of the same inward work: reflecting takes in an object's surface features by way of scriptural authority — that body, feeling, mind, and dharmas are suffering, empty, impermanent, without self; weighing pursues that same insight through inference, more deeply; and observing confirms it through direct perception itself, most deeply of all. All three depths together are what "wisdom is foremost" actually names in practice, aimed always at advancing toward concentration and wisdom.

121. Section: "Liberation Is Firm" Demonstrated — Never Regressing, the Very Fruit of Noble Wisdom

Relying on the wondrous wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, one is able to realize liberation; this liberation's own nature admits no regression, and so it is called firm — it is the fruit of transcendent noble wisdom.

Chinese Source

依聞思修所生妙慧，能證解脫，此解脫性無退法故，說名堅固，是出世間聖智果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Relying on the wondrous wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, one is able to realize liberation; this liberation's own nature admits no regression, and so it is called firm — it is the fruit of transcendent noble wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Once liberation is genuinely realized through the threefold wisdom, it never falls back into bondage again — this permanence of result is exactly why it is named firm, the direct fruit of the noble path's own wisdom rather than of ordinary reasoning. With this, "liberation is firm" is demonstrated.

122. Section: "Mindfulness Is What Governs" Demonstrated — Honest Self-Examination, and Three Sources of Right Mindfulness

And further, such a practitioner, by the power of right mindfulness, carefully examines themselves: is my own body of discipline complete? Do I have genuine, well-penetrating wisdom toward all dharmas? Have I genuinely realized liberation? Relying on the sustaining power of right mindfulness in this way, one is complete in the benefit of training, gives rise to foremost wisdom, and realizes firm liberation. This right mindfulness is, in brief, of three kinds: arising through teaching the dharma, or through receiving instruction, or through observing what should and should not be done.

Chinese Source

又此行者，由正念力審自觀察：我尸羅蘊為圓滿不？我於諸法為有正慧善通達不？我於解脫為善證不？

如是依止正念力持，具學勝利，發上首慧，證堅解脫。

又此正念略有三種，謂或因說法故，或依教授故，或由觀察應作、不應作故。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, such a practitioner, by the power of right mindfulness, carefully examines themselves: is my own body of discipline complete? Do I have genuine, well-penetrating wisdom toward all dharmas? Have I genuinely realized liberation?

Relying on the sustaining power of right mindfulness in this way, one is complete in the benefit of training, gives rise to foremost wisdom, and realizes firm liberation.

This right mindfulness is, in brief, of three kinds: arising through teaching the dharma, or through receiving instruction, or through observing what should and should not be done.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the Śrāvaka Ground's own account of the foundations of mindfulness

Mindfulness's governing role is exactly this honest, three-part self-examination — checking one's own discipline, wisdom, and liberation in turn, never assuming completeness prematurely — which is precisely why mindfulness sustains and advances all three of the other trainings rather than standing apart from them. Such mindfulness itself arises three ways: through the very act of teaching or hearing the dharma taught, through direct instruction from a good teacher, or through simply observing for oneself what ought and ought not be done. With this, all four phrases — training, wisdom, liberation, and mindfulness — have now been demonstrated in the manner already shown in full for "the benefit of training." This closes the total-inclusion half of resolving the uncertain-demonstration objection; a further account of what each phrase depends on remains to be shown.
