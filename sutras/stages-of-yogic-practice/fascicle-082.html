Fascicle 82 · 瑜伽師地論卷第八十二 · The Listener, and Praising the Buddha

How to Settle a Listener's Mind in Respectful, Undistorted Attention

Fascicle 81 closed the sūtra's own body, its five aspects of explanation, and the ten marks and eightfold speech of a genuine teacher. Fascicle 82, the second half of the Compendium of Explanations (攝釋分之下), turns to the listener: how a teacher settles the listener's mind in respectful, undistorted attention, unpacked through as many as ten reasons for listening with respect, and continuing on to the practice of praising the Buddha before teaching begins.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 82 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Compendium of Explanations Continues — the Listener Begins, with as Many as Ten Reasons to Listen Respectfully

The Compendium of Explanations, part two. The listener: this means that when a teacher of dharma such as this teaches the true dharma, he should settle others so that they abide in respectful, undistorted listening. What, then, is meant by "settle"? This is by way of one reason, or as many as ten.

Chinese Source

攝釋分之下。

聽者，謂如是說法者說正法時，應安處他，令住恭敬無倒聽聞。

云何安處？

謂或由一因，或乃至十。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Compendium of Explanations, part two.

The listener: this means that when a teacher of dharma such as this teaches the true dharma, he should settle others so that they abide in respectful, undistorted listening.

What, then, is meant by "settle"?

This is by way of one reason, or as many as ten.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second half of the Compendium of Explanations opens directly from the first: having explained how a teacher perfects his own marks and speech, the treatise turns to the other side of the encounter — how that teacher settles the listener's mind. A teacher equipped with the ten marks and eight kinds of speech, addressing any of the five assemblies, should bring each listener to genuinely respectful, error-free attention — not merely five assemblies reached, but each mind within them rightly settled. This settling is accomplished through as many as ten reasons, which the treatise now unfolds one by one, echoing the Śrāvaka Ground's own account of undistorted instruction: teaching dharma without distortion so that hearers retain it, recite it, and practice it, truly gaining release and reaching the very limit of suffering.

2. Section: The First Reason — Present Benefit and Ease, Through the Higher Trainings

The first reason: this means that respectful listening presently and directly realizes benefit and ease. "Thus what makes you benefited is spoken by way of the higher discipline; thus what makes you at ease is spoken by way of not relying on wretched, difficult, unfree conduct."

Chinese Source

一因者，謂恭敬聽法，現前能證利益安樂故。

如是能令汝利益者，依增上戒說；如是能令汝安樂者，謂不依止弊苦、艱難、不自在行。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first reason: this means that respectful listening presently and directly realizes benefit and ease.

"Thus what makes you benefited is spoken by way of the higher discipline; thus what makes you at ease is spoken by way of not relying on wretched, difficult, unfree conduct."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

A disciple at the stage of accumulation or preparation who respectfully receives and retains the twelve divisions of scripture, reflects on their meaning, holds them in mind, recites them aloud, and takes them into quiet contemplation — the first four of these belong to the wisdom born of hearing, the last to the wisdom born of reflection — realizes real benefit and ease of body and mind in this very life. The 披尋記 further ties benefit to the higher trainings in mind and wisdom, and ease to freedom from renunciation's harsher austerities; elsewhere it notes that wherever the Bhagavān praises ascetic virtues, that is benefit, and wherever he permits the enjoyment of fine food and rich robes, that is ease — and that wherever the three higher trainings in discipline, mind, and wisdom are established, both benefit and ease are established together, since the Tathāgata, by wisdom, teaches whichever of the three a given listener most needs.

3. Section: Four Ways Benefit and Ease Combine — Closing the First Reason

Here there are the four propositions — benefit without ease, and so on — as already explained in the Bodhisattva Ground's account of experiencing dharma.

Chinese Source

此中或有利益非安樂等四句，如菩薩地法受中已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here there are the four propositions — benefit without ease, and so on — as already explained in the Bodhisattva Ground's account of experiencing dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Bodhisattva Ground distinguishes benefit (concerning the next life) from ease (concerning this one) across four cases: ease without benefit — present pleasure ripening into future suffering, as when the ten evil deeds are committed gladly; benefit without ease — present hardship ripening into future pleasure, as when the ten wholesome deeds are practiced reluctantly; both benefit and ease — present pleasure ripening into future pleasure, as when good is practiced gladly; and neither — present suffering ripening into future suffering, as when the ten evil deeds are committed with anguish. With this fourfold analysis, the first of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

4. Section: The Second Reason Begins — Rightly Establishing All Dharmas

The second reason: this means rightly establishing all dharmas. Rightly establishing means being free of faults, and possessing great meaning.

Chinese Source

二因者，謂善建立一切法故。善建立者，離諸過故，具大義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second reason: this means rightly establishing all dharmas. Rightly establishing means being free of faults, and possessing great meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Ground of What Is Heard names four kinds of inner seed underlying all dharmas: worldly seeds (afflictions bound to the three realms), transcendent seeds (the noble paths and fruits of the three vehicles), impure seeds (the aggregates and activities bound to the desire realm), and pure seeds — themselves worldly-pure (activities bound to the form and formless realms) and transcendent-pure (the paths, fruits, and eightfold noble path of the three vehicles). Rightly establishing means the listener's mind is led to cultivate both kinds of pure seed: worldly purity through freeing oneself of the faults of affliction and evil karma — trusting good companions, not mixing carelessly with householders and renunciants alike, and inwardly applying right attention to awaken to the truth of what is known — and transcendent purity through fully entering the profound meaning of conditioned and unconditioned dharmas, grasping both their nature as they truly are and the whole extent of what they are, and so realizing nirvāṇa's vast, transcendent benefit and ease.

5. Section: A Surpassing Result for Both Speaker and Listener — Closing the Second Reason

And further, because the toil undertaken by both speaker and listener has a surpassing result. Were it not so, the one who speaks and the one who listens would only waste their own proper work, expend effort in vain, and have no result at all.

Chinese Source

又為說者、聽者所設劬勞有勝果故。若不爾者，能說、能聽徒廢己業，虛設功勞，應無有果。

Ru-Yi Translation

And further, because the toil undertaken by both speaker and listener has a surpassing result. Were it not so, the one who speaks and the one who listens would only waste their own proper work, expend effort in vain, and have no result at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Both the effort of teaching and the effort of listening bear real fruit, worldly and transcendent — which is exactly why a teacher must settle listeners in respectful attention, and why listeners in turn must listen respectfully and without distortion; otherwise the labor on both sides would be spent for nothing. With this, the second of the ten reasons is closed.

6. Section: The Third Reason Begins — Abandoning Evil Destinies, Gaining Good Ones, Swiftly Drawing In the Cause of Nirvāṇa

The third reason: respectful listening enables sentient beings to abandon evil destinies, to attain good destinies, and swiftly to draw in the cause of nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

三因者，恭敬聽法，能令眾生捨惡趣故，得善趣故，速能引攝涅槃因故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third reason: respectful listening enables sentient beings to abandon evil destinies, to attain good destinies, and swiftly to draw in the cause of nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Respectful listening deepens faith in karmic cause and effect — that every result comes from action, that action once done is never lost and never ripens unearned, and that any evil committed, whether premeditated or already carried out, calls for swift confession and remedy before it grows — which alone lets a listener abandon the ten evil deeds and the evil destinies they lead to, and gain a good destiny instead. Beyond this, respectful listening enables cultivation of the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest of the noble eightfold path, the very path leading out of the three realms' cycle of suffering toward nirvāṇa — suffering and its origin being the dark portion's result and cause, cessation and the path being the bright portion's result and cause. The Ground of Neither-With-nor-Without-Investigation names the causes of nirvāṇa as twofold: superior conditions — the true dharma's own supporting voice, chiefly the four noble truths, together with inward right reflection on what is heard — and lesser conditions, a longer list including one's own and others' perfections, wholesome aspiration, right going-forth, restraint by precepts and by the senses, moderation in eating, diligent wakeful yoga through the watches of the night, mindful abiding, delight in solitude, purifying the hindrances, and resting in concentration.

7. Section: All Three Attainments Come Only Through Respectful Listening — Closing the Third Reason

These three matters are attained only through respectful listening.

Chinese Source

如是三事，要由恭敬聽聞方得。

Ru-Yi Translation

These three matters are attained only through respectful listening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Abandoning evil destinies and gaining good ones together fulfill what the Ground of Thought Cultivation calls the fulfillment of higher rebirth — going toward a good destiny — while swiftly drawing in the cause of nirvāṇa fulfills the fulfillment of decisive excellence — craving exhausted, desire abandoned, cessation and nirvāṇa reached. With this, the third of the ten reasons is closed.

8. Section: The Fourth Reason — Understanding the Teaching, Taking Up Good and Abandoning Evil, and Swiftly Realizing Nirvāṇa

The fourth reason: first, respectful listening enables one to skillfully penetrate the sūtras and other such dharmas. Second, this true dharma enables sentient beings to abandon what is unwholesome and take up what is wholesome; one who listens well can then diligently abandon the one and take up the other. Third, through this taking-up and abandoning, one abandons the future suffering brought on by evil causes. Fourth, through taking up good causes and abandoning evil ones in this way, one swiftly realizes nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

四因者，一、恭敬聽法，能善了達契經等法。二、如是正法，能令眾生捨諸不善，攝受諸善；若善聽者，則能精勤若捨、若受。三、由捨受故，捨離惡因所招後苦。四、由此受捨善惡因故，速證涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fourth reason: first, respectful listening enables one to skillfully penetrate the sūtras and other such dharmas. Second, this true dharma enables sentient beings to abandon what is unwholesome and take up what is wholesome; one who listens well can then diligently abandon the one and take up the other. Third, through this taking-up and abandoning, one abandons the future suffering brought on by evil causes. Fourth, through taking up good causes and abandoning evil ones in this way, one swiftly realizes nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These four links form a single chain: understanding the twelve divisions of scripture makes it possible to abandon greed, hatred, and delusion and take up wholesome dharmas both worldly and transcendent; abandoning unwholesome causes means no further evil karma is created, and so no further suffering in the three evil destinies is incurred; and abandoning evil causes while taking up good ones in this way leads swiftly to nirvāṇa itself. With this, the fourth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

9. Section: The Fifth Reason Begins, First Meaning — Five Excellences of the Buddha's True Dharma

The fifth reason: this means that the true dharma spoken by the Bhagavān has cause and occasion, has leading-out, has what is relied upon, has boldness, and has miraculous transformation.

Chinese Source

五因者，謂佛世尊所說正法，有因緣、有出離、有依趣、有勇猛、有神變。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fifth reason: this means that the true dharma spoken by the Bhagavān has cause and occasion, has leading-out, has what is relied upon, has boldness, and has miraculous transformation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Speaking generally of the twelve divisions of scripture, these five excellences unfold as follows. Has cause and occasion: the Buddha speaks only when asked, as bodhisattvas and disciples request teaching on renunciation, concentration, and wisdom; he speaks the precepts only in response to an actual occasion of misconduct, since the Vinaya is always occasioned by some monk's specific failing; and every teaching is fitted to the particular cause, matter, and listener before him. Has leading-out: the dharma leads a practitioner out of every defiled state into every pure one — worldly concentration freeing one from the afflictions of desire, the transcendent path freeing one from the three realms' suffering entirely, up through the fruits of stream-entry and beyond to unsurpassed awakening. Has what is relied upon: in brief, four things — relying on the dharma rather than the person; relying on the meaning rather than the letter; relying on sūtras of fully drawn-out meaning rather than those not fully drawn out; and relying on wisdom rather than ordinary consciousness, since only wisdom, not the sixth consciousness's own discriminations, can be trusted before the truth is directly realized. Has boldness: the dharma that brings a being to unsurpassed awakening is vast in content and extremely lofty, demanding bold, unbroken diligence sustained across three great incalculable eons to reach completion — the very same boldness already described in fascicle 81's account of the eighth mark of a good teacher: constant delight in unheard teachings and ever-clearer mastery of what is already heard, without abandoning yoga, attentive application, or inward calm-abiding. Has miraculous transformation: the Śrāvaka Ground names three — miraculous transformation of spiritual power, evoking such reverence in the listener that he attends to yogic instruction with utmost respect; miraculous transformation of mind-reading, discerning the particular state of another's mind; and miraculous transformation of instruction, teaching each disciple exactly as his own faculties and conduct allow him to enter the path — together comprising every perfection of instruction the Buddha permits, so as to draw beings joyfully into the sacred teaching.

10. Section: Referred to the Compendium of Synonyms — Closing the First Meaning of the Fifth Reason

Statements such as these should be distinguished at length as in the Compendium of Synonyms.

Chinese Source

如是諸句，如攝異門分當廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Statements such as these should be distinguished at length as in the Compendium of Synonyms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Compendium of Synonyms restates these five in terms closer to monastic discipline: having cause and occasion, since every precept is established only upon an actual occasion of misconduct; having leading-out, since a proper method of confession is established so a violator may return to purity; having what is relied upon, since the four reliances — on robes, food, dwelling, and medicine, each kept simple — let a practitioner rise above every corrupted precept; having what transcends, since the precepts steer a practitioner in the middle way, away from both indulgence in desire and fruitless austerity; and having miraculous transformation, the same three already explained, established so that practitioners swiftly gain spiritual mastery. Though the wording differs slightly from the account just given, the meaning is the same. With this, the first of the two meanings under the fifth reason is closed. The true dharma's five excellences give disciples every reason to listen and study it well.

11. Section: A Second Meaning of the Fifth Reason — the Threefold Wisdom of Hearing, Reflection, and Cultivation — Closing the Fifth Reason Entirely

There is further a fivefold reason: I shall hear what has not yet been heard; I shall, having heard, investigate it; I shall cut off the net of doubt; I shall cast aside all views; I shall, through wisdom, penetrate all profound words and meanings. All buddha bhagavāns speak these five so as to reveal the pure means of the threefold wisdom accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation. The first two reveal the wisdom accomplished through hearing; the next two reveal the wisdom accomplished through reflection; the last reveals the wisdom accomplished through cultivation.

Chinese Source

復有五因。謂我當聞所未聞，我當聞已研究，我當除斷疑網，我當棄背諸見，我當以慧通達一切甚深句義。

諸佛世尊說此五種，顯聞思修三所成慧清淨方便。謂初二種顯聞所成慧，次二種顯思所成慧，後一種顯修所成慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is further a fivefold reason: I shall hear what has not yet been heard; I shall, having heard, investigate it; I shall cut off the net of doubt; I shall cast aside all views; I shall, through wisdom, penetrate all profound words and meanings.

All buddha bhagavāns speak these five so as to reveal the pure means of the threefold wisdom accomplished through hearing, reflection, and cultivation. The first two reveal the wisdom accomplished through hearing; the next two reveal the wisdom accomplished through reflection; the last reveals the wisdom accomplished through cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Hearing what has not yet been heard, and investigating what has been heard, together purify the mind through the wisdom born of hearing; cutting off the net of doubt and casting aside every view together purify it through the wisdom born of reflection; and penetrating all profound words and meanings — the empty nature of all dharmas — purifies it through the wisdom born of cultivation, corresponding to the Grounds of What Is Heard, What Is Thought, and What Is Cultivated among the treatise's seventeen grounds: the three vehicles all rely on hearing, reflection, and cultivation as the means by which surpassing virtue is established. With this second meaning, and with both meanings of the fifth reason together, the fifth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

12. Section: The Sixth Reason — Repaying the Teacher's Kindness, and Five Further Excellences of the True Dharma

The sixth reason: first, in order to repay the great teacher's kindness with reverence — since the Bhagavān, for our sake, practiced measureless difficult and painful austerities to seek out this dharma, how could one now fail to listen to it? Second, contemplating one's own benefit — for the Buddha's true dharma has present benefit. Third, it can ultimately free one from every consuming affliction. Fourth, it accords well with true deportment. Fifth, it is easily seen and understood. Sixth, it is what the wise know through their own inner realization.

Chinese Source

六因者，一、為欲敬報大師恩德，謂佛世尊為我等故，行於無量難行苦行，求得此法，云何今者而不聽聞。二、觀自義利，謂佛正法有現義利。三、究竟能離一切熱惱。四、善順正儀。五、易可了見。六、諸聰慧者內證所知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth reason: first, in order to repay the great teacher's kindness with reverence — since the Bhagavān, for our sake, practiced measureless difficult and painful austerities to seek out this dharma, how could one now fail to listen to it? Second, contemplating one's own benefit — for the Buddha's true dharma has present benefit. Third, it can ultimately free one from every consuming affliction. Fourth, it accords well with true deportment. Fifth, it is easily seen and understood. Sixth, it is what the wise know through their own inner realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Buddha labored across three great incalculable eons to win this dharma for our sake — gratitude alone should move a listener to attend. Present benefit means every precept observed and every training undertaken now already elevates one's lifespan, appearance, and fortune, ripening toward nirvāṇa itself. Freedom from consuming affliction follows from rightly establishing all dharmas, worldly and transcendent, as already explained under the second reason. According with true deportment means the Buddha's own moral code, unlike the brahmins' — which the 披尋記 contrasts point by point: permitting harm where the brahmin code allows it, versus prohibiting it entirely; encouraging accumulation of land, house, and wealth, wife and servants, versus renouncing all such possessions but the means to end suffering; indulging adornment, song, dance, and sensual pleasure, versus enjoying only the blameless wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation — the monastic code proving superior in precept, in what is embraced, and in what is enjoyed, at every point. Easily seen and understood follows the same contrast: brahminical teaching is hollow and deliberately obscured in wording, while the Buddha's teaching is genuinely meaningful and stated in plain, undisguised language. And what the wise know through inner realization means suchness itself, tasted directly, not merely reasoned about. With this, the sixth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

13. Section: The Seventh Reason — Cultivating Seven True Dharmas, from Knowing the Teaching to Knowing Persons

The seventh reason: this means, I shall cultivate seven true dharmas — I shall know the teaching and know its meaning, and so on, up to skillfully knowing the distinctions of noble and lowly among persons.

Chinese Source

七因者，謂我當修集七種正法，我當知法知義，乃至善知補特伽羅尊卑差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The seventh reason: this means, I shall cultivate seven true dharmas — I shall know the teaching and know its meaning, and so on, up to skillfully knowing the distinctions of noble and lowly among persons.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, drawing on the Vinaya and the Śrāvaka Ground

Traditional Vinaya sources unpack this list as seven things a renunciant should know daily. Knowing the teaching: grasping the twelve divisions of scripture — their provisional names, how their terms interrelate, their proper sequence of practice, who among the noble ones spoke them, and the self- or other-benefiting intent behind each. Knowing the meaning: grasping the circumstances that occasioned a teaching, the different angles from which its words are framed, the sequence by which it is explained and established, the four principles of reasoning behind it, and its essential gist in brief. Knowing the time: recognizing, moment to moment in meditation practice, when dullness or agitation is present, approaching, subsiding, or already resolved, and applying the right corrective — exertion, calming, or equanimity — accordingly. Knowing the measure: moderation in food and in exertion alike, so that the body stays balanced and healthy and effort neither slackens into dullness nor strains into agitation. Knowing oneself: an honest, daily accounting of one's own present measure of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. Knowing the assembly: recognizing who is present — noble or common, monastic or lay, elder or junior, scholar of sūtra, treatise, or yogic practice. And knowing persons: discerning each listener's own capacity, sharp or dull, faithful or not, without attachment yet with real clarity, so as to give each the teaching actually suited to them. A teacher equipped with all seven forms of knowing is thereby equipped to settle any listener in respectful, undistorted attention.

14. Section: The Eighth Reason — Eight Marks of Excellence in the Dharma Itself

The eighth reason: first, the Buddha's dharma is easily obtained, taught even to outcasts. Second, it is easily practiced, practicable in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down. Third, it draws in benefit, able to draw in both the fruit of higher rebirth and the fruit of decisive excellence. Fourth, it is good in the beginning. Fifth, it is good in the middle. Sixth, it is good in the end. Seventh, it brings about a pleasant result in the present. Eighth, it draws in a pleasant result hereafter.

Chinese Source

八因者，一、佛法易得，乃至為旃荼羅等而開示故。二、易可修學，行住坐臥皆得修故。三、引發義利，謂能引發增上生果、決定勝果故。四、初善故。五、中善故。六、後善故。七、感現樂果故。八、引後樂果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eighth reason: first, the Buddha's dharma is easily obtained, taught even to outcasts. Second, it is easily practiced, practicable in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down. Third, it draws in benefit, able to draw in both the fruit of higher rebirth and the fruit of decisive excellence. Fourth, it is good in the beginning. Fifth, it is good in the middle. Sixth, it is good in the end. Seventh, it brings about a pleasant result in the present. Eighth, it draws in a pleasant result hereafter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The Buddha teaches every being alike regardless of caste or station, even the lowliest outcaste, in whatever way — provisional or ultimate, direct or skillful — fits the listener; his teaching is coherent and clear enough to practice in any posture at any time; and it draws out both worldly and transcendent fruit at once. The Compendium of Synonyms explains the three "good"s concretely: good in the beginning, since simply hearing it gives rise to joy; good in the middle, since practicing it involves no real hardship, keeping to the middle way between indulgence and austerity; and good in the end, since carried through it ultimately uproots every defilement and brings freedom from the three realms entirely. Present pleasant result and future pleasant result then round out the eight, teaching bearing fruit both now and in lives to come. With this, the eighth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

15. Section: The Ninth Reason — Freed from Nine Kinds of Worldly Oppression, the Prison of Birth-and-Death Foremost Among Them

The ninth reason: this means being freed from nine kinds of worldly oppression. First, it enables one to escape the great prison of birth-and-death. Second, it permanently severs the firm bonds of craving and the rest. Third, it casts off the poverty of the seven kinds of wealth and establishes their riches. Fourth, it delivers one from scarcity of good conduct and right dharma once heard, and establishes their abundance. Fifth, it extinguishes the darkness of ignorance and gives rise to the light of wisdom. Sixth, it crosses the four torrents and ascends to nirvāṇa's shore. Seventh, it ultimately cures the sickness of affliction. Eighth, it frees one from every snare of craving. Ninth, it carries one across the trackless wilderness and dense forest of beginningless birth-and-death. Among all prisons, the prison of birth-and-death is foremost — hence it is named first.

Chinese Source

九因者，謂能解脫九種世間逼迫事故。一、能出生死大牢獄故。二、永斷貪等堅牢縛故。三、棄捨七財貧，建立七財富故。四、超度善行聞正法儉，建立善行聞正法豐故。五、滅無明闇，起智慧明故。六、度四暴流，昇涅槃岸故。七、究竟能療煩惱病故。八、解脫一切貪愛羂故。九、能度無始生死曠野稠林行故。諸牢獄中，生死牢獄最為第一，是故先說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ninth reason: this means being freed from nine kinds of worldly oppression. First, it enables one to escape the great prison of birth-and-death. Second, it permanently severs the firm bonds of craving and the rest. Third, it casts off the poverty of the seven kinds of wealth and establishes their riches. Fourth, it delivers one from scarcity of good conduct and right dharma once heard, and establishes their abundance. Fifth, it extinguishes the darkness of ignorance and gives rise to the light of wisdom. Sixth, it crosses the four torrents and ascends to nirvāṇa's shore. Seventh, it ultimately cures the sickness of affliction. Eighth, it frees one from every snare of craving. Ninth, it carries one across the trackless wilderness and dense forest of beginningless birth-and-death. Among all prisons, the prison of birth-and-death is foremost — hence it is named first.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Nine images, one after another, for what the dharma frees a listener from. The prison of birth-and-death holds every being fast in craving, aversion, and the fetters of desire, shrouded in ignorance's dense thicket, unable to escape the three realms unaided — the dharma alone offers a way out. The seven kinds of noble wealth it establishes in place of poverty are faith, discipline, a sense of shame before oneself, a sense of shame before others, learning, generosity, and wisdom — each the remedy for a corresponding deficiency the 披尋記 names as a kind of poverty. Scarcity of good conduct gives way to abundance the more one hears and practices; ignorance's darkness gives way to wisdom's light; the four torrents — of desire, of becoming, of views, and of ignorance — are crossed only by hearing the true dharma, carrying one to nirvāṇa's far shore; affliction's sickness is cured, since the Buddha alone is the great physician; the net of craving, woven from endless attachment, is escaped; and the trackless wilderness of beginningless birth-and-death, thick with every conditioned activity, is finally crossed. With this, the ninth of the ten reasons for respectful listening is closed.

16. Section: The Tenth Reason Begins — Wealth Rightly Sought, Loss Rightly Borne, Desire Rightly Seen Through, and Dependent Origination Rightly Penetrated

The tenth reason: first, respectful listening gains the power of discernment, and through this one receives the benefit of hearing the dharma — seeking wealth rightly, not wrongly, and, seeing its faults clearly, making right use of it. Second, one comes to know release well: losing one's wealth, one feels no grief and utters no complaint, and so on, at length; losing one's family, or facing illness, one does not grieve deeply or fall into sorrow, and so on, at length. Third, seeing clearly the faults in the objects of desire, and the surpassing virtue of release from them, one goes forth in purity, abandoning attachment to fine bedding, up to realizing the wondrous states of meditative absorption. Fourth, respectful listening swiftly brings understanding in accord with the vast and profound, and the seemingly profound, dharmas of dependent origination, and draws forth the vast joy of wholesome roots and of release. As the Bhagavān said: my noble disciples, attending with the ear single-mindedly to the true dharma, can cut off five dharmas and cultivate seven dharmas, swiftly reaching completeness.

Chinese Source

十因者，一、恭敬聽法得思擇力，由此能受聞法勝利；如法求財，不以非法，深見過患而受用之。二、善知出離。謂喪失財寶，無憂無慼，亦不嗟怨，乃至廣說；眷屬離壞，若遭病苦，不甚悲歎，亦不愁惱，乃至廣說。三、於諸欲中深見過患，及見出離最勝功德，清淨出家，捨離上妙臥具貪著，乃至能證諸妙靜慮。四、恭敬聽法，速順證解廣大甚深、相似甚深諸緣起法，又能引發廣大善根出離歡喜。如世尊說：我聖弟子專心屬耳聽聞正法，能斷五法，能修七法，速疾圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

The tenth reason: first, respectful listening gains the power of discernment, and through this one receives the benefit of hearing the dharma — seeking wealth rightly, not wrongly, and, seeing its faults clearly, making right use of it. Second, one comes to know release well: losing one's wealth, one feels no grief and utters no complaint, and so on, at length; losing one's family, or facing illness, one does not grieve deeply or fall into sorrow, and so on, at length. Third, seeing clearly the faults in the objects of desire, and the surpassing virtue of release from them, one goes forth in purity, abandoning attachment to fine bedding, up to realizing the wondrous states of meditative absorption. Fourth, respectful listening swiftly brings understanding in accord with the vast and profound, and the seemingly profound, dharmas of dependent origination, and draws forth the vast joy of wholesome roots and of release. As the Bhagavān said: my noble disciples, attending with the ear single-mindedly to the true dharma, can cut off five dharmas and cultivate seven dharmas, swiftly reaching completeness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

The tenth and final reason unfolds in ten parts of its own. The first four appear here: discernment lets one earn and use wealth rightly, aware of its dangers; equanimity in loss — of wealth, of family, of health — comes from truly understanding cause and effect, so grief need not follow misfortune; seeing through the objects of desire clears the way to a genuinely pure renunciation and, eventually, meditative absorption; and swift insight into dependent origination, both in its vast profundity (emptiness itself, neither definitely existent nor definitely non-existent) and its seeming profundity (conditioned phenomena that, though truly impermanent and without self, appear constant), draws forth wholesome roots and real joy in release. The 披尋記 glosses the "five dharmas cut off" as the distorted views that mistake the impermanent for permanent, the painful for pleasant, and the selfless for a self, together with seeking personal gain and craving long life; and the "seven dharmas cultivated" as the seven factors of awakening — investigation of dharmas, energy, and joy belonging to insight; tranquility, concentration, and equanimity belonging to calm-abiding; and mindfulness common to both — the very state of unified calm and insight that brings the four noble truths to direct realization.

17. Section: The Remaining Six Parts of the Tenth Reason — Closing All Ten Reasons, and the Listener, Entirely

Fifth, when noble disciples listen respectfully, all dharmas of accumulation become dharmas of cessation. Sixth, having understood the true dharma, they become far from dust and free of defilement, and give rise to the dharma-eye among all dharmas. Seventh, it draws in the surpassing provisions for realizing the fruit of stream-entry, up to realizing arhatship, and the surpassing provisions for realizing arhatship. Eighth, it draws in the provisions for solitary awakening. Ninth, it skillfully draws in the provisions for unsurpassed complete awakening. Tenth, it draws in every worldly and transcendent meditative absorption, liberation, unified state of mind, and attainment.

Chinese Source

五、諸聖弟子恭敬聽法，所有集法皆成滅法。

六、解正法已，遠塵離垢，於諸法中生正法眼。

七、能引攝證預流果最勝資糧，乃至證得阿羅漢果，及能引攝阿羅漢果最勝資糧。八、能引攝獨覺資糧。九、能善引攝無上正等菩提資糧。十、能引一切世出世間靜慮、解脫、等持、等至。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, when noble disciples listen respectfully, all dharmas of accumulation become dharmas of cessation.

Sixth, having understood the true dharma, they become far from dust and free of defilement, and give rise to the dharma-eye among all dharmas.

Seventh, it draws in the surpassing provisions for realizing the fruit of stream-entry, up to realizing arhatship, and the surpassing provisions for realizing arhatship. Eighth, it draws in the provisions for solitary awakening. Ninth, it skillfully draws in the provisions for unsurpassed complete awakening. Tenth, it draws in every worldly and transcendent meditative absorption, liberation, unified state of mind, and attainment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Affliction and karma are dharmas of accumulation, belonging to the truth of origin; once craving and views are fully cut off, present and future suffering are stilled, becoming dharmas of cessation, belonging to the truth of cessation — respectful listening turns one into the other. "Far from dust, free of defilement" the Compendium of Synonyms glosses two ways: dust as the conceit still arising, intermittently or continuously, before direct realization is complete, and defilement as its residual latent tendencies together with those cut off at the path of seeing — both now permanently absent; or, more broadly, dust as self-conceit and every view-based affliction cut off at the path of seeing, defilement as the coarse latent residue of both. "Among all dharmas" means within the particular and shared characteristics dharmas rest in; the "dharma-eye" is the direct realization that there is only dharma — the five aggregates as illusory, without self or what belongs to a self. The last four parts then draw straight through every level of the path: the provisions for the four fruits from stream-entry to arhatship, for solitary buddhahood, for unsurpassed complete awakening, and for every worldly and transcendent meditative attainment — the four absorptions, eight liberations, eleven unified states, and six attainments detailed in the Ground of Unified Meditative States. With this tenth reason complete in all its ten parts, all ten reasons for respectful listening — presently realized benefit and ease, right establishment of all dharmas, escape from evil destinies, understanding what is taught, the dharma's five excellences and threefold wisdom, repaying the teacher's kindness, the sevenfold knowing, the dharma's eight further excellences, freedom from ninefold worldly oppression, and this tenfold final reason — are now fully set forth, closing the account of the listener entirely. The treatise turns next to praising the Buddha before teaching begins.

18. Section: Praising the Buddha Begins, Briefly or at Length

Praising the Buddha, briefly or at length: this means that a teacher of dharma, about to expound the sūtra, should first praise the Buddha. Praise is of two kinds: first, brief; second, extensive.

Chinese Source

讚佛略廣者，謂說法師將欲開闡，先當讚佛。讚有二種，一、略，二、廣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Praising the Buddha, briefly or at length: this means that a teacher of dharma, about to expound the sūtra, should first praise the Buddha. Praise is of two kinds: first, brief; second, extensive.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having closed the listener's own ten reasons for respectful attention, the treatise turns to the third of the four topics under explaining well — praising the Buddha before teaching begins. The Sūtra of the Great Adornment records that a Dharma-teacher in the time of Kāśyapa Buddha, praising that buddha before his assembly, was reborn in the heavens for it, ever after enjoying happiness among gods and humans with fragrance issuing naturally from his mouth — praise of the Buddha is no idle formality, but bears its own fruit.

19. Section: Brief Praise, Five Marks Named — Wondrous Form, Stillness, Surpassing Wisdom, Right Conduct, and Majestic Virtue

Brief praise of the Buddha should be known by five marks: first, wondrous form; second, stillness; third, surpassing wisdom; fourth, right conduct; fifth, majestic virtue.

Chinese Source

略讚佛者，由五種相應當了知。一者、妙色，二者、寂靜，三者、勝智，四者、正行，五者、威德。

Ru-Yi Translation

Brief praise of the Buddha should be known by five marks: first, wondrous form; second, stillness; third, surpassing wisdom; fourth, right conduct; fifth, majestic virtue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra's Principle of Wisdom Chapter

The source names these five in fuller color: wondrous form, complete with the thirty-two marks of a great being and the eighty subsidiary excellences adorning his limbs, radiant as a great mountain of gold; stillness, every faculty at rest, mind utterly at peace, having reached the utmost shore of calm and self-mastery, guarding the senses so completely as to be called the dragon among men, the bull leading the herd, the well-tamed horse, clear and unruffled as a still pool, every trace of affliction's habituation forever drawn out; surpassing wisdom, unobstructed and unattached toward all dharmas of the three times and beyond time; right conduct, the six perfections and four means of gathering beings, benefiting self and other, both brought to full completion; and majestic virtue, the blazing splendor of a tathāgata's unhindered play of spiritual powers.

20. Section: Wondrous Form — the Thirty-Two Marks and Eighty Excellences

Wondrous form means the thirty-two marks of a great being, and the eighty subsidiary excellences.

Chinese Source

妙色者，謂三十二大丈夫相、八十隨好。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wondrous form means the thirty-two marks of a great being, and the eighty subsidiary excellences.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha's body, golden-hued and radiant as a mountain of gold, adorned throughout with these thirty-two great marks and eighty subsidiary excellences born of vast merit, is a sight said to move heaven and earth, delighting all who behold it.

21. Section: Stillness — Guarding Every Sense Gateway, and Uprooting Affliction's Habituation

Stillness means well guarding every gateway of the senses, and permanently uprooting the habituation of affliction.

Chinese Source

寂靜者，謂善能密護諸根門等，及能永拔煩惱習氣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Stillness means well guarding every gateway of the senses, and permanently uprooting the habituation of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing Kuiji's commentary

The six senses meeting their six objects take in neither surface appearance nor pleasing detail — the eye meeting form, and so on, without ever grasping at what would stir desire. Master Kuiji's own praise captures it: mind still, bearing dignified and wondrous, every faculty utterly at rest, having reached the very peak of self-mastery and calm.

22. Section: Surpassing Wisdom, and Right Conduct

Surpassing wisdom means being unobstructed and unattached toward the dharmas of the past, future, and present, and toward what lies beyond time. Right conduct means self-benefiting and other-benefiting right conduct both brought to full completion.

Chinese Source

勝智者，謂於過去、未來、現在世法及非世法無礙無著。

正行者，謂自他利正行圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Surpassing wisdom means being unobstructed and unattached toward the dharmas of the past, future, and present, and toward what lies beyond time.

Right conduct means self-benefiting and other-benefiting right conduct both brought to full completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Sūtra on the Unsurpassed Reliance

The Buddha's wisdom takes in every worldly and transcendent dharma across all three times without hindrance or grasping. Self-benefit is his own complete liberation and pure dharma-body, every obstruction of affliction and of knowledge extinguished — accomplished by wisdom free of discrimination; other-benefit is his turning the wheel of dharma without limit or interruption for as long as birth-and-death continues, freeing beings from the suffering of the three evil destinies and establishing them in the good and in the three vehicles — accomplished by the wisdom attained after realization. Together, as the Bodhisattva Ground likewise teaches, the six perfections and the four means of gathering beings perfect both at once.

23. Section: Majestic Virtue — the Unhindered Play of Spiritual Power — Closing Brief Praise by Five Marks

Majestic virtue means the unhindered play of spiritual powers possessed by all tathāgatas.

Chinese Source

威德者，謂諸如來神通遊戲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Majestic virtue means the unhindered play of spiritual powers possessed by all tathāgatas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise

The Buddha's six perfected spiritual powers, wielded with great wisdom and great compassion, transform freely and rescue beings effortlessly, mind utterly pure and unstained — hence "play." The Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise explains the word directly: a magician conjures endless transformations at will and calls it play; a bodhisattva's spiritual powers transform just as freely, and so are likewise called play — most essential of all in maturing beings and purifying buddha-lands together. With this fifth mark, brief praise of the Buddha by five marks is complete.

24. Section: Six Further Marks of Brief Praise — Closing Brief Praise of the Buddha Entirely

There is further brief praise of the Tathāgata by six marks: complete in merit; free of every defilement; without turbidity or impurity; without equal; whose only work is benefiting sentient beings; and fully capable in this very work. This is explained at length in the Determinations.

Chinese Source

復有六種略讚如來。謂功德圓滿故、離垢染故、無濁穢故、無與等故、唯利有情以為業故、於此業用有堪能故。此廣分別，如攝決擇分。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is further brief praise of the Tathāgata by six marks: complete in merit; free of every defilement; without turbidity or impurity; without equal; whose only work is benefiting sentient beings; and fully capable in this very work. This is explained at length in the Determinations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary citing the Determinations, fascicle 74

Complete in merit means every worldly and transcendent virtue the Buddha has achieved, surpassing what any word or song could capture. Free of every defilement means free of the seven kinds of taint — no taint of desiring others' recognition, no taint of clinging to views, no taint of doubt, no taint of comparing himself to others, no taint of intoxicated self-satisfaction, no taint of any latent tendency of affliction whatsoever, and no taint of stinginess. Without turbidity or impurity means unshakable in seven further ways: no outsider can shake it; no army of Māra — the aggregates, afflictions, death, or the god of the sixth heaven — can shake it; no thief can seize the noble wealth of faith, discipline, learning, shame before self, shame before others, generosity, and wisdom he has perfected, since it is formless and beyond every calamity; no kinsman can destroy it; no king's decree can remove it; no elemental disaster of fire, water, or wind can alter it; and even when his lifespan ends and he enters cessation, his merit never diminishes, enduring to the very end of future time. Without equal means his conditioned and unconditioned virtues are so vast, so exalted, so many, and so powerfully great that neither pure-minded śrāvakas nor bodhisattvas already on the grounds can match him, let alone beings still cycling through the six destinies — his merit stands foremost in all three realms. Whose only work is benefiting sentient beings means that, rather than resting in the vast, faultless ease of the nirvāṇa he has attained, he skillfully manifests himself again and again for others' benefit. And fully capable in this work means he accomplishes this benefiting effortlessly and spontaneously, without needing to form any intention, sustaining it without interruption to the very end of future time. The 披尋記 traces all six back to the Determinations' fourfold account of great awakening's own nature — as the ground on which arising and its cessation both rest, as what rightly discerns the fruit of all that is known, and as the pure dharma-realm itself — together with its ten masteries (over lifespan, mind, requisites, karma, birth, resolve, aspiration, spiritual power, wisdom, and dharma, detailed in the Bodhisattva Ground's chapter on abiding) and its skillful means for teaching beings through four kinds of transformative activity: ripening the unripe, liberating the ripened across countless world-systems at once, and appearing as a śrāvaka or a solitary buddha as each occasion calls for. With this sixfold account, brief praise of the Buddha — whether by the five marks or by these six — is now fully closed. The treatise turns next to extensive praise.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Extensive praise of the Buddha means: the Bhagavān is of boundless renown, his virtue being measureless; he is able to bestow light, kindling the light of wisdom; he is able to dispel darkness, permanently extinguishing all the darkness of ignorance; he has accomplished the eye of clarity, complete with the three eyes; he sees ultimate truth, understanding the unequaled noble truths; he has accomplished the moral precepts, complete and venerable in discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Extensive praise unfolds across fourteen sections in all, each gathering several virtues at once; this first section gathers six. His renown is boundless because his merit itself is measureless. He bestows the light of wisdom on all beings, and permanently dispels the darkness of ignorance in its place. He is complete with three eyes — the Ground of What Is Heard names them as the fleshly eye, seeing unobstructed visible things directly before it; the heavenly eye, seeing things hidden or exposed, obstructed or unobstructed alike; and the eye of wisdom, seeing all dharmas, form and formless, in their empty nature — or, by another accounting, five eyes, adding the dharma-eye that sees reality's own principle and the buddha-eye that gathers all four. He sees the noble truths as no śrāvaka or bodhisattva can fully match, directly and completely. And he has accomplished the moral precepts perfectly and purely, a venerable elder in this respect above all others.

26. Section: Foremost Among the Two-Footed, Tamer Supreme, Finest Among Renunciants, a Rare Jewel of the World

Thus he is foremost among the two-footed; supreme and highest among all tamers; the very finest among the assembly of renunciants; a rare jewel difficult to find in any world.

Chinese Source

如是兩足中尊、諸調御中最勝最上、沙門眾中最為殊美、是諸世間難得珍寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he is foremost among the two-footed; supreme and highest among all tamers; the very finest among the assembly of renunciants; a rare jewel difficult to find in any world.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four further virtues here: foremost among the two-footed, his merit and wisdom — the two "feet" — both perfected beyond any other being; supreme among tamers, the finest guide for training and transforming beings that the world has ever known; finest among renunciants, his body adorned with the thirty-two marks and eighty excellences beyond any other monastic's bearing; and a rare jewel of the world, his very appearance in the world an occurrence beyond ordinary reckoning.

27. Section: One of Great Compassion, Seeking Only Benefit for Beings

Thus he is one who pities; one of great compassion; one who delights in what is meaningful; one who seeks benefit; one who is constantly compassionate.

Chinese Source

如是為哀愍者、為大悲者、樂為義者、求利益者、常悲愍者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he is one who pities; one of great compassion; one who delights in what is meaningful; one who seeks benefit; one who is constantly compassionate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Five virtues of compassion: seeing foolish beings drowning in the river of craving, swept along by wrong thinking, filled with the three poisons and utterly without transcendent wisdom, the Buddha deeply pities them — hence "one who pities"; his compassion for all beings runs profound — hence "one of great compassion"; he delights in bringing beings benefit — hence "one who delights in what is meaningful"; he actively seeks their good — hence "one who seeks benefit"; and this compassion never lapses — hence "one who is constantly compassionate."

28. Section: Eye, Wisdom, Meaning, and Dharma — Six Virtues by Which He Illuminates and Rightly Resolves

Thus he is eye, wisdom, meaning, and dharma; skillfully resolving what is to be made clear; whatever he does, all of it grounded in meaning.

Chinese Source

如是為眼、為智、為義、為法，於明了義能善決定，凡有所作皆依於義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he is eye, wisdom, meaning, and dharma; skillfully resolving what is to be made clear; whatever he does, all of it grounded in meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary referring to the Compendium of Synonyms

Having attained unsurpassed complete awakening, the Buddha teaches gods and humans and draws forth their innate wisdom — hence "eye"; draws forth the wisdom cultivated through practice — hence "wisdom"; draws forth the wisdom born of reflection — hence "meaning"; and draws forth the wisdom born of hearing — hence "dharma." He skillfully resolves whatever meaning is to be made clear, cutting off every doubt, and whatever he does — body, speech, and mind alike — brings about worldly and transcendent flourishing, grounded always in what is truly meaningful.

29. Section: Knower of the Middle Way, Realizer, Revealer, Teacher, and Guide of the Path — Seven Virtues

Thus he can realize every meaning not yet realized, having first realized the noble eightfold path, realized spontaneously; he skillfully establishes what has never before been established, the supreme conduct of purity; he is knower of the middle way; realizer of the path; revealer of the path; teacher of the path; guide upon the path.

Chinese Source

如是能證一切所未證義，由先證聖八支道故，自然證故；善能制立所未曾立，勝梵行故；是知中道者、是證道者、是示道者、是說道者、是引道者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he can realize every meaning not yet realized, having first realized the noble eightfold path, realized spontaneously; he skillfully establishes what has never before been established, the supreme conduct of purity; he is knower of the middle way; realizer of the path; revealer of the path; teacher of the path; guide upon the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Seven virtues concerning the path itself. He realized the noble eightfold path entirely without a teacher, and so can realize what no one else has yet realized; having become a buddha, he establishes precepts never established before, fitted to each occasion, so beings may cultivate a pure conduct free of desire; he truly knows the middle way, apart from suffering and pleasure, permanence and annihilation, existence and non-existence, excess and deficiency; he has realized buddhahood itself, together with the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, and every other excellent quality; he reveals the path, skillfully unfolding the supreme truth and cutting through every net of doubt by his own example; he teaches the path, expounding the many stages, faculties, persons, kinds of faith, kinds of understanding, and kinds of reflection, and the domain known only to one of all-encompassing wisdom, so that beings realize the fruits of the three vehicles; and he guides upon the path, leading each being through whatever approach they themselves find congenial, according to their own capacity, until they are carried across. With this, the fifth of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

Thus he is the lion among men, free of all fear; he is the bull-king among men, leading the great herd; he is the guide among men, foremost of the assembly; he is the dragon-king among men, free of all error; he is the fine steed among men, his mind well tamed; he is the foremost among men, his lineage and bearing outshining all others; he is the highest among men, his discipline, right conduct, surpassing wisdom, and majestic virtue outshining all others; he is the lotus among men, unstained by the eight worldly winds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, with the 披尋記 commentary

Eight images drawn from the natural world. Fiercest and most fearless of beasts, the lion pictures the Buddha's fearless nirvāṇa, beyond every calamity; the bull that leads its herd pictures him leading all beings in form and wisdom alike; a nation's own leader pictures his place as foremost of all beings; the dragon-king's flawless power pictures the unerring action among his eighteen unique buddha-qualities; the well-tamed horse pictures a mind that answers only to its own wisdom; his royal lineage and princely bearing, together with the thirty-two marks and eighty excellences already described under wondrous form, outshine all others; his discipline (already named "stillness" in brief praise), right conduct, surpassing wisdom, and majestic virtue outshine all others; and the lotus, rising unstained from the mud, pictures him living untouched by the world's eight winds — gain and loss, honor and disgrace, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. With this, the sixth of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

31. Section: Without Equal, Equal Only to the Buddhas, Foremost, Great Sage-King, Great Muni, and Beyond All Argument — Twelve Virtues

Thus he is without equal, having no equal; equal to the unequaled, equal to all buddhas of the three times; foremost, supreme among all sentient beings; the great sage-king, venerable in discipline, having long accumulated the supreme conduct of purity, having realized the very path realized by the great sages of old; the most excellent, able to prevail over all outsiders, afflictions, and every kind of demonic hindrance; the great muni, free of all restlessness, conceit, and the rest; complete with the three stillnesses; unable to be swayed, unmoved by any birth or any rival doctrine whatsoever; well bathed, forever free of every evil dharma; and one who has reached the far shore, having transcended the view of a self entirely.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he is without equal, having no equal; equal to the unequaled, equal to all buddhas of the three times; foremost, supreme among all sentient beings; the great sage-king, venerable in discipline, having long accumulated the supreme conduct of purity, having realized the very path realized by the great sages of old; the most excellent, able to prevail over all outsiders, afflictions, and every kind of demonic hindrance; the great muni, free of all restlessness, conceit, and the rest, complete with the three stillnesses; unable to be swayed, unmoved by any birth or any rival doctrine whatsoever; well bathed, forever free of every evil dharma; and one who has reached the far shore, having transcended the view of a self entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Twelve further titles. No being equals the Buddha's wisdom, merit, compassion, and power; yet every buddha equals every other buddha across all three times — "the unequaled equal." He is foremost among beings, having realized unsurpassed awakening in full. He is the great sage-king, an elder venerable in discipline through three great incalculable eons of accumulated pure conduct, who has realized the very ancient path — the noble eightfold path — that every noble one before him has walked. He prevails over every outsider and every demonic hindrance — the afflictions, the aggregates, death, and the sixth-heaven god alike. He is the great muni ("muni" meaning stillness): body, speech, and mind all stilled, free of restlessness, conceit, and ignorance — the very three stillnesses just named. No being's arising and no rival philosophy can shift his understanding. He is "well bathed," forever free of every affliction and evil karma, having washed himself clean of dust. And he has reached the far shore, having gone beyond every view of a self, wisdom encompassing the whole dharma-realm, both forms of self-clinging extinguished, unmoving in his own true nature. With this, the seventh of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.

32. Section: The Ten Epithets — Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly Enlightened, and the Rest, Ending in Bhagavān

Thus he is Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Enlightened, and so on, at length, ending with Bhagavān.

Chinese Source

如是如來應正等覺，乃至廣說是薄伽梵。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus he is Tathāgata, Worthy One, Perfectly and Fully Enlightened, and so on, at length, ending with Bhagavān.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, drawing on the Bodhisattva Ground's chapter on awakening

The traditional ten epithets, each explained here in turn. Tathāgata — his every word, from the day of his awakening to his final nirvāṇa, was never once false. Worthy One — in the threefold sense of having attained every attainment to be attained, being fit to serve as the unsurpassed field of merit for the world, and being worthy of all reverence and offering. Perfectly and Fully Enlightened — awakened to all dharmas exactly as their ultimate nature truly is, both obstructions cut off, wisdom unobstructed toward everything knowable. Perfect in Wisdom and Conduct — complete in the three knowledges (of the heavenly eye, of past lives, and of the exhaustion of contaminants) together with the practice of calm-abiding and insight. Well-Gone — risen to the very highest, never to return. Knower of the World — skillfully understanding every realm of beings and their vessel-world alike, defiled and pure. Unsurpassed Tamer — the one true great being of the world, master of his own mind, guided by wisdom alone. Teacher of Gods and Humans — explained through four aspects (the true eye, true wisdom, true meaning, and true dharma he offers as refuge and guide) that together let him rightly instruct gods and humans out of every suffering. Buddha — awakened effortlessly and spontaneously, without needing to reflect, to all three gatherings of teaching: what leads to benefit, what leads away from it, and what is neither. Bhagavān — victorious over the aggregates, afflictions, death, and the sixth-heaven god, and, by another accounting, possessed of sovereignty, blazing glory, dignity, renown, auspiciousness, and honor all at once. With this, the eighth of extensive praise's fourteen sections is closed.
