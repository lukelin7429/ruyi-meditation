The Three Higher Trainings

Fascicle 28 opens the fifth of the second yogic process's seventeen questions — training (學) — picking up directly where fascicle 27's account of instruction ended. It unpacks the three higher trainings: higher discipline, higher mind-training (the four absorptions and four formless attainments), and higher wisdom.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 28 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Training Opens — Three Higher Trainings Named

Fascicle 28 continues directly from fascicle 27's close, opening the fifth of seventeen questions: what is training? The treatise names the three higher trainings shared by no non-Buddhist path — discipline, mind, and wisdom — before unpacking each in turn.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之三……云何為學？謂三勝學。一、增上戒學，二、增上心學，三、增上慧學。云何增上戒學？謂安住具戒等，如前廣說。是名增上戒學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the third part of the second yogic process … What is training? It is the three higher trainings: higher discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom. What is higher discipline? It is abiding endowed with discipline and so on, as extensively explained earlier. This is called higher discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating this fascicle: the second yogic process's seventeen questions have now closed practitioner-types, classification, objects of meditation, and instruction; training, the fifth question, opens here and — together with the sixth question, conduct in accordance with training — spans this entire fascicle and into the next. He notes all three trainings are called "higher" (增上) precisely because they exist nowhere outside the Buddha's teaching — a non-Buddhist ascetic may keep vows, cultivate concentration, even develop insight of a kind, but only within the framework the Buddha taught do these three converge on actual liberation rather than merely refined worldly attainment.

Higher discipline itself, Cháng Bǎi notes, gets the shortest treatment here simply because it was already covered in full much earlier in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples — the sixfold structure of abiding endowed with discipline, well guarding the prātimokṣa vows, perfected conduct and range of activity, seeing great danger in the smallest fault, and properly undertaking the training precepts. The treatise simply cross-references that earlier account rather than repeating it, and moves directly to unpacking higher mind-training — concentration — in full.

2. Higher Mind-Training Opens: The Four Absorptions

Chinese Source

云何增上心學？謂離欲惡不善法，有尋、有伺，離生喜樂，入初靜慮具足安住，乃至能入第四靜慮具足安住，是名增上心學。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is higher mind-training? It is being free of desire and unwholesome states, accompanied by initial and sustained thought, with the joy and bliss born of seclusion, entering and fully abiding in the first absorption — up through entering and fully abiding in the fourth absorption. This is called higher mind-training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes higher mind-training spans two ranges: the four form-realm absorptions here, and the four formless attainments to follow. Each absorption has its own defining factors — the first, five: initial thought, sustained thought, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness of mind; the second, four: inner clarity, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness (initial and sustained thought having dropped away); the third, five: mindfulness, clear comprehension, the experience of bliss, one-pointedness, and equanimity (joy itself now having dropped away as too coarse); and the fourth, four: neutral feeling, purified equanimity, purified mindfulness, and one-pointedness — the most refined and stable of the four, and, as established earlier in this project, the last absorption in which breath itself still moves. Cháng Bǎi cites the Abhidharma tradition's criteria for what actually counts as "absorption" proper, as opposed to mere concentration: it must be able to both suppress affliction and sustain correct contemplation together, completely and pervasively — the desire realm's concentration can contemplate but not fully suppress affliction; the formless attainments can suppress but not fully contemplate; only the four form-realm absorptions do both at once, which is why they alone earn the name "static contemplation" (靜慮) in the strict sense.

3. The Formless Attainments; Higher Wisdom Opens

Chinese Source

又諸無色，及餘所有等持、等至，亦皆名為增上心學。……然依靜慮，能最初入聖諦現觀，正性離生，非全遠離一切靜慮能成此事，是故靜慮最為殊勝，故偏說為增上心學。云何增上慧學？謂於四聖諦等所有如實智見。是名增上慧學。

Ru-Yi Translation

The formless attainments, and all other forms of meditative composure and attainment as well, are likewise called higher mind-training. Yet it is specifically in dependence on the four absorptions that one first enters direct insight into the noble truths, entering the true nature that departs from arising — this cannot be accomplished by relying entirely on states apart from the four absorptions. It is for this reason that the absorptions are the most excellent, and higher mind-training is spoken of with particular reference to them. What is higher wisdom-training? It is the as-it-really-is knowledge and vision regarding the four noble truths and so on. This is called higher wisdom-training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the four formless attainments and every other named meditative state described elsewhere in the treatise's Basic Section all fall generically under higher mind-training — but the treatise is careful to specify that the four form-realm absorptions are what actually count as its emphasis, since it is only through them that a practitioner first breaks into direct insight and stream-entry; the formless attainments alone, however refined, cannot accomplish this. He adds the traditional reason the fourth absorption in particular carries such weight: it is the one state where concentration and wisdom are perfectly balanced, which is why the Buddha himself is said to have attained supreme awakening while abiding in the fourth absorption specifically. Higher wisdom, closing the three trainings, is described with deliberate brevity — as-it-really-is knowledge and vision of the four noble truths (and, by extension, dependent origination and every other teaching on the nature of things) — precise, undistorted seeing rather than inference or belief.

4. Why Exactly Three Trainings, No More and No Fewer

Chinese Source

問：何緣唯有三學，非少非多？答：建立定義故，智所依義故，辦所作義故。……建立定義者，謂增上戒學。所以者何？由戒建立心一境性，能令其心觸三摩地。智所依義者，謂增上心學。所以者何？由正定心念一境性，於所知事有如實智、如實見轉。辦所作義者，謂增上慧學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why are there exactly three trainings, no fewer and no more? Answer: because of establishing concentration, because of serving as wisdom's basis, and because of accomplishing what is to be done. Establishing concentration refers to higher discipline. Why? Because discipline establishes one-pointedness of mind, enabling the mind to touch true concentration. Serving as wisdom's basis refers to higher mind-training. Why? Because through a rightly concentrated mind with one-pointed mindfulness, as-it-really-is knowledge and as-it-really-is vision arise regarding what is to be known. Accomplishing what is to be done refers to higher wisdom-training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise's own explanation of why the three trainings can be neither reduced nor expanded: they form a strict functional chain, not three independent options. Discipline's entire purpose is establishing the mental stability concentration requires — a mind still pulled by unrestrained craving and aversion never settles enough to concentrate at all. Concentration's entire purpose is providing wisdom's necessary basis — only a sufficiently stable, undistracted mind can observe reality clearly enough for genuine insight to arise; an agitated mind can think about the four noble truths but never truly see them. And wisdom's entire purpose is accomplishing the actual task — ending affliction and attaining liberation — which neither discipline nor concentration alone can do, however perfectly cultivated. Cháng Bǎi's summary: discipline builds the foundation, concentration builds on that foundation the clarity wisdom needs, and wisdom alone crosses the finish line — remove any one link, and the chain cannot reach its result, which is exactly why the count is fixed at three.

5. Milestone: The Twelve-Link Chain from Pure Discipline to Final Nirvana

Having explained why there are exactly three trainings, the treatise now explains why they unfold in this specific order — an unbroken causal chain running from the simple fact of a clean conscience all the way to final liberation.

Chinese Source

答：先於尸羅善清淨故，便無憂悔；無憂悔故，歡喜安樂；由有樂故，心得正定；心得定故，能如實知、能如實見；如實知見，故能起厭；厭故離染；由離染故，便得解脫；得解脫故，證無所作究竟涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: First, through discipline well purified, there is no anxiety or regret. Through no anxiety or regret, there is gladness and ease. Through this ease, the mind attains right concentration. Through a concentrated mind, one is able to know and see as things really are. Through this true knowing and seeing, revulsion arises. Through revulsion, dispassion follows. Through dispassion, liberation is attained. Through liberation, one realizes final nirvana, in which nothing further remains to be done.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this one link-by-link chain the whole path compressed into a single sentence: clean discipline first removes the low hum of guilt and anxiety that a habitually careless life produces; a mind free of that hum naturally becomes glad, and gladness settles into genuine ease; ease of body and mind is the precondition concentration actually needs (a tense, guilty mind cannot settle, however hard it tries); a settled mind can finally see the five aggregates as they truly are — impermanent, suffering, empty, without self; seeing this clearly produces real revulsion, not moralized guilt but simple disenchantment; revulsion naturally cools into dispassion, the fading of craving's grip; dispassion is itself liberation; and liberation, followed through to its end, is final nirvana — "nothing further to be done," the same phrase, Cháng Bǎi notes, that closes every account of arhatship in this treatise. He adds the traditional teaching on why "higher discipline, higher mind, higher wisdom" are named "higher" (增上) at all: two reasons — first, because each exists specifically to lead toward the next (discipline toward concentration, concentration toward wisdom, wisdom toward liberation itself, called the "what-it-leads-toward" meaning); and second, because this precise threefold structure exists nowhere outside the Buddha's teaching, making it "supreme" in the strict sense, unique among every spiritual system a non-Buddhist ascetic might otherwise practice.

6. Partial Trainings, and Three Practitioners by Degree of Detachment

Chinese Source

又或有增上戒學，無增上心、無增上慧；或有增上戒學，亦有增上心，唯無增上慧；非有增上慧學，而無增上戒及無增上心。是故若有增上慧學，當知必定具足三學。……復有三種補特伽羅，依此三學入諦現觀。何等為三？一、未離欲，二、倍離欲，三、已離欲。……於一切欲全未離者……證預流果。倍離欲者，當於爾時證一來果。已離欲者，當於爾時證不還果。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner may have higher discipline alone, without higher mind or higher wisdom; or may have both higher discipline and higher mind, but lack higher wisdom alone; but there is no case of having higher wisdom while lacking higher discipline and higher mind. Therefore, wherever higher wisdom is present, the other two trainings are necessarily present as well. There are, further, three kinds of practitioner who enter direct insight into the truths by means of these three trainings: one not yet detached from desire, one half detached from desire, and one fully detached from desire. One entirely not yet detached from desire … realizes the fruit of stream-entry. One half detached from desire, at that same point, realizes the fruit of once-returning. One fully detached from desire, at that same point, realizes the fruit of non-returning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the "partial trainings" teaching as a clean hierarchy: bare discipline alone is possible (someone keeps precepts without ever developing concentration); discipline plus concentration is possible (someone attains absorption on a foundation of pure conduct, without yet gaining liberating wisdom); but wisdom never appears alone — genuine liberating insight is never found in a practitioner who lacks both stable discipline and concentration, since wisdom depends on both as its foundation. He adds the classical three grades of moral restraint this implies: precept-based restraint (prātimokṣa discipline, kept independent of meditative attainment), concentration-based restraint (the natural restraint that comes bundled with absorption itself), and path-based restraint (the effortless, fully liberated restraint of one who has actually attained the path) — the third being restraint in its most complete form, since it flows from freedom rather than effort.

The three practitioners are distinguished purely by how much desire-realm affliction has already been suppressed before direct insight is attained. One who has suppressed none of it, entering insight purely through effort at the preparatory stage, becomes a stream-enterer upon breakthrough — the ordinary, "step-by-step" path. One who has already suppressed six, seven, or eight of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction (doubling the ordinary practitioner's progress before ever attaining insight) leaps directly to once-returning upon breakthrough — the "transcending" path. And one who has suppressed all nine grades — genuinely free of desire-realm craving already — leaps directly to non-returning. Cháng Bǎi notes this shows the same moment of breakthrough insight can land a practitioner at three entirely different fruits, purely depending on how much groundwork was already laid beforehand — insight itself doesn't vary, but what it reveals to a more prepared mind goes further.

7. The Three Faculties: Not-Yet-Knowing, Already-Knowing, Fully-Knowing

Chinese Source

復有三根。一、未知欲知根，二、已知根，三、具知根。……謂於諸諦未現觀者，加行勤修諸諦現觀，依此建立未知欲知根。若於諸諦已得現觀，而居有學，依此建立已知根。若阿羅漢所作已辦，住無學位，依此建立具知根。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, three faculties: the faculty of wishing to know what is not yet known; the faculty of having already known; and the faculty of complete knowing. Regarding one who has not yet attained direct insight into the truths, diligently applying effort toward that insight — on this basis the faculty of wishing to know what is not yet known is established. Regarding one who has already attained direct insight into the truths but still remains a trainee — on this basis the faculty of having already known is established. Regarding an arhat who has accomplished all that is to be accomplished, abiding beyond training — on this basis the faculty of complete knowing is established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps these three faculties onto the entire path in one sweep: the faculty of wishing-to-know covers everyone from the very first stirring of genuine spiritual motivation through the whole preparatory and applied-effort stages, right up to the sixteenth mind-moment of the path of seeing — every practitioner who has not yet broken through, however advanced their preparation. The faculty of already-knowing covers every trainee from that breakthrough moment through the vajra-like concentration that finally destroys the subtlest remaining affliction — every stage from stream-enterer through the very threshold of arhatship. And the faculty of complete knowing covers the arhat alone, from the moment of full realization until entering final nirvana without residue. Each of these three, Cháng Bǎi notes, is built from the same core set of faculties — faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — differing only in maturity and intensity, with the highest grade alone having fully shed the faculty of distress that still shadows even a third-fruit non-returner. Discipline, concentration, and wisdom describe what is cultivated; these three faculties describe who is doing the cultivating, at each stage of the same single journey.

8. Milestone: The Three Gates of Liberation Close Training

Training closes with the three gates of liberation — emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness — each a distinct doorway through which a practitioner passes out of the three realms entirely.

Chinese Source

復有三解脫門。一、空解脫門，二、無願解脫門，三、無相解脫門。……於有為中見過失故、見過患故，無所祈願；無祈願故，依此建立無願解脫門。於有為中無祈願故，便於涅槃深生祈願，見極寂靜、見甚微妙、見永出離；由於中見永出離故，依此建立無相解脫門。於其非有無所有中，非有祈願、非無祈願；如其非有，還則如是知為非有、見為非有，依此建立空解脫門。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, three gates of liberation: the gate of emptiness, the gate of wishlessness, and the gate of signlessness. Seeing fault and defect within conditioned phenomena, one has nothing further to wish for within them — on this basis the gate of wishlessness is established. Having nothing to wish for within conditioned phenomena, one instead wishes deeply for nirvana, seeing it as utterly peaceful, deeply sublime, and a final departure — on this basis, seeing this final departure, the gate of signlessness is established. Regarding the self, which does not exist at all, there is neither wishing nor not-wishing — knowing and seeing it, just as it is, to be non-existent — on this basis the gate of emptiness is established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each gate's specific logic. Wishlessness comes first: a practitioner who has genuinely seen the pervasive fault in every conditioned state — however pleasant — stops wishing for any further rebirth within the three realms at all; the wish for continued existence simply falls away. Signlessness follows: with no more wishing directed at conditioned existence, the mind turns instead toward nirvana itself, seen as utterly peaceful, refined beyond ordinary experience, and a genuine final departure — free of every characteristic (色, 聲, 香, 味, 觸, and all the marks by which conditioned things are recognized), hence "signless." And emptiness underlies both: since there was never a real self there to wish or not wish for anything in the first place, both the aversion to saṃsāra and the longing for nirvana are themselves seen as free of any real self doing the aversion or the longing — knowledge and direct vision both confirming that no self exists to be found anywhere in this process. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise also gives a Yogācāra reading matching each gate to one of the three natures: emptiness corresponds to the imagined nature (the falsely constructed sense of self), wishlessness to the dependent nature (the causally conditioned process itself, worth no further wishing for), and signlessness to the perfected nature (reality's own true, sign-free character). This closes training, the fifth of the seventeen questions, in full.

9. Milestone: Conduct in Accordance with Training Opens

The sixth question opens: what conduct actually accords with — supports rather than undermines — the three trainings just explained? The treatise frames this negatively first, naming ten obstructions to be overcome.

Chinese Source

云何隨順學法？謂有十種違逆學法，對治彼故，應知十種隨順學法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is conduct in accordance with training? There are ten kinds of conduct that run contrary to training; know that the ten kinds of conduct in accordance with training are simply what counters them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's approach here is deliberately practical rather than abstract: rather than describing ten virtues in isolation, it first names ten specific ways a practitioner's conduct actively works against the three trainings already explained — presumably habits and attitudes that undermine discipline, scatter concentration, or cloud wisdom — and defines the corresponding ten supportive behaviors simply as their direct opposites. This mirrors the broader logic of training itself as a chain: just as discipline, concentration, and wisdom build on each other in sequence, undermining any one of them at the level of daily conduct threatens the whole structure. The ten specific obstructions, and their ten counters, are unpacked next.
