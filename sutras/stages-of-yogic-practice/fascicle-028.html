The Three Higher Trainings

Fascicle 28 opens the fifth of the second yogic process's seventeen questions — training (學) — picking up directly where fascicle 27's account of instruction ended. It unpacks the three higher trainings: higher discipline, higher mind-training (the four absorptions and four formless attainments), and higher wisdom.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 28 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Training Opens — Three Higher Trainings Named

Fascicle 28 continues directly from fascicle 27's close, opening the fifth of seventeen questions: what is training? The treatise names the three higher trainings shared by no non-Buddhist path — discipline, mind, and wisdom — before unpacking each in turn.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之三……云何為學？謂三勝學。一、增上戒學，二、增上心學，三、增上慧學。云何增上戒學？謂安住具戒等，如前廣說。是名增上戒學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the third part of the second yogic process … What is training? It is the three higher trainings: higher discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom. What is higher discipline? It is abiding endowed with discipline and so on, as extensively explained earlier. This is called higher discipline.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating this fascicle: the second yogic process's seventeen questions have now closed practitioner-types, classification, objects of meditation, and instruction; training, the fifth question, opens here and — together with the sixth question, conduct in accordance with training — spans this entire fascicle and into the next. He notes all three trainings are called "higher" (增上) precisely because they exist nowhere outside the Buddha's teaching — a non-Buddhist ascetic may keep vows, cultivate concentration, even develop insight of a kind, but only within the framework the Buddha taught do these three converge on actual liberation rather than merely refined worldly attainment.

Higher discipline itself, Cháng Bǎi notes, gets the shortest treatment here simply because it was already covered in full much earlier in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples — the sixfold structure of abiding endowed with discipline, well guarding the prātimokṣa vows, perfected conduct and range of activity, seeing great danger in the smallest fault, and properly undertaking the training precepts. The treatise simply cross-references that earlier account rather than repeating it, and moves directly to unpacking higher mind-training — concentration — in full.

2. Higher Mind-Training Opens: The Four Absorptions

Chinese Source

云何增上心學？謂離欲惡不善法，有尋、有伺，離生喜樂，入初靜慮具足安住，乃至能入第四靜慮具足安住，是名增上心學。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is higher mind-training? It is being free of desire and unwholesome states, accompanied by initial and sustained thought, with the joy and bliss born of seclusion, entering and fully abiding in the first absorption — up through entering and fully abiding in the fourth absorption. This is called higher mind-training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes higher mind-training spans two ranges: the four form-realm absorptions here, and the four formless attainments to follow. Each absorption has its own defining factors — the first, five: initial thought, sustained thought, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness of mind; the second, four: inner clarity, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness (initial and sustained thought having dropped away); the third, five: mindfulness, clear comprehension, the experience of bliss, one-pointedness, and equanimity (joy itself now having dropped away as too coarse); and the fourth, four: neutral feeling, purified equanimity, purified mindfulness, and one-pointedness — the most refined and stable of the four, and, as established earlier in this project, the last absorption in which breath itself still moves. Cháng Bǎi cites the Abhidharma tradition's criteria for what actually counts as "absorption" proper, as opposed to mere concentration: it must be able to both suppress affliction and sustain correct contemplation together, completely and pervasively — the desire realm's concentration can contemplate but not fully suppress affliction; the formless attainments can suppress but not fully contemplate; only the four form-realm absorptions do both at once, which is why they alone earn the name "static contemplation" (靜慮) in the strict sense.

3. The Formless Attainments; Higher Wisdom Opens

Chinese Source

又諸無色，及餘所有等持、等至，亦皆名為增上心學。……然依靜慮，能最初入聖諦現觀，正性離生，非全遠離一切靜慮能成此事，是故靜慮最為殊勝，故偏說為增上心學。云何增上慧學？謂於四聖諦等所有如實智見。是名增上慧學。

Ru-Yi Translation

The formless attainments, and all other forms of meditative composure and attainment as well, are likewise called higher mind-training. Yet it is specifically in dependence on the four absorptions that one first enters direct insight into the noble truths, entering the true nature that departs from arising — this cannot be accomplished by relying entirely on states apart from the four absorptions. It is for this reason that the absorptions are the most excellent, and higher mind-training is spoken of with particular reference to them. What is higher wisdom-training? It is the as-it-really-is knowledge and vision regarding the four noble truths and so on. This is called higher wisdom-training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the four formless attainments and every other named meditative state described elsewhere in the treatise's Basic Section all fall generically under higher mind-training — but the treatise is careful to specify that the four form-realm absorptions are what actually count as its emphasis, since it is only through them that a practitioner first breaks into direct insight and stream-entry; the formless attainments alone, however refined, cannot accomplish this. He adds the traditional reason the fourth absorption in particular carries such weight: it is the one state where concentration and wisdom are perfectly balanced, which is why the Buddha himself is said to have attained supreme awakening while abiding in the fourth absorption specifically. Higher wisdom, closing the three trainings, is described with deliberate brevity — as-it-really-is knowledge and vision of the four noble truths (and, by extension, dependent origination and every other teaching on the nature of things) — precise, undistorted seeing rather than inference or belief.

4. Why Exactly Three Trainings, No More and No Fewer

Chinese Source

問：何緣唯有三學，非少非多？答：建立定義故，智所依義故，辦所作義故。……建立定義者，謂增上戒學。所以者何？由戒建立心一境性，能令其心觸三摩地。智所依義者，謂增上心學。所以者何？由正定心念一境性，於所知事有如實智、如實見轉。辦所作義者，謂增上慧學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why are there exactly three trainings, no fewer and no more? Answer: because of establishing concentration, because of serving as wisdom's basis, and because of accomplishing what is to be done. Establishing concentration refers to higher discipline. Why? Because discipline establishes one-pointedness of mind, enabling the mind to touch true concentration. Serving as wisdom's basis refers to higher mind-training. Why? Because through a rightly concentrated mind with one-pointed mindfulness, as-it-really-is knowledge and as-it-really-is vision arise regarding what is to be known. Accomplishing what is to be done refers to higher wisdom-training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise's own explanation of why the three trainings can be neither reduced nor expanded: they form a strict functional chain, not three independent options. Discipline's entire purpose is establishing the mental stability concentration requires — a mind still pulled by unrestrained craving and aversion never settles enough to concentrate at all. Concentration's entire purpose is providing wisdom's necessary basis — only a sufficiently stable, undistracted mind can observe reality clearly enough for genuine insight to arise; an agitated mind can think about the four noble truths but never truly see them. And wisdom's entire purpose is accomplishing the actual task — ending affliction and attaining liberation — which neither discipline nor concentration alone can do, however perfectly cultivated. Cháng Bǎi's summary: discipline builds the foundation, concentration builds on that foundation the clarity wisdom needs, and wisdom alone crosses the finish line — remove any one link, and the chain cannot reach its result, which is exactly why the count is fixed at three.

5. Milestone: The Twelve-Link Chain from Pure Discipline to Final Nirvana

Having explained why there are exactly three trainings, the treatise now explains why they unfold in this specific order — an unbroken causal chain running from the simple fact of a clean conscience all the way to final liberation.

Chinese Source

答：先於尸羅善清淨故，便無憂悔；無憂悔故，歡喜安樂；由有樂故，心得正定；心得定故，能如實知、能如實見；如實知見，故能起厭；厭故離染；由離染故，便得解脫；得解脫故，證無所作究竟涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: First, through discipline well purified, there is no anxiety or regret. Through no anxiety or regret, there is gladness and ease. Through this ease, the mind attains right concentration. Through a concentrated mind, one is able to know and see as things really are. Through this true knowing and seeing, revulsion arises. Through revulsion, dispassion follows. Through dispassion, liberation is attained. Through liberation, one realizes final nirvana, in which nothing further remains to be done.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this one link-by-link chain the whole path compressed into a single sentence: clean discipline first removes the low hum of guilt and anxiety that a habitually careless life produces; a mind free of that hum naturally becomes glad, and gladness settles into genuine ease; ease of body and mind is the precondition concentration actually needs (a tense, guilty mind cannot settle, however hard it tries); a settled mind can finally see the five aggregates as they truly are — impermanent, suffering, empty, without self; seeing this clearly produces real revulsion, not moralized guilt but simple disenchantment; revulsion naturally cools into dispassion, the fading of craving's grip; dispassion is itself liberation; and liberation, followed through to its end, is final nirvana — "nothing further to be done," the same phrase, Cháng Bǎi notes, that closes every account of arhatship in this treatise. He adds the traditional teaching on why "higher discipline, higher mind, higher wisdom" are named "higher" (增上) at all: two reasons — first, because each exists specifically to lead toward the next (discipline toward concentration, concentration toward wisdom, wisdom toward liberation itself, called the "what-it-leads-toward" meaning); and second, because this precise threefold structure exists nowhere outside the Buddha's teaching, making it "supreme" in the strict sense, unique among every spiritual system a non-Buddhist ascetic might otherwise practice.

6. Partial Trainings, and Three Practitioners by Degree of Detachment

Chinese Source

又或有增上戒學，無增上心、無增上慧；或有增上戒學，亦有增上心，唯無增上慧；非有增上慧學，而無增上戒及無增上心。是故若有增上慧學，當知必定具足三學。……復有三種補特伽羅，依此三學入諦現觀。何等為三？一、未離欲，二、倍離欲，三、已離欲。……於一切欲全未離者……證預流果。倍離欲者，當於爾時證一來果。已離欲者，當於爾時證不還果。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner may have higher discipline alone, without higher mind or higher wisdom; or may have both higher discipline and higher mind, but lack higher wisdom alone; but there is no case of having higher wisdom while lacking higher discipline and higher mind. Therefore, wherever higher wisdom is present, the other two trainings are necessarily present as well. There are, further, three kinds of practitioner who enter direct insight into the truths by means of these three trainings: one not yet detached from desire, one half detached from desire, and one fully detached from desire. One entirely not yet detached from desire … realizes the fruit of stream-entry. One half detached from desire, at that same point, realizes the fruit of once-returning. One fully detached from desire, at that same point, realizes the fruit of non-returning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the "partial trainings" teaching as a clean hierarchy: bare discipline alone is possible (someone keeps precepts without ever developing concentration); discipline plus concentration is possible (someone attains absorption on a foundation of pure conduct, without yet gaining liberating wisdom); but wisdom never appears alone — genuine liberating insight is never found in a practitioner who lacks both stable discipline and concentration, since wisdom depends on both as its foundation. He adds the classical three grades of moral restraint this implies: precept-based restraint (prātimokṣa discipline, kept independent of meditative attainment), concentration-based restraint (the natural restraint that comes bundled with absorption itself), and path-based restraint (the effortless, fully liberated restraint of one who has actually attained the path) — the third being restraint in its most complete form, since it flows from freedom rather than effort.

The three practitioners are distinguished purely by how much desire-realm affliction has already been suppressed before direct insight is attained. One who has suppressed none of it, entering insight purely through effort at the preparatory stage, becomes a stream-enterer upon breakthrough — the ordinary, "step-by-step" path. One who has already suppressed six, seven, or eight of the nine grades of desire-realm affliction (doubling the ordinary practitioner's progress before ever attaining insight) leaps directly to once-returning upon breakthrough — the "transcending" path. And one who has suppressed all nine grades — genuinely free of desire-realm craving already — leaps directly to non-returning. Cháng Bǎi notes this shows the same moment of breakthrough insight can land a practitioner at three entirely different fruits, purely depending on how much groundwork was already laid beforehand — insight itself doesn't vary, but what it reveals to a more prepared mind goes further.

7. The Three Faculties: Not-Yet-Knowing, Already-Knowing, Fully-Knowing

Chinese Source

復有三根。一、未知欲知根，二、已知根，三、具知根。……謂於諸諦未現觀者，加行勤修諸諦現觀，依此建立未知欲知根。若於諸諦已得現觀，而居有學，依此建立已知根。若阿羅漢所作已辦，住無學位，依此建立具知根。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, three faculties: the faculty of wishing to know what is not yet known; the faculty of having already known; and the faculty of complete knowing. Regarding one who has not yet attained direct insight into the truths, diligently applying effort toward that insight — on this basis the faculty of wishing to know what is not yet known is established. Regarding one who has already attained direct insight into the truths but still remains a trainee — on this basis the faculty of having already known is established. Regarding an arhat who has accomplished all that is to be accomplished, abiding beyond training — on this basis the faculty of complete knowing is established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps these three faculties onto the entire path in one sweep: the faculty of wishing-to-know covers everyone from the very first stirring of genuine spiritual motivation through the whole preparatory and applied-effort stages, right up to the sixteenth mind-moment of the path of seeing — every practitioner who has not yet broken through, however advanced their preparation. The faculty of already-knowing covers every trainee from that breakthrough moment through the vajra-like concentration that finally destroys the subtlest remaining affliction — every stage from stream-enterer through the very threshold of arhatship. And the faculty of complete knowing covers the arhat alone, from the moment of full realization until entering final nirvana without residue. Each of these three, Cháng Bǎi notes, is built from the same core set of faculties — faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — differing only in maturity and intensity, with the highest grade alone having fully shed the faculty of distress that still shadows even a third-fruit non-returner. Discipline, concentration, and wisdom describe what is cultivated; these three faculties describe who is doing the cultivating, at each stage of the same single journey.

8. Milestone: The Three Gates of Liberation Close Training

Training closes with the three gates of liberation — emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness — each a distinct doorway through which a practitioner passes out of the three realms entirely.

Chinese Source

復有三解脫門。一、空解脫門，二、無願解脫門，三、無相解脫門。……於有為中見過失故、見過患故，無所祈願；無祈願故，依此建立無願解脫門。於有為中無祈願故，便於涅槃深生祈願，見極寂靜、見甚微妙、見永出離；由於中見永出離故，依此建立無相解脫門。於其非有無所有中，非有祈願、非無祈願；如其非有，還則如是知為非有、見為非有，依此建立空解脫門。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, three gates of liberation: the gate of emptiness, the gate of wishlessness, and the gate of signlessness. Seeing fault and defect within conditioned phenomena, one has nothing further to wish for within them — on this basis the gate of wishlessness is established. Having nothing to wish for within conditioned phenomena, one instead wishes deeply for nirvana, seeing it as utterly peaceful, deeply sublime, and a final departure — on this basis, seeing this final departure, the gate of signlessness is established. Regarding the self, which does not exist at all, there is neither wishing nor not-wishing — knowing and seeing it, just as it is, to be non-existent — on this basis the gate of emptiness is established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each gate's specific logic. Wishlessness comes first: a practitioner who has genuinely seen the pervasive fault in every conditioned state — however pleasant — stops wishing for any further rebirth within the three realms at all; the wish for continued existence simply falls away. Signlessness follows: with no more wishing directed at conditioned existence, the mind turns instead toward nirvana itself, seen as utterly peaceful, refined beyond ordinary experience, and a genuine final departure — free of every characteristic (色, 聲, 香, 味, 觸, and all the marks by which conditioned things are recognized), hence "signless." And emptiness underlies both: since there was never a real self there to wish or not wish for anything in the first place, both the aversion to saṃsāra and the longing for nirvana are themselves seen as free of any real self doing the aversion or the longing — knowledge and direct vision both confirming that no self exists to be found anywhere in this process. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise also gives a Yogācāra reading matching each gate to one of the three natures: emptiness corresponds to the imagined nature (the falsely constructed sense of self), wishlessness to the dependent nature (the causally conditioned process itself, worth no further wishing for), and signlessness to the perfected nature (reality's own true, sign-free character). This closes training, the fifth of the seventeen questions, in full.

9. Milestone: Conduct in Accordance with Training Opens

The sixth question opens: what conduct actually accords with — supports rather than undermines — the three trainings just explained? The treatise frames this negatively first, naming ten obstructions to be overcome.

Chinese Source

云何隨順學法？謂有十種違逆學法，對治彼故，應知十種隨順學法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is conduct in accordance with training? There are ten kinds of conduct that run contrary to training; know that the ten kinds of conduct in accordance with training are simply what counters them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's approach here is deliberately practical rather than abstract: rather than describing ten virtues in isolation, it first names ten specific ways a practitioner's conduct actively works against the three trainings already explained — presumably habits and attitudes that undermine discipline, scatter concentration, or cloud wisdom — and defines the corresponding ten supportive behaviors simply as their direct opposites. This mirrors the broader logic of training itself as a chain: just as discipline, concentration, and wisdom build on each other in sequence, undermining any one of them at the level of daily conduct threatens the whole structure. The ten specific obstructions, and their ten counters, are unpacked next.

10. Six of the Ten Obstructions to Training

Chinese Source

一者、所有母邑少年、盛壯、可愛形色，是正修學善男子等上品障礙。二者、於薩迦耶所攝諸行生起愛著。三者、懶惰懈怠。四者、薩迦耶見。五者、依於段食貪著美味。六者、於諸世間種種戲論非一眾多別別品類所思念中，發欲貪愛。

Ru-Yi Translation

First: the youthful, vigorous, attractive appearance of women is a severe obstruction for a good son rightly engaged in training. Second: giving rise to attachment toward the formations comprised in the view of a substantial self. Third: laziness and indolence. Fourth: the view of a substantial self. Fifth: craving fine flavor in ordinary food. Sixth: giving rise to desire and craving amid the many varied kinds of worldly idle talk one dwells upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the first six obstructions as a practical inventory of what actually derails practitioners day to day, not abstract theory. The first — attraction to an appealing appearance — heads the list because, in his reading, it is the single most common cause of a practitioner abandoning discipline outright. The second and fourth are closely related: attachment to one's own body (bathing, grooming, protecting, indulging it) and the underlying self-view that makes such attachment feel reasonable in the first place — both stand in direct opposition to the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, which require treating this very attachment as the problem to be solved, not indulged. Laziness (undeveloped virtue never begun) and indolence (developed virtue not sustained) are distinguished as two related but separate failures. Craving fine flavor in food seems minor but, Cháng Bǎi notes, a well-fed, comfortable body quickly generates further appetites — for status, wealth, influence — that leave no room for practice. And worldly idle talk — poetry, music, entertainment, and (Elder Miàojìng is cited making this point pointedly) secular academic study pursued instead of genuine dharma study — cultivates exactly the discursive craving breath-mindfulness and the other purifying objects exist to dismantle.

11. Eleven Faults of Meditative Attention, and the Remaining Obstructions

Chinese Source

七者、思惟諸法瑜伽作意所有過患。……謂十一種。一、於諸諦寶蘊業果中猶豫疑惑。二、樂修斷者，身諸麤重。三、有慢緩者……令心極略。四、太猛精進者，身疲心惱。五、太劣精進者，不得勝進，善品衰退。六、於少利養名譽稱讚隨一樂中，深生欣喜。七、掉舉不靜踊躍躁擾。八、於薩迦耶永滅涅槃而生驚恐。九、於諸言說非量加行，言論太過……起諍方便。十、於先所見、所聞、所受……心馳、心散。十一、不應思處而彊沈思。

Ru-Yi Translation

Seventh: the faults belonging to attending, with yogic mental engagement, to the phenomena one contemplates — briefly, eleven kinds: doubt and hesitation regarding the truths, the three jewels, the aggregates, and karma and its fruit; a body weighed down by grossness even while one wishes to cultivate cessation; dullness — the mind covered by torpor and drowsiness, contracted to the barest minimum; excessively forceful effort, leaving body fatigued and mind agitated; excessively feeble effort, gaining no advance and letting wholesome qualities decline; deep delight in even a small gain of support, reputation, or praise; restlessness — unsettled, elated, and agitated; terror at the thought of the substantial self's final cessation in nirvana; excessive argumentative speech, delighting in debate and refutation even while ostensibly discussing the dharma; a mind scattered and racing among the many varied objects previously seen, heard, and experienced; and forcibly dwelling in thought on matters that should not be thought about at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this eleven-fold list, folded into the seventh obstruction, as the treatise's own meditation troubleshooting guide. Doubt about the basic framework (the truths, the three jewels, karma) unsettles the very object one is trying to contemplate. Bodily heaviness — often traced, per the commentary, to careless eating during the day — leaves no energy for practice. Dullness and drowsiness at night are answered by the famous story of the lazy meditation teacher who slept through every session while urging his students on, only to be exposed and corrected by the Buddha himself: neither excessive slackness nor its opposite serves. Too-forceful effort exhausts body and mind through sheer strain; too-feeble effort, content with mere stillness and never developing insight, lets practice stagnate. Attachment to small gains of praise or support, restlessness, and endless argumentative debate (even about the dharma itself) all scatter exactly the stability concentration needs. Terror at nirvana deserves special note: a practitioner still gripped by self-view can genuinely recoil from the idea of the self's complete cessation, mistaking liberation for annihilation and stopping short of the very goal being sought. A mind racing through vivid past experience is simply old craving replaying itself. And forcing thought onto genuinely unanswerable questions — what am I, in some ultimate sense; what is another's inner nature; the origin of the world; the precise workings of karma; what a deep meditator's inner experience is actually like; what a Buddha's own realization actually is — produces only distraction and unwholesome mental agitation, since these are matters resolved only by direct realization, never by speculation. This eleven-fold breakdown is itself only the seventh of the ten obstructions named earlier; three further obstructions, and then the ten supportive practices that directly counter all ten, follow next.

12. Milestone: The Ten Obstructions Close

Three final obstructions complete the list of ten: delight in meditative bliss itself, scattering while pursuing signless concentration, and the fear of death that clings to life even amid unbearable pain.

Chinese Source

八者、於諸靜慮、等至樂中深生愛味。九者、樂欲證入無相定者，於諸行中隨順流散。十者、觸身苦受乃至奪命苦受時，貪愛壽命，希望存活；隨此希望，傷歎迷悶。是名十種違逆學法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eighth: deep attachment to the pleasure found in the absorptions and meditative attainments. Ninth: for one who wishes to enter signless concentration, the mind still scattering outward, drawn along by conditioned formations. Tenth: when touched by bodily suffering — even suffering severe enough to take one's life — clinging to life, hoping to go on living, and, following that hope, falling into grief and confused distress. These are the ten obstructions to training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the eighth obstruction catches even successful meditators: absorption is genuinely blissful, and that very bliss can become its own subtle attachment, mistaking a refined corner of saṃsāra for the goal itself. The ninth names an honest difficulty — wanting signless concentration is not the same as having the settled mind actually capable of it; wanting it while still pulled along by ordinary conditioned phenomena is itself an obstruction. And the tenth, clinging to life amid mortal suffering, Cháng Bǎi illustrates with Shàndǎo's classical instruction to a dying practitioner: don't fear death, don't grieve, recognize this body as a worn-out garment finally being exchanged for something better — a practitioner who has genuinely trained should, at the moment of death, feel something closer to relief than terror. He closes with the Abhidharma tradition's own verse for exactly this moment: "the holy life well established, the noble path well cultivated — at life's end, rejoice, as one sets aside a poisoned cup."

13. Milestone: The Ten Counter-Perceptions

Against each of the ten obstructions, the treatise sets a matching perception to cultivate — ten in total, each the direct antidote for one specific obstruction, in the same order as the obstructions themselves.

Chinese Source

謂有十種。一、不淨想，二、無常想，三、無常苦想，四、苦無我想，五、厭逆食想，六、一切世間不可樂想，七、光明想，八、離欲想，九、滅想，十、死想。如是十想善修、善習、善多修習，能斷十種障礙學法、違逆學法。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are ten: the perception of impurity, the perception of impermanence, the perception of impermanence as suffering, the perception of suffering as without self, the perception of aversion toward food, the perception that nothing in the entire world is truly enjoyable, the perception of light, the perception of dispassion, the perception of cessation, and the perception of death. These ten perceptions, well cultivated, well practiced, and thoroughly developed, are able to cut off the ten obstructions to training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the exact one-to-one matching the treatise intends. Impurity-perception (contemplating the body's thirty-six unclean components) counters attraction to youthful appearance. Impermanence-perception counters attachment to the self-view-bound aggregates. Impermanence-as-suffering counters laziness — recognizing that only diligent practice actually ends the suffering impermanence guarantees. Suffering-as-non-self counters self-view directly. Aversion toward food counters craving fine flavor. Seeing the whole world as unenjoyable counters delight in worldly idle talk. Light-perception — of four kinds, Cháng Bǎi explains: the light of the teaching itself, the light of its meaning, the light of calm-abiding, and the light of insight, any one of which can be drawn on as needed — counters the eleven faults of meditative attention specifically. Dispassion-perception (recognizing that even the bliss of absorption remains within saṃsāra, not worth clinging to) counters delight in meditative bliss. Cessation-perception (taking signless nirvana itself as object, contemplating all phenomena as inherently at peace) counters the scattering that undermines the pursuit of signless concentration. And death-perception — reflecting that life is impermanent and death certain, or, in Elder Miàojìng's deeper reading, that the five aggregates are already dying moment to moment, so that there is, in the most rigorous sense, nothing continuous there to lose — counters the grief and confusion that arise from clinging to life. Cultivated together and thoroughly, these ten perceptions dismantle all ten obstructions in turn, completing this account of conduct in accordance with training.

14. Milestone: A Second Framework — Ten Conditions That Favor Training

The treatise now offers a second, positively-framed account of conduct in accordance with training: ten conditions, cultivated directly, that favor the three trainings — beginning with past causes and closing, for now, with calming the body and mind's four sources of grossness.

Chinese Source

復有十種隨順學法。一者、宿因，二者、隨順教，三者、如理加行，四者、無間、殷重所作，五者、猛利樂欲，六者、持瑜伽力，七者、止息身心麤重，八者、數數觀察，九者、無有怯弱，十者、離增上慢。……謂如有一，或由身勞身乏，發身麤重、發心麤重；此因易脫威儀而便止息。或由太尋太伺，發身麤重、發心麤重；此因內心寂止方便而便止息。或由心略心劣，惛沈睡眠之所纏繞……此因增上慧法毗鉢舍那順淨作意而便止息。或由本性煩惱未斷……此因相續勤修正道而便止息。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, further, ten kinds of conduct favoring training: past causes; conduct according with instruction; correct application; work done without interruption and with earnest care; intense aspiration; sustaining the power of yogic practice; calming grossness of body and mind; repeated examination; freedom from timidity; and freedom from overestimation. … As for one whose bodily grossness and mental grossness arise from bodily fatigue and exhaustion — this is calmed simply by changing posture. As for one in whom it arises from excessive initial and sustained thought — this is calmed through the technique of inner calm-abiding. As for one in whom it arises from a contracted, feeble mind entangled in dullness and drowsiness — this is calmed through the higher-wisdom technique of insight practice, engaging the mind toward what is purifying. As for one in whom it arises because the underlying afflictions themselves remain uncut — this is calmed through sustained, diligent cultivation of the correct path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this second framework as more constructive than the first, naming what to build rather than what to remove. Past causes are simply the good roots already planted in prior lives, ripening now as ready capacity. Conduct according with instruction and correct application both circle back to the undistorted and gradual instruction defined earlier — receiving sound teaching, then actually practicing it without distortion. Uninterrupted, earnest practice means never squandering time on the idle talk already flagged as an obstruction. Intense aspiration is the burning wish to actually attain what the noble ones have attained, not merely admire it from a distance. Sustaining yogic power comes from two sources: an inherently sharp faculty carried over from past cultivation, and — available to everyone regardless of natural aptitude — sheer sustained repetition over a long enough time.

Calming grossness of body and mind, the seventh condition, gets the most detailed treatment: four distinct sources of "grossness" (a heaviness and lack of capability in body or mind), each with its own specific remedy. Simple physical fatigue from sitting or working too long is answered by nothing more sophisticated than changing posture — walking meditation instead of forcing a tired body to remain still. Excessive discursive thought — too much analytical churning over the teaching — is answered by shifting into calm-abiding practice rather than more thinking. Dullness and drowsiness, a mind gone slack and contracted, is answered by insight practice specifically, engaging one of the purifying objects of meditation, or the six recollections (of the Buddha, the teaching, the community, generosity, discipline, or the heavens), to sharpen a sinking mind back into clarity. And grossness rooted in genuinely uncut underlying affliction — not merely a passing dip in energy but the deeper problem itself — has only one real remedy: continued, patient cultivation of the entire path, since no quick technique substitutes for actually removing the affliction at its root. Four different problems, Cháng Bǎi notes, four different tools — misapplying calm-abiding to what actually needs insight, or vice versa, wastes exactly the effort this whole framework is meant to conserve.

15. Milestone: Conduct in Accordance with Training Closes

The final three conditions — repeated self-examination, freedom from discouragement, and freedom from overestimating one's own attainment — close both the second framework and the sixth question in full.

Chinese Source

謂依尸羅，數數觀察惡作不作，數數觀察善作而作。……又於煩惱斷與未斷觀察作意增上力故，數數觀察。若知已斷，便生歡喜；若知未斷，則便數數勤修正道。云何無有怯弱？謂於後時應知、應見、應證得中，未知、未見、未證得故，發生怯弱……彼既生已而不堅執，速能斷滅。云何離增上慢？謂於所得、所觸、所證，無增上慢，離顛倒執。於真所得起於得想，於真所觸起於觸想，於真所證起於證想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Repeated examination based on discipline means repeatedly checking that one has not committed what should not be done, and has continued what should be done. Repeated examination based on mental engagement means, by the power of examining whether affliction has or has not been cut off, checking again and again: if one finds it has been cut off, gladness arises; if one finds it has not, one diligently cultivates the correct path once more. What is freedom from timidity? It is this: even when discouragement arises upon finding that what should eventually be known, seen, and realized remains as yet unknown, unseen, and unrealized — leaving the mind weary and depleted — once this discouragement has arisen, not clinging to it, but swiftly letting it dissolve. What is freedom from overestimation? It is having no inflated conceit regarding what one has attained, touched, or realized, free of any distorted grasping — forming the notion of "attained" only where something is truly attained, "touched" only where truly touched, "realized" only where truly realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads repeated examination as a twofold check-in — against the precepts (catching and correcting faults quickly, sustaining what is already wholesome) and against one's own progress in cutting affliction (celebrating real gains honestly, redoubling effort where none has yet occurred) — the same unglamorous, ongoing audit any serious practice requires. Freedom from timidity addresses a real hazard of long practice: discouragement after years without dramatic breakthrough is natural, even expected, but a resilient practitioner lets that weariness pass through without setting up camp in it. And freedom from overestimation — the antidote to spiritual overconfidence — closes the list by insisting on scrupulous honesty about exactly what has and has not actually been attained, since claiming an attainment one hasn't genuinely reached is its own serious obstacle. With this, the sixth question — conduct in accordance with training — closes in full, having been given two complete treatments: ten obstructions matched to ten counter-perceptions, and these ten favorable conditions.

16. Milestone: The Failure of Yogic Practice Opens — Four Kinds

The seventh question opens: what does it mean for yogic practice itself to fail? The treatise names four distinct kinds of failure, beginning with the most severe — failure that is final and beyond repair.

Chinese Source

云何瑜伽壞？謂壞瑜伽略有四種。何等為四？一者、畢竟瑜伽壞，二者、暫時瑜伽壞，三者、退失所得瑜伽壞，四者、邪行所作瑜伽壞。畢竟瑜伽壞者，謂無種性補特伽羅。何以故？由彼身中無能趣向涅槃法故，畢竟失壞出世瑜伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the failure of yogic practice? Briefly, the failure of yogic practice is of four kinds. What are the four? Final failure; temporary failure; failure through the regression of what has been attained; and failure brought about by wrong conduct. Final failure refers to a person without the lineage for liberation. Why? Because within such a person there is no capacity that tends toward nirvana, and so the supramundane yogic practice is finally and completely lost to them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the seventh question as a necessary counterweight to everything said so far about training: having explained what supports practice, the treatise now turns to what can go wrong with it, in increasing order of how repairable each failure actually is. Final failure is the most severe: a being who genuinely lacks any of the three vehicles' spiritual lineage in their eighth consciousness has, in the treatise's own account, no seed capable of ever sprouting into liberation — not a moral judgment but a straightforward statement about causal capacity, the same logic that says a stone can't become a tree no matter how it's watered. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own teaching elsewhere still holds such beings are worth teaching and guiding toward favorable human or heavenly rebirth, even though full liberation isn't presently open to them — this category exists to explain a real limit within the treatise's system of skillful teaching, not to write anyone off from all benefit whatsoever. The remaining three kinds of failure — temporary, involving regression, and caused by wrong conduct — describe genuinely reversible setbacks that can affect any practitioner who does have the lineage, and are explained next.

17. Temporary Failure and the Regression of Attainment

Chinese Source

由彼身中有能趣向涅槃法故，雖闕外緣，時經久遠，定當緣會；修習瑜伽，令其現起；善修習已，當般涅槃。是故說彼所有瑜伽暫時失壞。……退失所得瑜伽壞者，謂如有一，退失所得、所觸、所證，若智、若見、若安樂住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because within such a person there is a capacity tending toward nirvana, even lacking the outer conditions for now, given enough time these conditions will certainly come together; yogic practice will be cultivated and caused to arise, and once well cultivated, nirvana will be attained. This is why such a person's yogic practice is said to be only temporarily lost. Failure through the regression of what has been attained refers to one who loses what has been attained, touched, or realized — the knowledge, the vision, or the peaceful abiding that went with it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats temporary failure as simply describing a being with genuine spiritual lineage who hasn't yet met a qualified teacher — the capacity is real and intact, only the outer condition of encountering the right guidance is missing, and given enough time, across enough lifetimes, that condition will eventually arise. Regression, by contrast, describes an actual loss after real attainment: a practitioner who has genuinely reached one of the four absorptions can, without the underlying afflictions being truly uprooted, lose that attainment again — concentration suppresses affliction rather than destroying its seed, so it remains vulnerable to falling away. Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's position differs slightly from some Abhidharma schools, which hold that only the second and third fruits of the noble path can regress while the first and fourth cannot: this treatise instead locates regression specifically in meditative attainment itself, while holding that the supramundane path, once genuinely attained, does not regress at all.

18. Milestone: The Fraudulent Teacher — Failure Through Wrong Conduct

The fourth and most elaborately described kind of failure: a naturally clever but deeply afflicted person who fakes spiritual attainment for material gain, then invents a counterfeit meditation system to protect the deception — resembling true dharma, but neither true nor harmless.

Chinese Source

邪行所作瑜伽壞者，謂如有一，不如正理精勤修行，雖多用功，無所成辦……又如有一，多諸煩惱，性多塵穢，而識聰銳，覺慧猛利……又行矯詐，妄現種種身語相應調善所作。由是因緣，招集利養恭敬稱頌、大福德想……咸共謂之是阿羅漢。……然此瑜伽，不順契經、不現戒律、違逆法性。……如是名為邪行所作瑜伽失壞，像似正法，非真正法，能障正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Failure brought about by wrong conduct refers to one who practices diligently but not in accordance with correct principle, and so, however much effort is expended, accomplishes nothing. There is a further case: one who has many afflictions and a nature much given to defilement, yet whose intelligence is sharp and whose innate discernment is keen … who further practices deceit, falsely displaying various well-composed bodily and verbal conduct. Through this cause, such a one gathers support, reverence, and praise, and a reputation for great merit … until all alike declare him an arhat. … Yet this system he has devised does not accord with the scriptures, does not appear in the discipline, and runs contrary to the true nature of things. … This is called the failure of yogic practice brought about by wrong conduct — resembling true dharma, yet not true dharma at all, and in fact obstructing the true dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as the treatise's own detailed case study in religious fraud, and reads it as strikingly contemporary. The figure described is genuinely gifted — sharp, quick-witted, able to absorb and repeat what he's heard — but riddled with unexamined affliction underneath a carefully composed exterior: calm bearing, controlled senses, an appearance of accomplishment he hasn't actually earned. Teaching a little dharma to simple, trusting people (deliberately chosen for their guilelessness rather than their discernment) earns him devoted followers, material support, and eventually a popular reputation as a fully realized arhat.

The trap tightens from there, per Cháng Bǎi's reading: aware that any follower well-versed in the actual scriptures, discipline, and teachings could expose him with a single hard question about genuine meditative practice, he privately invents his own counterfeit system of "yogic practice" — through exactly the same three-stage process (initial reflection, then extended consideration, then fine-grained analysis) a genuine practitioner would use to investigate real dharma, but aimed at fabricating a plausible fake instead. This invented system inevitably contradicts the actual scriptures, deviates from the actual monastic discipline, and violates the true nature of things — because it was never derived from reality in the first place, only from the need to sound convincing. Knowing this, he conceals it specifically from anyone learned enough to check it against the canon, teaching it only in secret to loyal, uncritical followers under strict instruction not to publicize it — his fear is twofold, Cháng Bǎi notes: being exposed as a fraud by knowledgeable critics, and losing the material support his false reputation currently secures. Followers who accept this counterfeit teaching in good faith, however sincerely and diligently they practice it, gain nothing — Cháng Bǎi's blunt summary of the treatise's own verdict: it looks like the true dharma, is not the true dharma, and actively blocks the true dharma from reaching those who might otherwise have found it. This closes all four kinds of the failure of yogic practice.

19. Milestone: Yogic Practice Itself Opens — Faith's Two Aspects and Two Bases

After a brief exhortation to recognize and abandon all four kinds of failure, the eighth question opens: what is yogic practice itself? The treatise names four components — faith, aspiration, diligence, and means — beginning with faith.

Chinese Source

諸有苾芻勤修靜慮，是瑜伽師，於此四種瑜伽壞法，應正遍知，當遠捨離。云何瑜伽？謂四瑜伽。一、信，二、欲，三、精進，四、方便。當知其信有二行相及二依處。二行相者，一、信順行相，二、清淨行相。二依處者，一、觀察諸法道理依處，二、信解補特伽羅神力依處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Any monk diligently cultivating absorption, being a yoga practitioner, should fully understand these four kinds of failure of yogic practice and should keep far from them. What is yogic practice? It is fourfold: faith, aspiration, diligence, and means. Know that faith has two aspects and two bases. The two aspects are: the aspect of trusting compliance, and the aspect of purifying clarity. The two bases are: the basis of observing the principle of phenomena, and the basis of trusting in a person's spiritual power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eighth question as the positive counterpart to the seventh: having just catalogued everything that can go wrong with practice, the treatise now defines what genuine yogic practice actually consists of, structurally, in four components. Faith opens the list because nothing else can get started without it. Its two aspects: trusting compliance is the willingness to actually follow what one has come to trust, aligning one's own conduct with the teaching rather than merely admiring it from a distance; purifying clarity is the further quality of genuine confidence in the three jewels and the ultimate truth actually calming and clearing the mind — the kind of faith intense enough, per the Compendium of Distinct Gateways Cháng Bǎi cites, to raise the hair on one's arms or bring a person to tears upon hearing the teaching. Its two bases: one kind of faith rests on having personally examined the principle of things — seeing for oneself, through reasoning, that dependent origination or mind-only teaching genuinely holds; the other rests on trust in a realized being's own attainment and spiritual power, taking the Buddha's or a teacher's demonstrated capability as sufficient grounds for confidence even before full personal verification. Cháng Bǎi notes both are legitimate and mutually reinforcing — reasoned confidence and trust in a genuine teacher's attainment together, not either alone.

20. The Four Kinds of Aspiration

Chinese Source

欲有四種。一、為證得欲，二、為請問欲，三、為修集資糧欲，四、為隨順瑜伽欲。為證得欲者，謂如有一，於上解脫發生希慕，如前廣說。為請問欲者，謂如有一，生希慕已，往僧伽藍，詣諸有識同修梵行成就瑜伽妙智者所，為聽未聞，為聞究竟。為修集資糧欲者……於食知量、減省睡眠、正知住中，展轉增勝，發生希慕。為隨順瑜伽欲者，謂於無間加行、殷重加行修習道中，發生希慕、發生欣樂，欲有所作。

Ru-Yi Translation

Aspiration is fourfold: the aspiration for realization, the aspiration to inquire, the aspiration to gather the provisions, and the aspiration to comply with yogic practice. The aspiration for realization is, for one who has come to long for the highest liberation, as extensively explained earlier. The aspiration to inquire is, for one who has already come to long for it, going to the monastic community, approaching those knowledgeable companions in the holy life who have attained the wondrous wisdom of yogic practice, in order to hear what has not yet been heard and to hear it through to its completion. The aspiration to gather the provisions is longing for ever-increasing excellence in moderate eating, reduced sleep, and clear awareness in conduct. The aspiration to comply with yogic practice is longing and taking delight, wishing to actively engage, within a course of practice pursued without interruption and with earnest care.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these four as a natural sequence mirroring how genuine practice actually develops. First comes the aspiration for realization itself — the same intense longing for liberation named earlier as "intense aspiration" among the favorable conditions for training. This naturally leads to the aspiration to inquire: genuine longing for realization drives a serious practitioner to actually seek out qualified teachers and ask real questions, rather than remaining content with vague admiration. That inquiry then generates the aspiration to gather the provisions — recognizing that moderate eating, reduced sleep, and clear awareness (the very practices unpacked at length back in fascicles 23 and 24, on the fourteen provisions for the path) are the actual groundwork realization requires, and increasingly wanting to perfect them. And finally, the aspiration to comply with yogic practice is the mature stage where a practitioner genuinely wants to engage the practice itself, continuously and with real earnestness, not merely prepare for it indefinitely. Cháng Bǎi notes the whole progression traces a single arc: wanting the goal leads to wanting instruction, which leads to wanting the foundation, which leads to actually wanting the practice — each aspiration setting up the next.

21. Milestone: Diligence and Means Close Yogic Practice — Sixteen Components in All

Diligence and means, the remaining two of the four components, close the eighth question — and the treatise then reveals that each of the four unfolds into four sub-types, sixteen in total, together constituting what "yogic practice" actually names.

Chinese Source

精進有四。一、為聞精進，二、為思精進，三、為修精進，四、為障淨精進。……方便有四。謂尸羅律儀增上力故，善守其念；善守念故，能無放逸，防護其心，修諸善法；無放逸故，心正於內修奢摩他、增上慧法毗鉢舍那。……此四瑜伽有十六種。當知此中，初由信故，於應得義深生信解；信應得已，於諸善法生起樂欲；由樂欲故，晝夜策勵，安住精勤、堅固、勇猛；發精進已，攝受方便，能得未得、能觸未觸、能證未證；故此四法說名瑜伽。

Ru-Yi Translation

Diligence is fourfold: diligence for hearing, diligence for reflection, diligence for cultivation, and diligence for purifying obstruction. Means is fourfold: through the strength of discipline, one guards mindfulness well; through guarding mindfulness well, one becomes free of negligence, protecting one's own mind and cultivating wholesome states; through freedom from negligence, the mind rightly cultivates, within, both calm-abiding and the insight of higher wisdom. These four components of yogic practice, in sixteen forms altogether: first, through faith, one comes to deep confidence regarding what is to be attained; having such confidence, aspiration for wholesome states arises; through that aspiration, one exerts oneself day and night, abiding diligent, firm, and courageous; having generated diligence, one takes up the means by which what is not yet attained is attained, what is not yet touched is touched, and what is not yet realized is realized. This is why these four factors are called yogic practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through diligence's four forms as tracking the classical hearing-reflection-cultivation sequence, with a fourth added specifically for removing obstruction: diligence in actually seeking out and absorbing teaching not yet heard; diligence in withdrawing to solitude to genuinely think through what's been heard, checking it against one's own experience; diligence in the sustained, moment-to-moment cultivation of calm-abiding and insight themselves; and diligence, day and night, in actively clearing away the five hindrances that block all of the above. Means, the fourth component, describes a clean causal sequence rather than four independent items: discipline makes guarded mindfulness possible; guarded mindfulness makes freedom from negligence possible; and freedom from negligence is what actually allows the mind to turn inward and cultivate calm-abiding and insight — each link enabling the next, with nothing skippable.

Cháng Bǎi closes by tracing the treatise's own summary of the whole eighth question: faith generates confidence that the goal is genuinely attainable; that confidence generates aspiration; aspiration drives diligence, sustained day and night; and diligence, finally, takes up the concrete means by which attainment, contact, and realization actually happen — the first absorption or the first fruit not yet attained, the second and third not yet touched, the fourth or arhatship not yet realized. Four components, each unfolding into four of its own, sixteen altogether: this is what "yogic practice" names in full, closing the eighth of the seventeen questions.

22. Mental Engagement Opens: Forceful and Interrupted Operation

Chinese Source

云何作意？謂四作意。一、力勵運轉作意，二、有間運轉作意，三、無間運轉作意，四、無功用運轉作意。云何力勵運轉作意？謂初修業者，令心於內安住等住，或於諸法無倒揀擇，乃至未得所修作意，爾時作意力勵運轉。……云何有間運轉作意？謂得所修作意已後，世出世道漸次昇進了相作意，由三摩地思所間雜，未能一向純修行轉，故名有間運轉作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is mental engagement? It is fourfold: forceful operation, interrupted operation, uninterrupted operation, and effortless operation. What is forceful mental engagement? It is that of a beginning practitioner, causing the mind to abide and evenly abide inwardly, or to discriminate phenomena without distortion, up until the mental engagement one is cultivating has not yet been attained — at that time, mental engagement operates forcefully. What is interrupted mental engagement? It is that which, after the mental engagement being cultivated has been attained, gradually advances through the mundane or supramundane path beginning with the mental engagement of identifying the characteristic — still interspersed with reflective thought within concentration, not yet flowing in wholly uninterrupted practice. This is why it is called interrupted operation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces the ninth question as tracking the actual felt texture of practice as it matures, through four stages of increasing ease. Forceful operation describes every beginner's experience: a mind still thoroughly habituated to scattering has to be dragged back to its object again and again, by real effort, before access concentration (the preparatory stage bordering the first absorption) is even reached — nothing here happens naturally yet. Interrupted operation begins only once access concentration has actually been attained: the practitioner now advances through the seven mental engagements, starting with "identifying the characteristic" (the first attempt to genuinely grasp what, say, impurity or the breath actually looks like as a meditation object) — but concentration at this stage still alternates with ordinary reflective thought, checking back against what's been heard and considered, not yet able to sustain unbroken absorbed practice. Cháng Bǎi notes this stage is aptly named "interrupted" precisely because it isn't a failure but a necessary, transitional mixture — genuine practice has begun, but hasn't yet become continuous.

23. Milestone: Eight Kinds of Mental Engagement Complete

Uninterrupted and effortless operation close the first set of four mental engagements — spanning the entire arc from a beginner's first grip on the object to an arhat's fully spontaneous realization. A second set of four then names how mental engagement functions moment to moment: conforming, counteracting, inclining toward purity, and inclining toward observation.

Chinese Source

云何無間運轉作意？謂從了相作意已後，乃至加行究竟作意，是名無間運轉作意。云何無功用運轉作意？謂加行究竟果作意，是名無功用運轉作意。……復有所餘四種作意。一、隨順作意，二、對治作意，三、順清淨作意，四、順觀察作意。云何隨順作意？謂於所緣深生厭壞，起正加行，而未斷惑。云何對治作意？謂能斷惑。云何順清淨作意？謂心下蹙，取淨妙相策令歡悅。云何順觀察作意？謂觀察作意。由此作意增上力故，順觀煩惱斷與未斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is uninterrupted mental engagement? It is that which runs from the mental engagement of identifying the characteristic through to the mental engagement of the culmination of applied effort — this is called uninterrupted operation. What is effortless mental engagement? It is the mental engagement that is the fruit of the culmination of applied effort — this is called effortless operation. There are, further, four other kinds of mental engagement: conforming, counteracting, inclining toward purity, and inclining toward observation. Conforming mental engagement is generating deep revulsion toward the object and undertaking correct effort, without yet having cut off affliction. Counteracting mental engagement is that which is actually able to cut off affliction. Inclining-toward-purity engagement is, when the mind sinks low, taking up a pure and wondrous image to rouse it to gladness. Inclining-toward-observation engagement is observational mental engagement — through its power, one accurately observes whether affliction has, or has not yet, been cut off.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi completes the first fourfold set by mapping it onto the seven mental engagements of the mundane path in full: uninterrupted operation spans everything from identifying the characteristic, through resolute understanding (once the object is grasped clearly enough that no further checking against scripture is needed), through withdrawal (beginning to suppress the coarsest desire-realm affliction), delight in withdrawal (finding genuine pleasure in practice as the middling affliction is suppressed), and observation (checking how thoroughly the affliction has actually been suppressed), up to the culmination of applied effort itself (the finest remaining desire-realm affliction finally subdued) — all of this counts as "uninterrupted" because, once resolute understanding is reached, there's no more need to break off and consult scripture or reasoning; practice simply flows. Effortless operation is the seventh and final engagement's own fruit — actually attaining the first absorption, or the corresponding fruit on the supramundane path, arising spontaneously without any further exertion, the single point where all four levels of operation — forceful, interrupted, uninterrupted, effortless — trace one continuous maturation from a beginner's first labored effort to an arhat's fully spontaneous realization.

The second set of four, Cháng Bǎi explains, describes function rather than developmental stage — a different lens on the very same seven engagements. Conforming engagement names the earlier stages (identifying the characteristic, resolute understanding) where genuine revulsion toward the object has arisen and correct effort is underway, but affliction has not yet actually been cut. Counteracting engagement names withdrawal, delight in withdrawal, and the culmination of applied effort — the stages doing the actual work of removing affliction. Inclining-toward-purity engagement is a rescue technique usable at any stage: when the mind sinks and loses energy, deliberately recalling something genuinely inspiring — the virtues of the three jewels, one's own generosity or discipline, the prospect of a favorable rebirth — to lift it back into gladness. And inclining-toward-observation engagement is simply honest self-checking, verifying without self-flattery whether affliction has actually been reduced or not, the same scrupulous honesty already named among the favorable conditions for training. Together, these eight kinds of mental engagement give a complete account of how attention itself functions and matures across the entire path.

24. The Causal Characteristic: Resources, Eight Discursive Thoughts, and Mutual Causation

Chinese Source

因緣相者，謂三摩地資糧積集，隨順教導，與修俱行猛利樂欲，於可厭法深生厭患，能審遍知亂與不亂，他不惱觸。……若毗鉢舍那而為上首，內略其心，極猛盛觀後因緣相。若奢摩他而為上首，發起勝觀，極猛盛止後因緣相。

Ru-Yi Translation

The causal characteristic is: the accumulation of the provisions for concentration; conduct according with instruction; intense aspiration accompanying practice; deep revulsion toward what should be shunned; the ability to thoroughly discern disturbance from its absence; and freedom from external disturbance. … Where insight practice leads, calm-abiding — the mind gathered inward — is the causal characteristic following an intensely vivid observation. Where calm-abiding leads, an excellent insight arising is the causal characteristic following an intensely vivid stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the causal characteristic as everything, beyond the bare object itself, that makes genuine mental engagement possible. The provisions for concentration are the entire earlier apparatus of this project's work — discipline, guarding the senses, moderate eating, wakeful practice, clear awareness, good companionship, hearing and reflecting on the teaching, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the ornaments of a renunciant — none of it optional groundwork, all of it the actual precondition for sitting down and engaging an object of meditation successfully. "Disturbance" specifically names two families of obstruction: the eight kinds of discursive thought (desire, aversion, harm, homeland, kin, the illusion of not-yet-dying, contempt of others, and cultivating connections with wealthy patrons — each a specific, well-worn groove the wandering mind falls into, unpacked at length in the Compendium of Topics, which Cháng Bǎi cites arguing against each in turn: kin-thought forgets that one already renounced attachment to family upon ordination; homeland-thought forgets that every country has its own faults, so there is no perfect place to flee to; not-dying-thought is the deadliest illusion of all, since death's timing is never guaranteed and postponing practice "until later" risks never practicing at all) — together with the five hindrances already named throughout this project. Freedom from external disturbance means simply an environment free of noise, biting insects, and any threatening presence, human or otherwise.

The final piece of the causal characteristic describes calm-abiding and insight as each other's cause in an ongoing cycle: a mind gathered by calm-abiding becomes the basis for a more vivid insight; that more vivid insight, in turn, purifies and strengthens the calm-abiding that follows it — round after round, each discipline lending the other its own gains, neither one able to reach its full maturity alone.

25. Milestone: Four Characteristics to Avoid

Having named what to attend to and what supports that attention, the treatise names the four faulty conditions a practitioner must actively watch for and avoid: sinking, agitation, attachment, and scattering.

Chinese Source

應遠離相復有四種。一者、沈相，二者、掉相，三者、著相，四者、亂相。沈相者，謂由所緣相、因緣相故，令心下劣。掉相者，謂由所緣相、因緣相故，令心高舉。著相者，謂由所緣相、因緣相故，令心於境起染、起著，作諸惱亂。亂相者，謂由所緣相、因緣相故，令心於外馳散擾動。

Ru-Yi Translation

The characteristics to avoid are, further, of four kinds: sinking, agitation, attachment, and scattering. Sinking is when, through some fault in the object or the causal conditions, the mind becomes weak and low. Agitation is when, through some fault in the object or the causal conditions, the mind becomes elevated and restless. Attachment is when, through some fault in the object or the causal conditions, the mind gives rise to defilement and clinging toward the object, generating disturbance. Scattering is when, through some fault in the object or the causal conditions, the mind races and stirs outward.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes all four faults trace back to the same two sources already explained — a poorly grasped object, or missing causal support — making troubleshooting straightforward in principle, if not always in practice. Sinking is a mind gone dull and low-energy, often from an object that fails to interest or from neglected provisions (poor diet, exhaustion, insufficient prior discipline); the remedy is honestly diagnosing which specific condition is missing and restoring it, rather than simply forcing more effort. Agitation is the opposite excess, the mind lifted and restless, often from an attractive but distracting train of thought — a strong desire-type temperament is especially prone to it. Attachment is the mind actually clinging to and delighting in the object in a way that itself becomes disturbance, unable to settle because it keeps reaching for more. And scattering is the mind racing outward, losing its grip on the object entirely — sometimes from genuine unfamiliarity with the technique (switching counting methods too soon, for instance), sometimes from the same neglected provisions that produce sinking. Cháng Bǎi closes by noting the treatise cross-references its own earlier, fuller account of these four faults in the section on concentration proper — this passage assumes that foundation and simply restates it in the context of mental engagement specifically.

26. Milestone: Nine Kinds of Resolute Understanding Close Mental Engagement

The treatise closes the ninth question with nine kinds of resolute understanding — the vivid, effortless grasp of an object that mental engagement matures into — sorted by inner luminosity, sharpness of faculty, scope, and purity.

Chinese Source

如是作意於所緣境起勝解時，有幾勝解？答：九。一、有光淨勝解，二、無光淨勝解，三、遲鈍勝解，四、捷利勝解，五、狹小勝解，六、廣大勝解，七、無量勝解，八、清淨勝解，九、不清淨勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

When mental engagement gives rise to resolute understanding of its object, how many kinds of resolute understanding are there? Answer: nine. Resolute understanding accompanied by inner light; resolute understanding without inner light; dull resolute understanding; sharp resolute understanding; narrow resolute understanding; vast resolute understanding; immeasurable resolute understanding; pure resolute understanding; and impure resolute understanding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi sorts the nine into four natural pairs and a note on each. Luminous versus non-luminous understanding describes whether a practitioner's inner mental image is genuinely bright — able, with eyes closed, to see an object (a corpse for impurity-contemplation, a Buddha-image, the breath) as clearly as with eyes open — or remains habitually dim and indistinct; this is itself a trainable skill, not fixed by temperament. Dull versus sharp simply names how quickly a practitioner's own faculties allow the object to become vivid, itself changeable through sustained practice regardless of where one starts. Narrow, vast, and immeasurable track the scope of both the aspiring mind and its object together — a small object grasped with small faith and aspiration (roughly desire-realm-level absorption), a vast object grasped with vast aspiration (form-realm absorption, contemplating an entire ocean of bones rather than a single corpse), and an immeasurable, boundless object matching a boundless aspiration (the formless attainments of infinite space and infinite consciousness) — three degrees of the very same understanding, distinguished only by how far it reaches. And pure versus impure understanding closes the list with the simplest distinction of all: whether one's practice — whether mundane, up through the four absorptions and formless attainments, or supramundane, up through the four fruits — has actually been well cultivated, brought to completion, and carried to its conclusion, or remains unfinished and still entangled with affliction. With this, the ninth question — mental engagement — closes in full.
