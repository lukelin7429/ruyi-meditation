Contact as Condition and the Rest Begins

Fascicle 94 opens dependent origination's third particular verse, contact as condition and the rest (以觸為緣等) — its own eight-topic summary verse names: contact as condition, perfected view, the real, understanding, taking no delight, the wisdom that abides in the Dharma, diligence, and the place of birth and the rest. This fascicle closes the first two topics entirely. Contact as condition (觸緣) covers the three kinds of undistorted reply available to a Buddhist disciple confronted with heretical views that suffering and pleasure are self-made, other-made, both, or causeless — opening one's own tenet, refuting the other's tenet, and distinguishing attachment from its absence — illustrated by the Buddha's own dialogue with the wanderers, and by Venerables Bhūmija, Śāriputra, and Ānanda. Perfected view (見圓滿) covers the five causes from which arising dharmas spring — the causes of miserable and fortunate destinies, of consciousness's abiding (the four foods), of defilement, and of purity (the four truths and dependent origination) — illustrated by Śāriputra's extended teaching to Mahākoṭṭhita.

2. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — Contact as Condition: Marking and Listing Three Kinds of Reply

Within every case where contact conditions feeling, if renunciant wanderers or brahmins proclaim theories of no cause or of wrong cause, they should be questioned as before: is this made by this very self and experienced by it — and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

於一切觸緣受有中，若諸沙門、或婆羅門，宣說無因、惡因論者，如前請問，此作此受，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within every case where contact conditions feeling, if renunciant wanderers or brahmins proclaim theories of no cause or of wrong cause, they should be questioned as before: is this made by this very self and experienced by it — and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens the long-form explanation (長行釋), whose eight sections match the summary verse's eight topics, beginning with the first, contact as condition (觸緣). Wrong theories of causation take two forms: "this very self makes and this very self experiences" (identifying self with the aggregates) and "another makes and another experiences" (positing a self apart from the aggregates) — both incoherent, per the 披尋記.

3. Section: Three Undistorted Replies

10. Section: Explaining the Cause

Why is this? Where there is attachment, this is defilement; where there is no attachment, this is purity.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining the cause (釋因) — attachment to a real self breeds craving, aversion, and delusion, which in turn breed karma and its suffering fruit; freedom from that attachment breaks the chain entirely, even while using the same conventional language.

12. Section: Purity, Closing "Distinguishing the Marks" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

With this, concluding the name (結名) closes entirely, closing the reply distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity (有執無執離染清淨記4: 舉記/釋因/辨相/結名) entirely, closing contact as condition (觸緣2: 標列三記/隨釋差別), the first of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely.

A companion sūtra tells of wanderers questioning Venerable Bhūmija at Vulture Peak whether suffering and pleasure are self-made, other-made, both, or neither — to each he replies "the Buddha calls this undeclared," leaving the wanderers displeased. Seeking Śāriputra's confirmation, Bhūmija learns he answered rightly, since the Buddha's own teaching is that suffering and pleasure arise from contact as condition, and any of the four proposed causes, examined closely, are themselves simply further instances of conditions arising from contact — none stands outside dependent origination. When Ānanda reports this exchange to the Buddha, the Buddha praises Śāriputra's ready wisdom and confirms the same teaching in his own words: he does not accept the outsiders' claim to exclusive certainty, since his own account of causation — from feeling through craving, grasping, existence, birth, and aging-and-death, and their reverse cessation — differs from theirs at the root. This illustrates all three replies: opening the Buddhist tenet (all arises from conditions), subduing the outsiders' tenet (their four positions collapse into one, since all are conditioned by contact), and distinguishing attachment from its absence (defilement continuing life to life for the attached, versus final nirvāṇa for the unattached).

21. Section: The Cause of Consciousness's Abiding

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the cause that causes consciousness to abide in its abiding: namely, the four foods.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The four foods: edible food (material nourishment, sustaining the faculties through the desire realm's meals), contact-food (pleasant sensory contact that nourishes through delight), volitional food (the mental wish for a desired object, sustaining life the way a starving traveler is kept alive by hope of reaching food), and consciousness-food (the eighth consciousness, sustained by the other three, which in turn holds every faculty in place). Craving for these four foods is the root that nourishes saṃsāra; the arhat, free of craving for them, is thereby liberated.

Ru-Yi Translation

With this, displaying the meaning (顯義) closes entirely, closing explaining perfected view (釋見圓滿2: 標名/顯義) entirely, closing bringing forth the distinctions of view (出見差別2: 釋行相/配所緣, together with 釋五種因2 and 釋見圓滿2 as its three matters) entirely, closing perfected view (見圓滿3: 出見差別/釋五種因/釋見圓滿), the second of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely — the five causes exhaust every possible dependently-arisen matter; ignorance marks contemplation's outermost limit, beyond which no further cause need be sought.

A long companion sūtra has Venerable Mahākoṭṭhita question Śāriputra on what makes a learned disciple's view perfected, upright, and verified-pure in the Buddha. Śāriputra answers in five successive rounds, each building on the last: first, truly knowing the unwholesome and its root, and the wholesome and its root (the causes of miserable and fortunate destinies); second, truly knowing the four foods, their arising (through craving for future existence, delight in what is possessed, and longing for what is not yet obtained), their cessation, and the path to their cessation (the eightfold path); third, likewise for the contaminants (sense-desire, existence, and ignorance); fourth, likewise for suffering itself, defined as birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from the loved, union with the hated, and not getting what is wanted; and fifth, likewise for aging-and-death and, in turn, every other link back through formations — in each case, knowing the thing, its arising, its cessation, and the eightfold path to that cessation. This traditionally yields forty-four kinds of wisdom, one truth-pattern applied to each of eleven links apart from ignorance itself. When Mahākoṭṭhita presses for still more, Śāriputra replies that the ocean of the Buddha's teaching cannot be exhausted by endless questioning — one drop is enough to know it is salty; sever ignorance, and wisdom needs nothing further.

26. Section: The Real Begins — Marking and Listing the Objects of Its Domain

Toward these, one beyond learning reflects thus: everything of mine is exhausted, nothing further remains to be exhausted — this is called discerning the dharma, established through the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising. 34. Section: By the Dharma's Predominance Begins — Named the Dharma Realm

This opens by the dharma's predominance (由法增上), the second of two matters under explaining its designation, beginning with named the dharma realm (名法界) — names, phrases, and syllables (the words themselves, the sentences built from them, and the letters underlying both) are the vehicle through which the object of the sixth consciousness is understood; undistorted use of them is what makes them "predominant." The Yogācārabhūmi Commentary gives three reasons the treatise calls this the dharma realm: it flows from the Buddha's own utterly pure dharma realm as its cause; relying on it and cultivating accordingly, one can realize the dharma realm as its fruit; and, simply as that which expresses the dharma realm, it is itself also called the dharma realm — this last sense being the one emphasized here.

36. Section: Able to Widely Proclaim Begins — Toward the Truthful Meaning

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and the Madhyānta-vibhāga

This opens able to widely proclaim (能廣宣說), beginning with toward the truthful meaning (於真實義) — since different listeners awaken through different words, the same emptiness can be taught under many names. Per the 披尋記, citing the Madhyānta-vibhāga: emptiness, suchness, the true limit, signlessness, ultimate nature, and selflessness are among its many names — emptiness meaning neither a real graspable nor a real grasper is found, yet the illusion-like appearance is not nothing either; suchness meaning it never changes regardless of a being's destiny; the true limit meaning it is the undistorted object of noble wisdom alone, never a deluded ordinary being's domain; signlessness meaning every conditioned mark utterly falls away in the realization of non-discriminating wisdom; ultimate nature meaning it is the very object supreme wisdom operates upon; and selflessness meaning no permanent, unitary, sovereign self is ever found within dependent origination.

A companion sūtra has the Buddha ask Śāriputra to explain the verses on the learner and the one beyond learning from the Pārāyaṇa (the questions of Ajita/Maitreya). Śāriputra at first sits silent through three askings; when the Buddha rephrases the question in terms of contemplating the six bases, contact, and feeling, Śāriputra readily explains: the learner still has the arising of the four foods' craving even while cultivating disenchantment and freedom from desire toward the six bases, contact, and feeling; the arhat has fully severed that craving, realized the wisdom of exhaustion and non-arising, and abides in unregressing joy through earnest, ceaseless effort. Afterward, Śāriputra explains to the assembly that his initial silence was simply to let the Buddha's second framing draw out a fuller answer, and declares that he could answer the same question phrased differently every day for seven days and nights without exhausting his eloquence. The Buddha confirms this before the assembly, attributing it to Śāriputra's complete mastery of the four unobstructed understandings — this illustrates the topic's own teaching that one who has fully penetrated the dharma realm can proclaim the same truth endlessly, through the ultimate meaning and through conditioned dharmas alike.

With this, concluding the count (結數) closes entirely, closing understanding (解3: 標列/隨釋/結數), the fourth of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely. The six named kinds resolve into four major categories: pronouncement with alternative expressions, without alternative expressions, of wisdom, and of severance — the latter two each further unfolding into their own particular and general forms, yielding six named kinds in total from four root categories.

A companion sūtra from the Madhyama-āgama (not the Saṃyukta this time) tells of the monk Kṛṣṇadanta ("Black-Tooth") bringing Śāriputra news that a fellow monk has disrobed, and asking whether Śāriputra himself still delights in the holy life. Śāriputra answers only "I have no doubt about the Dharma," then, pressed about the future, "I have no uncertainty about that either" — illustrating pronouncement with alternative expressions, since he answers indirectly rather than declaring arhatship outright. When Kṛṣṇadanta reports this to the Buddha as if Śāriputra had claimed final knowledge directly, the Buddha summons Śāriputra, who clarifies that he spoke only the meaning, not the customary formula "birth is exhausted" itself — illustrating the distinction between pronouncement with and without alternative expressions. The Buddha then poses, one by one, exactly the questions a fellow renunciant might ask, and Śāriputra answers each in turn, with the Buddha praising each reply as "well spoken": how he knows birth is exhausted (through birth's condition, existence, having ceased — pronouncement of wisdom's causes-and-conditions-of-the-graspings); tracing existence back through grasping, craving, and feeling to their conditions in turn (pronouncement of wisdom's general form: all feeling is suffering); what it means to have "turned one's back" on rebirth (freedom from fear, doubt, and confusion through the six constant abidings — pronouncement of severance's second aspect); and, when the Buddha invites an even more concise summary, Śāriputra offers two: "all that is felt is suffering" (the concise form of general pronouncement of wisdom) and "the fetters spoken of are none of mine" (the concise form of the first aspect of general pronouncement of severance). Afterward, exactly as in the previous topic, Śāriputra declares his readiness to answer the same questions rephrased across seven days and nights without exhausting his eloquence, and the Buddha confirms this before Kṛṣṇadanta, attributing it to Śāriputra's thorough penetration of the dharma realm — illustrating, in sequence, all six kinds of pronouncement this topic has just explained.

A companion sūtra (Saṃyukta Āgama #25) has the Buddha, unprompted, teach the assembly a striking ten-link reverse chain of causation, working backward from old age, sickness, and death: these persist because craving, aversion, and delusion are unabandoned; these persist because self-view, attachment to precepts and vows, and doubt are unabandoned; these persist because improper reflection, habituation to wrong paths, and a lazy mind are unabandoned; these persist because forgetfulness, lack of clear comprehension, and a scattered mind are unabandoned; these persist because restlessness, lack of restraint, and not learning the precepts are unabandoned; these persist because faithlessness, resistance to teaching, and laziness are unabandoned; these persist because unwillingness to see the noble, unwillingness to hear the Dharma, and habitually finding others' faults are unabandoned; these persist because disrespect, harsh speech, and companionship with the unwise are unabandoned; and these persist because shamelessness, lack of conscience, and heedlessness are unabandoned — a root chain matching, almost point for point, this topic's own six causes of wrong conduct in discipline. The Buddha then reverses the whole chain forward: shame and conscience prevent heedlessness; heedlessness's absence brings respect, gentle speech, and good companionship; this brings joy in seeing the noble and hearing the Dharma, and no fault-finding; this brings faith, teachability, and diligence; diligence prevents restlessness and secures the precepts; this secures mindfulness and clear comprehension; this secures proper reflection and the right path; this severs self-view, wrong precept-attachment, and doubt; and this finally severs craving, aversion, and delusion — whereby sickness, old age, and death themselves are at last transcended. The Buddha's own reason for teaching this at all, stated at the sūtra's opening, is that these three unwelcome realities are exactly why buddhas appear in the world in the first place.

With this, the concluding statement (結) closes entirely, closing the wisdom of nirvāṇa (涅槃智3: 徵/釋/結) entirely, closing explaining broadly (廣釋2: 法住智/涅槃智) entirely, closing the wisdom that abides in the Dharma (法住智2: 略辨/廣釋), the sixth of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely — this is called the wisdom of nirvāṇa precisely because it is also called true wisdom, able to truly touch and realize nirvāṇa itself, not merely reason toward it.

A long companion sūtra follows — the well-known story of Susīma, sent by rival ascetics, resentful at losing royal patronage to the Buddha's growing community, to secretly ordain, memorize the Buddha's teaching, and bring it back to repackage as their own doctrine. The Buddha, aware of Susīma's true purpose yet foreseeing his capacity for genuine realization, permits the ordination. After a fortnight, Susīma hears newly-arhat monks declare "birth is exhausted, the pure life established" and questions them about their meditative attainments, expecting this to require the four dhyānas and four formless absorptions — each monk answers "no" to every one. Susīma objects that this seems contradictory, and the monks explain: "we are liberated through wisdom" (慧解脫), not both-ways-liberated. Confused, Susīma brings the puzzle to the Buddha, who confirms it: those monks first knew the wisdom that abides in the Dharma, and only afterward the wisdom of nirvāṇa — contemplating dependent origination alone, in solitude, even from mere access concentration, is sufficient to sever affliction and touch nirvāṇa. When Susīma still doesn't understand, the Buddha walks him through the twelve links forward and backward, link by link, confirming each — birth conditions aging-and-death, and birth's cessation conditions aging-and-death's cessation, and so with every link back to ignorance — and asks whether Susīma, holding this very understanding, has himself already left behind unwholesome states through the four dhyānas. Susīma says no. The Buddha confirms: this is exactly the wisdom that abides in the Dharma preceding the wisdom of nirvāṇa. At this teaching, Susīma attains the pure Dharma-eye and, freed from doubt, immediately confesses his original scheme to steal the Buddha's teaching for his own sect. The Buddha accepts the confession and teaches him the proper form of repentance, then illustrates the gravity of stealing the Dharma with the simile of a thief sentenced to be stabbed three hundred times with spears — an agony Susīma agrees would be unbearable — declaring that stealing the true Dharma under false pretenses of ordination brings suffering far worse still. Hearing this, Susīma's remaining afflictions are exhausted, and he too attains arhatship.

Per the Bodhisattva Stage, "Well-Gone" names one who has risen to the ultimate and never turns back; per the 攝異門分, one who through the long night has perfected both self-benefit and benefiting others. A long companion sūtra has the Buddha, again unprompted, teach that the Tathāgata's ten powers and four fearlessnesses let him roar the lion's roar of dependent origination before the assembly — this rests on his mastery not only of the heavenly and divine abodes shared with other meditators, but the noble abode unique to him, displayed solely to teach the pure life for the sake of beings' freedom from craving. The Buddha declares that this true teaching alone gives a son of good family confidence to go forth and press on with diligence undeterred, even to the point of exhaustion, until the noble path is realized. He then contrasts laziness, which breeds unwholesome states, intensifies future affliction, and forfeits the great benefit of the noble path, against diligence practiced in joyful solitude, which secures nirvāṇa's benefit and the foremost place before the Teacher himself — realizing cessation, tending toward the three vehicles' awakening, and finally the Well-Gone One's own virtue. The Buddha closes by urging the assembly to reflect on both their own benefit and others', and declares of himself: because I have gone forth without delusion or doubt, and my practice has borne fruit, all who offer me robes, food, and the rest attain great fruit, great merit, and great benefit in turn — you should train just so.

Ru-Yi Translation

Where it is said there are learned noble disciples, one should know this refers to ordinary beings; where it speaks only of noble disciples, one should know this refers to those who have already seen the truth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

With this, distinguishing the different kinds (料簡異類) closes entirely, closing being without doubt (無疑惑2: 標能正觀/料簡異類) entirely, closing contemplating the flow of existence (觀察流轉2: 有疑惑/無疑惑), the second of the eighth topic's three matters, entirely — "learned noble disciples" spans a wide range, from ordinary beings still learning the Dharma through those who have already realized it.

A companion sūtra has the Buddha explain that a learned noble disciple, unlike outside contemplatives, never wonders whether the world is uncaused, externally created, self-made, other-made, or jointly made — nor, moving in reverse, wonders what must be absent for it to cease. Instead such a disciple simply and confidently knows: this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises — ignorance conditions formations, on through the whole mass of suffering; and, in reverse, ignorance's cessation brings formations' cessation, on through the whole mass of suffering's cessation. Cause and result here are related without being identical — ignorance is not formations, formations are not ignorance — a flowing, ever-renewable relationship rather than a fixed identity, which is precisely why liberation from it remains possible at all.
