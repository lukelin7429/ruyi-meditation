Fascicle 94 · 瑜伽師地論卷第九十四 · Contact as Condition and the Rest Begins

Contact as Condition and the Rest Begins

Fascicle 94 opens dependent origination's third particular verse, contact as condition and the rest (以觸為緣等) — its own eight-topic summary verse names: contact as condition, perfected view, the real, understanding, taking no delight, the wisdom that abides in the Dharma, diligence, and the place of birth and the rest. This fascicle closes the first two topics entirely. Contact as condition (觸緣) covers the three kinds of undistorted reply available to a Buddhist disciple confronted with heretical views that suffering and pleasure are self-made, other-made, both, or causeless — opening one's own tenet, refuting the other's tenet, and distinguishing attachment from its absence — illustrated by the Buddha's own dialogue with the wanderers, and by Venerables Bhūmija, Śāriputra, and Ānanda. Perfected view (見圓滿) covers the five causes from which arising dharmas spring — the causes of miserable and fortunate destinies, of consciousness's abiding (the four foods), of defilement, and of purity (the four truths and dependent origination) — illustrated by Śāriputra's extended teaching to Mahākoṭṭhita.

Chinese Source

復次，嗢柁南曰：

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, a summary verse says:

Chinese Source

觸緣見圓滿　實解不愛樂　法住智精進　生處等為後

Ru-Yi Translation

Contact as condition, and perfected view; the real, understanding, and taking no delight; the wisdom that abides in the Dharma, and diligence; the place of birth and the rest, coming last.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the third particular verse (以觸為緣等) of dependent origination's embrace — per this fascicle's own opening, both establishing and the rest (立等) and the two truths and the rest (二諦等) belong to Taishō fascicle 93 in the treatise's own framework, though the lecture source available to this project only reached the first five of establishing and the rest's ten topics before ending, leaving its remaining five topics and the whole of the two truths and the rest untranslated. This fascicle continues directly with the third particular verse, marking and listing (標列) eight topics to be explained in the long-form section that follows: contact as condition, perfected view, the real, understanding, taking no delight, the wisdom that abides in the Dharma, diligence, and the place of birth and the rest.

2. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — Contact as Condition: Marking and Listing Three Kinds of Reply

Within every case where contact conditions feeling, if renunciant wanderers or brahmins proclaim theories of no cause or of wrong cause, they should be questioned as before: is this made by this very self and experienced by it — and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

於一切觸緣受有中，若諸沙門、或婆羅門，宣說無因、惡因論者，如前請問，此作此受，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within every case where contact conditions feeling, if renunciant wanderers or brahmins proclaim theories of no cause or of wrong cause, they should be questioned as before: is this made by this very self and experienced by it — and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens the long-form explanation (長行釋), whose eight sections match the summary verse's eight topics, beginning with the first, contact as condition (觸緣). Wrong theories of causation take two forms: "this very self makes and this very self experiences" (identifying self with the aggregates) and "another makes and another experiences" (positing a self apart from the aggregates) — both incoherent, per the 披尋記.

3. Section: Three Undistorted Replies

Disciples of the Teacher abiding in the true Dharma, whether superior or lesser, have, in brief, three kinds of undistorted reply. First, opening one's own tenet's reply; second, subduing the other's tenet's reply; third, the reply distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity.

Chinese Source

安住正法大師弟子，若勝、若劣，略有三種無倒記別。一、開自宗記，二、伏他宗記，三、有執無執雜染清淨記。

Ru-Yi Translation

Disciples of the Teacher abiding in the true Dharma, whether superior or lesser, have, in brief, three kinds of undistorted reply. First, opening one's own tenet's reply; second, subduing the other's tenet's reply; third, the reply distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Any Buddhist disciple, regardless of their own level of attainment, has these three replies available when questioned by outsiders about the cause of suffering and pleasure.

4. Section: Explaining Each Difference in Turn Begins — Opening One's Own Tenet's Reply

One should know that here, toward what is asked, the undifferentiated reply is: all suffering and pleasure arise from conditions — this is our tenet's essence. This alone is called opening one's own tenet's reply.

Chinese Source

當知此中，於彼所問無差別記：謂諸苦樂皆從緣生，是我宗致。斯則名為開自宗記。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that here, toward what is asked, the undifferentiated reply is: all suffering and pleasure arise from conditions — this is our tenet's essence. This alone is called opening one's own tenet's reply.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining each difference in turn (隨釋差別), beginning with opening one's own tenet's reply (開自宗記) — regardless of how the question is framed (self-made, other-made, both, or neither), the single undifferentiated answer is: all arises from conditions, neither self-made nor other-made nor both nor causeless.

5. Section: Subduing the Other's Tenet's Reply Begins — Bringing Forth What Can Subdue: Raising the Reply

If, toward their question, one gives this reply: those who suppose suffering and pleasure to be self-made, other-made, both-made, or neither-made and causeless — everywhere, feeling arises from contact, so why falsely suppose self-made and the rest? If feeling apart from contact as its cause could ever be found, seeking a further cause might seem clever; but since feeling with contact as its cause is directly evident, seeking a further cause is not clever at all.

Chinese Source

若於彼問作如是記：諸計苦樂自作、他作、俱作、俱非無因而生，於一切處由觸生受，何用妄計自他作等。若觸因受現不可得，更求餘因可為巧妙；然觸因受既現可得，故求餘因非為巧妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, toward their question, one gives this reply: those who suppose suffering and pleasure to be self-made, other-made, both-made, or neither-made and causeless — everywhere, feeling arises from contact, so why falsely suppose self-made and the rest? If feeling apart from contact as its cause could ever be found, seeking a further cause might seem clever; but since feeling with contact as its cause is directly evident, seeking a further cause is not clever at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens subduing the other's tenet's reply (伏他宗記), beginning with bringing forth what can subdue it (出能伏), raising the reply (舉記) — direct experience already shows feeling arising from contact at every sense-door; no further speculative cause is needed or credible.

7. Section: Explaining Why Begins — the First Meaning

Why is this? By two causes and conditions, they are subdued. First, apart from the mere union of faculty, object, and consciousness, no further agent can ever be shown; second, one cannot deny the entire world's directly-evident, rightly-obtained cause of contact.

Chinese Source

所以者何？由二因緣，彼為摧伏。一者、除唯根、境、識合，不能顯示餘作者故；二者、不能誹撥一切世間現量如理所得觸因緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? By two causes and conditions, they are subdued. First, apart from the mere union of faculty, object, and consciousness, no further agent can ever be shown; second, one cannot deny the entire world's directly-evident, rightly-obtained cause of contact.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining why (釋所以), beginning with the first meaning (第一義) — the union of the six senses, their objects, and consciousness is all that can ever be found; no separate agent stands behind it, and direct experience itself confirms this.

9. Section: The Reply Distinguishing Attachment and Its Absence, Defilement and Purity Begins — Raising the Reply

If, toward their question, one gives this reply: I too, relying merely on faculty, object, and consciousness, provisionally posit self-made, other-made, or both-made suffering and pleasure — yet toward a real self, I hold no attachment whatsoever; you, in this matter, hold a wrong attachment, and so I do not concede to your claim.

Chinese Source

若於彼問作如是記：我亦唯依根、境界、識，假立自作、他作、俱作若苦若樂，而於實我都無所執；汝於此中有邪執著，故不隨許。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, toward their question, one gives this reply: I too, relying merely on faculty, object, and consciousness, provisionally posit self-made, other-made, or both-made suffering and pleasure — yet toward a real self, I hold no attachment whatsoever; you, in this matter, hold a wrong attachment, and so I do not concede to your claim.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the reply distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity (有執無執雜染清淨記), the third of the three replies, beginning with raising the reply (舉記) — a Buddhist may use the same provisional language of "self-made" and "other-made" as an outsider, but without ever attaching to a real self behind it.

10. Section: Explaining the Cause

Why is this? Where there is attachment, this is defilement; where there is no attachment, this is purity.

Chinese Source

所以者何？若有執著即為雜染，若無執著即為清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Where there is attachment, this is defilement; where there is no attachment, this is purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining the cause (釋因) — attachment to a real self breeds craving, aversion, and delusion, which in turn breed karma and its suffering fruit; freedom from that attachment breaks the chain entirely, even while using the same conventional language.

11. Section: Distinguishing the Marks Begins — Defilement

What is it called, where there is attachment, this is defilement? Namely, those unwise in the world, if attached within the prior period, ignorance conditioning formations, as explained fully before — then, within the middle period, suffering and pleasure become defiled; if attached within the middle period, likewise as before, then, within the latter period, suffering and pleasure will become defiled.

Chinese Source

云何名為若有執著即為雜染？謂彼世間不聰慧者，若於前際有所執著，無明緣行，廣說如前，便於中際苦樂雜染；若於中際有所執著，彼亦如前，當於後際苦樂雜染。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is it called, where there is attachment, this is defilement? Namely, those unwise in the world, if attached within the prior period, ignorance conditioning formations, as explained fully before — then, within the middle period, suffering and pleasure become defiled; if attached within the middle period, likewise as before, then, within the latter period, suffering and pleasure will become defiled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens distinguishing the marks (辨相), beginning with defilement (雜染) — attachment to self and self's-possession in one period of life plants the seed for defiled suffering and pleasure in the next, the same twelve-linked chain from fascicle 93 now repeating across every life.

12. Section: Purity, Closing "Distinguishing the Marks" Entirely

What is it called, where there is no attachment, this is purity? Namely, the wise, whether within the prior period or the middle period, do not attach to self or self's-possession within the formations; hence, for them, the causes of feeling within the prior period cease, and they have already attained final nirvāṇa — or the causes of feeling within the latter period will cease, and they will attain final nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

云何名為若無執著即為清淨？謂聰慧者，若於前際、或於中際，不於諸行執我、我所，彼於前際諸受因滅，已般涅槃；或於後際諸受因滅，當般涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is it called, where there is no attachment, this is purity? Namely, the wise, whether within the prior period or the middle period, do not attach to self or self's-possession within the formations; hence, for them, the causes of feeling within the prior period cease, and they have already attained final nirvāṇa — or the causes of feeling within the latter period will cease, and they will attain final nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, purity (清淨) closes entirely, closing distinguishing the marks (辨相2: 雜染/清淨) entirely.

13. Section: Concluding the Name, Closing "The Reply Distinguishing Attachment and Its Absence" and "Contact as Condition" — the First of Eight Topics — Entirely

This is called the third reply, distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity.

Chinese Source

是名第三有執無執雜染清淨記。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called the third reply, distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

With this, concluding the name (結名) closes entirely, closing the reply distinguishing attachment and its absence, defilement and purity (有執無執離染清淨記4: 舉記/釋因/辨相/結名) entirely, closing contact as condition (觸緣2: 標列三記/隨釋差別), the first of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely.

A companion sūtra tells of wanderers questioning Venerable Bhūmija at Vulture Peak whether suffering and pleasure are self-made, other-made, both, or neither — to each he replies "the Buddha calls this undeclared," leaving the wanderers displeased. Seeking Śāriputra's confirmation, Bhūmija learns he answered rightly, since the Buddha's own teaching is that suffering and pleasure arise from contact as condition, and any of the four proposed causes, examined closely, are themselves simply further instances of conditions arising from contact — none stands outside dependent origination. When Ānanda reports this exchange to the Buddha, the Buddha praises Śāriputra's ready wisdom and confirms the same teaching in his own words: he does not accept the outsiders' claim to exclusive certainty, since his own account of causation — from feeling through craving, grasping, existence, birth, and aging-and-death, and their reverse cessation — differs from theirs at the root. This illustrates all three replies: opening the Buddhist tenet (all arises from conditions), subduing the outsiders' tenet (their four positions collapse into one, since all are conditioned by contact), and distinguishing attachment from its absence (defilement continuing life to life for the attached, versus final nirvāṇa for the unattached).

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, the cause of fortunate destinies: namely, all wholesome actions and their wholesome roots.

21. Section: The Cause of Consciousness's Abiding

Third, the cause that causes consciousness to abide in its abiding: namely, the four foods.

Chinese Source

三、於識住令識住因，謂四種食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the cause that causes consciousness to abide in its abiding: namely, the four foods.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The four foods: edible food (material nourishment, sustaining the faculties through the desire realm's meals), contact-food (pleasant sensory contact that nourishes through delight), volitional food (the mental wish for a desired object, sustaining life the way a starving traveler is kept alive by hope of reaching food), and consciousness-food (the eighth consciousness, sustained by the other three, which in turn holds every faculty in place). Craving for these four foods is the root that nourishes saṃsāra; the arhat, free of craving for them, is thereby liberated.

22. Section: The Cause of Defilement

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The afflictions of craving and views, rooted in ignorance and not yet severed, keep defiling both the present life and lives to come.

24. Section: Explaining Perfected View Begins — Marking Its Name

If one truly knows these causes' own-nature as their own-nature; truly knows their causes and conditions as their causes and conditions; truly knows the cessation of these causes and conditions as truly cessation; truly knows the path leading to that cessation as truly the path — this is called perfected view.

Chinese Source

若有於此諸因自性，如實了知是其自性；於此因緣，如實了知是其因緣；於因緣滅，如實了知真實是滅；於趣滅道，如實了知真實是道；名見圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one truly knows these causes' own-nature as their own-nature; truly knows their causes and conditions as their causes and conditions; truly knows the cessation of these causes and conditions as truly cessation; truly knows the path leading to that cessation as truly the path — this is called perfected view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining perfected view (釋見圓滿), beginning with marking its name (標名) — the same fourfold pattern of the four noble truths (suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path) now applied to all five causes at once.

25. Section: Displaying the Meaning, Closing "Explaining Perfected View," "Bringing Forth the Distinctions of View," and "Perfected View" — the Second of Eight Topics — Entirely

Contemplating the matter of dependent arising, right up to ignorance as its final boundary — beyond this, there is no further contemplation of dependently-arisen causes. Only through this contemplation does its own aim reach completion.

Chinese Source

觀緣生事，乃至無明為邊際故，過此更無緣生因觀，唯由此觀，自義究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Contemplating the matter of dependent arising, right up to ignorance as its final boundary — beyond this, there is no further contemplation of dependently-arisen causes. Only through this contemplation does its own aim reach completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

With this, displaying the meaning (顯義) closes entirely, closing explaining perfected view (釋見圓滿2: 標名/顯義) entirely, closing bringing forth the distinctions of view (出見差別2: 釋行相/配所緣, together with 釋五種因2 and 釋見圓滿2 as its three matters) entirely, closing perfected view (見圓滿3: 出見差別/釋五種因/釋見圓滿), the second of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely — the five causes exhaust every possible dependently-arisen matter; ignorance marks contemplation's outermost limit, beyond which no further cause need be sought.

A long companion sūtra has Venerable Mahākoṭṭhita question Śāriputra on what makes a learned disciple's view perfected, upright, and verified-pure in the Buddha. Śāriputra answers in five successive rounds, each building on the last: first, truly knowing the unwholesome and its root, and the wholesome and its root (the causes of miserable and fortunate destinies); second, truly knowing the four foods, their arising (through craving for future existence, delight in what is possessed, and longing for what is not yet obtained), their cessation, and the path to their cessation (the eightfold path); third, likewise for the contaminants (sense-desire, existence, and ignorance); fourth, likewise for suffering itself, defined as birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from the loved, union with the hated, and not getting what is wanted; and fifth, likewise for aging-and-death and, in turn, every other link back through formations — in each case, knowing the thing, its arising, its cessation, and the eightfold path to that cessation. This traditionally yields forty-four kinds of wisdom, one truth-pattern applied to each of eleven links apart from ignorance itself. When Mahākoṭṭhita presses for still more, Śāriputra replies that the ocean of the Buddha's teaching cannot be exhausted by endless questioning — one drop is enough to know it is salty; sever ignorance, and wisdom needs nothing further.

26. Section: The Real Begins — Marking and Listing the Objects of Its Domain

Next, there are, briefly, three matters truly stilled within the present life, continuing to turn in unbroken succession right up until the lifespan itself has not yet come to rest, forming the known-object matters. Toward these, when a learner is rightly practicing, the nature of a learner is designated. Toward these, one beyond learning reflects thus: everything of mine is exhausted, nothing further remains to be exhausted — this is called discerning the dharma, established through the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising. What are the three? First, the six bases; second, the six bases conditioning contact; third, contact conditioning feeling.

Chinese Source

復次，略有三種於現法中真實寂滅，乃至壽量未永止息，恆相續轉所知境事。於彼有學正修行時，施設學性。於彼無學作是思惟：我一切盡，不復當盡。盡、無生智所思擇故，名思擇法。云何為三？一、六處，二、六處緣觸，三、觸緣受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, there are, briefly, three matters truly stilled within the present life, continuing to turn in unbroken succession right up until the lifespan itself has not yet come to rest, forming the known-object matters. Toward these, when a learner is rightly practicing, the nature of a learner is designated. Toward these, one beyond learning reflects thus: everything of mine is exhausted, nothing further remains to be exhausted — this is called discerning the dharma, established through the wisdom of exhaustion and of non-arising. What are the three? First, the six bases; second, the six bases conditioning contact; third, contact conditioning feeling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the third topic, the real (實), beginning with marking and listing the objects of its domain (標列境事) — the six sense-bases, their conditioning of contact, and contact's conditioning of feeling remain the constant field of experience for learner and arhat alike, distinguished only by how each relates to it: the learner still practicing toward realization, the arhat resting in what has already been fully exhausted.

27. Section: Explaining Its Designation Begins — By the Person's Predominance: the First Meaning: Diligent Constancy of the Learner, Explaining Its Cultivated Aspect: Distinguishing Three Kinds — Toward Feeling

One should know that here, all learned noble disciples, whatever they experience, truly and comprehensively know that very experience.

Chinese Source

當知此中，所有多聞諸聖弟子，隨所領受，即於彼受如實遍知。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that here, all learned noble disciples, whatever they experience, truly and comprehensively know that very experience.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens explaining its designation (釋其施設), beginning with by the person's predominance (由補特伽羅增上), the first meaning (第一義), diligent constancy of the learner (學常委), explaining its cultivated aspect (釋修相), distinguishing three kinds (別辨三種), the first, toward feeling (於受) — "learned noble disciples" here includes both ordinary learners with worldly right view and stream-enterers and above, all counted among those still in training.

28. Section: Toward Contact and the Six Bases

Further, they truly know that feeling is drawn forth by contact, and that contact is in turn drawn forth by the six bases; toward the six bases that draw forth this contact, they diligently practice right conduct tending toward disenchantment, freedom from desire, and cessation.

Chinese Source

又能如實了知彼受觸所引生，觸復由彼六處引生，即於彼觸引因六處厭離欲滅勤修正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, they truly know that feeling is drawn forth by contact, and that contact is in turn drawn forth by the six bases; toward the six bases that draw forth this contact, they diligently practice right conduct tending toward disenchantment, freedom from desire, and cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This is the second of the three kinds, toward contact and the six bases (於觸六處) — since the resultant six bases are the very basis of suffering, disenchantment with them, and diligent practice toward freedom from desire, follows directly from truly knowing them.

29. Section: Briefly Gathering Them As Impermanent

Further, gathering feeling, contact, and the six bases — every one of these three actual matters — together as one, and knowing that all are extinguished by impermanence and are thus called dharmas of cessation, then, within the present dharma, toward all three of these impermanent, ceasing matters, one practices, as said before, tending toward disenchantment and freedom from desire.

Chinese Source

又於彼受、觸及六處一切實事略攝為一，了知一切由無常滅名滅法已，於現法中，於此一切三種實事無常滅法，如前修行厭離欲滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, gathering feeling, contact, and the six bases — every one of these three actual matters — together as one, and knowing that all are extinguished by impermanence and are thus called dharmas of cessation, then, within the present dharma, toward all three of these impermanent, ceasing matters, one practices, as said before, tending toward disenchantment and freedom from desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This is the third kind, briefly gathering them as impermanent (略攝無常) — feeling, contact, and the six bases, though distinguished individually above, are here gathered as a single impermanent, ceasing whole.

30. Section: Concluding the Name, Closing "Explaining Its Cultivated Aspect" Entirely

By this right conduct, this is called diligent constancy of the learner.

Chinese Source

由此正行，名學常委。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this right conduct, this is called diligent constancy of the learner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, concluding the name (結得名) closes entirely, closing explaining its cultivated aspect (釋修相2: 別辨三種/略攝無常) entirely — constant, unbroken contemplation of the six bases, contact, and feeling as impermanent, suffering, empty, and not-self is the learner's diligent constancy.

31. Section: Diligent Constancy in Discerning the Dharma, Closing "The First Meaning" Entirely

Further, by this right conduct, one becomes utterly without any further activity, ultimately liberated — hence this is called diligent constancy in discerning the dharma.

Chinese Source

又由修行此正行故，無所造作，究竟解脫，是故說名擇法常委。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by this right conduct, one becomes utterly without any further activity, ultimately liberated — hence this is called diligent constancy in discerning the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, diligent constancy in discerning the dharma (擇法常委) closes entirely, closing the first meaning (第一義2: 學常委/擇法常委) entirely — where the learner still cultivates, the arhat, having exhausted every contaminant and attained the wisdom of exhaustion and non-arising, abides in constant discernment with nothing further to do.

32. Section: The Second Meaning Begins — Diligent Constancy of the Learner

For the sake of realizing what has not yet been attained, not yet been realized, one cultivates without interruption, with earnest, weighty skillful means — this is called diligent constancy of the learner.

Chinese Source

為欲證得曾所未得、曾所未證，修行無間、殷重方便，名學常委。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of realizing what has not yet been attained, not yet been realized, one cultivates without interruption, with earnest, weighty skillful means — this is called diligent constancy of the learner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the second meaning (第二義), beginning again with diligent constancy of the learner (學常委) — where the first meaning simply described practicing according to what is experienced, this second meaning marks a deeper degree: unbroken, earnest striving specifically toward fruits not yet realized.

33. Section: Diligent Constancy in Discerning the Dharma, Closing "The Second Meaning" and "By the Person's Predominance" Entirely

For the sake of never regressing from all the joy of abiding in present dharmas, one acts without interruption, with earnest, weighty effort — hence this is called diligent constancy in discerning the dharma.

Chinese Source

為於所有現法樂住無有退失無間所作、殷重所作，由是說名擇法常委。

Ru-Yi Translation

For the sake of never regressing from all the joy of abiding in present dharmas, one acts without interruption, with earnest, weighty effort — hence this is called diligent constancy in discerning the dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, diligent constancy in discerning the dharma (擇法常委) closes entirely, closing the second meaning (第二義2: 學常委/擇法常委) entirely, closing by the person's predominance (由補特伽羅增上2: 第一義/第二義) entirely — even an arhat not yet liberated both ways must keep cultivating concentration lest the joy of meditative absorption itself regress, though the liberating wisdom never does.

34. Section: By the Dharma's Predominance Begins — Named the Dharma Realm

Equally proclaiming every matter and dharma through the predominant collection of names, phrases, and syllables — this is called the dharma realm.

Chinese Source

等說一切事法增上名句文身，名為法界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Equally proclaiming every matter and dharma through the predominant collection of names, phrases, and syllables — this is called the dharma realm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and the Explanation of the Different Aspects

This opens by the dharma's predominance (由法增上), the second of two matters under explaining its designation, beginning with named the dharma realm (名法界) — names, phrases, and syllables (the words themselves, the sentences built from them, and the letters underlying both) are the vehicle through which the object of the sixth consciousness is understood; undistorted use of them is what makes them "predominant." The Yogācārabhūmi Commentary gives three reasons the treatise calls this the dharma realm: it flows from the Buddha's own utterly pure dharma realm as its cause; relying on it and cultivating accordingly, one can realize the dharma realm as its fruit; and, simply as that which expresses the dharma realm, it is itself also called the dharma realm — this last sense being the one emphasized here.

35. Section: Named Skillfully Penetrating the Dharma Realm Begins — By Unobstructed Understanding

Those who have attained unobstructed understanding wield names, phrases, and syllables freely, according to their wish — hence this is called skillfully penetrating the dharma realm.

Chinese Source

諸有獲得無礙解故，名句文身隨欲自在，是故說名善達法界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who have attained unobstructed understanding wield names, phrases, and syllables freely, according to their wish — hence this is called skillfully penetrating the dharma realm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens named skillfully penetrating the dharma realm (名善達法界), beginning with by unobstructed understanding (由無礙解) — the four unobstructed understandings of dharma (the words that express dependent origination's profound meaning), meaning (what those words point to), etymology (fluency across languages), and eloquence (joyful, unhindered teaching), found complete only from the ninth bodhisattva ground onward.

36. Section: Able to Widely Proclaim Begins — Toward the Truthful Meaning

Because one has thoroughly penetrated the dharma realm, toward this very true, contemplated meaning, one further proclaims it, with distinctions, through other names, according to one's wish.

Chinese Source

由於法界善通達故，即於如是真實想義，更以餘名，隨其所樂差別宣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because one has thoroughly penetrated the dharma realm, toward this very true, contemplated meaning, one further proclaims it, with distinctions, through other names, according to one's wish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and the Madhyānta-vibhāga

This opens able to widely proclaim (能廣宣說), beginning with toward the truthful meaning (於真實義) — since different listeners awaken through different words, the same emptiness can be taught under many names. Per the 披尋記, citing the Madhyānta-vibhāga: emptiness, suchness, the true limit, signlessness, ultimate nature, and selflessness are among its many names — emptiness meaning neither a real graspable nor a real grasper is found, yet the illusion-like appearance is not nothing either; suchness meaning it never changes regardless of a being's destiny; the true limit meaning it is the undistorted object of noble wisdom alone, never a deluded ordinary being's domain; signlessness meaning every conditioned mark utterly falls away in the realization of non-discriminating wisdom; ultimate nature meaning it is the very object supreme wisdom operates upon; and selflessness meaning no permanent, unitary, sovereign self is ever found within dependent origination.

37. Section: Even Across Seven Days and Nights, Closing "Toward the Truthful Meaning" Entirely

Even across seven days and seven nights, or beyond that measure, one's eloquence never runs dry.

Chinese Source

乃至能於七日七夜，或過彼量，辭辯無竭。

Ru-Yi Translation

Even across seven days and seven nights, or beyond that measure, one's eloquence never runs dry.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, toward the truthful meaning (於真實義) closes entirely — the first of two matters under able to widely proclaim, the second, toward conditioned dharmas, still to follow.

38. Section: Toward Conditioned Dharmas, Closing "Able to Widely Proclaim," "Named Skillfully Penetrating the Dharma Realm," "By the Dharma's Predominance," "Explaining Its Designation," and "The Real" — the Third of Eight Topics — Entirely

Further, by such distinct categories, one truly proclaims that this is conditioned, fashioned by volition, moving and faltering, like a sickness, like a boil — and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

復以如是差別種類，如實宣說彼是有為，思所造作，動轉羸頓，如病、如癰，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by such distinct categories, one truly proclaims that this is conditioned, fashioned by volition, moving and faltering, like a sickness, like a boil — and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and a companion sūtra

This is the second of two matters under able to widely proclaim, toward conditioned dharmas (於有為法). Per the 披尋記, "and so on" extends the simile to like an arrow and like affliction — together the four aspects of the contemplation of aversion, detailed in the aggregates section: like a sickness (the elements' imbalance breeding bodily suffering), like a boil (afflictive seeds festering within the aggregates), like an arrow (suffering piercing as sharply and inextricably as an enemy's arrow), and affliction (grief at loss of loved ones, wealth, or health, born of clinging to self and self's-possession). With this, toward conditioned dharmas closes entirely, closing able to widely proclaim (能廣宣說2: 於真實義/於有為法) entirely, closing named skillfully penetrating the dharma realm (名善達法界2: 由無礙解/能廣宣說) entirely, closing by the dharma's predominance (由法增上2: 名法界/名善達法界) entirely, closing explaining its designation (釋其施設2: 由補特伽羅增上/由法增上) entirely, closing the real (實2: 標列境事/釋其施設), the third of contact-as-condition-and-the-rest's eight topics, entirely.

A companion sūtra has the Buddha ask Śāriputra to explain the verses on the learner and the one beyond learning from the Pārāyaṇa (the questions of Ajita/Maitreya). Śāriputra at first sits silent through three askings; when the Buddha rephrases the question in terms of contemplating the six bases, contact, and feeling, Śāriputra readily explains: the learner still has the arising of the four foods' craving even while cultivating disenchantment and freedom from desire toward the six bases, contact, and feeling; the arhat has fully severed that craving, realized the wisdom of exhaustion and non-arising, and abides in unregressing joy through earnest, ceaseless effort. Afterward, Śāriputra explains to the assembly that his initial silence was simply to let the Buddha's second framing draw out a fuller answer, and declares that he could answer the same question phrased differently every day for seven days and nights without exhausting his eloquence. The Buddha confirms this before the assembly, attributing it to Śāriputra's complete mastery of the four unobstructed understandings — this illustrates the topic's own teaching that one who has fully penetrated the dharma realm can proclaim the same truth endlessly, through the ultimate meaning and through conditioned dharmas alike.

39. Section: Understanding Begins — Marking and Listing Six Kinds of Pronouncement

Next, one should know that a fully understanding arhat has, in brief, six kinds of pronouncement of what is understood. First, pronouncement with alternative expressions; second, pronouncement without alternative expressions; third, pronouncement of wisdom; fourth, pronouncement of severance; fifth, general pronouncement; sixth, particular pronouncement.

Chinese Source

復次，當知具解諸阿羅漢，略有六種記別所解。一、有異門記別，二、無異門記別，三、智記別，四、斷記別，五、總記別，六、別記別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, one should know that a fully understanding arhat has, in brief, six kinds of pronouncement of what is understood. First, pronouncement with alternative expressions; second, pronouncement without alternative expressions; third, pronouncement of wisdom; fourth, pronouncement of severance; fifth, general pronouncement; sixth, particular pronouncement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the fourth topic, understanding (解) — the arhat's mastery of the dharma realm expressed through six distinct modes of self-declaration — beginning with marking and listing (標列) all six.

40. Section: Explaining Each in Turn Begins — Pronouncement With Alternative Expressions

Pronouncement with alternative expressions means: as with a certain one who, whether questioned by another or spontaneously, wishing to inspire in others deep reverence toward the Buddha's noble teaching, pronounces thus: "For me now, there is not a single doubt remaining."

Chinese Source

有異門記別者，謂如有一，或他請問，或復自然為欲令他於佛聖教多起恭敬，故如是記：我於今者無一疑惑。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pronouncement with alternative expressions means: as with a certain one who, whether questioned by another or spontaneously, wishing to inspire in others deep reverence toward the Buddha's noble teaching, pronounces thus: "For me now, there is not a single doubt remaining."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens explaining each in turn (隨釋), beginning with pronouncement with alternative expressions (有異門記別) — since the Buddha teaches the same truth through countless different approaches suited to different listeners, an arhat's freedom from doubt across every one of these approaches is itself a mode of declaration.

41. Section: Pronouncement Without Alternative Expressions, Closing "Explaining Each in Turn" (First Two) Entirely

Pronouncement without alternative expressions means making this declaration: "For me, birth is exhausted" — and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

無異門記別者，謂作是記：我生已盡，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pronouncement without alternative expressions means making this declaration: "For me, birth is exhausted" — and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

With this, pronouncement without alternative expressions (無異門記別) closes as its own item — the single, universally shared declaration of every arhat: "birth for me is exhausted, the pure life established, what was to be done has been done, no further existence awaits." Per the 披尋記, citing the Explanation of the Different Aspects: birth's exhaustion covers both the resultant embodiment (no eighth rebirth cycle for a stream-enterer) and affliction's own diminishment (for a once-returner); the pure life's establishment marks the non-returner's severance of the last three grades of desire-realm affliction; what was to be done marks the arhat's completed path; and no further existence marks the arhat's final freedom from the three realms at the lifespan's end — one shared verse spanning all four fruits of the path.
