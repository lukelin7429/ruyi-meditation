Fascicle 30 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十 · The Third Yogic Process Opens

Seeking a Teacher and Establishing Yogic Practice

Fascicle 30 opens the third of the four yogic processes within the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples — the actual "establishment" of yogic practice itself. Where the second yogic process (fascicles 26–29) classified practitioners and mapped the path's structure, this process shows how practice is concretely set in motion: a sincere seeker travels to a qualified teacher, is questioned, welcomed, and encouraged, and then instructed in how to safeguard the provisions for concentration and cultivate seclusion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 30 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Third Yogic Process Opens

Fascicle 30 begins a new major division of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples. Having finished classifying practitioners and their objects of meditation (the second yogic process, fascicles 26–29), the treatise now turns to actually setting up yogic practice — how it is established, safeguarded, and brought to one-pointedness of mind.

Chinese Source

如是已說補特伽羅品類、建立及所緣等，乃至趣修有果、無果。如應安立，我今當說。總嗢柁南曰：往慶問尋求　方安立護養　遠離一境性　障淨修作意

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far has been explained the kinds of practitioners, their establishment, and their objects of meditation, up to the causes of setting out toward cultivation with or without fruit. How yogic practice should rightly be established, I shall now explain. The summary verse says: going, being welcomed, being questioned, and seeking — then the establishing, the safeguarding, the seclusion, the one-pointedness — the purification of obstructions, and the cultivation of mental engagement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the whole of fascicles 30 through 32 with this single nine-word verse. The Ground of the Hearer-Disciples runs from fascicle 22 through fascicle 34, and within it the third yogic process — "establishing yogic practice" — occupies fascicles 30 to 32, organized around five loci: safeguarding the provisions for concentration, seclusion, one-pointedness of mind, purifying obstructions, and cultivating mental engagement. Fascicle 30 itself covers only the first two loci in full, plus the first two of five objects belonging to the third locus (one-pointedness); the remaining three objects, the purification of obstructions, and the cultivation of mental engagement are left for fascicles 31 and 32. Before any of the five loci can be addressed, though, the treatise first shows the human scene that makes them possible at all: a sincere practitioner travels to find a teacher, is questioned about his aspiration and capacity, and — only once that groundwork is laid — is actually given the teaching. Cháng Bǎi stresses this opening sequence is not mere formality; it establishes that yogic practice is never self-taught in isolation, but transmitted through a living relationship between a genuine seeker and a qualified guide.

2. Traveling to the Teacher: Four Abiding Thoughts

Chinese Source

若有自愛補特伽羅初修業者，始修業時，為隨證得自義利故，先應四處安住正念，然後往詣善達瑜伽或軌範師、或親教師、或餘尊重、似尊重所。云何四處？一、專求領悟，無難詰心處；二、深生恭敬，無憍慢心處；三、唯求勝善，非顯己能處；四、純為安立自他善根，非求利養恭敬名聞處。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is a self-loving individual newly beginning practice, then at the very start of practice, in order to accord with realizing his own true benefit, he should first abide in right mindfulness with respect to four matters, and only then go to a teacher well versed in yogic practice — whether a preceptor, a personal instructor, or another elder or one resembling an elder worthy of respect. What are the four? First, the abode of single-mindedly seeking understanding, free of any intent to challenge or interrogate. Second, the abode of deeply arising reverence, free of arrogance. Third, the abode of seeking only what is genuinely excellent, not of displaying one's own ability. Fourth, the abode of purely establishing wholesome roots in oneself and others, not of seeking material support, honor, or renown.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold checklist as a diagnostic any student can apply to their own motives before approaching a teacher. The first thought rules out coming merely to debate or test the teacher; the second rules out the pride that makes real learning impossible; the third rules out using the encounter as a stage for showing off one's own understanding; and the fourth — citing the companion explanation from the "Compendium of Determinations" — clarifies that a genuinely accomplished teacher is one able to plant wholesome roots of faith, precepts, learning, generosity, and wisdom in beginners, and that the student's task is to seek out exactly that capacity, not to curry favor with a teacher for the sake of donations or reputation. Only a seeker who has first stabilized these four attitudes is, in Cháng Bǎi's reading, actually prepared to receive teaching rather than merely to collect it.

3. Requesting Teaching with Proper Manner

Chinese Source

如是正念到師處已，先求開許請問時分，然後安詳躬申請問。將請問時，偏覆左肩，右膝著地，或居下坐曲躬而坐，合掌恭敬，深生愧畏，低顏軟語請問瑜伽：我於如是瑜伽行中欲求受學，唯願慈悲為我宣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having arrived at the teacher's place with right mindfulness thus established, he should first seek permission for a time to ask his questions, and only then calmly and personally present his request. When about to ask, he bares his left shoulder, places his right knee on the ground — or sits below the teacher with body bowed — joins his palms in reverence, gives rise to deep humility and awe, and with lowered face and gentle words requests the teaching: "In this yogic practice I wish to receive instruction; may you compassionately expound it for me."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the physical etiquette here — bared shoulder, knee to the ground, bowed posture, joined palms — as the outward expression of the four inward attitudes just established, not a separate requirement layered on top of them. The bodily lowering makes visible a mind that has already set aside pride and the urge to test the teacher; the gentle, humble speech makes audible a motive purified of any wish for self-display. He notes that "personally" (躬) rules out sending a written request in place of showing up, since the relationship being formed is a living one, not a transaction conducted at a distance.

4. The Teacher's Welcome Opens

Chinese Source

如是請已，善達瑜伽諸瑜伽師，為欲安立初修業者瑜伽作意，應以慈愍柔軟言詞讚勵慶慰，又應稱揚修斷功德。歎言：善哉！善哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the request has been made in this way, the teacher well versed in yogic practice — wishing to establish the beginner's mental engagement in yoga — should praise, encourage, and console him with compassionate and gentle words, and should also extol the merit of cultivating and severing what needs to be severed. He exclaims: "Excellent! Excellent!"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the teacher's response is not a formality but itself part of "establishing" the student's yogic mental engagement — encouragement is treated as pedagogically necessary, not merely polite. What follows in the treatise is a sustained sequence of nine specific grounds for praise, each naming a different way the ordinary run of beings fails and the sincere seeker alone succeeds.

5. Nine Praises for the Seeker, Part One: Non-Heedlessness, Seeking Liberation, Severing Bondage

Chinese Source

賢首！汝今乃能於墮放逸、樂著放逸、沈沒境界、樂著境界眾生類中，獨不放逸，樂修出行。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Excellent one! Among beings who fall into heedlessness, delight in heedlessness, are submerged in sense-objects, and delight in attachment to sense-objects, you alone are free of heedlessness and take joy in cultivating the path of release."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於久墮在種種憂苦險惡牢獄眾生類中，獨求解脫如是牢獄。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings who have long fallen into the various dangerous prisons of grief and suffering, you alone seek liberation from such a prison."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於彼種種貪瞋癡等杻械枷鎖常所固縛眾生類中，獨求斷壞如是固縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings constantly and firmly fettered by the shackles, chains, and cangues of craving, hatred, and delusion, you alone seek to sever and break such firm bondage."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these first three praises as a single escalating diagnosis of the ordinary condition: beings are first submerged in sense-pleasure without even recognizing its dangers (the first praise), which in turn keeps them cycling through the lower realms' "prison" of suffering (the second), bound there by the underlying fetters of craving, hatred, and delusion that never loosen on their own (the third). On the second praise, he tells of an arhat who, though already liberated, still cooked daily for the assembly; asked why he still troubled himself, the arhat recalled five hundred past lives as a dog, in which he was properly fed only twice — once eating a drunkard's vomit, once nearly killed for stealing meat from a pot — and explained that beings trapped in the lower realms are simply too consumed by suffering to ever practice at all, so he continued serving the monastic community out of gratitude for the rare chance to do so himself. Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson that the very fact of being able to seek the teaching at all is already a rare achievement, worth this first round of praise before any actual instruction begins.
