Fascicle 30 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十 · The Third Yogic Process Opens

Seeking a Teacher and Establishing Yogic Practice

Fascicle 30 opens the third of the four yogic processes within the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples — the actual "establishment" of yogic practice itself. Where the second yogic process (fascicles 26–29) classified practitioners and mapped the path's structure, this process shows how practice is concretely set in motion: a sincere seeker travels to a qualified teacher, is questioned, welcomed, and encouraged, and then instructed in how to safeguard the provisions for concentration and cultivate seclusion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 30 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Third Yogic Process Opens

Fascicle 30 begins a new major division of the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples. Having finished classifying practitioners and their objects of meditation (the second yogic process, fascicles 26–29), the treatise now turns to actually setting up yogic practice — how it is established, safeguarded, and brought to one-pointedness of mind.

Chinese Source

如是已說補特伽羅品類、建立及所緣等，乃至趣修有果、無果。如應安立，我今當說。總嗢柁南曰：往慶問尋求　方安立護養　遠離一境性　障淨修作意

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus far has been explained the kinds of practitioners, their establishment, and their objects of meditation, up to the causes of setting out toward cultivation with or without fruit. How yogic practice should rightly be established, I shall now explain. The summary verse says: going, being welcomed, being questioned, and seeking — then the establishing, the safeguarding, the seclusion, the one-pointedness — the purification of obstructions, and the cultivation of mental engagement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames the whole of fascicles 30 through 32 with this single nine-word verse. The Ground of the Hearer-Disciples runs from fascicle 22 through fascicle 34, and within it the third yogic process — "establishing yogic practice" — occupies fascicles 30 to 32, organized around five loci: safeguarding the provisions for concentration, seclusion, one-pointedness of mind, purifying obstructions, and cultivating mental engagement. Fascicle 30 itself covers only the first two loci in full, plus the first two of five objects belonging to the third locus (one-pointedness); the remaining three objects, the purification of obstructions, and the cultivation of mental engagement are left for fascicles 31 and 32. Before any of the five loci can be addressed, though, the treatise first shows the human scene that makes them possible at all: a sincere practitioner travels to find a teacher, is questioned about his aspiration and capacity, and — only once that groundwork is laid — is actually given the teaching. Cháng Bǎi stresses this opening sequence is not mere formality; it establishes that yogic practice is never self-taught in isolation, but transmitted through a living relationship between a genuine seeker and a qualified guide.

2. Traveling to the Teacher: Four Abiding Thoughts

Chinese Source

若有自愛補特伽羅初修業者，始修業時，為隨證得自義利故，先應四處安住正念，然後往詣善達瑜伽或軌範師、或親教師、或餘尊重、似尊重所。云何四處？一、專求領悟，無難詰心處；二、深生恭敬，無憍慢心處；三、唯求勝善，非顯己能處；四、純為安立自他善根，非求利養恭敬名聞處。

Ru-Yi Translation

If there is a self-loving individual newly beginning practice, then at the very start of practice, in order to accord with realizing his own true benefit, he should first abide in right mindfulness with respect to four matters, and only then go to a teacher well versed in yogic practice — whether a preceptor, a personal instructor, or another elder or one resembling an elder worthy of respect. What are the four? First, the abode of single-mindedly seeking understanding, free of any intent to challenge or interrogate. Second, the abode of deeply arising reverence, free of arrogance. Third, the abode of seeking only what is genuinely excellent, not of displaying one's own ability. Fourth, the abode of purely establishing wholesome roots in oneself and others, not of seeking material support, honor, or renown.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold checklist as a diagnostic any student can apply to their own motives before approaching a teacher. The first thought rules out coming merely to debate or test the teacher; the second rules out the pride that makes real learning impossible; the third rules out using the encounter as a stage for showing off one's own understanding; and the fourth — citing the companion explanation from the "Compendium of Determinations" — clarifies that a genuinely accomplished teacher is one able to plant wholesome roots of faith, precepts, learning, generosity, and wisdom in beginners, and that the student's task is to seek out exactly that capacity, not to curry favor with a teacher for the sake of donations or reputation. Only a seeker who has first stabilized these four attitudes is, in Cháng Bǎi's reading, actually prepared to receive teaching rather than merely to collect it.

3. Requesting Teaching with Proper Manner

Chinese Source

如是正念到師處已，先求開許請問時分，然後安詳躬申請問。將請問時，偏覆左肩，右膝著地，或居下坐曲躬而坐，合掌恭敬，深生愧畏，低顏軟語請問瑜伽：我於如是瑜伽行中欲求受學，唯願慈悲為我宣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having arrived at the teacher's place with right mindfulness thus established, he should first seek permission for a time to ask his questions, and only then calmly and personally present his request. When about to ask, he bares his left shoulder, places his right knee on the ground — or sits below the teacher with body bowed — joins his palms in reverence, gives rise to deep humility and awe, and with lowered face and gentle words requests the teaching: "In this yogic practice I wish to receive instruction; may you compassionately expound it for me."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the physical etiquette here — bared shoulder, knee to the ground, bowed posture, joined palms — as the outward expression of the four inward attitudes just established, not a separate requirement layered on top of them. The bodily lowering makes visible a mind that has already set aside pride and the urge to test the teacher; the gentle, humble speech makes audible a motive purified of any wish for self-display. He notes that "personally" (躬) rules out sending a written request in place of showing up, since the relationship being formed is a living one, not a transaction conducted at a distance.

4. The Teacher's Welcome Opens

Chinese Source

如是請已，善達瑜伽諸瑜伽師，為欲安立初修業者瑜伽作意，應以慈愍柔軟言詞讚勵慶慰，又應稱揚修斷功德。歎言：善哉！善哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Once the request has been made in this way, the teacher well versed in yogic practice — wishing to establish the beginner's mental engagement in yoga — should praise, encourage, and console him with compassionate and gentle words, and should also extol the merit of cultivating and severing what needs to be severed. He exclaims: "Excellent! Excellent!"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the teacher's response is not a formality but itself part of "establishing" the student's yogic mental engagement — encouragement is treated as pedagogically necessary, not merely polite. What follows in the treatise is a sustained sequence of nine specific grounds for praise, each naming a different way the ordinary run of beings fails and the sincere seeker alone succeeds.

5. Nine Praises for the Seeker, Part One: Non-Heedlessness, Seeking Liberation, Severing Bondage

Chinese Source

賢首！汝今乃能於墮放逸、樂著放逸、沈沒境界、樂著境界眾生類中，獨不放逸，樂修出行。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Excellent one! Among beings who fall into heedlessness, delight in heedlessness, are submerged in sense-objects, and delight in attachment to sense-objects, you alone are free of heedlessness and take joy in cultivating the path of release."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於久墮在種種憂苦險惡牢獄眾生類中，獨求解脫如是牢獄。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings who have long fallen into the various dangerous prisons of grief and suffering, you alone seek liberation from such a prison."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於彼種種貪瞋癡等杻械枷鎖常所固縛眾生類中，獨求斷壞如是固縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings constantly and firmly fettered by the shackles, chains, and cangues of craving, hatred, and delusion, you alone seek to sever and break such firm bondage."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these first three praises as a single escalating diagnosis of the ordinary condition: beings are first submerged in sense-pleasure without even recognizing its dangers (the first praise), which in turn keeps them cycling through the lower realms' "prison" of suffering (the second), bound there by the underlying fetters of craving, hatred, and delusion that never loosen on their own (the third). On the second praise, he tells of an arhat who, though already liberated, still cooked daily for the assembly; asked why he still troubled himself, the arhat recalled five hundred past lives as a dog, in which he was properly fed only twice — once eating a drunkard's vomit, once nearly killed for stealing meat from a pot — and explained that beings trapped in the lower realms are simply too consumed by suffering to ever practice at all, so he continued serving the monastic community out of gratitude for the rare chance to do so himself. Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson that the very fact of being able to seek the teaching at all is already a rare achievement, worth this first round of praise before any actual instruction begins.

6. Nine Praises, Part Two: Crossing the Wilderness of Birth-and-Death, Gaining Wholesome Roots, Attaining Peace

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於入生死曠野嶮道眾生類中，獨求超度曠野嶮道。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings who have entered the perilous wilderness road of birth-and-death, you alone seek to cross beyond that wilderness road."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於遭窮儉種種善根眾生類中，獨求獲得豐饒善根。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings whose various wholesome roots are impoverished and scant, you alone seek to gain abundant, flourishing wholesome roots."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於墮種種煩惱怨賊、廣大怖畏眾生類中，獨求證得究竟安隱常樂涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings who have fallen among the brigand-enemies of the various afflictions, subject to vast dread, you alone seek to attain the ultimate, peaceful, eternally blissful nirvāṇa."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the "wilderness road" image through the Sūtra on the Noble Path of Dependent Origination: a traveler lost in a dangerous, overgrown wilderness suddenly comes upon an old path once trodden by the ancient sages — the eightfold noble path itself — and follows it to safety; without that path, any road one invents oneself is simply another way of being lost. The fifth praise names the five wholesome roots — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — as what most beings, especially in an age of increasing turbidity and wrong view, have starved rather than cultivated. The sixth praise names affliction itself as a "brigand-enemy" precisely because it steals away one's merit the way a thief steals goods, guaranteeing future birth and its attendant death; every being's dread, Cháng Bǎi notes, ultimately traces back to this single robber hiding inside the mind.

7. Nine Praises, Part Three: Freedom from Sickness and Crossing the Floods

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於為煩惱重病吞食眾生類中，獨求證得第一無病常樂涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings devoured by the grave sickness of affliction, you alone seek to attain the foremost, sickness-free, eternally blissful nirvāṇa."

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於為四種瀑流漂溺眾生類中，獨求越度如是瀑流。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings swept away and drowning in the four floods, you alone seek to cross beyond such floods."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats affliction as a sickness harder to cure than any bodily illness, since it requires the specific medicine of wisdom rather than any physical remedy — beings driven along by it, unable to help themselves, are said to be "devoured" by it. He adds that a strong bodhisattva vow lets a practitioner keep functioning even while afflictions still arise: irritation or distraction may still surface in daily encounters, but the vow itself is never forgotten, so lapses simply get set aside and practice resumes, again and again, until the power of resolve gradually outlasts the power of habitual karma. On the four floods, he distinguishes desire (craving for the ordinary pleasures of the desire realm), existence (the subtler attachment to the bliss of form and formless-realm concentration, which still pulls a being back into birth once that concentration's karma is spent), views (wrong philosophical positions — permanence, annihilation, extreme views, self-view, and clinging to wrong precepts — which only right view, first of the eightfold path, can correct), and ignorance (self-grasping and phenomena-grasping woven through beginningless time). All four, he stresses, are equally capable of sweeping a being back into the round of birth-and-death, however far they may have already climbed.

8. Milestone: The Ninth Praise — Attaining the Great Light of Wisdom

The final and longest of the nine praises closes the sequence with an extended teaching on wisdom as light: five benefits it brings, and fifteen ways it surpasses ordinary physical light entirely.

Chinese Source

汝今乃能於入廣大無明黑闇眾生類中，獨求獲得大智光明。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Among beings who have entered the vast darkness of ignorance, you alone seek to attain the great light of wisdom."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi, drawing on the "Compendium of Synonyms" and the "Determinations" section, first unpacks ignorance itself: not knowing the object of knowledge (the five aggregates, twelve sense-fields, eighteen elements) is "ignorance," and not correctly understanding that all such things are empty, like illusions, is "darkness." He then lists wisdom's five benefits — it extinguishes every darkness regarding what is to be known; it brings mundane and supramundane merit that makes the very body it depends on feel at ease; it lets one directly witness truths never before experienced; it grants, in this very life, the foremost happiness (the happiness of waking from ignorance's great dream, unpurchasable by any worldly wealth); and after the body's dissolution, it delivers one to the foremost destination, nirvāṇa itself. He then works through all fifteen ways wisdom's light outshines the sun, moon, starlight, or lamplight: it is wisdom itself as its nature rather than physical form; it cures the inner blindness of ignorance rather than merely an outer defect of the eye; everyone loves it without exception, unlike sunlight some find scorching and moonlight others find cold; it can be shared without limit through teaching and giving, where physical light cannot be handed to another; once genuinely attained it never again disappears, unlike light that rises and sets or can simply be switched off; it is formless, so a wise person may look, to outward eyes, entirely unremarkable; it is subtle enough to read the smallest sign — a single falling leaf revealing the whole of autumn — where physical light is comparatively coarse; it has no opposing darkness, since wisdom itself is precisely the absence of the ignorance that darkness represents; it never wavers, only ever deepens; it alone can accomplish a being's ultimate benefit, nirvāṇa itself, since ordinary light, being insentient, cannot; it can lead beings to places never yet reached, whereas in beginningless saṃsāra there is nowhere among the five destinies left untouched; it can uncover whatever ignorance has covered over, where sunlight always leaves some shadow untouched; a higher wisdom can even eclipse what a lesser wisdom had previously revealed, the way realizing emptiness supersedes an earlier, subtler attachment to purity; it can generate boundless illumination without limit, letting one perceive the true nature of countless dependently arisen phenomena at once; and finally, unlike light that damages the eyes when stared at too long, wisdom's light only ever strengthens the eye that beholds it, each turning of insight deepening the next until everything encountered becomes an occasion for prajñā. With this, Cháng Bǎi closes: the nine praises altogether commend a seeker for standing utterly alone against the current of beings absorbed in heedlessness, bondage, suffering, poverty of merit, sickness, flood, and darkness — a rare enough resolve to deserve, before any actual teaching is given, this full-throated welcome.

9. Praising the Seeker's Practice and Motivation

Chinese Source

長老當知，汝若定能如是精勤修瑜伽行，乃得名為不虛受用國人信施，真實奉行如來聖教，不捨靜慮，成就勝觀，增長樂居空閑法侶，精勤修學自義瑜伽，不辱有智同梵行者。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Elder, you should know: if you can indeed practice yogic conduct with such diligence, then you may truly be said not to squander the faithful offerings of this land's people, to genuinely carry out the Tathāgata's noble teaching, never to abandon meditative stillness, to accomplish superior insight, to help fellow companions who delight in dwelling in seclusion flourish, to diligently cultivate yogic practice for your own true benefit, and never to bring shame upon your wise fellow practitioners of the pure life."

Chinese Source

汝今為欲勤修自利利他正行，汝今為欲利益安樂無量眾生，哀愍世間及諸天、人、阿素洛等，為令獲得義利安樂，故來問爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

"You now, wishing to diligently cultivate the correct practice that benefits both self and others, wishing to bring benefit and happiness to countless beings, and out of compassion for this world and for gods, humans, asuras, and the rest — so that they may attain true benefit and happiness — have therefore come to ask this question."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the teacher's welcome closes not on the seeker's suffering but on his motivation — the two praises together commend both the discipline to actually practice (never abandoning stillness, achieving superior insight, strengthening a whole community of fellow practitioners) and the breadth of intent behind it (seeking one's own benefit precisely in order to be able to benefit countless others). He points out this is not an incidental detail: even at the very threshold of instruction, before a single technique has been taught, the treatise already frames genuine practice as inseparable from care for other beings — a note that will recur throughout the third yogic process's more technical material still to come.

10. Four Questions to Assess the Seeker

Chinese Source

告言：長老！汝已一向歸佛法僧，非外道師及彼邪法弟子眾不？汝已最初淨修梵行、善淨尸羅、正直見不？汝已於其總標、別辯諸聖諦法，若少、若多，聞受持不？汝於涅槃深心信解，為證寂滅而出家不？

Ru-Yi Translation

He says: "Elder! Have you already taken refuge single-heartedly in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, and are not a teacher of an outside path nor a disciple of its wrong teachings? Have you from the very start purely cultivated the pure conduct of celibacy, well purified your moral discipline, and formed a correct and upright view? Have you heard and upheld — whether much or little — the noble truths as both summarized and individually analyzed? Do you, with deep confidence and understanding regarding nirvāṇa, go forth from home precisely to realize peaceful cessation?"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these four questions as a gate that must be passed before any instruction in yogic practice proper begins. The first, on refuge, cites the "Determinations" section's five marks of a genuinely worthy refuge — purity of body, speech, and mind, great compassionate resolve, and the attainment of the unsurpassed teaching — since only an object possessing all five deserves to be relied upon at all; most people, he notes, only take refuge as death approaches, when it would have served them far better taken up while young. The second, on pure conduct and right view, unpacks "correct and upright view" into four supporting conditions: view joined with pure faith in the Dharma and Vinaya, joined with confident understanding of what has not yet been personally verified but is trusted as attainable through practice, free of deceit and flattery (confessing faults openly rather than concealing them), and grounded in careful reflection on the teaching so that no room for doubt remains. The third simply checks whether the seeker has any real exposure to the four noble truths at all — if not, that must be supplied before practice can begin. The fourth, on true motivation for renunciation, Cháng Bǎi illustrates with a monk who, by his own account, took ordination chiefly because he admired the fashionable socks worn by monastics — and later disrobed, precisely because the original motivation was never actually oriented toward liberation. A teacher who skips this fourth question, he warns, risks guiding someone who was never actually seeking what the teaching offers.

11. Seeking Out Aspiration, Lineage, Faculty, and Conduct — Four Loci, Four Methods

Chinese Source

如是問已，彼若云爾，次後復於四種處所，以四因緣應正尋求。何等名為四種處所？一、應尋求其願，二、應尋求種性，三、應尋求其根，四、應尋求其行。云何名為四種因緣？一、應以審問而正尋求，二、應以言論而正尋求，三、應以所作而正尋求，四、應以知他心差別智而正尋求。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having asked thus, if the seeker answers affirmatively, the teacher should next rightly seek out four matters through four means. What are the four matters? First, one should seek out his aspiration. Second, one should seek out his spiritual lineage. Third, one should seek out his faculty. Fourth, one should seek out his conduct. What are the four means? First, one should rightly seek through interrogation. Second, one should rightly seek through discourse. Third, one should rightly seek through observed conduct. Fourth, one should rightly seek through the wisdom that discerns another's mind.

Chinese Source

謂如是問：長老！於何已發正願？聲聞乘耶？獨覺乘耶？無上正等菩提乘耶？彼得此問，隨自所願，當如是答。如是名為應以審問尋求其願。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, one asks thus: "Elder! Toward which have you set your correct aspiration — the Hearer's vehicle? The Solitary Realizer's vehicle? Or the vehicle of unsurpassed, perfect enlightenment?" Receiving this question, the seeker answers according to his own aspiration. This is called rightly seeking out his aspiration through interrogation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold grid — four things to discover, crossed with four ways of discovering them — as the treatise's method for genuinely individualized teaching rather than a one-size-fits-all curriculum. Aspiration is simply asked outright, since a seeker generally already knows which of the three vehicles he has set his heart on. Lineage, faculty, and conduct, by contrast, often are not consciously known even to the seeker himself, which is why the remaining three methods — discourse, observed behavior, and (for an accomplished teacher) direct mind-reading — become necessary; a seeker's own self-report about his root nature is not automatically reliable in the way his stated aspiration is.

12. Milestone: Discerning Lineage Through Discourse — Three Vehicles, Three Reactions

Since lineage is not always self-evident even to the seeker, the teacher tests it directly: teach a portion of each vehicle's characteristic doctrine in turn, and watch which one moves the listener to genuine, unmistakable joy.

Chinese Source

謂對其前，應以顯了正理相應眾雜美妙易解言詞，說聲聞乘相應言論。彼聞宣說此言論時，若身中有聲聞種性，於此言論便發最極踊躍歡喜，深生信解。若身中有獨覺種性、大乘種性，於此言論不發最極踊躍歡喜，不生信解。次復為其說獨覺乘相應言論……後復為其宣說大乘相應言論……

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, before the seeker, one should speak, with clear, principle-aligned, well-ordered, beautiful, and easily understood words, a discourse corresponding to the Hearer's vehicle. When he hears this discourse, if he has the Hearer's lineage within him, he gives rise to the utmost leaping joy over this discourse and deep confidence and understanding. If he has the Solitary Realizer's or Great Vehicle's lineage within him, he does not give rise to such utmost joy, nor such confidence and understanding regarding it. Next, one speaks to him a discourse corresponding to the Solitary Realizer's vehicle … And afterward, one speaks to him a discourse corresponding to the Great Vehicle …

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the full three-part test. The Hearer's-vehicle discourse centers on the four noble truths, delivered in the properly graduated order — first the suffering of the three realms' worldly result, then its cause, then the wondrous function of nirvāṇa's result, then the path that accomplishes it — and only a listener carrying the Hearer's lineage responds with unmistakable, visible joy; a Solitary Realizer or Great Vehicle listener may feel it is true but somehow insufficient, sensing a deeper teaching is called for. The Solitary Realizer's-vehicle discourse centers on dependent origination, from ignorance conditioning volitional formations through to the whole twelve-link chain culminating in aging-and-death; here a Hearer-lineage listener may feel the path described takes too long, while a Great Vehicle listener finds the compassion for others too thin to sustain lifetimes of benefiting beings. The Great Vehicle discourse draws on the Buddha's own past-life stories — as a nine-colored deer, an elephant king, a rabbit king, each sacrificing itself for others across countless eons, and the three incalculable eons of the bodhisattva path culminating in buddhahood — and here it is the Hearer or Solitary Realizer lineage listener who cannot help doubting whether "the Great Vehicle is even the Buddha's own word," while a true Great Vehicle listener recognizes it instantly as exactly what he came for: not just self-benefit but benefit for boundless others. Cháng Bǎi stresses the test works precisely because lineage expresses itself as spontaneous resonance, not reasoned preference — a settled inclination that surfaces as joy before it can be argued into being.

13. Discerning Faculty and the Five Kinds of Conduct Through Discourse

Chinese Source

若有鈍根，雖聞宣說麤淺言論，而於法義勵力審思，方能領受解了通達。若有利根，雖聞宣說深細言論，而於法義速能領受解了通達。若有中根，則不如是。

Ru-Yi Translation

If he has a dull faculty, then even hearing a coarse and shallow discourse, he must still labor and reflect carefully upon its meaning before he can receive, understand, and penetrate it. If he has a sharp faculty, then even hearing a deep and subtle discourse, he can swiftly receive, understand, and penetrate its meaning. If he has a middling faculty, it is neither of these.

Chinese Source

若有貪行，彼聞為說淨妙言論，便發最極淨信愛樂，悟入其趣；身毛皆豎，悲涕墮淚……若有瞋行，當知一切與上相違。若有癡行，彼聞為說決定通達涅槃離染相應言論，便生最極驚恐怖畏……若有慢行，彼聞為說正法言論，不甚恭敬屬耳樂聞……若尋思行，彼聞為說正法言論，雖攝耳聽，而心散亂，惡受所受，凡所領受不堅不住，隨受隨失，數重請問。

Ru-Yi Translation

If his conduct is dominated by craving, then hearing a discourse on giving, precepts, and heavenly rebirth, he gives rise to the utmost pure faith and delight, awakening into its meaning — his body hair standing on end, tears of emotion falling. If his conduct is dominated by hatred, know that everything is the reverse of this. If his conduct is dominated by delusion, then hearing a discourse establishing, with certainty, the penetration of nirvāṇa and freedom from defilement, he gives rise to the utmost fright and terror. If his conduct is dominated by pride, then hearing a discourse on the true Dharma, he does not listen with much reverence or delight. And if his conduct is dominated by discursive thought, then hearing a discourse on the true Dharma, though he strains to listen, his mind is scattered — what he grasps he grasps wrongly, and whatever he receives neither stays firm nor abides, being lost as soon as it is gained, so that he must ask the same question again and again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the faculty test simply measures speed and depth of comprehension against the same material, while the conduct test reads the listener's emotional signature against five different kinds of teaching. A craving-dominated listener is visibly moved by teachings on giving, precepts, and their pleasant results, since these promise exactly what such a temperament already values. A hatred-dominated listener shows none of that response. A delusion-dominated listener, confronted with teachings on impermanence, no-self, and nirvāṇa, reacts with outright fear rather than interest — Cháng Bǎi reads this as fear of losing the very sense of a fixed, continuous self that delusion depends on. A pride-dominated listener remains outwardly unmoved by true teaching no matter how skillfully offered, since pride resists being taught by anyone. And a discursive-thought-dominated listener cannot hold onto anything at all — attentive in the moment yet unable to retain it, needing to ask the same question over and over, since a mind given over to restless mental chatter has no stillness in which learning can actually settle.

14. Discerning Through Observed Conduct and Through Reading the Mind Directly

Chinese Source

謂如前說聲聞種性，及貪等行補特伽羅所有相狀，是名所作。由此所作，如其所應，當正尋求種性、根、行。……謂如有一善達瑜伽修瑜伽師，已得知他心差別智。彼由如是他心智故，如實了知種性、根、行。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, the marks belonging to one of the Hearer's lineage, and to individuals of craving-dominated conduct and the rest, as previously explained, are called "observed conduct." Through such observed conduct, one should rightly seek out lineage, faculty, and conduct as is fitting. … That is, suppose there is a teacher well versed in yogic practice who has already attained the wisdom that discerns another's mind. Through this mind-reading wisdom, he truly and accurately knows the seeker's lineage, faculty, and conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi points back to the twenty-eight kinds of practitioner cataloged earlier in the second yogic process (fascicle 26) as the reference list a teacher draws on for this third method: lineage, faculty, and the various temperaments each carry their own recognizable bearing in ordinary body, speech, and daily behavior, visible to an attentive teacher without any question being asked at all. The fourth method — direct mind-reading — Cháng Bǎi treats as available only to a teacher of genuine meditative attainment, and therefore not something every teacher can rely on; interrogation, discourse, and observed conduct together are meant to serve even where this highest method is unavailable. With this, all four methods for uncovering aspiration, lineage, faculty, and conduct are complete.

15. Milestone: The Five Loci Where Yogic Practice Is Established

Having assessed the seeker fully, the teacher is now ready to actually establish yogic practice — organized, the treatise says, around five loci. This structure governs the whole of fascicles 30 through 32.

Chinese Source

於四種處，以四因緣正尋求已，復於五處如應安立。云何五處？一、護養定資糧處，二、遠離處，三、心一境性處，四、障清淨處，五、修作意處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having rightly sought out the four matters through the four means, one next establishes yogic practice, as is fitting, at five loci. What are the five loci? First, the locus of safeguarding the provisions for concentration. Second, the locus of seclusion. Third, the locus of one-pointedness of mind. Fourth, the locus of purifying obstructions. Fifth, the locus of cultivating mental engagement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the architecture that will structure the remainder of this fascicle and the two that follow: safeguarding and nurturing the provisions for concentration (precepts, sense-restraint, moderation in eating, wakefulness practice, clear comprehension, and reliance on good companions); seclusion of place, body, and mind; one-pointedness of mind (the nine stages of mental abiding for calm-abiding, and the four kinds of wisdom-conduct for insight); purifying the obstructions (thoroughly knowing the nature, causes, dangers, and antidotes of the obstructions of timidity, being covered over, discursive thought, and self-conceit); and cultivating mental engagement (the mental engagements of taming, moistening, generating pliancy, and purifying view). Fascicle 30 itself will complete only the first two loci in full, plus the first two of the third locus's five objects — the remaining three objects, together with the fourth and fifth loci, are left for fascicles 31 and 32.

16. Safeguarding the Provisions for Concentration: Precepts as the First Example

Chinese Source

云何護養定資糧？謂若成就戒律儀者，即於是處為令不退，住不放逸；如佛所誡、如佛所許，圓滿戒蘊學處差別，精進修行，常無懈廢。如是能於已所證得尸羅相應學道無退，亦能證得先所未證尸羅相應殊勝學道。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is safeguarding the provisions for concentration? That is, if he has already accomplished the moral discipline of restraint, then, right at this point, in order to prevent his falling back from it, he abides in non-heedlessness; in accordance with what the Buddha has prohibited and what the Buddha has permitted, he perfects the various precepts making up the aggregate of moral discipline, cultivating them diligently and without ever slackening or abandoning them. In this way he neither falls back from the path already realized in conjunction with moral discipline, nor fails to realize the superior path in conjunction with moral discipline not yet realized.

Chinese Source

如說成就戒律儀，如是成就根律儀、於食知量、初夜後夜悎寤瑜伽、正知而住，如是乃至成就所有沙門莊嚴，隨所獲得資糧所攝善法差別，皆能防護令不退失。於後勝進善法差別，為速圓滿，為如所說無增無減平等現行，發生樂欲，增上欣慕，恆常安住勇猛精進。是名護養定資糧。

Ru-Yi Translation

As said of accomplishing the moral discipline of restraint, so too with accomplishing restraint of the faculties, moderation in eating, the yoga of wakefulness in the first and last watches of the night, and abiding with clear comprehension — and so on up through accomplishing every ornament of a true renunciant: whatever wholesome states belonging to the provisions have been attained, all of them are safeguarded so that they do not decline. As for the further, more advanced wholesome states still to come, in order to swiftly perfect them, and to manifest them evenly, neither adding to nor subtracting from what has been taught, one gives rise to the wish for them, increasingly cherishes and longs for them, and constantly abides in courageous diligence. This is called safeguarding the provisions for concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the precept discipline's two modes — restraint (not committing killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and the rest) and active observance (positively carrying out one's duties, such as attending to one's teacher) — and reads "safeguarding" and "nurturing" as two distinct tasks: safeguarding prevents decline in what has already been accomplished, while nurturing actively grows it further, ultimately allowing the discipline tied to the prātimokṣa vows to mature first into the discipline that accompanies concentration itself and finally into the discipline that arises spontaneously, without effort, alongside the noble path. He then generalizes the pattern from precepts to the entire fourteen-item list of the twofold provisions given earlier in fascicle 22 — sense-restraint, moderation in eating, wakefulness practice, clear comprehension, good companionship, and the rest — all safeguarded the same way. On the "further, more advanced wholesome states" (the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment awaiting cultivation in the path of application), Cháng Bǎi observes that the fervent motivation typical of a newly ordained monastic — vivid, urgent, certain of its own progress — very commonly fades after some years of routine, only to be reawakened by some small taste of genuine progress; the treatise's point, he stresses, is that this longing must be actively generated and sustained rather than left to fluctuate on its own, since sustained courageous diligence across decades, even without full sagehood, reliably produces a genuinely virtuous person.

17. Milestone: The Locus of Seclusion Opens

With the provisions for concentration safeguarded, the treatise turns to the second locus: cultivating seclusion of place, body, and mind as the condition for higher wholesome states to actually take root.

Chinese Source

如是遠離順退分法，修習能順勝分法時，樂住遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus abandoned the states conducive to decline, at the time of cultivating states conducive to what is superior, one takes joy in dwelling in seclusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "states conducive to decline" with the negative mirror of everything just covered — neglecting precepts, sense-restraint, moderation in eating, wakefulness practice, clear comprehension, and good companionship — all of which amount to simple heedlessness regarding the provisions for concentration. Once these are set aside, cultivating the more advanced factors that lead toward the noble path requires a practitioner to actually delight in seclusion: seclusion of place, of body, and of mind, the three forms of seclusion detailed across the rest of this locus. This closes the fascicle's transition into its second major topic.

18. Seclusion Defined: Three Components

Chinese Source

云何遠離？謂處所圓滿、威儀圓滿、遠離圓滿，是名遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is seclusion? That is, perfection of place, perfection of deportment, and perfection of seclusion — this is called seclusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "states conducive to decline" as simply living heedlessly amid the five sense-pleasures, or worse, feeling one's practice has already reached "enough" and need go no further; "states conducive to what is superior" means never allowing that sense of sufficiency to arise, since only a practitioner who feels perpetually short of the goal keeps pressing forward. He cites the classical image that pride is a mountain too high for the water of the Dharma to flow into — only genuine humility, like the low-lying ocean gathering every stream, lets the teaching actually enter. This threefold structure — a suitable place, a disciplined bodily deportment, and true inward seclusion of body and mind from affliction and idle discursive thought — is what the treatise now unpacks at length, item by item.

19. Perfection of Place: Where a Practitioner May Dwell

Chinese Source

謂或阿練若、或林樹下、或空閑室。山谷、巖穴、稻稈積等，名空閑室。大樹林中，名林樹下。空迥、塚間、邊際臥坐，名阿練若。當知如是山谷、巖穴、稻稈積等，大樹林中，空迥、塚間、邊際臥坐，或阿練若、或林樹下、或空閑室，總名處所。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, either a forest hermitage, or beneath a grove of trees, or an unoccupied dwelling. Mountain valleys, rocky caves, heaps of rice-straw, and the like are called an unoccupied dwelling. The midst of a great forest is called beneath a grove of trees. Open wilderness, a charnel ground, or a bare minimum of seating and bedding are called a forest hermitage. Know that all of these — mountain valleys, rocky caves, heaps of rice-straw, the midst of a great forest, open wilderness, a charnel ground, bare minimum seating and bedding, whether a forest hermitage, beneath a grove of trees, or an unoccupied dwelling — are together called "a place."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Buddha's own rule that a forest meditator should not sit beneath the same tree more than three nights running, precisely so the sense of "my tree" — the seed of self and self-possession — never has time to take root; he draws the parallel to a modern classroom or meditation hall, where always taking the same seat quietly plants the same proprietary feeling. "Bare minimum" seating and bedding he reads literally: simple enough that not even a thief passing by would think it worth stealing. While contemporary monastics mostly live in established monasteries rather than literal wilderness, Cháng Bǎi treats the underlying qualities named here — quiet, unclaimed, free of the pull of ownership — as still the standard against which any dwelling should be measured.

20. Milestone: The Five Qualities of a Perfect Place

Having named the kinds of places suitable for practice, the treatise now specifies five qualities any of them should ideally possess.

Chinese Source

謂若處所，從本已來形相端嚴，眾所喜見，清淨無穢；園林池沼悉皆具足，清虛可樂；地無高下，處無毒刺，亦無眾多磚石瓦礫；能令見者心生清淨，樂住其中修斷加行；心悅、心喜，任持於斷。是名第一處所圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

First: a place whose form has always been dignified and pleasing to behold, pure and free of filth; complete with gardens, groves, and ponds, clear, open, and delightful; its ground level, free of thorns and poisonous growths, and free of brick, stone, and rubble; able to make anyone who sees it feel purified at heart and glad to dwell there applying effort toward severing affliction — the mind delighted, the mind joyful, sustaining that severing. This is the first perfection of place.

Chinese Source

又若處所，晝無憒鬧，夜少音聲，亦少蚊蝱、風日、蛇蝎諸惡毒觸。是名第二處所圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second: a place free of clamor by day, with little sound by night, and with few mosquitoes and gadflies, little harsh wind or sun, and little contact with snakes, scorpions, or other venomous, harmful creatures. This is the second perfection of place.

Chinese Source

又若處所，無惡師子、虎、豹、豺、狼、怨敵、盜賊、人非人等諸恐怖事；於是處所，身意泰然，都無疑慮，安樂而住。是名第三處所圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third: a place without fierce lions, tigers, leopards, jackals, wolves, hostile enemies, bandits, or menacing humans and non-humans — a place where body and mind are at ease, entirely without apprehension, dwelling there in peace and happiness. This is the third perfection of place.

Chinese Source

又若處所，隨順身命眾具易得，求衣服等不甚艱難，飲食支持無所匱乏。是名第四處所圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth: a place where the various necessities that sustain body and life are easily obtained, where seeking clothing and the like is not too difficult, and where the sustenance of food and drink is never lacking. This is the fourth perfection of place.

Chinese Source

又若處所，有善知識之所攝受，及諸有智同梵行者之所居止；未開曉處能正開曉，已開曉處更令明淨；甚深句義以慧通達，善巧方便殷勤開示，能令智見速得清淨。是名第五處所圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth: a place where one is cared for by a good spiritual friend, and where wise fellow practitioners of the pure life also dwell; where what is not yet understood can be correctly clarified, and what is already understood can be made yet more luminous and pure; where profound meanings are penetrated with wisdom, taught tirelessly and skillfully, so that one's wisdom and insight are swiftly purified. This is the fifth perfection of place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each quality's practical logic: a beautiful, uncluttered site naturally lifts the mind toward practice, its very appeal generating what the sub-commentary calls "delight in the place, joy in one's own resolve, and the sustained energy to actually sever affliction." The second quality rules out biting insects and venomous creatures — which the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice* traces to beings reborn from hell through the hungry-ghost realm into such forms precisely because of habitual anger, illustrated by the folk saying "the strength of gnats and gadflies is no match for an ox or horse, yet the ox and horse are still tormented by them," since even a small irritant relentlessly applied can overwhelm a much larger creature's peace of mind. The third rules out both literal predators and the "non-human" presences popularly associated with dark, gloomy sites. The fourth is a plainly practical point: a hermitage too remote to reliably resupply — cut off entirely, say, when a single access road washes out — invites its own kind of distraction through material anxiety. The fifth quality Cháng Bǎi treats as in fact the most important of the five: proximity to a genuine teacher and community of wise companions, since even a physically ideal site accomplishes little if there is no one nearby able to clarify what remains unclear or deepen what is already understood.

21. Perfection of Deportment: The Daily Rhythm and Cross-Legged Sitting's Five Reasons

Chinese Source

謂於晝分經行、宴坐；於初夜分亦復如是；於中夜分右脅而臥；於後夜分疾疾還起，經行、宴坐。即於如是圓滿臥具，諸佛所許大小繩床、草葉座等結加趺坐，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

That is, during the day one paces in walking meditation and sits in stillness; during the first watch of the night one does likewise; during the middle watch one lies down on the right side; and during the last watch one rises quickly and again paces and sits in stillness. Upon such proper bedding — rope-beds large or small, or grass-and-leaf seats permitted by the buddhas — one sits cross-legged, and so on at length.

Chinese Source

何因緣故結加趺坐？謂正觀見五因緣故。一、由身攝斂速發輕安。二、由此宴坐能經久時。三、由此宴坐是不共法。四、由此宴坐形相端嚴。五、由此宴坐佛佛弟子共所開許。正觀如是五種因緣，是故應當結加趺坐。

Ru-Yi Translation

For what reason does one sit cross-legged? That is, because one rightly observes five reasons. First, because the body being drawn together swiftly gives rise to pliancy. Second, because sitting thus one can endure for a long time. Third, because this sitting posture is a teaching not shared with outside paths. Fourth, because this sitting posture's form is dignified and proper. Fifth, because this sitting posture is what the buddhas and their disciples have, together, permitted. Rightly observing these five reasons, one should therefore sit cross-legged.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps out the schedule practically: sitting and walking meditation by day and in the first watch of evening, sleep taken lying on the right side during the middle watch (the traditional posture the Buddha himself is said to have used), and a rapid return to practice at the last watch before dawn. On the five reasons for the cross-legged posture, he notes the body drawn compactly together lets energy and blood circulate smoothly, producing pliancy quickly and sustaining long sitting without early fatigue; that no outside path teaches this particular posture, some instead practicing standing on one or two legs; that its very form — echoed in nearly every Buddha image ever carved — inspires reverence in onlookers; and that it carries the unanimous sanction of every buddha and disciple across the three times. He adds the traditional distinction between the "auspicious" posture (right foot over left, right hand over left) associated with wisdom and the "demon-subduing" posture (the mirror image) associated with concentration, and notes that women are traditionally taught the half rather than full cross-legged posture, out of concern recorded in the vinaya for physical safety during menstruation.

22. Perfection of Deportment, Continued: An Erect Body, a Right Aspiration, and Mindfulness Turned Away from the World

Chinese Source

端身正願者，云何端身？謂策舉身令其端直。云何正願？謂令其心離諂離詐，調柔正直。由策舉身令端直故，其心不為惛沈睡眠之所纏擾。離諂詐故，其心不為外境散動之所纏擾。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for an erect body and right aspiration — what is an erect body? It means bracing and raising the body so that it is upright and straight. What is right aspiration? It means causing the mind to be free of flattery and deceit, gentle, pliant, and upright. Because the body is braced and raised so as to be upright, the mind is not entangled and disturbed by dullness and drowsiness. Because it is free of flattery and deceit, the mind is not entangled and disturbed by distraction toward outer objects.

Chinese Source

安住背念者，云何名為安住背念？謂如理作意相應念，名為背念，棄背違逆一切黑品故。又緣定相為境念，名為背念，棄背除遣一切不定地所緣境故。如是名為威儀圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for abiding in mindfulness turned away — what is called abiding in mindfulness turned away? It means mindfulness in accord with sound reasoning attention, called mindfulness turned away, because it turns away from and opposes every category of defiled states. Further, mindfulness that takes the mark of concentration as its object is also called mindfulness turned away, because it turns away from and dispels every object belonging to an unconcentrated state. This is called perfection of deportment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three elements — cross-legged posture, an erect body with right aspiration, and mindfulness turned away — as the complete instruction for how to actually sit down to practice. Before sitting, a practitioner should renew the vow that gives the sitting its direction — the Hearer's vow to realize nirvāṇa, or the bodhisattva's vow toward unsurpassed enlightenment — quoting the classical formula from the *Śāsana-prakāśana-śāstra*: "May the buddhas and bodhisattvas bear witness: I, disciple so-and-so, from this day forward give rise to the unsurpassed mind of enlightenment; from now on, whatever I cultivate — giving, precepts, patience, diligence, meditative stillness, and wisdom — is entirely for the sake of realizing complete, perfect enlightenment." He warns against sitting merely to look diligent to onlookers while the mind wanders, and lists the very distractions likely to intrude — desire for the five sense-pleasures, thoughts of one's homeland, of relatives, of wealthy patrons one might approach, even the arrogant thought that "only I am capable of sitting like this" — each already a departure from the very point of practice, which is to work against affliction, not display it. Especially striking is his warning against "the thought that death will not come soon," which he treats as a subtle laziness that discounts the sitting's urgency; illness, he notes, can make even folding one's hands difficult, so a healthy body and the leisure to sit at all is itself a rare and fragile condition not to be taken lightly. With posture, aspiration, and turned-away mindfulness all in place, perfection of deportment is complete.

23. Milestone: Body Seclusion and Mind Seclusion — the Locus of Seclusion Closes

The treatise now names the last of seclusion's three components — body seclusion and mind seclusion — and with their close, the entire second locus, seclusion, is complete.

Chinese Source

云何遠離圓滿？謂有二種：一、身遠離，二、心遠離。身遠離者，謂不與在家及出家眾共相雜住，獨一無侶，是名身遠離。心遠離者，謂遠離一切染汙、無記所有作意，修習一切其性是善能引義利定地作意，及定資糧加行作意，是名心遠離。如是此中，若處所圓滿、若威儀圓滿、若身遠離、若心遠離，總攝為一，說名遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is perfection of seclusion? That is, it is of two kinds: first, seclusion of body; second, seclusion of mind. Seclusion of body means not dwelling intermingled with householders and monastics of differing purpose, alone and without such company — this is called seclusion of body. Seclusion of mind means turning away from every defiled and every morally neutral mental engagement, and cultivating every mental engagement whose nature is wholesome, able to bring true benefit, whether an engagement fixed in concentration or an engagement belonging to the provisions and preparatory practice for concentration — this is called seclusion of mind. Thus, herein, perfection of place, perfection of deportment, seclusion of body, and seclusion of mind, gathered together as one, are called seclusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to correct a common misreading of "seclusion of body": it does not strictly mean living alone, but rather not living intermingled with those of a genuinely different purpose — a householder or monastic sincerely committed to the same path may share a dwelling without breaking this seclusion, while sharing a roof with someone pursuing an entirely different aim (he gives the example of one wanting to sit in meditation while another only wants to perform funeral rites for donors) counts as impurely "mixed dwelling" even if physically together. Genuine solitary dwelling, he adds, is reserved for those who have already reached at least the stage of an inward worthy, since a true beginner living entirely alone, with no one to check or encourage him, easily drifts into self-indulgence. Seclusion of mind he distinguishes from seclusion of body as an entirely inward discipline: worldly wholesome states are still "defiled" so long as they carry self-grasping or phenomena-grasping, and morally neutral mental engagement merely reinforces habitual tendencies without advancing anything — both must be set aside in favor of mental engagement genuinely aligned with the four foundations of mindfulness, the eightfold path, and the rest of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, whether at the stage of gathering the provisions or of the four preparatory stages (heat, summit, patience, and supreme worldly dharma) leading directly into the path of seeing. With body and mind both secluded, this closes perfection of seclusion — and with it, the entire second locus, seclusion, in full: place, deportment, body, and mind, all gathered as a single, unified discipline.

24. Milestone: The Locus of One-Pointedness of Mind Opens — What Is Samādhi?

With seclusion complete, the treatise turns to its third locus, one-pointedness of mind — and opens with the treatise's own formal definition of samādhi itself, built from four precisely worded components.

Chinese Source

云何心一境性？謂數數隨念同分所緣，流注、無罪適悅相應，令心相續，名三摩地，亦名為善心一境性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is one-pointedness of mind? That is, repeated mindful recollection of an object of the same type, flowing in unbroken continuity, joined with blameless ease — causing the mind to continue uninterrupted — this is called samādhi, also called wholesome one-pointedness of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this the single most detailed and authoritative definition of samādhi found anywhere in Buddhist literature, needing no supplement from any other text. He previews its four components, each of which the treatise will unpack in turn: repeated mindful recollection (數數隨念) — sustained attention that never lapses; an object of the same type (同分所緣) — the sixth consciousness's inner image matching what the physical senses first perceived, the way a corpse visualized with eyes closed matches the corpse actually seen; flowing continuity joined with ease (流注適悅相應) — attention pouring onto the object the way rain gathers, drop after drop, in a single spot, undisturbed by any of the ten distracting marks; and blameless ease (無罪適悅相應) — a mind free of the coarse and subtle afflictions that would otherwise taint the very concentration being cultivated, most importantly the five hindrances. Only when all four converge does the mind qualify as genuinely, wholesomely one-pointed — a technical, exacting standard, not simply "feeling calm."

25. Repeated Mindful Recollection, and an Object of the Same Type

Chinese Source

何等名為數數隨念？謂於正法聽聞受持，從師獲得教誡教授增上力故，令其定地諸相現前。緣此為境，流注、無罪適悅相應，所有正念隨轉安住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called repeated mindful recollection? That is, through hearing and upholding the true teaching, and through the empowering influence of the instruction and guidance received from a teacher, the various marks belonging to the stage of concentration are made to appear. Taking these as one's object, flowing in continuity and joined with blameless ease, the whole of right mindfulness continues to abide accordingly.

Chinese Source

云何名為同分所緣？謂諸定地所緣境界，非一眾多種種品類，緣此為境，令心正行，說名為定，此即名為同分所緣。問：此所緣境是誰同分，說為同分？答：是所知事相似品類，故名同分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called an object of the same type? That is, the objects belonging to the stage of concentration are not of a single kind but many various categories; taking one of these as the object and causing the mind to move correctly upon it is called concentration, and this is called an object of the same type. Question: of what is this object "of the same type" — of what is it said to be "same"? Answer: it belongs to a category resembling the very thing to be known — hence it is called "of the same type."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "repeated mindful recollection" describes what right instruction from a genuine teacher makes possible: the provisions, preparatory practice, object, and mental engagement proper to concentration all actually arising and functioning, rather than remaining theoretical knowledge. "An object of the same type" he explains through the mechanics of meditation itself: the physical senses (the first five consciousnesses) first perceive something — a corpse, the rise and fall of the breath — and then, eyes closed, the sixth consciousness recreates an inner image of that same thing to sustain concentration on. That inner image must genuinely resemble what was originally perceived — mistaking the breath's fall for its rise, for instance, breaks the correspondence — and it is this resemblance, this "sameness of type" between the inner image and the original object, that makes the practice a genuine act of concentration rather than mere disconnected imagination.

26. Milestone: Flowing Continuity and Blameless Ease — the Five Hindrances in Full

The definition's final two components explain what makes attention "flow" without break, and what makes that flow genuinely blameless — which requires the treatise to name, in detail, the five hindrances capable of breaking it.

Chinese Source

復由彼念，於所緣境無散亂行，無闕無間，無間、殷重加行適悅相應而轉，故名流注適悅相應。又由彼念，於所緣境無有染汙，極安隱住，熟道適悅相應而轉，故名無罪適悅相應。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, through that mindfulness, engaging the object of meditation without distraction, without lapse, without interruption, continuing on with earnest, sustained effort joined with ease — this is called flowing continuity joined with ease. Further, through that mindfulness, engaging the object of meditation free of defilement, abiding in the utmost peace, continuing on a well-worn path joined with ease — this is called blameless ease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties "flowing continuity" to the eighth of the nine stages of mental abiding, single-pointed continuity, where attention never lapses (missing a breath count) and never grows mixed with intrusions (drifting mid-count into planning tomorrow); it should be sustained with earnest effort yet without strain, the way rain gathers drop by drop in exactly one place. "Blameless ease" he ties to the ninth and highest stage, effortless equipoise, where the mind rests on its object with no defiling admixture at all — meaning, above all, freedom from the five hindrances, which Cháng Bǎi then details one by one as the concrete content of "blameless": the hindrance of desire, craving the pleasing appearance of the five sense-objects, worst of all sexual desire; the hindrance of ill will, arising from being corrected by a fellow practitioner, from dwelling on a past grievance, or from plotting future harm, any of which displaces the meditation object with a burning resentment; the hindrance of dullness and drowsiness, traceable to compromised precepts, unguarded senses, poorly regulated eating (too much, too little, or the wrong kind of food), insufficient effort to reduce sleep, absence of clear comprehension, lack of energetic application, or simply accumulated fatigue — its milder form leaving the meditator uncertain whether the object is even present, its severe form outright unnoticed sleep; the hindrance of restlessness and worry, restlessness surging up as nostalgic memories of family, homeland, or past amusements, worry as regret over having left one's parents, over hardships taken on, or — just as often — regret over having renounced too young when worldly success still beckoned; and the hindrance of doubt, uncertainty about the Buddha, the teaching, the training, one's teacher's instruction, or one's own capacity to actually attain concentration or nirvāṇa, which saps the resolve needed to keep practicing at all. Only when the mind has genuinely worked through and set aside every one of these five — not suppressed but actually pacified — does its continuity on the object count as truly "blameless," and only then is one-pointedness of mind, in the treatise's exact sense, complete.

27. Two Kinds of One-Pointedness: Calm-Abiding and Insight

Chinese Source

復次，如是心一境性，或是奢摩他品，或是毗鉢舍那品。若於九種心住中心一境性，是名奢摩他品；若於四種慧行中心一境性，是名毗鉢舍那品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, this one-pointedness of mind is either of the calm-abiding type or of the insight type. If it is one-pointedness of mind within the nine stages of mental abiding, this is called the calm-abiding type. If it is one-pointedness of mind within the four kinds of wisdom-conduct, this is called the insight type.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces the two tracks that all subsequent concentration practice will fall under: the nine stages of mental abiding (inward abiding, continuous abiding, patched-together abiding, close abiding, taming, pacifying, thoroughly pacifying, single-pointed continuity, and effortless equipoise) build calm-abiding, while the four kinds of wisdom-conduct (correct discernment, utmost discernment, thorough investigation, and thorough examination) build insight — the same structure previewed already in fascicle 30's own opening summary verse. He notes both tracks can converge on the very same one-pointedness of mind, at which point calm-abiding and insight are said to operate "in tandem" — a level of practice generally regarded as already quite advanced. With this, the treatise has fully defined what one-pointedness of mind actually is, before turning next to detail the nine stages of mental abiding themselves.

28. Milestone: The Nine Stages of Mental Abiding Named

The treatise now names, in order from shallowest to deepest, the nine stages by which the mind is gradually brought to rest on its object — the backbone of all calm-abiding practice detailed for the rest of this locus.

Chinese Source

云何名為九種心住？謂有苾芻，令心內住、等住、安住、近住、調順、寂靜、最極寂靜、專注一趣，及以等持，如是名為九種心住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called the nine stages of mental abiding? That is, a monk causes the mind: to abide inwardly, to abide continuously, to abide by patching together, to abide closely, to become tamed, to become pacified, to become thoroughly pacified, to attain single-pointed continuity, and to attain equipoise. These are called the nine stages of mental abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the whole arc before detailing each stage: inward abiding first drags the mind back from its habitual outward chase after sense-objects; continuous and patched-together abiding gradually smooth that captured attention into something less coarse; close abiding lets the practitioner catch a wandering thought before it fully strays; taming and pacifying work through, respectively, the ten external distracting marks and the internal hindrances and discursive thoughts that would otherwise pull attention away; thorough pacification refines this further, refusing to tolerate even a momentary lapse; single-pointed continuity sustains unbroken concentration through deliberate, sustained effort; and equipoise, the ninth and final stage, is where that same continuity finally becomes effortless and spontaneous — the threshold of actual absorption. Cháng Bǎi recommends memorizing the nine terms in sequence, since they will recur constantly through the rest of the treatise's discussion of concentration practice.

29. Inward Abiding: The Minister and the Bowl of Oil

Chinese Source

云何內住？謂從外一切所緣境界攝錄其心，繫在於內，令不散亂。此則最初繫縛其心，令住於內，不外散亂，故名內住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is inward abiding? That is, gathering the mind back from every external object and binding it inward, so that it does not become distracted. This is the very first binding of the mind, causing it to abide inwardly and not scatter outward — hence it is called inward abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates inward abiding with the classical story of a king testing a candidate minister's focus: told to carry a bowl brimful of oil through the busy streets without spilling a drop, on pain of execution, the candidate walked on utterly unmoved — deaf to his own worried parents calling out to him, oblivious to the famously beautiful woman whose appearance sent the whole city running to look, unmoved even as a rampaging drunken elephant sent bystanders fleeing and later as bandits passed by, his attention never once leaving the bowl. He completed the walk and won the post precisely because his mind never once left its object. Cháng Bǎi notes that beginners typically manage only a second or two of comparable focus before the mind wanders back outward — normal at this earliest stage, where the whole task is simply learning, again and again, to draw a habitually outward-roaming mind back inside.

30. Continuous Abiding and Patched-Together Abiding

Chinese Source

云何等住？謂即最初所繫縛心，其性麤動，未能令其等住、遍住故。次即於此所緣境界，以相續方便、澄淨方便，挫令微細，遍攝令住，故名等住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is continuous abiding? That is, the mind as first bound is still coarse and agitated by nature, not yet able to abide continuously or pervasively. Next, upon this same object, through the means of sustained continuity and the means of purification, one refines it toward subtlety and gathers it pervasively into abiding — hence it is called continuous abiding.

Chinese Source

云何安住？謂若此心雖復如是內住、等住，然由失念於外散亂，復還攝錄安置內境，故名安住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is patched-together abiding? That is, although the mind has thus achieved inward abiding and continuous abiding, still, through a lapse of mindfulness, it strays outward — and is then gathered back once more and settled upon the inner object. Hence it is called patched-together abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains continuous abiding as the point where the practitioner starts to hold the object across successive moments rather than just an instant — the way one breath-count matches the next in stability — accomplished by two working methods: sustaining effort without interruption, and purifying the mind of the coarse discursive thoughts and hindrances that would otherwise break the thread. Patched-together abiding, by contrast, openly acknowledges relapse as simply part of the process: mindfulness lapses, attention drifts outward, and the practitioner notices and draws it back again — repeated as many times as needed, each return itself another small act of practice, until this coming-and-going settles into genuine stability.

31. Close Abiding

Chinese Source

云何近住？謂彼先應如是如是親近念住，由此念故，數數作意內住其心，不令此心遠住於外，故名近住。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is close abiding? That is, having first drawn close to mindfulness again and again in this way, through that mindfulness one repeatedly directs attention to hold the mind inward, not allowing it to stray far outward. Hence it is called close abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Compendium of Determinations*' three marks of genuine mindfulness — guarding attention, keeping it free of defiling contact with its object, and settling it firmly on that object — as what "close abiding" now brings to maturity: not merely catching a wandered mind after the fact (as in the previous stage) but anticipating the drift before it happens, the way a wandering thought is sensed rising and is headed off before it fully forms. He also notes an alternate ninefold scheme from the *Sūtrālaṃkāra* using different names for the same progression, and reads "closeness" itself through two supporting practices: wisdom born of hearing (repeated study of the teaching until its essential point is grasped) and the mental engagement of learning the object's mark (knowing precisely how the chosen object — counting the breath in and out, for instance — is meant to be tracked).

32. Milestone: Taming — the Ten Distracting Marks, and the Boy Who Learned to Think Ahead

The fifth stage, taming, requires the practitioner to actively work against the ten external marks capable of pulling attention away — accomplished by deliberately reflecting on their danger until that reflection gains real force.

Chinese Source

云何調順？謂種種相令心散亂，所謂色、聲、香、味、觸相，及貪、瞋、癡、男、女等相，故彼先應取彼諸相為過患想。由如是想增上力故，於彼諸相折挫其心，不令流散，故名調順。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is taming? That is, various marks cause the mind to become distracted — namely, the marks of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, and the marks of craving, hatred, delusion, male, and female. One should therefore first take these marks as objects of the perception of their danger. Through the empowering force of this perception, one curbs the mind with respect to these marks and does not allow it to stray — hence it is called taming.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the power of genuinely internalized "perception of danger" with a story of a not-especially-clever boy whose brilliant but disabled friend could always solve any problem that arose. When the friend fell fatally ill, the despairing boy begged to know the secret of his cleverness — and was told: think everything through in advance, the way a good cook mentally rehearses an entire meal's preparation, portion sizes, and contingencies before ever entering the kitchen, so that when the moment comes, the response is already fluent. Cháng Bǎi applies the same logic to the mind's habitual chase after sense-objects: an untrained mind, when a tempting or troubling image (color, sound, desire, aversion, delusion, an attractive or repulsive person) arises, simply follows it out of sheer habit, the way beginningless saṃsāra's cycle of confusion-karma-suffering has always worked. But a mind that has genuinely rehearsed the danger of such distraction in advance reacts to its arising the way a hand recoils from fire — instantly, without deliberation — snapping straight back to the meditation object. He tells a companion story of a novice monk whose covetous daydream about inheriting his uncle's fine cloth spiraled, in his imagination, all the way through disrobing, marriage, children, and a fatal accident to an imagined child — at which point, still lost in the fantasy, he struck his real uncle (an arhat with mind-reading power) across the head with the very fan he was using to cool him, only to be gently told, "hit your wife, not me." Cháng Bǎi draws the lesson that idle, habitual thought is never as harmless as it feels — and that different temperaments need different remedies: those whose minds run quiet may simply return again and again to a single object like the breath, while those whose minds run busy may need to work through such reflections on danger before returning to stillness at all.

33. Pacifying and Thoroughly Pacifying: From the Ten Marks to the Subtler Hindrances

Chinese Source

云何寂靜？謂有種種欲恚害等諸惡尋思，貪欲蓋等諸隨煩惱，令心擾動，故彼先應取彼諸法為過患想。由如是想增上力故，於諸尋思及隨煩惱，止息其心，不令流散，故名寂靜。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is pacifying? That is, various unwholesome discursive thoughts — of desire, ill will, harm, and the rest — and various secondary afflictions such as the hindrance of craving, cause the mind to become agitated. One should therefore first take these states as objects of the perception of their danger. Through the empowering force of this perception, one calms the mind with respect to these discursive thoughts and secondary afflictions and does not allow it to stray — hence it is called pacifying.

Chinese Source

云何名為最極寂靜？謂失念故，即彼二種暫現行時，隨所生起諸惡尋思及隨煩惱，能不忍受，尋即斷滅、除遣、變吐，是故名為最極寂靜。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called thorough pacification? That is, owing to a lapse of mindfulness, when these two kinds — discursive thoughts and secondary afflictions — momentarily manifest, one cannot bear to tolerate whatever arises, and immediately cuts it off, dispels it, and expels it entirely. Hence it is called thorough pacification.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the fifth and sixth stages by what they target: taming works against the ten coarse marks perceived directly through the five physical senses, while pacifying works against subtler distractions generated entirely within the sixth consciousness — the eight kinds of unwholesome discursive thought (of desire, ill will, harm, homeland, relatives, denying death's nearness, powerful patrons, and contempt for others) together with the five hindrances already detailed earlier in this locus. Both stages rely on the same tool, the deliberately cultivated "perception of danger." Thorough pacification, the seventh stage, raises the bar further still: where pacifying is content to notice and calm a disturbance once it has already arisen, thorough pacification refuses to tolerate even the briefest lapse, catching and expelling a stray thought or hindrance the instant it appears, before it can gain any foothold at all — Cháng Bǎi likens this to a hand's reflexive recoil from fire, now applied at the very threshold of a thought's arising rather than after the fact.

34. Milestone: Single-Pointed Continuity and Equipoise — the Nine Stages Complete

The eighth and ninth stages close the sequence: sustained, effortful continuity finally gives way to a fully effortless, spontaneous settling upon the object — the threshold of genuine meditative absorption.

Chinese Source

云何名為專注一趣？謂有加行、有功用，無闕無間三摩地相續而住，是故名為專注一趣。云何等持？謂數修、數習、數多修習為因緣故，得無加行、無功用任運轉道。由是因緣，不由加行、不由功用，心三摩地任運相續，無散亂轉，故名等持。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called single-pointed continuity? That is, with effort and application, samādhi continues to abide without lapse and without interruption — hence it is called single-pointed continuity. What is equipoise? That is, through the causes and conditions of repeated cultivation, repeated practice, and extensive repeated practice, one attains a path that proceeds spontaneously, without effort and without application. Owing to this, without effort and without application, the mind's samādhi continues spontaneously, proceeding without distraction — hence it is called equipoise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eighth stage as the last point at which real, sustained exertion is still required: the ten marks, eight discursive thoughts, and five hindrances have all been mastered, but the mind still needs deliberate effort to remain unbroken on its object. The ninth and final stage, equipoise, is where that same continuity finally becomes self-sustaining — proceeding "spontaneously," requiring no further application at all, the object simply remaining present without the mind ever needing to be pulled back to it. He stresses this maturity is never simply given: it accrues strictly through "repeated cultivation, repeated practice, and extensive repeated practice" — a practitioner with strong dispositions carried over from past lives may reach it quickly, but for most, only years of sustained, unglamorous repetition get there, precisely illustrating that samādhi, like everything else in the treatise, is squarely a matter of cause and effect, never granted for free. With equipoise attained, the mind stands at the very threshold of the "near-attainment concentration" that precedes the first dhyāna proper, pliancy already beginning to appear — and with this, the nine stages of mental abiding, the complete architecture of calm-abiding practice, are fully named and explained.
