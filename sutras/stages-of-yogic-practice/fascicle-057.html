Cow Dung, Ganges Water, and What Purity Actually Means

The treatise opens its fifth kind of skillful understanding — the valid and the invalid, examining the very same cause-and-effect principle behind dependent origination from a fresh angle — beginning with the householder Cunda's question about ritual bathing, and the Buddha's own answer about what genuinely makes a person pure.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 57 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Cunda's Question About Ritual Purity, and the Fifth Skillful Understanding Opens

Before opening this fascicle's own topic, the lecture recalls a story about the householder Cunda, who once came before the Buddha and was asked which kind of ascetic or brahmin he found most admirable. Cunda described a group who served and made offerings to water itself, worshipping a certain water-deity, always carrying a staff and a water-jar, believing that washing the hands made a person pure, or that bathing in the Ganges washed away impurity; on the fifteenth day of each month, such practitioners would rise early to wash their hair with sesame husks and a certain fruit's rind, fast for the day, drape themselves in white cloth over their freshly washed hair, smear the ground with sacred cow dung, kneel at dawn holding fresh grass, touch the earth, and declare to the sky that they were as pure as the earth itself — anyone able to perform this whole sequence being considered pure, anyone unable to being considered impure. Cunda asked the Buddha whether such practitioners were, in fact, genuinely pure, since he wished to make offerings only to the truly pure. The Buddha answered plainly that this was not what purity actually meant: purity or impurity is decided by whether a person performs the ten wholesome deeds or the ten unwholesome ones — a mind full of greed, hatred, and delusion, given to killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, remains impure no matter how much cow dung or fresh grass is involved, and yields exactly the dark result such deeds produce, while a person who performs the ten wholesome deeds remains genuinely pure whether or not any hair-washing or earth-touching ritual was ever performed at all. The Buddha's own answer to Cunda, the lecture notes, is itself a perfect illustration of what this fascicle's own new topic — the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid — is actually about: examining, with real precision, which claimed causes truly produce which results, and which do not, so that no one mistakes an invalid cause for a valid one, or a valid result for one it could never actually produce. With this story as introduction, the treatise formally opens its fifth kind of skillful understanding, following immediately upon the fourth: having now finished explaining the skillful understanding of dependent origination, it will next explain the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid — offering first a summary verse promising to cover, in order, this topic's own essential nature, its manifestation, the reason for its being taught, and finally a fourfold set of distinctions.

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the skillful understanding of dependent origination has been explained. The skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid shall now be explained. The summary verse states: essential nature, manifestation, and the reason for its being taught come first; the fourfold gate of distinctions comes after.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Cunda's story as an unusually vivid opening for what could otherwise seem like a purely abstract logical topic: an elaborate purification ritual, complete with cow dung, fresh grass, and a declaration to the sky, set directly against the Buddha's own strikingly simple answer — purity is just the presence or absence of the ten wholesome deeds, nothing more elaborate required, and nothing more elaborate sufficient without it. He notes that this fifth kind of skillful understanding is not a wholly separate subject from the fourth, dependent origination, but rather the very same cause-and-effect principle viewed from a different angle — instead of tracing the twelve links forward, this topic examines directly which claimed causes genuinely lead to which results, calling a coherent, genuine cause-and-effect relationship "the valid" and an incoherent, false one "the invalid." He flags the summary verse's own promise — essential nature, then manifestation, then the reason for the teaching, then a fourfold set of distinctions — as the roadmap for this entire fascicle's opening topic, worth keeping in mind as each piece unfolds in turn.

2. Section: Defining "Valid" and "Invalid," and Why the Buddha Bothered Teaching the Difference

The treatise now defines its central terms with characteristic precision. What is "the valid"? It is whenever, for any given matter, there is no contradiction between the matter and its underlying principle — a cause producing exactly the kind of result it should, in accordance with the basic truth that such-and-such a cause yields such-and-such a result, without conflict between what actually happens and what genuinely should happen given real cause-and-effect reasoning. What, conversely, is "the invalid"? It is whenever, for any given matter, there is a real contradiction between the matter and its underlying principle — an outcome that clashes with, rather than follows from, the actual logic of cause and effect. This is named the essential nature of the valid and the invalid. The treatise then asks why the Buddha bothered to reveal this particular skillful understanding at all: it is precisely in order to demonstrate that correct wisdom regarding both defiled and pure phenomena, and the proper means of dealing with each, remains undamaged and unimpaired — defiled causes yielding defiled results, pure causes yielding pure results, exactly as they should, so that the Buddha's followers might hold onto correct reflection on cause and effect without ever losing it, rather than falling into confusion about which practices genuinely purify and which do not, as Cunda's own bathing-ritual practitioners had. The treatise then asks through how many approaches this entire topic of the valid and the invalid should be examined, and answers: four, since the Buddha taught the whole of the valid and the invalid using exactly four approaches — the gate of accomplishment, the gate of combination, the gate of attainment, and the gate of active manifestation — each addressing a different facet of how causes actually bring about their results, together comprehensive enough to cover every possible variety of both valid and invalid across all things whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the definitions of "valid" and "invalid" as deceptively simple — no contradiction with underlying principle, or a genuine contradiction with it — yet notes that the entire remainder of this topic exists precisely to work out what "underlying principle" really means across an enormous range of specific cases, from the workings of karma to the structure of the path itself. He connects this topic directly back to the lecture's own citation, in an earlier fascicle, of the Discourse Basis's treatment of the very same six kinds of skillful understanding used for meditative contemplation — this fifth kind, he notes, is what actually protects a practitioner from what the text calls "taking a non-cause for a cause, or a non-result for a result," precisely the mistake Cunda's own bathing-ritual practitioners had fallen into. He flags the fourfold gate — accomplishment, combination, attainment, active manifestation — as this fascicle's own organizing structure going forward, each gate offering its own distinct lens on the same underlying question of what genuinely causes what.

3. Section: Why the Eye Can Never Hear, and Why Earth Can Never Turn Into Fire

The treatise's detailed elaboration of the four gates opens with a general question — what exactly counts as "every distinguishing approach" — before working through the first gate, the gate of accomplishment, in careful detail. Whatever an effect can actually be brought to completion by is subsumed under this gate and called valid; whatever exceeds what a given cause could ever actually accomplish, and so fails to be subsumed under this gate, is called invalid. Three specific kinds of such overreach are named: the sense faculties exceeding what they can actually do, the four great elements exceeding what they can actually do, and life's own sustaining resources exceeding what they can actually do. Concerning the sense faculties, the treatise states plainly that there is no place, no time, no circumstance whatsoever in which the eye could ever hear sound, smell scent, taste flavor, or feel touch — only seeing form is ever valid for the eye, and the same reasoning extends, by analogy, to each of the other four faculties in turn, each faculty capable only of its own single kind of activity, never another's. The lecture pauses here to note a genuine scholastic debate about whether enlightened beings possess a special ability for their faculties to function interchangeably — some commentators holding that a realized meditator's six faculties lose all such restriction entirely, the eye becoming able to hear as freely as it sees; others holding instead that each faculty's own domain always surrounds itself with a halo of the other four faculties' own subtle sensitive matter, so that what looks like the eye hearing is really the ear's own faculty, present all along in that same location, quietly doing the hearing — with this treatise itself simply affirming, for ordinary purposes, that the eye sees and nothing more, leaving the more exotic case of the fully realized to more specialized discussions elsewhere. Concerning the four great elements, there is likewise no place or time in which earth could ever perform water's function of moistening, fire's function of warming, or wind's function of moving — earth can only ever perform earth's own function, and the same reasoning extends, by analogy, to water, fire, and wind themselves, each mutually incapable of producing what belongs properly only to another.

Ru-Yi Translation

Concerning the four great elements exceeding what they can do: there is no place, no circumstance, in which earth could perform the function of water, fire, or wind — this is never valid; that it performs earth's own function is valid. As with earth, so too, by analogy, should the mutual overreach of the remaining great elements be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the faculties-and-elements discussion as this fascicle's own gentle, almost playful way of building up to a serious point through the most obvious possible examples — of course the eye cannot hear, of course earth cannot burn like fire, the reader is meant to nod along easily before the same reasoning is extended to far less obvious cases later in the fascicle. He highlights the scholastic aside on interchangeable faculties as a small but genuine window into how differently even careful commentators can read the same tradition: one view granting the fully realized an almost magical sensory freedom, the other preserving each faculty's own strict boundary even for saints by quietly relocating the explanation to overlapping subtle matter instead — a disagreement this treatise itself declines to adjudicate, content to state the ordinary rule and move on. He notes that "valid" and "invalid" are being illustrated here in their most concrete, almost proverbial form — an eye that hears, an earth that burns — precisely so that the same underlying logic will be recognizable later when the treatise turns to far subtler claims about karma, liberation, and the nature of mind.

4. Section: Milking a Cow's Horn, Churning Butter from Water, and Five More Reasons Cause and Effect Cannot Be Faked

The treatise turns to its third category of overreach — life's own sustaining resources exceeding what they can actually accomplish — illustrated through five vivid, almost proverbial examples. First, concerning seeds and sprouts: there is no place or time in which a seed of one kind could ever produce a sprout of a different kind — a melon seed can never yield a bean sprout — only a seed's own kind ever produces a sprout of that same kind, precisely the everyday wisdom captured in the saying that melons are reaped from melon seeds and beans from bean seeds, cause and result never contradicting one another. Second, concerning the extraction of milk: there is no place or time in which squeezing a cow's horn could ever yield milk — only squeezing the udder itself is valid. Third, concerning the production of cream: there is no place or time in which churning a jar of plain water could ever yield cream — only churning yogurt, from which cream is properly derived, is valid. Fourth, concerning the extraction of oil: there is no place or time in which pressing sand could ever yield oil — only pressing sesame and similar oil-bearing seeds is valid. Fifth, concerning the production of fire: there is no place or time in which drilling into damp wood could ever produce fire — only drilling into dry wood is valid. All such examples, the treatise concludes, should be observed as illustrating this first gate's own distinguishing characteristic — the gate of accomplishment — through the ordinary stuff of daily life itself: sense faculties, elements, and life's own sustaining resources, each incapable of producing anything beyond its own proper function, however cleverly a skilled debater might try to argue otherwise. With this gate now fully illustrated, the treatise turns to the second gate, the gate of combination, asking what specifically distinguishes it, and answering first through the example of light and darkness: there is no place or time in which light and darkness could ever coexist together in a single location — wherever there is light, there is no darkness, and wherever there is darkness, there is no light, this mutual exclusion itself being what is valid — a principle the lecture extends to like-minded companions naturally gathering together, since those who share a habit of taking life tend to keep company with others of the same habit, exactly as those committed to the five precepts tend to keep company with each other in turn, genuinely opposed dispositions no more able to comfortably combine than light and darkness themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five household examples — seed and sprout, horn and udder, water and yogurt, sand and sesame, damp and dry wood — as a small masterclass in teaching through concrete, everyday observation rather than abstract argument, precisely the kind of illustration a farmer, a herder, or a cook would immediately recognize as true without needing any philosophy at all. He notes the light-and-darkness example opening the second gate as a natural bridge from physical impossibility to moral and social observation: just as two incompatible physical states cannot occupy the same place at the same time, incompatible dispositions — killing versus precept-keeping, for instance — tend not to comfortably combine within the same circle of friends either, birds of a feather gathering together in exactly the way this gate's own name, "combination," suggests. He flags this social extension as worth remembering going forward, since the treatise appears ready to move from purely physical examples toward increasingly psychological and karmic ones as this second gate continues to unfold.

5. Section: Closing the Combination Gate — Minds That Cannot Overlap, and Fortunes That Cannot Mix

The gate of combination continues through five further examples. Coarse water and coarse fire, the kind perceivable directly by eye, ear, or touch, can never coexist together in one place at one time — wherever there is water there is no fire, and wherever there is fire there is no water, this being the very same mutual incompatibility ordinary language points to when describing two people or two factions as utterly "water and fire" toward one another, unable to coexist at all. Two coarse material aggregates, similarly, can never occupy exactly the same location at the same time, since coarse matter is inherently obstructive — a table and a chair cannot both occupy precisely the same spot — though this restriction applies only to coarse aggregates and not to the subtlest particles themselves, which, unlike coarse matter, can genuinely interpenetrate other coarse matter without displacing it, exactly as the element earth's own subtlest particles can pervade water's own coarse matter without displacing it in the least. Two instances of the very same class of mind or mental factor — two eye-consciousnesses, say, sharing the same faculty and the same object — can never arise together at exactly the same moment, since mind, unlike matter, always requires its own immediately preceding moment to cease before the next moment of that same class can arise, one mental moment strictly following another rather than ever truly coexisting alongside it. Two instances of the same class of wholesome and unwholesome mental states, or wholesome and neutral, or unwholesome and neutral, or painful and pleasant feeling, likewise can never coexist within a single moment, since their very natures are mutually exclusive — only one such quality is ever actually present in the mind at any given instant. And desired and undesired karmic results — birth as a human or a god as against birth as a hungry ghost, an animal, or a hell-being — can likewise never coexist within one and the same life: whichever result a being actually experiences, it is always exactly one of these, never some blend of both at once. All such cases, the treatise concludes, illustrate this second gate's own distinguishing characteristic, closing the gate of combination entirely.

There is no place, no circumstance, in which two mental phenomena of the same class could combine together at the same moment — this is not valid; that they arise one at a time is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which wholesome and unwholesome, wholesome and neutral, unwholesome and neutral, or painful and pleasant states of the same class could combine together — this is not valid; that only one such state is present is valid. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the distinction drawn here between coarse matter and the subtlest particles as a genuinely subtle piece of Abhidharma physics folded into an otherwise everyday-sounding discussion: two ordinary objects cannot share the same space, yet the very finest particles of one element can pervade another's coarse matter without displacing it, exactly the reasoning this treatise uses elsewhere to explain how the four elements can be present together within a single grain of matter. He notes the mind-moment example as a compact restatement of a principle this fascicle has relied on before — mental phenomena, unlike physical ones, always arise one at a time in strict succession, never truly simultaneously, a subtlety worth remembering since it will matter again whenever this treatise discusses the pace and structure of meditative experience. He treats the desired/undesired-result example as this gate's own quiet reminder that a single life's overall karmic result, however mixed its individual moments may feel, is always fundamentally one thing or the other at its root — never some genuine blend of a fortunate and an unfortunate rebirth occurring at once.

6. Section: Why a Barren Woman Cannot Bear Children, and Why Liberation Cannot Be Attained Without the Proper Provisions

The treatise turns to its third gate, the gate of attainment, working through a further set of examples. There is no place or time in which a woman whose reproductive faculty is destroyed could ever bear a child — only a woman without such an impairment can, this being valid. There is no place or time in which a person born without proper male faculties could ever father a child — only a properly constituted man can, this being valid. There is no place or time in which a blind person could see form, a deaf person could hear sound, or someone with a destroyed sense of smell or taste could smell or taste — only someone whose faculties remain intact can do these things, this being valid. There is no place or time in which someone lacking the proper provisions — genuine learning, reflection, and meditative cultivation, and the corresponding development of ethical discipline, concentration, and wisdom — could, in this very life, attain the liberation of a trainee or an adept beyond training; only someone who has genuinely assembled these provisions can attain such liberation, this being valid. And there is no place or time in which someone who has not yet attained the noble path could realize nirvāṇa, or the awakening of a hearer, a solitary realizer, or unsurpassed perfect awakening itself; only someone who has genuinely attained the noble path can realize any of these, this being valid.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this third gate shifts the discussion's register from purely physical impossibility toward something closer to the spiritual path itself: a barren woman and a damaged eye are physical facts, but "lacking the proper provisions" and "not having attained the noble path" describe a practitioner's own spiritual condition, the treatise treating both kinds of impossibility with exactly the same rigorous logic. He highlights the provisions example — genuine learning, reflection, cultivation, and the corresponding ethical discipline, concentration, and wisdom — as a quiet but firm rebuke of any shortcut mentality in spiritual practice: liberation is not granted arbitrarily or by wishing, but strictly follows from having actually done the necessary preparatory work, exactly as a barren woman cannot simply wish a child into being. He flags that this same gate of attainment is about to turn to still more examples — concerning food, realms of rebirth, and the body itself — extending this same principle into territory that will include one of this fascicle's own most affecting stories.

7. Section: King Bimbisāra's Fatal Craving for Heavenly Food, and Closing the Attainment Gate

The gate of attainment continues with a sixth example concerning realms and their proper food: there is no place or time in which a human being could sustain and nourish the body by eating grass and the like, the proper food of animals; nor in which the heavenly gods could eat human food; nor in which beings of the form and formless realms could take solid food at all — a human eating an animal's food, or a god eating a human's, being no more fitting than a person eating what a dog eats, since each realm's own food is calibrated to its own being, the higher realms sustained instead by the very joy of meditative absorption, having long since abandoned scent, taste, and the corresponding faculties of nose and tongue altogether. The lecture illustrates this with a story from the Buddha's own time: King Bimbisāra, imprisoned by his own son Ajātaśatru and left to starve, wondered silently why the Buddha did not come to rescue him; the Buddha, perceiving this thought, sent Maudgalyāyana by supernatural power to explain that he had already done everything within his power to help, and that what remained was Bimbisāra's own karma to bear, which Maudgalyāyana should now help him accept through teaching. Ravenously hungry, Bimbisāra asked Maudgalyāyana which realm's food was most delicious, and Maudgalyāyana described in turn what the Four Heavenly Kings ate, what Śakra's own retinue ate, and what beings throughout the six heavens of the desire realm ate — but Bimbisāra, consumed by longing for that very food, could take in none of the genuine teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness that followed, fixating instead only on the description of the especially fragrant food enjoyed in Vaiśravaṇa's own heavenly realm. Though his accumulated wholesome karma should have carried him to rebirth in Tuṣita heaven, his overwhelming craving for that one remembered food, combined with an intermediate-state being's own nature of feeding on scent alone, drew him instead toward the fragrance he could not stop craving, and he was reborn in that lower heaven instead of the higher one his karma had actually earned — a sobering illustration, the lecture notes, of how a mind's final overwhelming craving at the moment of death can redirect even karma that had already, in principle, secured a better destination. The gate of attainment closes with a seventh and final example: there is no place or time in which a being could obtain a human body without first relinquishing whatever body-form it currently holds — a hell-being, for instance, cannot become human while still bound to its hellish form, and must first exhaust that karmic term and be released from it before any new body can be obtained — this relinquishing-before-obtaining principle applying, by analogy, to every possible transition between one body-form and another.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats King Bimbisāra's story as one of this fascicle's most quietly devastating illustrations, precisely because nothing supernatural or villainous causes his misfortune — only his own uncontrolled craving, arising at the single worst possible moment, redirects karma that had already, by his own genuine goodness, secured him a better rebirth. He draws the practical lesson explicitly: a dying mind's final, overwhelming preoccupation carries enormous weight in determining what comes next, which is exactly why traditional guidance for the dying emphasizes keeping the mind fixed on wholesome objects, rather than allowing hunger, pain, or unfulfilled longing to seize the very last moments of attention. He notes the closing point about relinquishing one body before obtaining another as a small but important corrective to any sentimental picture of rebirth as a smooth, gradual drift from one life to the next — a hell-being's own karmic term must run its full course, with genuine finality, before anything new can begin, exactly as no new body can be obtained while still bound to an old one.

8. Section: The Fourth Gate Opens — Elements That Never Betray Their Own Nature, and Why a Greedy Mind Cannot Feel Free

The treatise turns to its fourth and final gate, the gate of active manifestation, working through it with a fresh set of examples grounded in how things actually behave when active. There is no place or time in which earth could relinquish its own characteristic of solidity and take on the characteristic of another element instead — earth stays hard, water stays moist, fire stays warm, and wind stays mobile, each element invariably holding to its own characteristic rather than ever surrendering it, this being valid, and the same reasoning extends, by analogy, to water, fire, and wind in turn. There is no place or time in which someone born in the desire realm, without having attained the heavenly eye, could nonetheless see the forms belonging to the heavens — an ordinary person's eye sees only what belongs to the human realm, this being valid, and the same reasoning extends to the other faculties: without the heavenly ear, heavenly sounds likewise cannot be heard. There is no place or time in which someone whose mind remains covered by greed, whose greed has not yet been severed, could nonetheless feel genuinely free of attachment toward wealth and material gain — only someone free of such covering greed, or who has already severed it, can feel that genuine freedom, this being valid, and the very same reasoning extends to hatred and delusion: wherever the corresponding affliction still covers the mind and remains unsevered, the corresponding freedom from anger or confusion, when the triggering circumstance actually arises, simply cannot occur.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourth gate's opening examples as circling back, quite deliberately, to territory the very first gate already covered — elements holding to their own nature, faculties limited to their own proper domain — but now examined specifically through the lens of active manifestation rather than mere capacity, a difference he characterizes as the difference between what something could in principle do and what it is actually doing right now. He highlights the greed example as the fascicle's own transition point from physical and perceptual impossibility toward a directly practical, psychological application: an affliction that has not actually been severed does not simply lie dormant and harmless, but actively covers the mind and actively prevents the corresponding freedom, whatever a person might sincerely wish or claim about their own inner state. He notes that this same reasoning is about to be extended even further, into the specific conditions required for genuinely practicing the path itself — a topic that will lead, a little further on, into one of this fascicle's richest discussions, on what it actually means to see the Buddha.

9. Section: No Genuine Meditation Without First Subduing Affliction, and What It Truly Means to See the Buddha

The fourth gate continues: there is no place or time in which someone could genuinely cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness while still entangled by the active manifestation of greed and every other root and secondary affliction — only once such afflictions have been suppressed, or their arising otherwise prevented, can this cultivation genuinely proceed, this being valid, and the very same principle extends, by analogy, to every other factor belonging to enlightenment, from the four right efforts through the noble eightfold path itself: none of these can genuinely be cultivated while affliction remains actively manifest, precisely because such cultivation itself functions as the means by which afflictions come to be severed in the first place. A further example turns directly to what it means to truly see the Buddha: there is no place or time in which someone who has not relinquished contentious views, contentious desires, and a mind still given to contention — none of which the Buddha ever sanctions — could nonetheless genuinely behold the Buddha face to face; only by relinquishing these, or by the specific manner of seeing the Buddha that the Buddha himself does sanction, is such genuine seeing possible. The lecture explains that contention arises from three sources: clinging to particular views and doctrines, in dispute with those who hold different ones; craving directed at particular objects, disputed with those who would also possess them; and an already-defiled mind acting out through bodily, verbal, or mental misconduct provoked by resentment at being slighted — and that anyone still caught up in these three, even a nominal disciple sitting at the Buddha's own feet, cannot be said to have genuinely realized dependent origination's own true nature, and hence cannot be said to have genuinely seen the Buddha at all, exactly as both the early scriptures and the Mahāyāna alike affirm: to see dependent origination is to see the Dharma, and to see the Dharma is to see the Buddha — never a matter of merely observing his physical form or bodily sound.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the bodhipakṣa-dharma example as a precise, practical warning against a common misunderstanding: meditation is not a technique that works regardless of one's underlying mental state, but something that itself requires affliction to already be held in check before it can function at all — trying to cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness while greed or anger is actively manifest is, on this treatise's own logic, simply not possible, however much effort is exerted. He treats the three sources of contention — view, desire, and an already-defiled mind's reactive misconduct — as worth distinguishing carefully in one's own experience, since each calls for a slightly different remedy: settling a disputed opinion is not the same task as releasing a competitive craving, which is again not the same task as simply cooling down a provoked temper. He flags the closing citation — to see dependent origination is to see the Dharma, and to see the Dharma is to see the Buddha — as the very same maxim this fascicle's own predecessor invoked when first introducing dependent origination, now returning here to anchor an entirely different, and unusually rich, discussion about what "seeing the Buddha" genuinely requires.

10. Section: Five Verses on Seeing the Buddha, and a Gas-Station Attendant Who Had Never Seen a Monk

The lecture unpacks the "seeing the Buddha" discussion further by citing the Diamond Sūtra's own famous warning: "If one seeks to see me through physical form, or to seek me through sound, that person walks a wrong path and cannot see the Tathāgata" — mistaking the Buddha's thirty-two excellent physical marks, or his sixty kinds of beautiful voice, for the Buddha himself, rather than for the genuine liberating wisdom those marks merely accompany. Five verses from this treatise's own earlier discussion of wisdom gained through reflection unpack exactly what kind of person can, and cannot, be said to genuinely see the Buddha. The first describes an ordinary person still gripped by craving, who sees the Buddha's physical marks and concludes, correctly enough, "this must be a buddha" — yet remains someone who, the moment an unfulfilled craving or a persuasive false teacher arrives, can still be led away into wrong views or even into slandering the Three Jewels, since recognizing the Buddha's outward form is not the same as having genuinely realized anything at all. The second describes a meditator, even one trained in non-Buddhist traditions, who through the heavenly eye perceives the Buddha inwardly from a great distance and thinks, "this is the Buddha" — yet, lacking any actual grounding in genuine teaching, remains just as susceptible to being swayed by a more persuasive outside doctrine, meditative attainment alone proving no more reliable a foundation than ordinary craving-driven recognition. The third describes someone with no meditative attainment at all, who nonetheless sees the Buddha's physical body with ordinary eyes and recognizes him as the Buddha, yet who, lacking any inner realization to anchor that recognition, remains equally susceptible to later being swayed away by outside voices. The fourth describes someone blocked by especially heavy karmic obstruction, who has neither the inner meditative perception nor even the outer physical perception to recognize the Buddha at all — the most impoverished of the four failed conditions. The lecture pauses here to note how genuinely rare even the ordinary "outer seeing" described in the third verse can be, recalling a story of a young attendant at a gas station who, when a customer casually referred to the monastic beside her as "miss," admitted she had simply never seen a Buddhist monk before in her entire life — a small but telling illustration that mere physical exposure to genuine teaching cannot be taken for granted even now, let alone the far deeper kind of seeing these verses are actually concerned with.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five verses as an unusually precise diagnostic tool, distinguishing four separate ways that seeing the Buddha's own outward or inward form can fail to constitute genuinely "seeing" him at all — craving-driven recognition, ungrounded meditative perception, ordinary sightless-of-anything-deeper recognition, and total obstruction — each one sharing the same underlying vulnerability of being swayable by a sufficiently persuasive outside voice, since none of them rest on any actual realized wisdom of their own. He treats the gas-station story as a small, almost humorous corrective against complacency: it is easy to assume that recognizing a Buddhist monk on sight is the most basic possible level of religious literacy, yet even this ordinary threshold cannot be taken for granted in every place and every generation, however commonplace it may feel to a longtime practitioner. He notes that only the fifth verse's own "hero" — someone with both genuine inward realization and outward perception together — is described as truly unswayable, setting up the fascicle's next story as an illustration of exactly this kind of unshakeable resolve.

11. Section: The Dying Monk Who Asked to Be Carried Outside, and Reached Arhatship Before He Died

The lecture illustrates the fifth verse's own genuine "hero" with a story from the Buddha's own time. A monk of unshakeable faith in the Three Jewels, though he had not yet attained any of the noble fruits, fell gravely ill; rather than continue suffering without being able to practice, he asked his attendants to carry him outside, intending to end his own life, insisting all the while that his faith in the Dharma would never waver regardless. Two devas, observing this, discussed his situation — one lamenting that his suffering had driven him to attempt suicide, the other predicting that he would in fact attain the noble path — and reported what they had seen to the Buddha; when the Buddha's own disciples asked whether this was really so, the Buddha confirmed it and sent them to see for themselves. As he was carried outward, the dying monk poured his full remaining strength into contemplating the Buddha's own teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, and in that concentrated effort swiftly passed through the first, second, third, and fourth fruits in immediate succession, attaining arhatship, and died an arhat shortly afterward. When the disciples returned and asked the Buddha why he had not intervened to stop the suicide, the Buddha explained his own actual criterion plainly: he intervenes to stop such an act, and teaches the person the Dharma instead, whenever that person has no real hope of attaining arhatship and would otherwise simply carry unresolved karma into a further round of suffering — but when someone genuinely has the capacity to attain nirvāṇa in that very effort, there is no need to intervene at all, since that person already possesses what is needed to achieve liberation. The lecture is careful to note that this is not, in any way, an endorsement of suicide as a spiritual method — the monk's own extraordinary outcome depended entirely on a rare, unwavering faith already cultivated to something like the threshold of genuine sagehood, sharpened rather than shaken by the direct confrontation with his own body's imminent end, a combination the Buddha's own careful discernment alone could judge, and not a template for anyone to imitate.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own explicit criterion for when he intervenes — no real hope of arhatship, versus a genuine and immediate capacity for it — as the story's own single most important safeguard against misreading it as some kind of endorsement: the outcome depended entirely on a discernment only the Buddha himself possessed at that moment, not on any general principle that ending one's life in the grip of illness reliably leads to liberation. He notes that the monk's own faith, described as already unwavering before his illness ever struck, functioned here as exactly the reservoir of practice the fifth verse's "hero" draws on, sharpened rather than shattered by the extremity of his circumstance — a reminder that unshakeable faith is not a feeling cultivated in comfort, but something proven precisely under the hardest possible pressure. He closes by connecting this back to the treatise's own twofold summary of what "seeing the Buddha" ultimately means: ultimate seeing, through a noble wisdom-eye earned by genuinely transcending craving, and conventional seeing, through an ordinary eye that may or may not be reliable — the dying monk's own final moments being, in effect, his passage from the second kind of seeing into the first.

12. Section: Why the Buddha Never Loses Track of a Single Being, and Bodhisattvas Who Never Turn Back

The fourth gate closes with two final examples. Concerning omniscience: there is no place or time in which an all-knowing, all-seeing buddha could fail to fully comprehend some object within the scope of what can be known, or could ever lose mindfulness and act as a lesser, non-omniscient being would — a buddha's perfect, all-pervading knowledge leaves nothing whatsoever unknown, and the three unguarded conducts of body, speech, and mind mean a buddha never needs deliberate vigilance to remain spontaneously, effortlessly in accord with the Dharma, this being valid. The lecture cites the Lotus Sūtra's own words here: the Buddha always knows which beings are practicing the path and which are not, and teaches accordingly whatever each being's own capacity can actually receive, forever asking himself how to bring beings to supreme wisdom and swift buddhahood — a promise, the lecture notes, that should reassure any practitioner with the necessary provisions genuinely assembled that the Buddha's help remains available regardless of how things may outwardly appear, the only real question being whether one's own preparation is complete. Concerning bodhisattvas who have entered the ground levels of the path: there is no place or time in which such a bodhisattva, whose accumulated merit already spans everywhere like the very earth itself, could deliberately intend harm toward any being, or could ever regress from the aspiration for supreme awakening once genuinely attained at that level — the opposite alone being valid. With these two final examples, the fourth gate, active manifestation, closes, and with it the entire fourfold gate through which the valid and the invalid have now been examined from every angle: accomplishment, combination, attainment, and active manifestation together.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Lotus Sūtra citation here as directly reassuring in a way this dense, logic-heavy fascicle rarely allows itself to be: the Buddha's own constant attention to every practicing being is not a poetic flourish but stated here as a matter of strict logical necessity, following directly from what omniscience itself actually means — a buddha simply cannot fail to notice a genuinely prepared student, any more than water can fail to be moist. He notes the irreversible bodhisattva as this fourth gate's own fitting capstone: having worked through faculties, elements, afflictions, and ordinary human limitation, the gate closes by affirming that the very highest attainments on the path are, once genuinely reached, every bit as reliable and unshakeable as an eye's incapacity to hear or an earth's incapacity to burn — the same rigorous logic of valid and invalid applying at both the humblest and the loftiest ends of the entire spectrum of possibility.

13. Section: Eight Ways to Examine the Valid and Invalid, and What Truly Separates This Topic from Dependent Origination

The treatise now offers a second, more compact way of organizing this entire topic: in brief, there are four further kinds of valid and four further kinds of invalid, corresponding to four principles named earlier in this very treatise's own opening discussion — the principle of relativity, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of intrinsic nature. The principle of relativity observes that the aggregates arise only relative to their own causes and conditions, and are only ever designated relative to name, phrase, and syllable. The principle of function observes that each thing has its own specific function — the eye sees form, the ear hears sound, and so on, each faculty confined to what it alone can do. The principle of proof observes that direct perception, inference, and reliable testimony together establish the truth of the Buddha's own teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, both mundane and transcendent. And the principle of intrinsic nature observes simply that things are, at bottom, just however they inherently are — earth's nature is solidity, water's is moisture, fire's is warmth, wind's is movement — a nature not admitting of any further "why," since pursuing the question past this point serves no purpose. Whatever is taught in a manner not contradicting these four principles is called valid; whatever contradicts them is called invalid. All told, these four principle-based distinctions, added to the four gates already examined, together make eight distinct approaches to the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid. The treatise then poses a final clarifying question: what exactly distinguishes this topic from dependent origination, given how closely related the two clearly are? The answer: dependent origination's skillful understanding lies specifically in the noble wisdom that clearly reveals how cause and effect actually arise — the twelve links, ignorance through old age and death, in their own specific unfolding — while the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid lies in the noble wisdom that clearly reveals, without any distortion, whether any given thing accords with proper principle or not, across every phenomenon whatsoever, not only the twelve links themselves. With this distinction drawn, the treatise notes that everything else belonging to this topic has already been said, and closes it here, turning next to the sixth and final kind of skillful understanding: the faculties.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing Q&A as this entire topic's own clearest self-summary: dependent origination examines specifically how cause and effect arise through the twelve links, while the valid and the invalid examines whether anything whatsoever — not only the twelve links — accords with proper principle, making this fifth skillful understanding strictly broader in scope than the fourth, even though both ultimately rest on the very same underlying logic of cause and effect. He notes the four principles — relativity, function, proof, and intrinsic nature — as a set worth remembering well beyond this fascicle, since this treatise's own opening discussion first introduced them as the basic toolkit for examining any claim whatsoever, here simply reapplied to organize this specific topic's own eightfold structure. With 處非處善巧 (the skillful understanding of the valid and invalid) now closed, he flags that this fascicle is about to turn to the sixth and final of the six kinds of skillful understanding named at the very opening of this whole discussion many fascicles ago — the faculties — completing, at last, the full set: aggregates, elements, sense-fields, dependent origination, the valid and invalid, and now the faculties.

14. Section: From One Faculty to a Hundred and Twenty — Why the Buddha Settled on Exactly Twenty-Two

Before opening the treatise's sixth and final kind of skillful understanding, the lecture surveys the bewildering variety of claims other Indian traditions made about how many "faculties," or roots, actually exist. The Nirgrantha ascetics posited a single life-faculty pervading everything, internal and external alike, so thoroughly that their followers were forbidden from drinking cold water or cutting fresh grass, since both were believed to possess this same life-faculty. Other schools posited an external mind-faculty, or an external karma-faculty, attributing genuine sentience to things ordinary people would never think to consider alive. A teacher named Pārāśarya taught that "the eye not seeing form, the ear not hearing sound" constituted noble cultivation of the faculties, on the grounds that this represented total non-grasping; when his own disciple Uttara later visited the Buddha and repeated this teaching, the Buddha immediately objected that, on this logic, the blind and the deaf would have to be counted among the noble ones, and Ānanda, standing nearby fanning the Buddha, pressed the very same point even more directly. The followers of the Vaiśeṣika school posited five faculties; the followers of Sāṃkhya posited five faculties of cognition, five faculties of action, and mind, making eleven; and some traditions went so far as to claim a hundred and twenty faculties altogether — twenty faculties per being (the five sense faculties doubled to six by an added pair, plus tongue, body, mind, the life faculty, the five faculties of feeling, and the five faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom) multiplied across the six realms of rebirth, this proliferation of "lords" over each realm and each being supposedly needing to be personally attained before liberation could occur. Faced with this entire spectrum, from a single faculty up through a hundred and twenty, the Buddha settled the matter decisively: exactly twenty-two faculties, neither one more nor one fewer, fully account for everything that genuinely needs to be accounted for — a deliberate rejection of every other tradition's claims, and the foundation for everything this treatise is about to explain.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid has been explained. The skillful understanding of the faculties shall now be explained. The summary verse states: name and meaning, purpose, and establishment come first; extensive analysis comes after.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Uttara-and-Ānanda exchange as a small comic gem tucked into an otherwise heavily technical survey: a teaching meant to sound like the height of renunciate wisdom — refusing to see or hear at all — collapses the instant someone points out that this would make ordinary blindness and deafness into spiritual attainments, exactly the kind of sharp, common-sense rebuttal the early scriptures record the Buddha and his close disciples delivering again and again against confused ascetic doctrines. He notes the sheer range of rival faculty-counts — one, five, eleven, a hundred and twenty — as itself evidence of just how unsettled this question was across ancient Indian thought before the Buddha's own teaching, and treats the number twenty-two not as an arbitrary compromise but as this treatise's own claim to have found the genuinely correct, exhaustive accounting, no more and no less. He flags the summary verse's own fourfold roadmap — name and meaning, purpose, establishment, extensive analysis — as the structure this entire final topic will follow, promising it will occupy a great deal of the remainder of this notably long fascicle.

15. Section: What "Faculty" Actually Means — Eight Angles on a Single Word

The treatise opens its detailed explanation by asking the most basic question first: what does "faculty" actually mean? The answer given is that "dominant meaning" is what "faculty" means — something is called a faculty when it serves as a powerfully influential support for something else's arising, exactly as a root nourishes and supports what grows from it. This treatise's own earlier discussion of the stage of a hearer's path explains this further through two angles: the sense of "that toward which something tends," and the sense of "supreme." Concerning what something tends toward: cultivating pure ethical discipline tends toward concentration, and is accordingly called the heightened training in discipline; cultivating a concentrated mind tends toward wisdom, and is accordingly called the heightened training in mind; and cultivating wisdom and insight tends toward severing affliction, and is accordingly called the heightened training in wisdom. Concerning supremacy: these same three heightened trainings in discipline, mind, and wisdom exist, in this complete and mutually reinforcing form, only within the Buddha's own teaching, shared with no other tradition, and are for this reason called supreme above all else. The Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra, cited here for a fuller picture, offers eight distinct meanings of "faculty" altogether: dominant meaning, illuminating meaning, manifesting meaning, meaning of being joyfully beheld, dignified meaning, foremost meaning, supreme meaning, and sovereign meaning — eight different angles on the very same underlying word, each capturing some genuine facet of what it means for something to function as a true "root" for whatever depends on it.

16. Section: Why Twenty-Two, Not Any Other Number — the Six Functions That Establish Every Faculty

The treatise briefly states the purpose behind establishing the twenty-two faculties: it is simply to reveal that each one is, within its own specific domain of things and phenomena, the single most dominant, most powerful factor at work — nothing more mysterious than that. It then turns to the deeper question of exactly how the twenty-two faculties come to be established at all, answering through six distinct functional groupings. First, the six faculties of eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind are established because each is the single most powerful factor in grasping its own corresponding object — the eye grasping form, the ear sound, and so on. Second, the male and female faculties are established because each is the single most powerful factor in ensuring a family lineage continues unbroken across generations. Third, the life faculty alone is established because it is the single most powerful factor enabling every other life activity to continue at all — without life itself, nothing else could even be discussed. Fourth, the five faculties of feeling — pain, pleasure, sorrow, joy, and equanimity — are established because they are collectively the single most powerful factor in actually experiencing the fruit of one's own karma, whatever wholesome or unwholesome action has already been sown inevitably ripening as one or another of these five felt experiences. Fifth, the five faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom are established because they are collectively the single most powerful factor in achieving purification through mundane meditative attainment, functioning equally as the necessary foundation for the deeper purification of the transcendent path itself, once faith in what is wholesome, an eagerness born of that faith, sustained attention, stabilizing concentration, and finally discerning wisdom have all matured together. Sixth and finally, the three faculties tracing the path to full realization — the faculty of one who does not yet fully know but aspires to know, the faculty of one who has come to know, and the faculty of one whose knowledge is fully complete — are established because they are collectively the single most powerful factor in achieving the transcendent purification of full liberation itself, spanning everyone from a beginning practitioner still approaching the path of vision all the way through to a fully accomplished arhat.

17. Section: A Second Way to Count the Faculties Through Experience, and the Cosmic Origin of Male and Female

The treatise offers a second explanation of how the twenty-two faculties are established, this time organized around the very act of experiencing objects rather than around function alone, and does so in two distinct ways. In the first way, the six faculties of eye through mind are established because they are the single most powerful factor in experiencing manifest, openly visible objects — color, sound, and the rest. The male and female faculties are established because they are the single most powerful factor in experiencing a hidden, concealed kind of object instead. The life faculty is established because it is the single most powerful factor marking out the actual temporal boundary within which any experiencing of objects can occur at all. The five faculties of feeling are established because they are the single most powerful factor through which experiencing objects gives rise to affliction in the first place, craving and the rest arising precisely through pain, pleasure, and the other felt qualities of experience. And the remaining eight faculties — the five faculties of faith through wisdom, together with the three faculties tracing realization to its completion — are together established because they are the single most powerful factor in achieving purification, both mundane and transcendent alike. In the second way, the same six faculties are instead described as established to reveal experience occurring through an inward-facing gate, while the male and female faculties are described as established to reveal experience occurring through an outward-facing gate — reaching toward another's body rather than remaining within one's own — and the life faculty is described as established specifically to reveal the experiencing of one's own inward body's continued existence. The lecture pauses here to relate a striking cosmological story behind why the male and female faculties count as an "outward-facing," "hidden" kind of experience at all: in the earliest age of this world, beings originally descended from the Heaven of Radiant Sound, their own bodies still luminous, needing no sun or moon; only gradually, as they began consuming the earth's own increasingly coarse nourishment, did their bodies grow correspondingly coarser and their inner light fade, until beings began looking upon one another with newly arisen desire, gender itself first differentiating at that point — and only then, once such beings began transgressing against one another and drawing reproach for it, did they first build houses, precisely in order to conceal from others what had, until then, never needed concealing at all.

18. Section: A Third Pass — Establishing the Faculties Through What the Body Itself Depends On

The second explanatory scheme closes with its final two groupings: the five faculties of feeling are established because they are the single most powerful factor through which affliction arises in relation to both outer objects and the inner body together, and the remaining eight faculties — faith through wisdom, together with the three faculties of realization — are established because they are the single most powerful factor for counteracting that very affliction and achieving purification in its place. The treatise then offers a third distinct scheme, this time organized around what the body itself, as a physical support, actually depends on. The five faculties of eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body are established because they are the single most powerful factor in making that bodily support appear dignified and complete — a body missing eyes or ears would hardly look presentable. The mind faculty alone is established because it is the single most powerful factor enabling that same bodily support to move freely at its own command, exactly as an often-cited scriptural verse puts it: mind guides the world, mind gathers in everything, and this single factor, mind, governs all activity entirely on its own terms. The life faculty is established because it is the single most powerful factor allowing that bodily support to actually persist for its full natural span, whether fifty, sixty, or seventy years. The male and female faculties are established because they are the single most powerful factor in that bodily support's own capacity to produce further life. The five faculties of feeling are established because they are the single most powerful factor through which that bodily support is either benefited or harmed — joy and pleasure nourishing health and wellbeing, sorrow and pain undermining it, while equanimity runs through both. And the remaining eight faculties are established because they are the single most powerful factor by which that same bodily support ultimately achieves genuine liberation, stilling both its afflictions and the suffering-laden body itself.

19. Section: A Fourth Pass — What "Surpassing Rebirth" and "Decisive Superiority" Actually Mean

The treatise's fourth scheme organizes the same twenty-two faculties around sentient beings themselves. The six faculties of eye through mind are established because they are the single most representative factor revealing a being's own existence across the three realms and five destinies. The male and female faculties are established because they are the single most powerful factor in generating further such beings, increasing their overall number. The life faculty is established because it is the single most powerful factor governing whether a being's existence continues to abide or comes to an end. The five faculties of feeling are established because they are the single most powerful factor revealing how a being actually experiences whatever objects it encounters. The five faculties of faith through wisdom are established because they are the single most powerful factor enabling what this treatise calls "surpassing rebirth" — a life that genuinely improves upon the one before it, since a being strong in faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom naturally commits fewer unwholesome deeds and more wholesome ones, securing a favorable rebirth among humans or gods, and this same principle of one life surpassing the last applies just as well to a bodhisattva vowing to keep returning to this very world to continue the path, not only to an ascent from human to heavenly rebirth. And the three faculties of realization are established because they are the single most powerful factor enabling what this treatise calls "decisive superiority" — the definite, guaranteed attainment of nirvāṇa itself, forever escaping the great mass of inherent suffering that even the most favorable surpassing rebirth still carries, achieved specifically through the gradual progression from the stage of accumulation, through preparatory effort, the path of vision, the path of cultivation, and finally the stage beyond further training altogether.

20. Section: A Life That Only Ever Decreases, and the Faculties Named by Lay Life Versus Monastic Life

The treatise closes its second version of the sentient-being scheme with the male and female faculties established to reveal a being's own capacity for numerical increase, and the life faculty established to reveal something no other faculty reveals in quite the same way — that a being's own lifespan only ever diminishes, never increases, with each passing day and hour subtracted rather than added. The lecture cites the Dharmapada Simile Sūtra here, where the Buddha urges his monks directly: life is being spent away day and night, so exert effort while there is still time, since the world's truths are impermanent and one should not become confused and fall into the darkness of continued birth and death, but should instead kindle the lamp of wisdom through proper reflection, purify the mind of every stain, and hold that torch aloft to see the path clearly. The remaining faculties are established, in this same scheme, to reveal a being's prosperity or decline, and a being's own virtues as against its faults. The treatise then turns to a fifth scheme organized entirely around conventional naming and designation, itself divided between lay and monastic life. Fourteen faculties are said to be established according to how lay life itself gets named: the six sense faculties according to the very names "eye," "ear," and the rest; the male and female faculties according to lineage and family name; the five feeling faculties according to whichever of the four kinds of nourishment a being partakes of, and the resulting pleasure or pain; and the life faculty according to whether a given lifespan counts, by convention, as long-lived, long-abiding, or having reached its very furthest natural limit. 21. Section: The Sixth and Final Scheme — Guarding the Sense-Doors on the Way to a Fruit of Practice

The treatise's sixth and final scheme organizes the twenty-two faculties around the actual sequence of spiritual practice itself. The six sense faculties are established because they are the single most powerful factor in a practitioner's own discipline of guarding the sense-doors, the Buddha's own repeated instruction never to let the eye grow attached to form, the ear to sound, and so on through each remaining faculty in turn. The male and female faculties are established because their own proper, unimpaired condition is the single most powerful factor in being fit to renounce household life and realize a contemplative's fruit at all — one lacking either faculty being said, in an earlier treatise, to be incapable of either great wholesome or great unwholesome action, unable even to sever wholesome roots outright. The life faculty is established because a longer lifespan is the single most powerful factor supporting the accumulation of wholesome qualities — ethical discipline, concentration, and wisdom, both mundane and transcendent, all require time enough to actually cultivate, exactly as a well-known scripture on the bodhisattva's own vows describes a bodhisattva's complete lifespan as what allows uninterrupted cultivation across a very long span, wholesome roots accumulating and growing without pause. The five faculties of feeling are established because they are the single most powerful factor a practitioner must act upon with clear comprehension — noticing pain, pleasure, sorrow, joy, or equanimity the moment they arise, so as never to be swept along by them into further affliction, exactly the kind of watchfulness this treatise elsewhere describes as what genuinely sustains both ethical discipline and diligent practice. The five faculties of faith through wisdom are established because they are the single most powerful factor among the various means actually leading to a contemplative's fruit. And the three faculties of realization are established because they are the single most powerful factor in actually attaining that fruit — the faculty of aspiring-to-know supporting the path of vision, the faculty of one who has come to know supporting the path of cultivation, and the faculty of complete knowledge supporting the full, present enjoyment of realization's own fruit. With this sixth scheme, the treatise's entire explanation of how the twenty-two faculties come to be established, examined now from six genuinely distinct angles, finally draws to its close.

22. Section: A Summary Verse for Six Schemes, and What Each Faculty Actually Does

A closing summary verse gathers the six establishment-schemes just completed into a single compact reference: following objects and the rest, through manifestation and the inward gate, adornment and the two kinds of sentient-being basis, conventional designation and guarding — six lines corresponding exactly to the six angles already examined in full. With this, the treatise turns to its fourth and final major topic, an extensive analysis of the twenty-two faculties organized around eight further distinctions: their specific function, their real or provisional status, what broader category subsumes them, their moral quality, their connection to karmic ripening, their confinement to particular realms, their association with particular stages, and how each is obtained or relinquished. The first of these, function, is examined faculty by faculty. The eye's own function is simply to see form — whatever has already been seen, is being seen now, or will be seen in the future, the eye's own capacity spanning all three times even though an ordinary being's actual eye-consciousness only ever engages present form directly, the past and future scope belonging instead to the faculty's own underlying continuity of seed and potential. The same reasoning extends, without further elaboration needed, to ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind, each faculty defined by its own corresponding activity of hearing, smelling, tasting, touching, or cognizing across all three times. The male and female faculties function to draw parents, spouses, children, relatives, and extended family into mutual relationship and visible connection with one another. The life faculty functions to keep a being continuously alive, abiding, and sustained, falling squarely within the category of the living rather than the inert. The five faculties of feeling function to let a being actually experience prosperity and decline. The five faculties of faith through wisdom function doubly, both to secure a favorable rebirth and to bring the provisions for nirvāṇa itself to their full completion. And the three faculties of realization function to bring a being, in this very life, to the actual attainment of nirvāṇa.

23. Section: Sixteen Real Faculties, Six Merely Provisional, and What Category Each One Falls Under

The treatise turns to its second distinction: which of the twenty-two faculties are genuinely, substantially real, and which are only provisionally established labels. Sixteen are genuinely real: the six sense faculties, the five faculties of feeling, and the five faculties of faith through wisdom, each an actual, substantial form, mind, or mental factor in its own right. The remaining six are merely provisional: the male and female faculties, being simply a designated phase of the body faculty rather than any separately existing substance; the life faculty, being a mind-dissociated formation provisionally established upon the fixed duration that past karma has already determined for the continuity of form and mind together, without any separate substance of its own; and the three faculties of realization, being provisional labels for a particular phase jointly constituted by the mind faculty together with the faculties of joy, pleasure, and equanimity, and the five faculties of faith through wisdom — nine faculties altogether standing behind these three merely designated names, since a genuine practitioner naturally experiences joy, pleasure, and equanimity while engaging the mind faculty and the five wholesome faculties together in actual practice. The treatise then turns to a third distinction: what broader category each faculty falls under. Seven faculties — the five sense faculties together with the male and female faculties — are subsumed under form. One faculty, mind, is wholly subsumed under mind itself, with the three faculties of realization also partially subsumed there, since the mind faculty is one of their nine constituent parts. Ten faculties — the five feeling faculties and the five faculties of faith through wisdom — are subsumed under mental factors, again with the three faculties of realization partially subsumed there as well, since eight of their nine constituent parts belong to this same category. And one faculty, life, is subsumed under formations dissociated from mind altogether, belonging to neither form nor mind nor mental factors, but to this distinct fourth category of its own.

24. Section: Why Sorrow Alone Among the Faculties Can Never Be Morally Neutral

The treatise confirms that all twenty-two faculties, without exception, are conditioned phenomena — none whatsoever belongs to the unconditioned. Individual clarifications follow: the male and female faculties are simply a designated phase of the body faculty; the three faculties of realization are a designated phase jointly constituted by the mind faculty, the five faculties of faith through wisdom, and the faculties of joy, pleasure, and equanimity, nine faculties together; and the life faculty belongs to no other faculty at all, being instead provisionally established purely upon the fixed duration that past karma has already determined, with no substantial reality of its own. The treatise then examines moral quality. Counting both real and provisional faculties together, eight are wholesome — the five faculties of faith through wisdom, together with the three faculties of realization; counting only genuinely real faculties, five are wholesome — faith through wisdom alone; and the mind faculty together with the five faculties of feeling are only partially wholesome, wholesome whenever they accompany a wholesome mental orientation, and equally capable, in the very same six faculties, of being unwholesome whenever they instead accompany an unwholesome orientation. Eight faculties, by contrast — the five sense faculties, the male and female faculties, and the life faculty, every one of them a straightforward karmic-ripening phenomenon — together with a portion of five further faculties, are morally neutral. The lecture pauses over a striking exception here: of the five feeling faculties, sorrow alone can never be morally neutral, only ever wholesome or unwholesome. Four separate reasons are given for this, all resting on sorrow's own inherently deliberative, reflective character: an inborn, spontaneously arising sense of self is itself morally neutral in a subtle, obstructed sense, yet operates through a mood of contented ease entirely incompatible with sorrow's own troubled character, so the two cannot coexist; sorrow always involves active mental deliberation, whereas the neutral conduct of ordinary bodily comportment, skilled craftsmanship, and simple karmic ripening all proceed without any such deliberation whatsoever — a person mindfully performing correct comportment or completing a skilled task is, in the very act of that mindful performance, not simultaneously sorrowing over it; and karmic ripening itself unfolds strictly according to prior karmic force, never according to present-moment wishing, whereas sorrow arises and subsides precisely according to what one currently wishes were otherwise — for all these reasons, sorrow's own nature simply has no room left for a neutral form to occupy.

25. Section: Which Faculties Ripen Into Karmic Fruit, and Which Are That Fruit Itself

And turning to the deepest distinction of all, an ordinary, unenlightened being can possess nineteen faculties, excluding only the three faculties of realization, while one who has genuinely seen the truth of things — any noble one who has directly realized reality itself — can, the treatise begins to explain, possess the full range once again, this fine-grained survey of humanity's own faculties finally arriving at the very distinction between ordinary and awakened experience that the entire twenty-two-faculty framework has been building toward all along.

32. Section: Counting Down the Faculties Stage by Stage, from Stream-Entry to Arhatship

The treatise now traces exactly how many faculties are possessed at each successive stage of noble realization. A trainee still on the path can possess twenty-one, excluding only the faculty of complete knowledge, which belongs to full arhatship alone. One beyond further training — an arhat — can possess nineteen, excluding the aspirant's and the knower's faculties, both now superseded, together with the sorrow faculty, since an arhat's own perfected freedom leaves no room for renunciation's own residual grief. Moving through the four approaches and four fruits in sequence: one approaching stream-entry can possess twenty, excluding the knower's and the complete-knower's faculties; one who has actually attained the fruit of stream-entry can likewise possess twenty, now excluding the aspirant's faculty instead, already superseded, together with the complete-knower's faculty still to come; and this same count of twenty extends, by analogy, through the approach to once-returning, the fruit of once-returning, and the approach to non-returning as well. One who has actually attained the fruit of non-returning can possess nineteen, the sorrow faculty now additionally excluded alongside the first two faculties of realization, since a non-returner has entirely severed the desire-realm afflictions from which sorrow itself arises; the very same count of nineteen extends to one approaching arhatship, and to the fruit of arhatship itself, though for arhatship specifically it is the aspirant's and the knower's faculties, rather than the knower's and complete-knower's, that fall away, replaced at last by the complete-knower's faculty alone. Turning briefly to rebirth in the higher realms, one reborn in the form realm can possess eighteen faculties, excluding the male, female, pain, and sorrow faculties entirely; one reborn in the formless realm can possess only eleven, additionally excluding the five sense faculties along with pleasure and joy, having transcended matter and its accompanying pleasures altogether.

33. Section: Four Ways to Lose or Gain a Faculty Between One Life and the Next

The treatise turns from a static census of possessed faculties to the actual dynamic process of relinquishing and obtaining them across successive lives, beginning with a being who dies in the desire realm and is reborn there again. Considering the physical sense faculties alone, four distinct patterns are possible. A being may relinquish deficient faculties in one life and obtain deficient faculties again in the next, carrying the same impairment forward. A being may relinquish deficient faculties and obtain complete ones instead, an improvement across the transition. A being may relinquish complete, unimpaired faculties and obtain complete faculties once more, continuity without change. Or a being may relinquish complete, unimpaired faculties and obtain deficient ones instead — precisely the fate, the lecture notes, that can follow acts of cruelty toward other beings, such as deliberately maiming an animal, the very faculty once wrongly damaged in another potentially returning as one's own impairment in a future life. A second, related fourfold pattern concerns not mere completeness but relative quality: a being may relinquish inferior faculties and obtain inferior faculties again; relinquish inferior faculties and obtain superior ones, as when leaving behind the coarse faculties of a suffering animal or similarly unfortunate rebirth for the far more refined faculties of a human or heavenly existence; relinquish superior faculties and obtain inferior ones, falling from a fortunate human or heavenly rebirth into one of the miserable realms instead; or relinquish superior faculties and obtain superior faculties once more, continuing to enjoy a fortunate rebirth's own refined endowment across the transition.

34. Section: A Summary Verse Closes Eight Distinctions, and a Second, Even Longer List Begins

The treatise completes its account of obtaining and relinquishing. The mind and life faculties follow the very same fourfold pattern of inferior and superior already described for the physical senses. The faculties of feeling follow suit as well, though specifically with respect to karmic-ripening result — a fortunate human or heavenly rebirth carrying superior feeling-faculties, an unfortunate rebirth carrying inferior ones, though this says nothing about the different matter of an animal or ghost's own equal-outflow experience, which can still include real pleasure regardless of realm. The five faculties of faith through wisdom follow a different logic entirely: considered as equal-outflow result, each life's cultivation only ever builds upon what came before, relinquishing an inferior degree for a superior one — the only exception being a deliberate turn toward wrong practice severe enough to sever wholesome roots outright, or an unfortunate rebirth interrupting cultivation's own steady increase. The three faculties of realization, finally, stand entirely outside this framework of birth and death, since their very nature runs contrary to continued rebirth itself; they are instead obtained specifically through the means of actually realizing a contemplative's fruit, never through the ordinary process of dying and being reborn, and, once obtained, only ever increase in both their equal-outflow effect, growing more vigorous each time they are exercised, and their capacity to shape karmic ripening, securing an ever more excellent rebirth for as long as rebirth continues at all. 36. Section: Twelve Faculties, Twelve Objects — From Ordinary Perception to the Threshold of Nirvāṇa

The treatise completes its faculty-by-faculty survey of objects. The mind faculty's own object is simply all phenomena whatsoever, conditioned and unconditioned alike. The five feeling faculties take, as their object, whatever accords with pain, pleasure, sorrow, joy, or equanimity arising through the six ordinary faculties' own engagement with their objects. The faculty of faith takes, as its object, whatever should be attained as wholesome or relinquished as unwholesome. The faculty of diligence takes, as its object, that very same attaining and relinquishing, pursued fearlessly and without hesitation. The faculty of mindfulness takes, as its object, whatever has been heard, reflected upon, and cultivated, retained in memory without ever being forgotten. The faculty of concentration takes, as its object, both calm-abiding and insight together. The faculty of wisdom takes, as its object, reality as it is genuinely known, spanning four progressively deeper levels of established truth. The aspirant's faculty takes, as its object, everything belonging to the entire preparatory path a practitioner traverses from the very first arising of wholesome aspiration onward — precisely the same object as the five faculties of faith through wisdom, since the aspirant's faculty is itself simply a designated phase of those same five. The knower's faculty takes, as its object, that same fivefold territory as it operates from the attainment of stream-entry all the way up to the vajra-like concentration that finally destroys even the subtlest remaining affliction. And the faculty of complete knowledge takes, as its object, that same territory once more, now spanning from the very first moment of being beyond further training all the way to nirvāṇa without remainder itself.

38. Section: Why Pain and Sorrow Break the Usual Pattern of Defiled and Undefiled

The treatise turns to resistance: the seven physical faculties possess resistance and take resistant, physically obstructive objects as their own, while a portion of the formless faculties — specifically whichever consciousness operates directly alongside those seven, such as eye-consciousness alongside the eye — likewise takes resistant objects, since it shares its very field of activity with its own physical basis. The treatise then examines defilement, conditioned existence, and contentiousness together, since all three follow essentially the same underlying pattern. Only the seven physical faculties are purely defiled, taking only defiled objects; the faculty of complete knowledge and the knower's faculty are purely undefiled, taking only undefiled objects; and ten further faculties — mind, the faculties of pleasure, joy, and equanimity, the five faculties of faith through wisdom, and the aspirant's own faculty — span both defiled and undefiled, taking both kinds of object depending on circumstance. The pain and sorrow faculties, however, break this otherwise tidy pattern in genuinely interesting ways. Pain itself is ordinarily defiled, yet can become undefiled in the body of a fully accomplished arhat, exactly as Venerable Maudgalyāyana, his very body brutally beaten by hostile ascetics, still felt physical pain arise without any accompanying grasping at self or resentment whatsoever — pain present, defilement absent; even so, pain's own object remains strictly defiled either way, since pain itself, whether or not it defiles the mind experiencing it, only ever arises in dependence on some defiled physical condition. Sorrow, by contrast, is always itself defiled in nature, since it belongs to ordinary beings alone and never arises in one beyond training — yet it can take an undefiled object, precisely in the wholesome case of a renunciant's own impatient longing for liberation, grieving that the undefiled path has not yet been fully realized, a grief that, however defiled in its own nature, nonetheless spurs genuine diligence rather than despair.

39. Section: A Faculty That Has Already Passed Can Still Take the Present as Its Object

The treatise confirms that all twenty-two faculties, without exception, are conditioned; eight of them — the seven physical faculties together with the pain faculty — take only conditioned phenomena as their object, while the remaining thirteen non-physical faculties take both conditioned and unconditioned phenomena as their object, since mind and its accompanying mental factors can reflect upon the unconditioned, such as nirvāṇa itself, in a way no physical faculty ever can. The very same fourfold pattern already established for defilement is then said to extend, without further elaboration needed, to contentiousness, attachment to taste, habitual indulgence, and mundane versus transcendent status alike — each of these categories sorting the twenty-two faculties in essentially the same way defilement did. The treatise then turns to time itself: which faculties belong to the past, and which take the past as their object. Excepting the seven physical faculties and the pain faculty, a portion of the remaining faculties belonging to the past also take the past as their object — but the seven physical faculties and the pain faculty themselves, though they too pass into the past the very instant they cease, paradoxically never take the past as their object at all, since their own field of activity is confined strictly to the present moment; a physical faculty that has already ceased simply has no capacity to engage anything any longer, past, present, or future. The thirteen faculties associated with mind, by contrast, genuinely range across all three times freely — a present moment of mind can reflect on a past object, an already-past moment of mind, considered retrospectively, was itself once oriented toward the present, and so on across every possible combination, precisely because mind's own nature, unlike physical form's, is not restricted to acting only within its own present instant.

40. Section: Present Senses, Timeless Mind, and the Seven Ways a Faculty Belongs to a Realm

The treatise completes its examination of time: mind and its associated faculties, whether located in the past, present, or future, can always take any of the three times as their object, memory reaching backward and anticipation reaching forward regardless of which moment the reflecting mind itself currently occupies. The seven physical faculties and the pain faculty, by contrast, remain confined to the present no matter when they themselves occur — even a physical faculty that has not yet arisen, once it finally does arise, will still only ever engage a present object, never reaching into the future the way mind alone can. The treatise then turns to realm-confinement examined through the lens of a faculty's own object, distinguishing seven distinct groupings. The pain, sorrow, male, and female faculties belong exclusively to the desire realm and take only desire-realm objects. The nose and tongue faculties belong to both the desire and form realms, since form-realm beings retain these faculties even though unused, yet take only desire-realm objects, since scent and taste as actual objects exist nowhere but the desire realm. The eye, ear, and body faculties belong to both the desire and form realms and correspondingly take objects from either realm, active in both. The faculties of joy and pleasure belong to the desire realm, the form realm, and the undefiled state beyond all three realms, and take objects spanning the desire, form, and formless realms together with the undefiled state as well. Seven faculties — the five faculties of faith through wisdom, together with mind and equanimity — belong to all three realms and the undefiled state alike, and take the very same complete range as their object. The aspirant's faculty belongs to the form realm and the undefiled state, yet takes the complete range of all three realms and the undefiled state as its object, since a practitioner's own aspiration, even while dwelling in a specific meditative stage, can still reflect on the entire path ahead. 41. Section: Faculties That Are Only Ever Wholesome, and Faculties That Can Be Any of the Three

The treatise completes moral quality: the sorrow faculty spans only wholesome and unwholesome, never neutral, yet still takes objects of all three qualities; the nose, tongue, body, male, and female faculties are purely neutral and take only neutral objects; and the eye and ear faculties, though themselves neutral — considered here specifically as outwardly expressed action, itself a merely provisional phenomenon — nonetheless take objects spanning all three qualities. Nine faculties — mind, the faculties of joy, pleasure, and equanimity, together with the five faculties of faith through wisdom — span all three categories both in their own nature and in what they take as their object, since these same faculties operate at every stage of practice from ordinary beginner through full realization. Seven faculties — the physical senses together with the male and female faculties — belong to neither trainee nor adept in their own nature, and correspondingly take only that same "neither" category as their object, ordinary physical faculties standing entirely outside this specifically spiritual classification. 43. Section: What Must Be Severed by Insight, What by Sustained Practice, and What Needs No Severing At All

The treatise's seventh and final angle within this sevenfold clarification examines which faculties must be severed through the path of vision, which through the path of cultivation, and which require no severing whatsoever. Fourteen faculties — the seven physical faculties, the five faculties of feeling, together with the life and mind faculties — are partly to be severed through the path of vision and partly through the path of cultivation: whichever portion of these faculties is bound up with an unfortunate rebirth is severed already at the path of vision, since no one who has reached that path ever falls into the miserable realms again, while whichever portion is bound up with a fortunate rebirth persists to be severed only later, through the sustained cultivation that finally leads to nirvāṇa without remainder. Twelve faculties — the same feeling and mind faculties together with the five faculties of faith through wisdom and the aspirant's own faculty — are partly to be severed through cultivation and partly not to be severed at all, their defiled portion falling away through sustained practice while their undefiled portion needs no severing whatsoever, being already free of the very affliction that severing exists to remove. And the remaining two faculties, the knower's faculty and the faculty of complete knowledge, require no severing at all, standing already beyond every level at which severing could still apply. In brief, the treatise explains, every faculty possessing form takes only the path of vision and the path of cultivation as its object, since physical form itself is always bound up with some degree of affliction requiring one or the other, while every formless faculty takes all three — vision, cultivation, and no-severing-required — as its object, spanning the entire range from a beginner's earliest insight to the complete absence of anything left needing to be severed. With this seventh and final angle now closed, the treatise's entire sevenfold clarification of the faculties' object-meaning — form, visibility, resistance, defilement and its many parallels, the three times, realm-confinement, moral quality, trainee-status, and now this threefold severing — finally comes to rest.

44. Section: What Must Be Relinquished, What Distorts, and What Alone Can See the Fault

Fourteen faculties — the six faculties of eye through mind, the male and female faculties, the five faculties of feeling, and the life faculty — are manifested by the relinquishing of defilement, since defilement, whether afflictive, karmic, or the defilement of rebirth itself, is precisely what these fourteen must eventually be cleansed of; only the five faculties of faith through wisdom, together with the three faculties of realization, are excluded, being themselves already the very embodiment of purity rather than something requiring further purification. Turning to distortion — the tendency to grasp at permanence, pleasure, self, or purity where none genuinely exists — four further questions arise. Which serve as distortion's own supporting basis? The seven physical faculties, since distorted craving and aversion always arise dependent on some physical faculty's own engagement with its object. 45. Section: Four Virtues Non-Buddhists Claim for Conditioned Existence, and Why None of Them Hold

The lecture pauses here to relate a substantial objection non-Buddhist thinkers raised against this entire line of reasoning: if conditioned phenomena genuinely possessed four excellent qualities, would it not be wrong to observe only fault in them, with nothing good to be found at all? Four such qualities are named. First, an enduring quality: the form aggregate, if properly cared for through diet and exercise, can persist for a hundred years or more before finally failing, and this durability itself counts as a kind of permanence. Second, a powerful quality: engaging pleasant sights, sounds, and the rest genuinely produces pleasure, and this very capacity to produce pleasure in the present moment counts as a kind of genuine purity already available in the here and now. Third, a transformative quality: something called "self" draws the body and mind along, cultivating, employing, and eventually discarding them entirely at will, this very capacity for self-directed transformation counting as a kind of genuine, freely operating self. And fourth, a desirable quality: relying on this or that circumstance produces experiences resembling genuine purity and contentment, this very desirability counting as a kind of true purity already present within conditioned existence itself. Together, the objection runs, these four qualities — permanence, pleasure, self, and purity — show that conditioned phenomena are not simply defective through and through, and that Buddhist teaching's insistence on seeing only fault in them must therefore be one-sided and incomplete. The treatise's response is direct: however much the ordinary world may take these very qualities to be genuine virtues, every one of them is, in truth, thoroughly accompanied by fault from beginning to end — a claim the treatise now sets out to demonstrate one virtue at a time, refuting each supposed excellence in turn.

46. Section: Why Even Dung Beetles Enjoy What They Enjoy — Refuting the Four Claimed Virtues

The treatise now refutes each of the four claimed virtues in turn. Against the claim of an enduring quality: although conditioned things may persist a short while, they never persist ultimately, since the constant, observable transformation of even the most cherished things, and the equally observable inevitability of death, can both be witnessed directly at any time — a once-beautiful complexion inevitably fading into a withered, sallow one, a once-upright frame inevitably stooping with age, exactly the kind of transformation anyone can watch happen to themselves or to those around them. Against the claim of a powerful quality capable of producing genuine pleasure: conditioned existence just as readily, and just as observably, produces every variety of suffering and impurity instead, so no such reliable power to produce pleasure alone can honestly be attributed to it. Against the claim of a transformative quality freely operating at will: old age, sickness, and death themselves plainly do not yield to anyone's own wishes, however much a person might long to remain forever young, healthy, and alive — the very helplessness everyone actually experiences before these three is itself sufficient proof that no genuinely free, self-directing transformative power exists here at all. And against the claim of a desirable quality amounting to genuine purity: the lecture offers a strikingly earthy illustration — dung beetles, pigs, and dogs all take the greatest delight imaginable in filth and impurity, a delight every bit as intense, from their own point of view, as any pleasure an ordinary person takes in some supposedly "pure" and desirable circumstance, proving that mere subjective attraction is no evidence of genuine purity at all, only of whatever a given being happens to be conditioned to crave. Since conditioned existence is thus shown to be entirely without any of these four supposed virtues, and is instead thoroughly accompanied by fault in every respect, the treatise concludes that observing only fault in it is, after all, the correct and undistorted way to see it clearly. With this objection fully answered, the treatise turns to its next distinction: which faculty serves as the supporting basis for which activity. The eye faculty serves as the basis for seeing form; and, by the very same reasoning, the ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind faculties each serve as the basis for their own respective faculty's engagement with sound, scent, taste, touch, and phenomena in turn.

47. Section: The Lamp in a Windless Room — What Each Faculty Actually Grounds

The treatise's survey of supporting bases continues through the remaining faculties. The male and female faculties serve as the basis for engaging in sensory desire between beings. The life faculty serves as the basis for existence prior to the moment of death — both the very instant of conception and the entire span of life that follows it, since without the life faculty neither could continue at all. The five faculties of feeling serve as the basis for craving toward pleasant objects and aversion toward unpleasant ones. The faculty of faith serves as the basis for entering wholesome practice in the first place, exactly as a well-known verse puts it: one with faith who enters the treasury of the Buddha's teaching attains, according to their own capacity, the joy of liberation, while one without faith gains nothing at all even encountering that same teaching directly. The faculty of diligence serves as the basis for sustaining that same wholesome practice once entered. The faculty of mindfulness serves as the basis for acting with clear, unwavering comprehension moment to moment. The faculty of concentration serves as the basis for purifying genuine insight, illustrated by a striking simile from the Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom: a lamp exposed to a strong wind cannot illuminate anything properly, however bright its flame, while that same lamp placed within a sealed, windless room illuminates completely — wisdom scattered by an undisciplined mind functions the same way, unable to do its proper work until concentration provides the still, sheltered condition within which insight can finally see clearly. The faculty of wisdom serves as the basis for permanently severing affliction at its root, since concentration alone, however deep, cannot sever affliction without wisdom's own discerning insight joined to it — a point this tradition insists upon precisely because certain non-Buddhist meditators, for all their genuine meditative attainment, never escape wrong view for want of this same insight. And the aspirant's faculty serves as the basis for attaining the first, second, and even the third fruit of the contemplative life, since practitioners of gradual temperament advance to the first fruit immediately upon completing the fifteen moments of the path of vision, while more capable, so-called "leaping" practitioners can advance directly to the second or even the third fruit from that very same foundation, skipping the intervening stage entirely.

48. Section: Seventeen Ways to See Through the Body — Scripture Explained Through the Faculties

The treatise's survey of supporting bases closes with its final two faculties. The knower's faculty serves as the basis for everything from stream-entry up through the vajra-like concentration that immediately precedes full arhatship — the entire span covering the first, second, and third fruits and their corresponding approaches. And the faculty of complete knowledge serves as the basis for three distinct things at once: the uninterrupted, definitive severing of affliction actually accomplished in that very vajra-like concentration; an arhat's own present-life dwelling in bliss while the karmic body from a former life still remains; and the final, permanent cessation of that same three-realm-bound body when an arhat enters nirvāṇa without remainder. With this thirteen-part survey of supporting bases now complete, the treatise turns to an entirely new approach: explaining ten scriptural passages by showing which of the faculties each one actually rests upon. It begins by asking on which faculty's basis the Buddha spoke these words: that this body is made of form, composed of coarse remnants left over after nourishment is extracted, built from the four great elements, born from the impure union of parents, sustained by various kinds of food, constantly requiring covering, bathing, and massage, and ultimately subject to being cut, broken, dispersed, and destroyed. The answer is that the Buddha spoke this way resting on the seven physical faculties, describing precisely the common nature, own nature, causal nature, changing nature, and eventual decaying nature that these seven faculties, taken together, actually constitute. The lecture supplements this with a further body-contemplation from the Sūtra on the Transformation of the Female Body, offering seventeen distinct angles from which to see through the body entirely: as a puppet worked by strings; as a fragile thing of blood and flesh soon to collapse; as a latrine leaking impurity from nine openings; as something only the foolish cling to, being nothing but the four elements; as inhabited by aggregates that are themselves like enemies; as hollow and insubstantial, like an abandoned village; as ownerless, merely arising from parents and then decorated by one's own past deeds; as impure, filled through and through with foul odor; as a mere vessel for waste, soon to be discarded, with nothing in it worth craving; as destined for death the moment breath finally stops; as without any true self, no different from grass, wood, or stone; as without any real maker, arising only from causes and conditions; as food for birds, wolves, dogs, and jackals once abandoned in a charnel ground; as a mass of suffering, afflicted by four hundred and four kinds of disease; as constantly being worn down by wind, cold, and heat, sustained only by medicine; as ungrateful, endlessly fed yet never satisfied; and as utterly without awareness, having no true agent acting within it at all — a practitioner who genuinely takes these seventeen contemplations to heart, the lecture notes, will find nothing left in the body still worth clinging to or refusing to release.

49. Section: The Mind That Travels Far, Travels Alone, and Dwells Bodiless in Its Own Cave

The treatise turns to its second scriptural passage, asking on which faculty's basis the Buddha spoke these evocative words: traveling far, and traveling alone, bodiless, dwelling within a cave — words drawn from a verse elsewhere in this treatise praising one who has fully tamed the difficult-to-tame: mind travels far, travels alone, is without body, and dwells within a cave; whoever can tame this very thing, that one I call a true brahmin. The answer is that the Buddha spoke this way resting specifically on the mind faculty, and the lecture unpacks each of the verse's own four striking images in turn. "Traveling far" describes mind's own activity stretching back across beginningless time, ranging without limit over every object that can possibly be known — no starting point can be found for mind's own restless movement through experience after experience, life after life, always already underway.

He highlights "far-traveling" specifically as pointing to mind's own beginningless restlessness, never once at rest across the entire span of saṃsāra, always already reaching toward its next object before the last has even been released — precisely the restless quality any meditator discovers the moment they actually try to sit still and simply watch their own mind for the first time.

50. Section: No One Is Driving — Why Mind Has No Ruler, and the Eight Ways Attraction Binds

The treatise completes its explanation of mind's fourfold verse. "Alone-traveling" describes the fact that each moment of mind arises one at a time in strict succession, with no ruling master directing it at all — this point aimed specifically at rival philosophers who insisted some fixed self must be pulling mind's strings, steering it through its various objects; the treatise insists instead that mind simply arises however present conditions shape it, wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral by turns, with no one actually in control, which is itself precisely why transformation through practice remains possible at all — were some fixed owner genuinely running the show, that ownership could never be dislodged, but since no such controller exists, mind's own habitual reactions can genuinely be retrained over time. "Bodiless" describes mind's own formless nature — without color, without visibility, without any physically obstructive quality whatsoever. And "dwelling in a cave" describes simply that mind depends on the physical body for its own residence, the way a creature dwelling in a cave depends on that cave's shelter. The treatise then turns to a second scriptural passage concerning the male and female faculties, asking on what basis the Buddha described eight things by which a man becomes bound to a woman: dance, song, laughter, a meaningful glance, beauty of countenance, delightful touch, attentive service, and the completion of marriage. The answer rests on the male and female faculties themselves, the first four binding a man specifically during playful courtship — body through dance, voice through song, face through laughter, and eyes through a lingering, expressive glance — and the remaining four binding him during actual enjoyment together — beauty of countenance, softness of touch, devoted attentiveness, and marriage's own culminating bond.

51. Section: Five Words for Being Alive, and Why Pleasure Keeps the Body Upright

The treatise turns to its fourth scriptural passage, asking on what basis the Buddha described beings as living, existing, abiding, sustained, and at ease. The answer rests on the life faculty, unpacked through five distinct angles. "Living beings" describes simply having breath — wherever breath continues, a being is said to live. "Existing" describes being joined with thought — the capacity to reflect and consider showing that a being genuinely continues. "Abiding" describes remaining, in accordance with prior karma, for however long that karma has already determined — a being who created the karma for an eighty-year lifespan abides for precisely that span, no more and no less. "Sustained" describes the life faculty's own power to keep the five aggregates continuing together without interruption. And "at ease" describes simply being free of illness and affliction. The treatise then turns to the five faculties of feeling, beginning with joy and pleasure together, asking on what basis the Buddha spoke of an even, upright kind of feeling, subsumed under feeling in general. The answer rests specifically on the faculties of joy and pleasure, since these two alone benefit the body directly, keeping it in a genuinely balanced, upright condition, in clear contrast to the faculties of pain and sorrow, which instead disturb and unsettle it, and the faculty of equanimity, which spans both benefit and harm without leaning decisively toward either.

52. Section: A Monk Who Would Not Waver Even If the Buddha Himself Asked Him to Disrobe

The treatise closes its explanation of the feeling faculties by extending the very same pattern already established for joy and pleasure to the remaining feelings: just as an even, upright feeling is revealed through joy and pleasure's own characteristic, shared nature, and supporting basis, an uneven, disturbed feeling is revealed the same way through pain and sorrow, and a feeling neither upright nor disturbed is revealed through equanimity, each following the identical threefold pattern of characteristic, shared nature, and physical-and-mental support. The treatise then turns to a scriptural passage concerning faith, asking on what basis the Buddha spoke of a pure faith in the Tathāgata so deep and firm that no worldly teacher, brahmin, or even the heavenly lords of desire and form themselves could ever lead one away from it through any legitimate means. The answer rests on the faith faculty, its own firmness explained on two levels: first, that genuine faith, cultivated through learning, reflection, and meditative practice, becomes so resolute that not even the wisest renunciant, the highest-born brahmin, the desire realm's own ruling deity, or the form realm's own Brahmā could shake it, let alone an ordinary person; second, that this firmness itself develops in stages, worldly faith's own firm confidence in wholesome and unwholesome karma's inevitable results serving as the very root from which transcendent, unshakeable faith eventually grows. The lecture illustrates this unwavering quality with the case of a monk known for his own extraordinary confidence: asked what it would take to shake his resolve to remain ordained, he answered that even if Śākyamuni Buddha himself appeared and told him to disrobe, he would not believe it was truly the Buddha speaking, and would not disrobe regardless — the very same monk, having brought his own wife and children into monastic life together with him, spending entire days and nights, without sleep, gently persuading anyone who showed even the faintest interest in ordination to actually take the step, asking nothing for himself in return beyond the wish to see others ordained under his own teacher.

53. Section: Five Faces of Diligence — From Armored Resolve to Never Being Satisfied

The treatise turns to a scriptural passage on diligence, asking on what basis the Buddha described someone abiding with strength, effort, courage, and firm resolve, never abandoning the yoke of wholesome practice. The answer rests on the diligence faculty, its own characteristic unpacked into five distinct aspects. Armored diligence describes the resolve of one preparing for spiritual practice the way a soldier dons armor before battle, bracing to engage affliction head-on. Diligence in method describes the actual effort exerted once practice has begun. Undaunted diligence describes feeling no sense of inferiority before even the vastest wholesome goal, such as full buddhahood itself, however many countless eons its accomplishment is said to require. Unwavering diligence describes remaining undisturbed by external circumstance — cold, heat, insects, or any other discomfort — continuing to meditate regardless of whether conditions happen to be favorable or difficult. And diligence without complacent satisfaction describes never resting content with some small wholesome accomplishment already achieved, always continuing to press forward rather than mistaking a modest gain for the entire goal. The treatise then turns briefly to mindfulness, asking on what basis the Buddha spoke of memory, even memory, repeated memory, distinct memory, unforgetting memory, clear mental retention, and freedom from loss, forgetting, or error — the answer resting, as expected, on the mindfulness faculty itself, with its full detailed explanation left to a related discussion found elsewhere in this same treatise's own compendium of terms.

54. Section: Closing the Scriptural Explanations — Mind's Nine Stages of Settling, and Wisdom's Fourfold Investigation

The treatise closes mindfulness by pointing to the nine progressive stages of settling the mind explained in full elsewhere in this treatise's own discussion of the hearer's path — placement, equal placement, repeated placement, close placement, taming, pacifying, thorough pacifying, one-pointedness, and balanced sustaining — each of mindfulness's own nine named qualities corresponding exactly to one of these nine stages of an increasingly settled mind. Concentration receives the very same explanation and the very same ninefold correspondence, since concentration and mindfulness are simply two aspects of this same underlying process of settling the mind onto a single object. Wisdom, examined next, rests on four kinds of insight already explained in that same discussion of the hearer's path: precise investigation of phenomena according to their full range of particular characteristics, the most thorough investigation of phenomena according to their genuine underlying nature, comprehensive seeking that leaves no aspect of the object unexamined, and comprehensive, still deeper scrutiny of that same object once found. And the three faculties of realization require no further separate explanation at all, since they are, as already established, simply designated phases of the five faculties of faith through wisdom themselves — whatever has already been said of those five applies to these three without remainder. With this, the treatise's entire scriptural-explanation project, tracing ten memorable passages back to the specific faculty or faculties each one actually rests upon, comes to its close, and the treatise turns to a new task: correlating all twenty-two faculties with the four foundations of mindfulness. The foundation of mindfulness of the body rests on seven faculties, the physical senses together with the male and female faculties. The foundation of mindfulness of feeling rests on the five faculties of feeling. The foundation of mindfulness of mind rests on the mind faculty alone. And the foundation of mindfulness of mental phenomena rests on eight faculties — the five faculties of faith through wisdom together with the three faculties of realization — together with the life faculty as well, all of these being subsumed under the broader category of mental phenomena in this treatise's own classification.

55. Section: Mapping the Faculties onto the Four Noble Truths

The treatise now correlates the twenty-two faculties with the four noble truths in turn. Nine faculties — the seven physical faculties together with the mind and life faculties — serve as the truth of suffering's own most excellent basis, since these nine together constitute the very embodied existence in which the entire round of birth and death's suffering is actually experienced. Five faculties — the faculties of feeling — serve as the truth of origin's own most excellent basis, since it is precisely through pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral feeling that craving and aversion first arise, setting the whole engine of further karma into motion. All twenty-two faculties without exception serve as the basis for the truth of cessation, since every one of them, in some portion or another, is subject to being severed through the path of vision, the path of cultivation, or is already free of any need for severing at all, cessation itself simply being what remains once every faculty's own defiled portion has finally been exhausted. And the final eight faculties — the five faculties of faith through wisdom together with the three faculties of realization — serve as the truth of the path's own most excellent basis, being precisely the means by which cessation is actually brought about.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold mapping as this treatise's own way of showing that its elaborate twenty-two-faculty framework was never merely an intellectual exercise, but a genuine lens for understanding the four noble truths themselves — the very same truths at the heart of the entire Buddhist path — from yet another angle. He highlights the origin truth's own narrow basis, just the five feeling faculties, as a precise pinpointing of exactly where the chain from ignorance to suffering actually turns decisive: not the bare sense faculties themselves, nor even mind, but specifically the felt quality of pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral experience that first gives craving and aversion something to seize upon. He notes the cessation truth's own maximally broad basis — every single faculty without exception — as fittingly comprehensive, since cessation is not the elimination of some separate thing but simply the complete purification of everything that currently exists in its defiled form.

56. Section: How Many Faculties Does It Take to Think, Speak, or Act Well?

The treatise continues correlating the twenty-two faculties, turning next to the three kinds of action — thought, speech, and physical deed. Nine faculties are engaged in wholesome thinking: the five faculties of faith through wisdom, the three faculties of realization, and the mind faculty itself, since genuine reflection always requires mind together with whatever wholesome quality is actually being considered. Ten faculties are engaged in wholesome speaking: the same nine, together with the tongue faculty, since speech obviously cannot occur without the physical faculty that produces it. And sixteen faculties are engaged in wholesome physical action: the same nine, together with the eye, ear, and body faculties, and the faculties of pleasure, sorrow, joy, and equanimity, since a wholesome deed typically involves seeing, hearing, and physically acting within some accompanying emotional tone. The very same pattern, the treatise notes, extends by analogy to unwholesome thought, speech, and action as well, simply substituting an unwholesome orientation for the wholesome one throughout. The treatise then turns to affliction and karma themselves: nine faculties — the seven physical faculties together with mind and life — serve as their most excellent basis, since without this embodied foundation neither affliction nor karma could arise at all; five faculties — the faculties of feeling — are what actually give rise to affliction and karma in the first place, since it is through felt experience that craving and aversion first stir; and the final eight faculties — faith through wisdom together with the three faculties of realization — are what actually sever affliction and karma once cultivated to completion.

He highlights the affliction-and-karma correlation as this section's own compact restatement of the entire path in miniature: nine faculties provide the basic embodied ground where affliction becomes possible, five faculties are the actual trigger point where it arises, and eight faculties are the cultivated remedy that finally severs it — the whole spiritual journey from vulnerability through affliction to liberation traced across just twenty-two faculties.

57. Section: Five Conditions for a Truly Auspicious Life, Beginning with Genuine Patience

The treatise turns to its final correlation within this section: auspiciousness itself. Excepting the pain and sorrow faculties, the remaining three feeling faculties — joy, pleasure, and equanimity — are what actually receive auspicious things, whether mundane or transcendent. Eleven faculties — the seven physical faculties, mind, life, together with pain and sorrow — are what receive the collapse of auspiciousness instead. And the final eight faculties — faith through wisdom together with the three faculties of realization — are what actually draw auspiciousness forth in the first place. The treatise then offers a fuller explanation, citing the Buddha's own teaching that those who still enjoy sensory pleasure can, in brief, secure auspiciousness through five conditions: patience, gentleness, discerning restraint toward others, conduct that is genuinely virtuous, and freedom from negligence. Unpacking the first of these, patience is explained through three distinct aspects. Though the full explanation of each aspect is still to come, the very naming of patience as auspiciousness's own first condition already signals this closing section's own shift in register — from twenty-two faculties correlated with abstract categories toward a warm, practical teaching on how anyone, whatever their circumstances, can actually secure a genuinely auspicious life.

He notes that patience opening this list is itself significant, since impatience — with other people, with circumstances, with one's own slow progress — is so often what actually destroys whatever good fortune a person already has, making patience less a passive virtue than the active precondition for everything auspicious that follows. He flags that the three aspects of patience, though only just introduced, are about to be unpacked individually, promising a genuinely practical teaching to close out this long stretch of the faculties' extensive analysis.

58. Section: Ten Kinds of Patience, and Why Being Gentle Is Not the Same as Being Patient

The treatise names patience's own three basic aspects — not becoming enraged, not seeking revenge, and not harboring ill will — before offering a far more detailed, tenfold breakdown drawn from this treatise's own bodhisattva-stage teachings. There is patience toward harm already suffered in the past; patience toward harm currently being inflicted; patience toward anticipated future harm one already knows is coming; patience toward seeing an enemy receive some benefit, genuinely able to rejoice rather than resent it; patience toward harm done to one's own beloved friends and family, cultivated by remembering that across beginningless lifetimes, everyone now considered a stranger or even an enemy has, at some point, actually been one's own parent or sibling; comprehensive patience extending to every kind of being regardless of whether they are friend, enemy, or neither, whatever their own fortune or misfortune; causal patience, cultivated out of a bodhisattva's own fear of the painful karmic result that impatience itself would produce, and out of genuine compassion and love for all beings, and out of an intense aspiration for supreme awakening; patience cultivated specifically because one has been taught it, as when the Buddha states plainly that a genuine renunciant must possess the very power of patience, and so should never act otherwise; patience arising from discerning insight into the ultimate emptiness of all apparent conflict, seeing directly that no substantially real being stands on either side of any dispute at all; and innate patience, belonging to one whose very nature, cultivated through repeated practice across many lifetimes, now settles naturally into patience without any special effort required at all. All ten together, the treatise concludes, are simply called patience toward harm and opposition. Turning next to gentleness, the treatise explains it as a genuinely good-natured disposition, protecting others through one's own body, speech, and mind so that they suffer no harm or distress at all — helping wholesome, blameless joy to arise where it has not yet arisen and protecting it once it has, while preventing blameworthy sorrow from arising and helping free anyone already caught in it. The crucial difference between the two: patience is the passive capacity to endure harm done by others, while gentleness is the active practice of never inflicting harm on others in the first place — related virtues, yet genuinely distinct.

59. Section: Five Qualities Before Embracing Someone, and Five Aptitudes for Where to Place Them

The treatise turns to its third condition for an auspicious life, discerning restraint toward others, unpacked through two distinct moments: the moment of embracing someone as an ally, and the moment of actually placing them somewhere useful. When embracing someone, five qualities should first be observed: sincere devotion toward the Three Jewels, genuine skill or talent, real wisdom, conduct that follows the proper path rather than deviant ways, and honest frugality free of greed — someone who shows all five can genuinely be embraced and drawn close as a trusted ally. When it comes time to actually place that same person somewhere useful, five different aptitudes should be observed instead: whoever is capable of practical, executive work should be placed in charge of practical affairs; whoever is capable of careful deliberation should be placed where thoughtful judgment is needed; whoever is skilled at reconciling people and easing tension should be placed in a role fostering harmony among others; whoever is capable of protecting bodies and property should be placed in charge of security or resources, whether as a healer, guardian, or steward of shared wealth; and whoever shows a genuine aptitude for the Dharma itself, delighting in listening to teachings, chanting, and quiet meditation, should be placed and supported specifically in that pursuit, freed to dedicate themselves to cultivating ethical discipline, concentration, and wisdom without distraction.

60. Section: Five Kinds of Giving, Five Ways to Never Betray a Trust, and Five Fronts of Vigilance

The treatise names the five kinds of giving that complete "restraint," the third condition for auspiciousness: giving land, giving wealth, giving whatever happens to be genuinely needed in the moment — a smile when a smile is needed, a kind word when comfort is needed, money when money is needed — giving food to the hungry, and finally the most excellent gift of all, understood variously by different commentators as precious treasure, as the gift of the Dharma itself capable of genuinely transforming a person's thinking, or as the ultimate gift of one's own body, the most difficult offering of any kind to make. The treatise turns next to virtuous conduct, the fourth condition, describing it simply as a disposition that never betrays or deceives others, unpacked into five specific ways such betrayal is avoided: never fraudulently appropriating something entrusted to safekeeping as though it were one's own; never betraying someone's trust in whatever has been committed to one's care; never failing to follow through on a promise to serve or attend to someone; never breaking a formal agreement once made, as though a contract were merely paper to be discarded when inconvenient; and never scheming up any other conceivable way to betray someone at all — this last catch-all ensuring that no loophole remains for betrayal to slip through. The treatise turns finally to non-negligence, the fifth condition, describing it as the diligent cultivation of wholesome qualities together with vigilant guarding against unwholesome states of mind, unpacked into five specific fronts: vigilance in seeking wealth honestly, vigilance in guarding wealth once obtained without succumbing to greed, vigilance in protecting one's own body without excessive indulgence, vigilance in protecting one's own good reputation, and vigilance in continuously practicing the Dharma itself, sustaining a mind free of greed, hatred, and delusion throughout.

61. Section: Five Powers, Five Fortunes — Closing the Teaching on Auspiciousness

The treatise draws the entire five-condition teaching to its close by naming five powers these conditions together generate, and the five specific fortunes each power in turn produces. Patience and gentleness together generate the power of genuinely good ethical discipline. Discerning observation of others generates the power of good companionship. Generous giving generates the power of freedom from stinginess. Virtuous, trustworthy conduct generates the power of being genuinely relied upon by others. And diligent non-negligence generates the power of the Dharma itself, thoroughly mastered. Five specific fortunes correspond to these five powers in turn: being loved and delighted in by everyone; enjoying wealth, honor, and genuine freedom; having one's enemies retreat and submit; benefiting one's own body and mind directly; and, finally, proceeding after death toward a fortunate rebirth rather than a miserable one — the first four powers together producing the first four fortunes, while the fifth power, the Dharma's own mastery through non-negligence, alone produces this fifth and final fortune of a favorable destination beyond this very life. With this teaching now fully closed, the treatise returns to its own steady analytical rhythm, opening a new distinction concerning attainment itself: which faculties are obtained specifically as a result of past affliction and karma serving as their condition. Eight faculties are so obtained: the seven physical faculties together with the life faculty, all of them the direct, unavoidable product of karma already sown in some previous life.

62. Section: Which Faculties Need Guarding, Which Need Calming, and Which Are Already Composed

The treatise's brief correlation of attainment continues. The mind faculty alone results from name-and-form as its condition, mind's own arising always dependent on the interplay of the mental and physical factors that precede it. The five faculties of feeling result from contact as their condition, since it is precisely through contact between faculty and object that any feeling first arises at all. And the final eight faculties — faith through wisdom together with the three faculties of realization — result specifically from sustained exertion as their condition, since these, unlike the karmically inherited physical faculties, can only ever be obtained through deliberate, ongoing effort. The treatise then turns to which faculties require cultivation through protection, calming, stilling, or an already-composed nature. Eight faculties — the seven physical faculties together with the life faculty — should be actively protected, kept from recklessly chasing after external objects, and the life faculty especially safeguarded, since practice itself depends on continued life. The mind faculty alone should be calmed, prevented from running wild after one object after another, brought instead toward a settled stillness free of affliction. The five faculties of feeling should be stilled, so that pain, pleasure, sorrow, joy, and equanimity no longer stir up the erratic, karma-generating reactivity they otherwise would. And the final eight faculties are described as already possessing, in their own very nature, the qualities of composure, stillness, and settled calm, and are moreover themselves capable of actively producing that same composure, stillness, and calm in a practitioner who cultivates them, precisely because these eight only manifest once the ten disturbing objects, the five hindrances, and the eight kinds of harmful discursive thought have already been left behind.

63. Section: What Separates a Hearer's Faculties From a Bodhisattva's

The treatise turns to a categorical distinction among the faculties, first covering those subsumed under the defiled, outflow-bound category. Relinquishing faculties describes a being's faculties belonging to one category of realm and stage ceasing while faculties belonging to that very same category arise again — as when a desire-realm being dies and is reborn once more within the desire realm, whether as human, animal, or otherwise, the underlying category remaining unchanged even as the specific rebirth differs. Discarding faculties describes instead a being's faculties belonging to one category ceasing while faculties belonging to an entirely different category arise — as when a desire-realm being dies and is reborn instead in the form or formless realm, the underlying category itself now changing. Severing faculties describes the actual cutting away of every affliction that has bound a being's faculties in the first place. And regressing faculties describes any worldly flourishing, whether prosperity, meditative attainment, or a favorable rebirth already achieved, subsequently being lost and falling away. With these four terms — relinquishing, discarding, severing, and regressing — now defined for the defiled, outflow-bound category, the treatise turns to the undefiled category instead, asking what distinguishes the faculties of a hearer, a solitary realizer, and a bodhisattva from one another. The answer names five distinct differences: a difference in category, a difference in sustaining power, a difference in what binds them, a difference in proper conduct, and a difference in what is ultimately attained — five separate angles from which these three paths, though all genuinely undefiled, nonetheless remain meaningfully distinct from one another.

He flags the upcoming comparison of hearer, solitary realizer, and bodhisattva faculties as one of this fascicle's more conceptually interesting turns, since all three paths are genuinely undefiled and yet the treatise insists on five separate, substantive differences between them — a claim worth watching unfold in detail, since it speaks directly to how this tradition understands the relationship between its own three vehicles of liberation.

64. Section: Five Ways a Bodhisattva's Faculties Genuinely Differ from a Hearer's

The treatise unpacks all five differences between the faculties of a bodhisattva and those of a hearer or solitary realizer. Concerning category: a bodhisattva's own faculties are, by their very nature, of the highest grade, while a hearer's are of the mildest grade and a solitary realizer's of a middling grade. Concerning sustaining power: a bodhisattva's faculties skillfully master and sustain every one of the five fields of knowledge — inner wisdom, logical reasoning, linguistics, practical arts, and medicine — while a hearer's or solitary realizer's faculties need only master inner wisdom alone, their essential task already accomplished without needing to pursue such boundless further learning. Concerning what binds them: a bodhisattva's faculties are bound by great compassion itself, precisely to the very cycle of birth and death, vowing to remain within it across countless eons for the sake of liberating other beings, whether by returning to this very world lifetime after lifetime or by first securing rebirth in a pure land and returning from there — while a hearer's or solitary realizer's faculties carry no such binding at all, seeking instead to escape birth and death as swiftly as possible, finding this world's suffering more than enough reason to leave it behind for good. Concerning proper conduct: a bodhisattva's faculties manifest both self-benefit and other-benefit together in full, exemplifying the highest of three possible types of person — those who benefit both self and others, those who benefit only others, and those who benefit only themselves — while a hearer's or solitary realizer's faculties manifest only self-benefit, urgently seeking their own liberation from suffering above all else. And concerning attainment: a bodhisattva's faculties culminate in the unsurpassed fruit of supreme awakening itself, the most complete attainment of all, while a hearer's faculties culminate only in arhatship, the lesser of the two remaining fruits, and a solitary realizer's culminate in the middling fruit of individual enlightenment.

65. Section: Sharp Faculties, Dull Faculties, and Whether Joy Arises the Moment One Attains the Fruit

The treatise now turns to a detailed determination, opening with a specific question: for someone relying on access concentration to directly realize the four truths, does the joy belonging to the first absorption proper actually arise the moment that person attains the fruit, or does it not? The answer is that it depends: some can give rise to it, others cannot. This distinction tracks a fourfold classification of practitioners found elsewhere in this tradition — those who progress painfully and slowly, those who progress painfully but quickly, those who progress pleasantly but slowly, and those who progress pleasantly and quickly — "painful" describing anyone who has not yet attained a genuine meditative absorption and so lacks the bodily ease such absorption brings, "pleasant" describing anyone who has, "slow" describing those of duller faculties in faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, and "fast" describing those of sharper faculties in the very same five qualities. Someone relying only on access concentration yet possessing sharp faculties belongs to the painful-but-fast category, and it is precisely this person who can give rise to the first absorption's own joy at the very moment of attaining the fruit; someone relying on that same access concentration but possessing only dull faculties belongs instead to the painful-and-slow category, and cannot give rise to that joy at all. What actually makes the difference is whether a practitioner has been genuinely supported by an abundance of wholesome roots already cultivated — complete ethical discipline, guarding the sense-doors, moderation in eating, wakeful vigilance through the night's early and late watches, and clear comprehension in every activity, among the full set of provisions this tradition considers essential preparation for the path. Only someone who has genuinely assembled these provisions can give rise to joy the very instant realization dawns; without them, realization may still occur, but that particular fruit of joy simply does not follow.

66. Section: Which Faculties Can Actually Enter Each Absorption, and Why Bodhisattvas Avoid the Formless Attainments

The treatise turns to a comprehensive determination of which faculties can actually enter each successive meditative attainment. Eight faculties — the mind faculty, the faculties of joy and pleasure, and the five faculties of faith through wisdom — enter the first absorption; of the three faculties of realization, understood as a phase of nine underlying faculties, the portion belonging to mind, joy, pleasure, and the five wholesome faculties can likewise enter, while the portion belonging to equanimity cannot, since the first absorption's own qualities of examination, reflection, joy, pleasure, and one-pointed mind leave no room for equanimity at all. The second absorption follows exactly the same pattern. The third absorption likewise admits eight faculties, yet not quite the same eight — here it is joy, rather than equanimity, that must be excluded, since the third absorption is specifically described as having left joy's own more turbulent quality behind, its own qualities instead being mindfulness, clear comprehension, equanimity, pleasure, and one-pointed mind, savoring bodily bliss without joy's accompanying agitation. The fourth absorption and the four formless attainments beyond it admit only seven faculties — the five faculties of faith through wisdom together with mind and equanimity — since by this point neither joy nor even pleasure itself remains, only the most settled equanimity accompanying the most refined stillness. Concerning the three faculties of realization specifically in relation to the formless attainments, the treatise notes an important distinction: one kind of hearer can indeed enter these formless attainments with seven of the nine underlying faculties active, but a bodhisattva generally does not, since bodhisattvas deliberately avoid cultivating the formless attainments at all — a meditative depth so profound that the wisdom accompanying it typically grows correspondingly shallow, a trade-off entirely unsuited to a path that requires clear, active wisdom in constant service of other beings.

67. Section: Counting the Faculties Behind Each of the Four Fruits

The treatise turns to which faculties are actually involved in attaining each of the four noble fruits, offering multiple valid counts depending on whether provisional or genuinely real faculties are being counted, and whether a gradual or a sudden practitioner is in view. Stream-entry can be said to involve either one faculty, the aspirant's faculty alone, counted as a provisional label, or eight faculties, counting instead the genuinely real mind, pleasure, and equanimity faculties together with the five faculties of faith through wisdom, the joy faculty specifically excluded since only a sharper-natured stream-enterer, not every stream-enterer, actually manifests it. Once-returning can be said to involve either two faculties, the aspirant's and the knower's faculties together, since some practitioners attain this fruit gradually and others leap directly to it, or nine faculties, counting the same eight real faculties together with whichever of the two provisional faculties applies to that particular practitioner's own gradual or sudden progress. Non-returning can be said to involve either eleven faculties — now finally including joy, together with mind, pleasure, equanimity, the five wholesome faculties, and both the aspirant's and knower's faculties combined — or, more simply, just the same two provisional faculties alone. And arhatship can be said to involve either the single faculty of complete knowledge alone, or ten faculties, adding to it mind, pleasure, joy, equanimity, and the five faculties of faith through wisdom. The treatise notes that joy's own inconsistent appearance across these counts, present at non-returning but absent or merely optional at the two lower fruits, traces back to a genuinely unsettled question already discussed earlier — whether joy, when a fruit is attained through access concentration alone rather than a fully established absorption, remains stable enough to count at all, a question this treatise treats as varying by practitioner rather than admitting one single, fixed answer.

68. Section: Hope, Seeking, Grief, and Sorrow — The Sorrow Faculty's Own Path Toward Liberation

The treatise turns to establishing the specific function each faculty actually serves, beginning with the sorrow faculty, illustrated through a scriptural passage describing beings who hope for, seek, sorrow over, and grieve for liberation from what lies above their current attainment. Hope describes a practitioner's own reflection that this — nirvāṇa itself — is the very place every noble one can fully and completely abide within, and the sincere wish to reach it oneself. Seeking describes the further reflection that one should indeed come to fully abide in that very place, and the active effort toward actually doing so. Grief describes a practitioner's own refusal to feel satisfied with any merely inferior attainment already reached, always sensing that something more remains to be accomplished. And sorrow describes an intense, longing reflection directed specifically at the very highest, unsurpassed attainment of all, worrying whether one will actually manage to reach it. The treatise then clarifies exactly which of the four fruits actually involves this particular sorrow faculty: stream-entry and once-returning both involve it in its complete form, since practitioners at these two stages still experience the full range of hope, seeking, grief, and sorrow toward what remains unattained. Non-returning, by contrast, though it still involves hope and seeking directed toward arhatship, no longer involves grief or sorrow in the same way, since a non-returner has already become free of the desire realm's own afflictions and can trust that continued effort alone will swiftly bring arhatship near — what remains is simply wholesome aspiration, no longer touched by the anxious, sorrowful quality found at the two lower fruits.

69. Section: Relinquishing One Kind of Equanimity by Relying on a Deeper One

The treatise closes its examination of the feeling faculties' own mutual reliance. Just as relying on renunciation's own joy can relinquish the ordinary joy, sorrow, and equanimity bound up with sensory indulgence, so too relying on sorrow itself, when grounded in genuine renunciation, can relinquish the sorrow that comes from clinging to sensory pleasure. Relying on equanimity likewise admits its own layered structure: the single, uniform equanimity cultivated across the four formless attainments can be relied upon to relinquish the form realm's own more varied equanimity, tied as it still is to sight, sound, and the other sense objects; and an even deeper, wholly non-reliant equanimity — the equanimity of a mind free of any conceptual signs at all, or of the attainment of cessation itself — can in turn be relied upon to relinquish even that single, uniform formless equanimity, completing the entire ascent. The treatise then clarifies a subtle point about the aspirant's and the knower's faculties: when a practitioner attains stream-entry, the aspirant's faculty does indeed cease and is relinquished, yet not through some new arising-and-then-being-cast-off, nor through being severed as an affliction is severed, nor through any kind of regression — it simply transforms directly into the knower's faculty, since both are, after all, merely different names for the very same underlying combination of mind, feeling, and the five wholesome faculties, now operating at a further stage. The very same explanation applies, without further elaboration needed, to the knower's faculty transforming into the faculty of complete knowledge upon attaining arhatship.

70. Section: Sharpening Dull Faculties — What a Refined Stream-Enterer Can and Cannot Yet Reach

The treatise turns to differences among individual practitioners, beginning with the practice of refining one's own faculties. Every trainee still on the path, together with five specific kinds of dull-natured arhat beyond training — those prone to regression, those inclined toward ending their own life out of frustration, those who must actively safeguard their attainment, those who simply maintain it without advancing, and those merely capable of eventually penetrating further — all engage in this practice of refinement, gradually relinquishing a lower grade of faculty for a middling one, and a middling grade for a higher one, through sustained cultivation of the path. Neither solitary realizers nor bodhisattvas ever need this practice, both being naturally sharp-natured from the outset, unlike the sixth kind of arhat, the unshakeable one, whose own naturally sharp faculties likewise require no such refinement. The treatise then asks specifically what a stream-enterer practicing this refinement can and cannot actually achieve: such refinement genuinely can bring a stream-enterer all the way to the fruit of once-returning, but cannot bring that same practitioner to non-returning, since the afflictions of desire and hatred belonging to the desire realm are exceptionally difficult to overcome, and what must be accomplished to reach non-returning is simply too vast an undertaking for mere faculty-refinement alone to achieve without also directly confronting and severing those specific afflictions.

71. Section: Five Reasons to Refine the Faculties, and What "Complete" Actually Means

The treatise names five reasons a practitioner undertakes this refinement of the faculties: dissatisfaction with only slight progress, the wish to cultivate superior meditative power, the wish to cultivate the power gained through extensive learning, the wish to cultivate skill in debate and analysis, and the wish to cultivate the patient, penetrating insight that comes from contemplating the profoundest teachings. The treatise then turns specifically to how a bodhisattva's own three faculties of realization are established, quite differently from a hearer's: the aspirant's faculty is established already at the stage of resolute conduct through faith, still short of the actual noble path; the knower's faculty is established from the very first ground onward, through the tenth, once genuine purified aspiration and direct realization of the transcendent path have been achieved; and the faculty of complete knowledge is established only at the stage of a fully accomplished buddha. The treatise then asks how many kinds of completeness define a trainee's own perfection, naming three: completeness of faculty, meaning sharp faculties; completeness of concentration, meaning the eight liberations culminating in cessation itself; and completeness of fruit, meaning the attainment of non-returning, since a non-returner never again falls back into the desire realm. One beyond training, an arhat, is said to possess two of these three completenesses explicitly — completeness of faculty, belonging specifically to the unshakeable kind of arhat, and completeness of concentration, the very same eight liberations — yet every arhat without exception, whatever their own particular faculty-sharpness or meditative depth, is still called simply "complete" on the strength of fruit-completeness alone, since that fruit, arhatship itself, is the very highest attainment a hearer's path can reach, and remains the single decisive criterion regardless of any other variation among arhats.

72. Section: The Four Ways Ordinary Senses Go Untamed

Having completed its examination of the undefiled faculties, the treatise turns to the defiled faculties belonging to ordinary beings, opening with a scriptural passage describing the senses as untamed, unguarded, unprotected, unwarded, and uncultivated, and asking what distinguishes these five terms from one another. The answer is that, in brief, four specific causes together constitute what scripture calls the senses being untamed: lacking the discerning power that actually guards the senses in the first place, lacking the further protection that builds on that initial guarding, lacking the active warding-off of temptation once an object has already been encountered, and lacking the sustained cultivation that would otherwise keep the senses continuously trained over time — these four conditions, taken together, are simply what "untamed" itself means, the remaining terms in the scriptural passage each naming one of these same four causes individually rather than describing some entirely separate quality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this shift from undefiled to defiled faculties as a natural and necessary counterpart to everything just covered: having spent so much of this fascicle tracing the path toward liberation's own refined faculties, the treatise now turns back to examine, with the very same careful precision, the ordinary condition most beings actually start from. He notes the fourfold breakdown of "untamed" as a genuinely practical diagnostic any reflective person could apply to their own sense-discipline: is there discerning judgment guarding the senses in advance, further protection sustaining that guard, active resistance once temptation actually appears, and continued practice keeping all of this in good working order over time — a lapse in any one of these four being sufficient for the senses to count as genuinely untamed.

73. Section: Six Sufferings of an Untamed Mind, and the Joy That Comes From Genuine Discipline

The treatise explains what genuine taming of the senses actually looks like, resting on two distinct powers. Relying on the discerning power gained through learning and reflection, guarding means simply refraining from letting the senses indulge an object when indulgence is inappropriate, while protecting means allowing the senses to engage an object when engagement is genuinely appropriate, yet still preventing any affliction from actually arising in the process. Relying instead on the deeper power that actually cuts through affliction at its root, gained through meditative cultivation, warding-off means the senses engaging their objects without any underlying attachment at all, while cultivating means those very afflictions no longer manifesting even in potential, their seeds themselves having already been uprooted. Lacking all four of these — guarding, protecting, warding-off, and cultivating — is precisely what makes the senses untamed, while possessing all four is precisely what makes them genuinely well-tamed. The treatise then asks why untamed senses lead to suffering, naming six specific kinds: the suffering of an agitated, restless inner life; the suffering of being cast out or rejected by others on account of one's own misconduct; the suffering of being mocked and belittled for having no genuine spiritual substance; the suffering of persistent regret over past wrongdoing; the suffering of a miserable future rebirth as the karmic fruit of untamed conduct; and the whole remaining suffering of birth, aging, sickness, and death that continues cycling for as long as affliction itself remains unaddressed. Well-tamed senses, the treatise concludes, bring exactly the opposite result — all six of these sufferings cut off, and every kind of genuine joy brought forth in their place.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the six sufferings of untamed senses as a genuinely observable, almost sociological pattern rather than a purely abstract doctrinal claim: agitation, social rejection, mockery, regret, and their eventual karmic consequences are all things anyone can actually watch unfold in someone whose senses run unchecked, no special faith required to see the pattern play out. He highlights the two-power structure behind genuine taming — discerning power first learning to restrain and protect, and only later the deeper antidote power that removes attachment's very root — as tracing a realistic developmental sequence rather than expecting a beginner to leap straight to effortless non-attachment. With this, he confirms, the treatise's entire discussion of the defiled faculties belonging to ordinary beings comes to its close, completing the whole extensive determination of attainment that has occupied this fascicle for so long, and opening the way to a new distinction: how the twenty-two faculties are mutually subsumed together with other categories.

74. Section: How the Three Realms Contain Fourteen Faculties, and How Five Feelings Fold Into Three

With attainment now fully determined, the treatise turns to its fourth distinction within this second cycle: mutual subsumption. It first asks whether the fourteen genuinely real faculties belonging to ordinary existence are subsumed by the three groups of beings — those of the desire, form, and formless realms — or whether those three groups are instead subsumed by the fourteen faculties. The answer is that the three groups subsume the fourteen faculties, not the reverse, since these fourteen — the seven physical faculties, the mind faculty, the life faculty, and the five faculties of feeling — belong entirely to the very embodied existence of beings within these three realms, while the three realms themselves also include the surrounding external environment, the physical world itself, which these fourteen faculties, confined to a being's own body and mind, do not cover at all. The treatise then turns to a related question: are the five faculties of feeling subsumed by the three basic kinds of feeling — pleasant, painful, and neutral — or is it the other way around? The answer is that each subsumes the other equally, since both cover exactly the same underlying territory, merely divided differently — the painful category simply gathering together what the fivefold division separates into pain and sorrow, and the pleasant category gathering together what that same fivefold division separates into pleasure and joy, with neutral feeling remaining a single, undivided category either way.

He notes the five-feelings/three-feelings mutual subsumption as a simple but clarifying point about classification itself: dividing the same underlying reality into three categories or five is purely a matter of how finely one chooses to cut it, pain and sorrow both falling under the broader "painful" heading, pleasure and joy both falling under "pleasant," with neither scheme more truly correct than the other. He flags that the treatise is about to extend this same mutual-subsumption analysis to the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment next, promising to show precisely how those thirty-seven map onto the twenty-two faculties already examined at such length.

75. Section: Why Nine Kinds of "Complete Understanding" Are Not Faculties At All

The treatise continues its mutual-subsumption analysis. The thirty-seven factors of enlightenment subsume the five faculties of faith through wisdom, rather than the reverse, since six further items belonging to those thirty-seven factors — right speech, right action, and right livelihood from the noble eightfold path, together with joy, tranquility, and equanimity from the seven factors of awakening — fall outside the five faculties' own scope entirely, or, counted more simply, just four such items once joy, tranquility, and equanimity are treated together as a single category of feeling. The three faculties of realization likewise subsume the five faculties of faith through wisdom rather than being subsumed by them, since these three, understood as a phase of nine underlying faculties, also include mind, pleasure, joy, and equanimity, none of which belong to the five faculties of faith through wisdom themselves. The treatise then turns to nine kinds of complete understanding, asking which of the twenty-two faculties subsume them, and answers that none of the twenty-two faculties do at all, since complete understanding belongs instead to the specific category of understanding achieved through actually severing affliction — its own essential nature is the very cessation brought about through discernment, not any faculty as such, even though the discerning wisdom that leads to that cessation is itself, of course, a faculty. The treatise then names these nine kinds of complete understanding one by one, tracing the systematic severing of affliction across the desire realm and the combined form-and-formless realms, truth by truth: the first understanding covers what the path of vision severs concerning the desire realm's own truths of suffering and its origin; the second covers the very same two truths for the form and formless realms together; the third covers what the path of vision severs concerning the desire realm's truth of cessation; the fourth covers that same truth of cessation for the form and formless realms; the fifth covers the desire realm's truth of the path; and the sixth covers that same truth of the path for the form and formless realms — six distinct understandings in all, systematically covering every combination of the three higher truths across every relevant realm as severed specifically through the path of vision.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the point that complete understanding is not itself a faculty as a subtle but important distinction: the wisdom that leads to severing affliction is indeed a faculty, but the actual cessation that results from severing is a different kind of thing entirely, a genuine result rather than a capacity, and this treatise takes care never to blur that distinction even where the two are intimately related. He highlights the systematic structure of the first six understandings as a clean, exhaustive grid — three higher truths (suffering-and-origin combined, cessation, and the path) crossed with two realm-groupings (the desire realm alone, and the form and formless realms combined) — covering everything the path of vision severs without any gaps or overlaps. He flags that three further understandings, presumably covering what the path of cultivation itself severs, are likely to follow next, completing the full set of nine.

76. Section: Completing the Nine Understandings, and Opening the Buddha's Own Unique Qualities

The treatise completes its ninefold list: the seventh understanding is the permanent severing, through cultivation, of every remaining affliction bound to the desire realm, the "inferior" realm; the eighth is that same severing for the form realm, the "middle" realm; and the ninth is that same severing for the formless realm, the "sublime" realm, the most refined of all three, consisting of mind alone without any interference from matter whatsoever. All nine, the treatise explains, are established through just two underlying conditions: comprehending the truths, which establishes the first six, covering whatever affliction is severed specifically through the path of vision's own direct realization; and permanently transcending each realm in turn, which establishes the final three, covering whatever affliction is instead severed only through the path of cultivation's sustained, ongoing practice. The treatise notes that scripture actually organizes this same idea of "complete understanding" in several different ways elsewhere — sometimes as few as two or three categories, sometimes five, six, seven, or even ten — this ninefold version being simply one legitimate way among several of organizing exactly which affliction is severed and by which path, and, precisely because it concerns what is severed rather than any capacity for perceiving or acting, none of it is ever subsumed within the twenty-two faculties at all. With this, the treatise turns to the sixth and final topic within mutual subsumption: how the Buddha's own hundred and forty qualities, entirely unique to buddhahood and shared with neither hearers nor solitary realizers — the thirty-two excellent marks, the eighty minor marks of physical beauty, the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, the eighteen exclusive qualities, the three foundations of mindfulness, the three things never needing to be guarded, great compassion, the never-forgetting quality of mind, the wisdom knowing every kind of thing, the permanent destruction of even the subtlest habitual tendencies, and the four kinds of complete purity — relate to the twenty-two faculties already examined at such length throughout this entire fascicle.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the note on scripture's many different numbering schemes for "complete understanding" — two, three, five, six, seven, nine, or ten depending on the source — as a healthy reminder against treating any single numerical scheme as the one true accounting: different texts simply organize the same underlying process of severing affliction according to different useful criteria, none of them more correct than another. He highlights the transition to the Buddha's own hundred and forty unique qualities as this fascicle's own final, most exalted application of the entire twenty-two-faculty framework, tracing a path all the way from a hell-being's bare eight guaranteed faculties at the very start of this analysis to a fully awakened buddha's own unique and unsurpassed qualities at its very close.

77. Section: Which Faculties Ground a Buddha's Excellent Marks — Including Why Everything Tastes Like Nectar

The treatise answers that the excellent marks and minor marks are subsumed under the tongue and body faculties, together with the physical supporting bases of the male, eye, and tongue faculties. Among the thirty-two excellent marks, the broad and long tongue, capable of covering the entire face when extended, along with the Buddha's own perfect eloquence and his experience of the very finest taste in absolutely everything he eats, all rest on the tongue faculty — a quality the lecture explains through the Buddha's own mind being utterly free of greed, hatred, and delusion, so that whatever food actually enters his mouth becomes, in that very moment, the most delicious thing imaginable, even food that would ordinarily be poisonous transforming instead into something like ambrosia. The Buddha's own eyes, a deep, brilliant blue, rest on the physical basis of the eye faculty. And other physical marks distributed across the body rest on the body faculty and its own physical basis, together with the physical basis specifically associated with the male faculty, all these particular marks of physical excellence tracing back to these same specific faculties and their supporting bases, rather than to the mind faculty or to any of the wholesome or undefiled faculties examined at such length elsewhere in this fascicle.

He flags that this correlation is only the first of eight sub-topics covering the Buddha's full hundred and forty unique qualities, with the powers, fearlessness, and remaining exclusive qualities still to be examined in turn.

78. Section: The Empty Perfume Bottle — Why Only a Buddha Truly Severs Every Trace

The treatise completes its correlation of the Buddha's unique qualities. The ten powers rest on the wisdom faculty together with the faculty of complete knowledge. The four kinds of fearlessness rest on the five faculties of faith through wisdom together with that same faculty of complete knowledge — fearlessness in having permanently transcended the obstruction to knowledge itself, fearlessness in having permanently transcended affliction, fearlessness in teaching the very path of liberation without any possible refutation, and fearlessness regarding whatever genuinely obstructs that path. The three things a buddha never needs to guard follow this identical pattern. The three foundations of mindfulness are, strictly speaking, not themselves any faculty at all, but rather the freedom from greed and hatred that those same six faculties generate — describing the Buddha's own perfect equanimity toward three kinds of disciples: those who receive his teaching with genuine joy and diligent practice, toward whom he feels no attachment whatsoever; those who, like Devadatta, actively slander and oppose him, toward whom he feels no hatred whatsoever; and those of a mixed disposition, sometimes faithful and sometimes not, toward whom he shows neither special favor nor special aversion either way. Great compassion likewise rests not on any faculty directly, but on the freedom from hatred and delusion those same faculties generate. The never-forgetting quality of mind and the wisdom knowing every kind of thing both rest, like the ten powers, on wisdom together with complete knowledge. And the permanent severing of even the subtlest habitual tendencies rests not on any faculty itself, but on the complete severing of affliction those same faculties bring about — though here the treatise poses a striking further question: what residue remains even after an arhat or solitary realizer has already severed every affliction without exception, such that only a buddha's own severing counts as truly final and complete? The answer is the subtlest habitual tendency bound up with karmic ripening itself, something neither an arhat nor a solitary realizer has yet exhausted — illustrated by the image of a perfume bottle emptied of every last grain of incense, yet still, however faintly, retaining the incense's own lingering fragrance: an arhat's own liberation, however genuine and complete in its own terms, still carries this faintest trace, while only a buddha's own bottle carries no fragrance left at all.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the empty perfume bottle as one of this entire fascicle's most memorable closing images: liberation itself is not a single, undifferentiated achievement but admits its own subtlest gradations, an arhat's own genuine and complete freedom from affliction still carrying the faintest lingering trace that only a buddha's own attainment fully clears away. 79. Section: Four Kinds of Food That Nourish the Body and Mind Alike

With mutual subsumption now fully closed, the treatise turns to its fifth distinction within this second cycle: nourishment. It opens with a scriptural question — scripture speaks of four kinds of food, all capable of nourishing the faculties and their underlying great elements; what are these four, and how exactly do they nourish in this way? The answer names the four: solid food, taken in and consumed in the ordinary sense; the food of contact, nourishing through the very meeting of faculty and object; the food of mental volition, nourishing through the mind's own intentional engagement with what it hopes to obtain; and the food of consciousness, nourishing through consciousness's own continued activity sustaining the whole of embodied life. Through these four kinds of food together, the five physical sense faculties and the mind faculty are nourished, together with the very earth, water, fire, and wind elements that those faculties, and the coarser physical matter surrounding them, actually depend upon and are built from.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourfold classification of food as a genuinely expansive redefinition of what "nourishment" actually means, reaching well beyond ordinary eating: contact itself nourishes, intention itself nourishes, and consciousness's own continued activity nourishes, each in its own way sustaining some dimension of embodied life just as surely as solid food sustains the body. He notes that this fourfold scheme is the very same one already invoked earlier in this fascicle to explain the different foods proper to different realms — humans eating solid food, form-realm beings sustained instead by meditative joy — now returning here specifically to explain how these four kinds of nourishment actually keep the faculties and their underlying physical elements functioning at all, a fitting practical complement to everything this fascicle has said about the faculties' own nature and function.

80. Section: Why Sleep Doesn't Count as Food, Even Though It Also Sustains the Body

The treatise defines genuine food precisely: whatever is actually consumed and thereby nourishes the faculties and their underlying elements truly counts as food, while whatever fails to do so, however much it may be eaten, does not — and this same test applies equally to all four kinds of food, not solid food alone. Solid food nourishes specifically by strengthening consciousness itself, which in turn strengthens the faculties and elements that depend on it. The food of contact nourishes by absorbing a portion of joy, pleasure, or equanimity from a pleasant encounter, which likewise strengthens consciousness and, through consciousness, the faculties and elements in turn. The food of mental volition nourishes through a hopeful, joyful anticipation directed toward some desired future object, whether this hope is itself defiled, bound up with ordinary craving, or undefiled, bound up instead with the joy of genuine renunciation — either way strengthening consciousness and, through it, the faculties and elements once more. And the food of consciousness, understood as the storehouse consciousness itself, is nourished in turn by all three of the preceding foods, growing correspondingly more vigorous, and it is this very vigor that ultimately nourishes the faculties and elements directly. The treatise then explains why sleep, celibate discipline, and meditative attainment, despite also sustaining the body in their own way, are never classified as food: genuine food requires two distinct benefits together — a special, positive nourishment actively strengthening the faculties and elements, and simple freedom from harm — while sleep and the rest provide only the second of these, preventing damage to the body without ever actively strengthening it the way genuine food does. For this same reason, the treatise notes, the life faculty itself is likewise never classified as food, since without food to sustain it, the life faculty alone could never actually nourish the body at all.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this small, almost whimsical detail — instant, waste-free digestion meaning the heavens simply have no restrooms — as a welcome touch of lightness this deep into such a technically demanding fascicle, the kind of vivid, concrete image that makes an abstract cosmological claim about the desire-realm heavens genuinely memorable. He notes that this coarse/subtle distinction for solid food is likely to extend, by the same underlying principle, to the remaining three kinds of food as well, contact, mental volition, and consciousness each presumably admitting their own coarser and subtler forms in turn.

82. Section: Eating a Child's Flesh in the Desert — The Four Similes That Teach Disgust Toward Food

The treatise extends its coarse-and-subtle distinction to the remaining three foods: contact, mental volition, and consciousness are all coarse in the desire realm, where beings constantly reach outward, grasping at desired objects, and subtle in the form and formless realms, where beings are instead sustained by meditative joy rather than any outward grasping. The treatise then distinguishes beings by category — those already born, meaning any being currently existing and continuing to grow, and those still seeking existence, meaning an intermediate-state being caught between one life and the next, longing for a future rebirth — before explaining exactly how the four foods sustain each. For an already-born being, the four foods provide the very abiding and ease already described: ease being the special, positive nourishment that actively strengthens, and abiding being the simple freedom from harm that keeps a being intact. For a being still seeking existence, the process runs differently: through the three doors of solid food, contact, and mental volition, both karma and affliction — themselves entirely dependent on consciousness — continue to increase, and it is precisely through this same increase, feeding consciousness itself, that a being seeking existence is driven, without any interruption at all, to seize hold of its next rebirth. The lecture illustrates the deeply unappetizing nature of all four foods, once truly seen for what they are, through four vivid similes drawn from the early scriptures: solid food is to be regarded the way a desperate traveler lost in the wilderness might be forced to eat his own child's flesh simply to survive, taking no pleasure in it whatsoever, only revulsion; contact is to be regarded like a skinned, flayed cow being devoured alive by countless swarming insects; mental volition is to be regarded like a great mass of fire consuming one's own body; and consciousness itself is to be regarded like three hundred spears simultaneously piercing the body through — only by contemplating all four foods with this same unflinching honesty, the lecture notes, does craving for any of them finally lose its grip, opening the way to seeing that all four, ultimately dependent on conditions and empty of any real substance, can be transcended entirely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four food similes as among the most viscerally effective teachings in the entire early canon, deliberately designed to shock rather than merely inform: no amount of doctrinal explanation about food's ultimate emptiness lands with the same force as actually sitting with the image of eating one's own child's flesh out of sheer desperation, or three hundred spears piercing the body at once. He notes that these similes are not meant to induce a permanent revulsion toward eating itself, which would be both impossible and unhelpful, but rather to sever the specific craving and clinging that ordinarily accompanies food, sustaining the body without feeding the very attachment that keeps beings bound to it. He highlights the intermediate-state being's own dependence on this same fourfold nourishment as directly explaining why craving at the moment of death carries such weight — precisely the same point this fascicle illustrated earlier through King Bimbisāra's own fatal longing for a specific heavenly food.

83. Section: When Swallowed Food Actually Becomes Food, and Why Color Doesn't Count

The treatise turns to a detailed examination of solid food specifically, asking precisely when it becomes food in the technical sense. The answer is that this only occurs once the food has actually begun decomposing within the body — while merely being chewed and swallowed, it is technically still the food of contact, the pleasure of eating itself providing that particular nourishment, and only becomes solid food proper once broken down and absorbed as actual nourishment for the faculties and elements. Solid food is accordingly said to be subsumed under three sense-fields alone — scent, taste, and touch — never under the visual field of color and form; only once scent, taste, and touch actually undergo the process of decomposition does the nourishing function of food genuinely take effect. A citation from the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra explores at length why visual form specifically is excluded: several proposed reasons are considered and set aside — that form altogether lacks any capacity to nourish, that form is grasped too coarsely by the eye compared to the more penetrating grasping proper to true food, that form can nourish without direct bodily contact while true food requires it, and that seeing food would then satisfy hunger by sight alone, making the very act of offering food a waste, or that a monk would violate the precept against eating at improper times merely by looking at food. The genuinely accepted explanation is instead that food only becomes nourishment once it actually reaches the stage of physical decomposition — soaked, cooked, and broken down by the elements themselves — and form, unlike scent, taste, and touch, never undergoes this same kind of transformation, remaining simply what it visually is throughout. The apparent contradiction with the Buddha's own recorded praise of a donor's offered food as excellent in color, fragrance, and flavor together is resolved by recognizing that the Buddha spoke this way specifically to inspire the donor's own generous intention and confidence, not to reclassify color itself as a genuine kind of nourishment.

84. Section: Bitter Medicine, Sweet Poison, and the Invisible Wind That Feeds Beings in Hell

The treatise illustrates why solid food only truly becomes food at the moment of decomposition, not at the moment of eating: some foods, bitter and unpleasant medicines among them, actually cause discomfort while being eaten yet prove genuinely beneficial once digested, while other foods, sweet and delicious in the moment, prove genuinely harmful once digested instead, exactly as sugar or soda might taste wonderful going down while doing the body no favors afterward — food should accordingly be judged by what it actually does for the body once absorbed, never merely by how pleasant it tastes while being consumed. The treatise then addresses why, among the many conditions that actually sustain a being's continued existence — birth itself, the force of past karma, supernatural power, the mere coincidence of causal conditions not yet dispersing, and simple freedom from obstruction — only these four foods are specifically singled out and called "food": because they are the most common and universally applicable sustaining factor, because they are the easiest for anyone, however unlearned, to actually recognize as such, because they map so directly onto the four foundations of mindfulness that contemplating them provides a natural entry point into that very practice, and because they require constant, daily replenishment in a way the other sustaining factors do not. The treatise closes this discussion by noting an important exception: beings in the hells have no solid food whatsoever, sustained instead purely by the force of their own past karma, remaining unable to die however severely their bodies are damaged — yet even they are said to possess an exceedingly subtle form of solid food after all, a faint, moving current of wind circulating through their internal organs, so imperceptible that it goes essentially unmentioned in most accounts, yet still technically present, allowing even the most tormented beings to continue enduring their own suffering without release.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the bitter-medicine and sweet-poison examples as this treatise's own timeless nutritional wisdom, as relevant to a modern reader weighing sugar and soda against their own long-term health as to any reader many centuries ago: taste in the mouth is simply not a reliable guide to what actually benefits the body, only what happens afterward tells the true story. He highlights the image of an imperceptible wind sustaining even hell's own most tormented beings as a quietly devastating detail, closing this discussion of nourishment on the darkest possible note — a small mercy in the strictest technical sense, since it prevents an even greater cruelty of endless suffering compounded by starvation, yet inseparable from the horror of a suffering that, by design, cannot be escaped through death.

85. Section: One, Two, or Three — How Many Things Must a Faculty Depend On?

With nourishment now fully closed, the treatise turns to its sixth and final distinction within this second cycle: various logical propositions about the faculties. It first asks whether the faculties depend on the body, on their object, or on both together, distinguishing three possible counts of dependency. A faculty may depend on just one thing — the body alone — as with a physical faculty's own bare continuum, present and functioning as a physical basis even in a moment when no accompanying consciousness is actually engaging any object through it, such as an eye that remains physically intact and continuous even while its owner sits in meditation not actually seeing anything at all. A faculty may depend on two things — the body and its object together — as with that same physical faculty once consciousness genuinely is engaging an object through it, form actually being seen or sound actually being heard. And a faculty may depend on three things at once — the body, the object, and mind itself — as with the mind faculty and the other faculties associated with mind, such as the feeling faculties and the five faculties of faith through wisdom, while these operate within the form-possessing desire and form realms, where a physical body still exists to serve as their further support; in the formless realm, by contrast, these same mental faculties depend on only two things, their object and mind alone, since no physical body remains there to serve as any kind of support at all.
