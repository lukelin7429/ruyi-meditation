Cow Dung, Ganges Water, and What Purity Actually Means

The treatise opens its fifth kind of skillful understanding — the valid and the invalid, examining the very same cause-and-effect principle behind dependent origination from a fresh angle — beginning with the householder Cunda's question about ritual bathing, and the Buddha's own answer about what genuinely makes a person pure.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 57 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Cunda's Question About Ritual Purity, and the Fifth Skillful Understanding Opens

Before opening this fascicle's own topic, the lecture recalls a story about the householder Cunda, who once came before the Buddha and was asked which kind of ascetic or brahmin he found most admirable. Cunda described a group who served and made offerings to water itself, worshipping a certain water-deity, always carrying a staff and a water-jar, believing that washing the hands made a person pure, or that bathing in the Ganges washed away impurity; on the fifteenth day of each month, such practitioners would rise early to wash their hair with sesame husks and a certain fruit's rind, fast for the day, drape themselves in white cloth over their freshly washed hair, smear the ground with sacred cow dung, kneel at dawn holding fresh grass, touch the earth, and declare to the sky that they were as pure as the earth itself — anyone able to perform this whole sequence being considered pure, anyone unable to being considered impure. Cunda asked the Buddha whether such practitioners were, in fact, genuinely pure, since he wished to make offerings only to the truly pure. The Buddha answered plainly that this was not what purity actually meant: purity or impurity is decided by whether a person performs the ten wholesome deeds or the ten unwholesome ones — a mind full of greed, hatred, and delusion, given to killing, stealing, and sexual misconduct, remains impure no matter how much cow dung or fresh grass is involved, and yields exactly the dark result such deeds produce, while a person who performs the ten wholesome deeds remains genuinely pure whether or not any hair-washing or earth-touching ritual was ever performed at all. The Buddha's own answer to Cunda, the lecture notes, is itself a perfect illustration of what this fascicle's own new topic — the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid — is actually about: examining, with real precision, which claimed causes truly produce which results, and which do not, so that no one mistakes an invalid cause for a valid one, or a valid result for one it could never actually produce. With this story as introduction, the treatise formally opens its fifth kind of skillful understanding, following immediately upon the fourth: having now finished explaining the skillful understanding of dependent origination, it will next explain the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid — offering first a summary verse promising to cover, in order, this topic's own essential nature, its manifestation, the reason for its being taught, and finally a fourfold set of distinctions.

Chinese Source

如是已說緣起善巧。處非處善巧我今當說。總嗢柁南曰：體顯現初　門差別後。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus the skillful understanding of dependent origination has been explained. The skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid shall now be explained. The summary verse states: essential nature, manifestation, and the reason for its being taught come first; the fourfold gate of distinctions comes after.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Cunda's story as an unusually vivid opening for what could otherwise seem like a purely abstract logical topic: an elaborate purification ritual, complete with cow dung, fresh grass, and a declaration to the sky, set directly against the Buddha's own strikingly simple answer — purity is just the presence or absence of the ten wholesome deeds, nothing more elaborate required, and nothing more elaborate sufficient without it. He notes that this fifth kind of skillful understanding is not a wholly separate subject from the fourth, dependent origination, but rather the very same cause-and-effect principle viewed from a different angle — instead of tracing the twelve links forward, this topic examines directly which claimed causes genuinely lead to which results, calling a coherent, genuine cause-and-effect relationship "the valid" and an incoherent, false one "the invalid." He flags the summary verse's own promise — essential nature, then manifestation, then the reason for the teaching, then a fourfold set of distinctions — as the roadmap for this entire fascicle's opening topic, worth keeping in mind as each piece unfolds in turn.

2. Section: Defining "Valid" and "Invalid," and Why the Buddha Bothered Teaching the Difference

The treatise now defines its central terms with characteristic precision. What is "the valid"? It is whenever, for any given matter, there is no contradiction between the matter and its underlying principle — a cause producing exactly the kind of result it should, in accordance with the basic truth that such-and-such a cause yields such-and-such a result, without conflict between what actually happens and what genuinely should happen given real cause-and-effect reasoning. What, conversely, is "the invalid"? It is whenever, for any given matter, there is a real contradiction between the matter and its underlying principle — an outcome that clashes with, rather than follows from, the actual logic of cause and effect. This is named the essential nature of the valid and the invalid. The treatise then asks why the Buddha bothered to reveal this particular skillful understanding at all: it is precisely in order to demonstrate that correct wisdom regarding both defiled and pure phenomena, and the proper means of dealing with each, remains undamaged and unimpaired — defiled causes yielding defiled results, pure causes yielding pure results, exactly as they should, so that the Buddha's followers might hold onto correct reflection on cause and effect without ever losing it, rather than falling into confusion about which practices genuinely purify and which do not, as Cunda's own bathing-ritual practitioners had. The treatise then asks through how many approaches this entire topic of the valid and the invalid should be examined, and answers: four, since the Buddha taught the whole of the valid and the invalid using exactly four approaches — the gate of accomplishment, the gate of combination, the gate of attainment, and the gate of active manifestation — each addressing a different facet of how causes actually bring about their results, together comprehensive enough to cover every possible variety of both valid and invalid across all things whatsoever.

Chinese Source

問：何等為處？答：於彼彼事理無相違。問：何等非處？答：於彼彼事理有相違。是名處非處體。問：何故世尊顯示處非處善巧耶？答：為欲顯示染汙、清淨正方便智無失壞故。問：應以幾門觀察處非處耶？答：四。由佛世尊但以四門宣說一切處非處故。何等為四？一、成辦門，二、合會門，三、證得門，四、現行門。問：何緣以此四門說處非處？答：為欲示現遍一切種差別門故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: what is the valid? Answer: whenever, for a given matter, there is no contradiction with its principle. Question: what is the invalid? Answer: whenever, for a given matter, there is a contradiction with its principle. This is called the essential nature of the valid and the invalid. Question: why does the Blessed One reveal the skillful understanding of the valid and the invalid? Answer: in order to reveal that correct wisdom and skillful means regarding both the defiled and the pure remain undamaged and unimpaired. Question: through how many approaches should the valid and the invalid be observed? Answer: four, since the Blessed One taught the whole of the valid and the invalid using only four approaches. What are the four? First, the gate of accomplishment; second, the gate of combination; third, the gate of attainment; fourth, the gate of active manifestation. Question: for what reason are the valid and the invalid taught through these four approaches? Answer: in order to reveal every distinguishing approach across every category without exception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the definitions of "valid" and "invalid" as deceptively simple — no contradiction with underlying principle, or a genuine contradiction with it — yet notes that the entire remainder of this topic exists precisely to work out what "underlying principle" really means across an enormous range of specific cases, from the workings of karma to the structure of the path itself. He connects this topic directly back to the lecture's own citation, in an earlier fascicle, of the Discourse Basis's treatment of the very same six kinds of skillful understanding used for meditative contemplation — this fifth kind, he notes, is what actually protects a practitioner from what the text calls "taking a non-cause for a cause, or a non-result for a result," precisely the mistake Cunda's own bathing-ritual practitioners had fallen into. He flags the fourfold gate — accomplishment, combination, attainment, active manifestation — as this fascicle's own organizing structure going forward, each gate offering its own distinct lens on the same underlying question of what genuinely causes what.

3. Section: Why the Eye Can Never Hear, and Why Earth Can Never Turn Into Fire

The treatise's detailed elaboration of the four gates opens with a general question — what exactly counts as "every distinguishing approach" — before working through the first gate, the gate of accomplishment, in careful detail. Whatever an effect can actually be brought to completion by is subsumed under this gate and called valid; whatever exceeds what a given cause could ever actually accomplish, and so fails to be subsumed under this gate, is called invalid. Three specific kinds of such overreach are named: the sense faculties exceeding what they can actually do, the four great elements exceeding what they can actually do, and life's own sustaining resources exceeding what they can actually do. Concerning the sense faculties, the treatise states plainly that there is no place, no time, no circumstance whatsoever in which the eye could ever hear sound, smell scent, taste flavor, or feel touch — only seeing form is ever valid for the eye, and the same reasoning extends, by analogy, to each of the other four faculties in turn, each faculty capable only of its own single kind of activity, never another's. The lecture pauses here to note a genuine scholastic debate about whether enlightened beings possess a special ability for their faculties to function interchangeably — some commentators holding that a realized meditator's six faculties lose all such restriction entirely, the eye becoming able to hear as freely as it sees; others holding instead that each faculty's own domain always surrounds itself with a halo of the other four faculties' own subtle sensitive matter, so that what looks like the eye hearing is really the ear's own faculty, present all along in that same location, quietly doing the hearing — with this treatise itself simply affirming, for ordinary purposes, that the eye sees and nothing more, leaving the more exotic case of the fully realized to more specialized discussions elsewhere. Concerning the four great elements, there is likewise no place or time in which earth could ever perform water's function of moistening, fire's function of warming, or wind's function of moving — earth can only ever perform earth's own function, and the same reasoning extends, by analogy, to water, fire, and wind themselves, each mutually incapable of producing what belongs properly only to another.

Chinese Source

云何一切種差別？謂依初成辦門，彼所不攝餘差別相，我當顯示。當知此差別略說有三種。一、諸根越所作故；二、大種越所作故；三、資生越所作故。諸根越所作者，謂無處無位眼能聞聲、齅香、嘗味、覺諸觸等，必無是處；能見諸色，斯有是處。如眼根，如是所餘色根一一相望越用差別，如應當知。大種越所作者，謂無處無位地能造作水、火、風用，必無是處；能作地用，斯有是處。如是所餘大種展轉相望越用差別，如應當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is every distinguishing approach? Relying on the first, the gate of accomplishment, I shall reveal whatever remaining distinguishing characteristic is not subsumed under it. It should be understood that this distinction is, in brief, of three kinds: first, the sense faculties exceeding what they can do; second, the four great elements exceeding what they can do; third, life's sustaining resources exceeding what they can do. Concerning the sense faculties exceeding what they can do: there is no place, no circumstance, in which the eye could hear sound, smell scent, taste flavor, or feel touch — this is never valid; that it sees form is valid. As with the eye faculty, so too, by analogy, should the mutual overreach of each of the remaining faculties be understood. Concerning the four great elements exceeding what they can do: there is no place, no circumstance, in which earth could perform the function of water, fire, or wind — this is never valid; that it performs earth's own function is valid. As with earth, so too, by analogy, should the mutual overreach of the remaining great elements be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the faculties-and-elements discussion as this fascicle's own gentle, almost playful way of building up to a serious point through the most obvious possible examples — of course the eye cannot hear, of course earth cannot burn like fire, the reader is meant to nod along easily before the same reasoning is extended to far less obvious cases later in the fascicle. He highlights the scholastic aside on interchangeable faculties as a small but genuine window into how differently even careful commentators can read the same tradition: one view granting the fully realized an almost magical sensory freedom, the other preserving each faculty's own strict boundary even for saints by quietly relocating the explanation to overlapping subtle matter instead — a disagreement this treatise itself declines to adjudicate, content to state the ordinary rule and move on. He notes that "valid" and "invalid" are being illustrated here in their most concrete, almost proverbial form — an eye that hears, an earth that burns — precisely so that the same underlying logic will be recognizable later when the treatise turns to far subtler claims about karma, liberation, and the nature of mind.

4. Section: Milking a Cow's Horn, Churning Butter from Water, and Five More Reasons Cause and Effect Cannot Be Faked

The treatise turns to its third category of overreach — life's own sustaining resources exceeding what they can actually accomplish — illustrated through five vivid, almost proverbial examples. First, concerning seeds and sprouts: there is no place or time in which a seed of one kind could ever produce a sprout of a different kind — a melon seed can never yield a bean sprout — only a seed's own kind ever produces a sprout of that same kind, precisely the everyday wisdom captured in the saying that melons are reaped from melon seeds and beans from bean seeds, cause and result never contradicting one another. Second, concerning the extraction of milk: there is no place or time in which squeezing a cow's horn could ever yield milk — only squeezing the udder itself is valid. Third, concerning the production of cream: there is no place or time in which churning a jar of plain water could ever yield cream — only churning yogurt, from which cream is properly derived, is valid. Fourth, concerning the extraction of oil: there is no place or time in which pressing sand could ever yield oil — only pressing sesame and similar oil-bearing seeds is valid. Fifth, concerning the production of fire: there is no place or time in which drilling into damp wood could ever produce fire — only drilling into dry wood is valid. All such examples, the treatise concludes, should be observed as illustrating this first gate's own distinguishing characteristic — the gate of accomplishment — through the ordinary stuff of daily life itself: sense faculties, elements, and life's own sustaining resources, each incapable of producing anything beyond its own proper function, however cleverly a skilled debater might try to argue otherwise. With this gate now fully illustrated, the treatise turns to the second gate, the gate of combination, asking what specifically distinguishes it, and answering first through the example of light and darkness: there is no place or time in which light and darkness could ever coexist together in a single location — wherever there is light, there is no darkness, and wherever there is darkness, there is no light, this mutual exclusion itself being what is valid — a principle the lecture extends to like-minded companions naturally gathering together, since those who share a habit of taking life tend to keep company with others of the same habit, exactly as those committed to the five precepts tend to keep company with each other in turn, genuinely opposed dispositions no more able to comfortably combine than light and darkness themselves.

資生越所作者，謂無處無位從餘類種餘類芽生，必無是處；唯自種類，斯有是處。無處無位𣪬牛角等而出於乳，必無是處；𣪬彼乳房，斯有是處。無處無位鑽搖水瓶而出生酥，必無是處；鑽搖於酪，斯有是處。無處無位壓沙出油，必無是處；壓苣蕂等，斯有是處。無處無位鑽濕木等而出於火，必無是處；鑽於乾木，斯有是處。如是等類，應當觀察初處非處門差別之相。云何第二處非處門差別？謂無處無位光明、黑闇一時合會，無有是處；若有一處無第二生，斯有是處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Concerning life's sustaining resources exceeding what they can do: there is no place, no circumstance, in which a sprout of one kind could arise from a seed of a different kind — this is never valid; only a seed's own kind is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which squeezing a cow's horn could yield milk — this is never valid; squeezing the udder is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which churning a water-jar could yield cream — this is never valid; churning yogurt is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which pressing sand could yield oil — this is never valid; pressing sesame and the like is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which drilling damp wood could yield fire — this is never valid; drilling dry wood is valid. Cases such as these should be observed as illustrating the first gate's own distinguishing characteristic. What distinguishes the second gate of the valid and the invalid? There is no place, no circumstance, in which light and darkness could combine together at one time — this is not valid; that wherever one is present the other does not arise is valid.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five household examples — seed and sprout, horn and udder, water and yogurt, sand and sesame, damp and dry wood — as a small masterclass in teaching through concrete, everyday observation rather than abstract argument, precisely the kind of illustration a farmer, a herder, or a cook would immediately recognize as true without needing any philosophy at all. He notes the light-and-darkness example opening the second gate as a natural bridge from physical impossibility to moral and social observation: just as two incompatible physical states cannot occupy the same place at the same time, incompatible dispositions — killing versus precept-keeping, for instance — tend not to comfortably combine within the same circle of friends either, birds of a feather gathering together in exactly the way this gate's own name, "combination," suggests. He flags this social extension as worth remembering going forward, since the treatise appears ready to move from purely physical examples toward increasingly psychological and karmic ones as this second gate continues to unfold.

5. Section: Closing the Combination Gate — Minds That Cannot Overlap, and Fortunes That Cannot Mix

The gate of combination continues through five further examples. Coarse water and coarse fire, the kind perceivable directly by eye, ear, or touch, can never coexist together in one place at one time — wherever there is water there is no fire, and wherever there is fire there is no water, this being the very same mutual incompatibility ordinary language points to when describing two people or two factions as utterly "water and fire" toward one another, unable to coexist at all. Two coarse material aggregates, similarly, can never occupy exactly the same location at the same time, since coarse matter is inherently obstructive — a table and a chair cannot both occupy precisely the same spot — though this restriction applies only to coarse aggregates and not to the subtlest particles themselves, which, unlike coarse matter, can genuinely interpenetrate other coarse matter without displacing it, exactly as the element earth's own subtlest particles can pervade water's own coarse matter without displacing it in the least. Two instances of the very same class of mind or mental factor — two eye-consciousnesses, say, sharing the same faculty and the same object — can never arise together at exactly the same moment, since mind, unlike matter, always requires its own immediately preceding moment to cease before the next moment of that same class can arise, one mental moment strictly following another rather than ever truly coexisting alongside it. Two instances of the same class of wholesome and unwholesome mental states, or wholesome and neutral, or unwholesome and neutral, or painful and pleasant feeling, likewise can never coexist within a single moment, since their very natures are mutually exclusive — only one such quality is ever actually present in the mind at any given instant. And desired and undesired karmic results — birth as a human or a god as against birth as a hungry ghost, an animal, or a hell-being — can likewise never coexist within one and the same life: whichever result a being actually experiences, it is always exactly one of these, never some blend of both at once. All such cases, the treatise concludes, illustrate this second gate's own distinguishing characteristic, closing the gate of combination entirely.

Chinese Source

無處無位麤分水火一時合會，無有是處；隨有一種，斯有是處。無處無位二麤色聚同據一處，無有是處；若一極微，斯有是處。無處無位同一種類二心心法俱時合會，無有是處；一一而生，斯有是處。無處無位同一種類若善不善、若善無記不善無記、若苦若樂俱時合會，無有是處；隨有一種，斯有是處。無處無位愛非愛果俱時合會，無有是處；若隨有一，斯有是處。如是等類，應當觀察第二處非處門差別之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no place, no circumstance, in which coarse water and fire could combine together at one time — this is not valid; that only one of them is present is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which two coarse material aggregates could occupy the same location — this is not valid; that a single subtle particle can do so is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which two mental phenomena of the same class could combine together at the same moment — this is not valid; that they arise one at a time is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which wholesome and unwholesome, wholesome and neutral, unwholesome and neutral, or painful and pleasant states of the same class could combine together — this is not valid; that only one such state is present is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which a desired and an undesired result could combine together — this is not valid; that only one is present is valid. Cases such as these should be observed as illustrating the second gate's own distinguishing characteristic.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the distinction drawn here between coarse matter and the subtlest particles as a genuinely subtle piece of Abhidharma physics folded into an otherwise everyday-sounding discussion: two ordinary objects cannot share the same space, yet the very finest particles of one element can pervade another's coarse matter without displacing it, exactly the reasoning this treatise uses elsewhere to explain how the four elements can be present together within a single grain of matter. He notes the mind-moment example as a compact restatement of a principle this fascicle has relied on before — mental phenomena, unlike physical ones, always arise one at a time in strict succession, never truly simultaneously, a subtlety worth remembering since it will matter again whenever this treatise discusses the pace and structure of meditative experience. He treats the desired/undesired-result example as this gate's own quiet reminder that a single life's overall karmic result, however mixed its individual moments may feel, is always fundamentally one thing or the other at its root — never some genuine blend of a fortunate and an unfortunate rebirth occurring at once.

6. Section: Why a Barren Woman Cannot Bear Children, and Why Liberation Cannot Be Attained Without the Proper Provisions

The treatise turns to its third gate, the gate of attainment, working through a further set of examples. There is no place or time in which a woman whose reproductive faculty is destroyed could ever bear a child — only a woman without such an impairment can, this being valid. There is no place or time in which a person born without proper male faculties could ever father a child — only a properly constituted man can, this being valid. There is no place or time in which a blind person could see form, a deaf person could hear sound, or someone with a destroyed sense of smell or taste could smell or taste — only someone whose faculties remain intact can do these things, this being valid. There is no place or time in which someone lacking the proper provisions — genuine learning, reflection, and meditative cultivation, and the corresponding development of ethical discipline, concentration, and wisdom — could, in this very life, attain the liberation of a trainee or an adept beyond training; only someone who has genuinely assembled these provisions can attain such liberation, this being valid. And there is no place or time in which someone who has not yet attained the noble path could realize nirvāṇa, or the awakening of a hearer, a solitary realizer, or unsurpassed perfect awakening itself; only someone who has genuinely attained the noble path can realize any of these, this being valid.

Chinese Source

云何第三處非處門差別？謂無處無位石女生兒，無有是處；若非石女，斯有是處。無處無位生半擇迦能生男女，無有是處；若諸丈夫，斯有是處。無處無位盲眼見色、聾耳聞聲、鼻舌壞者齅香嘗味，無有是處；諸根不壞，斯有是處。無處無位未具資糧，於現法中證學無學究竟解脫，無有是處；已具資糧，斯有是處。無處無位未得聖道能證涅槃，及證聲聞、獨覺菩提，若證無上正等菩提，無有是處；已得聖道，斯有是處。

Ru-Yi Translation

What distinguishes the third gate of the valid and the invalid? There is no place, no circumstance, in which a barren woman could bear a child — this is not valid; that a woman without such impairment can is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one born without proper male faculties could father sons or daughters — this is not valid; that a properly constituted man can is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which the blind could see form, the deaf could hear sound, or one with destroyed nose or tongue could smell or taste — this is not valid; that one whose faculties are unimpaired can is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one lacking the proper provisions could, in this present life, attain the ultimate liberation of a trainee or an adept beyond training — this is not valid; that one who has assembled the provisions can is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one who has not attained the noble path could realize nirvāṇa, the awakening of a hearer or solitary realizer, or unsurpassed perfect awakening — this is not valid; that one who has attained the noble path can is valid.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this third gate shifts the discussion's register from purely physical impossibility toward something closer to the spiritual path itself: a barren woman and a damaged eye are physical facts, but "lacking the proper provisions" and "not having attained the noble path" describe a practitioner's own spiritual condition, the treatise treating both kinds of impossibility with exactly the same rigorous logic. He highlights the provisions example — genuine learning, reflection, cultivation, and the corresponding ethical discipline, concentration, and wisdom — as a quiet but firm rebuke of any shortcut mentality in spiritual practice: liberation is not granted arbitrarily or by wishing, but strictly follows from having actually done the necessary preparatory work, exactly as a barren woman cannot simply wish a child into being. He flags that this same gate of attainment is about to turn to still more examples — concerning food, realms of rebirth, and the body itself — extending this same principle into territory that will include one of this fascicle's own most affecting stories.

7. Section: King Bimbisāra's Fatal Craving for Heavenly Food, and Closing the Attainment Gate

The gate of attainment continues with a sixth example concerning realms and their proper food: there is no place or time in which a human being could sustain and nourish the body by eating grass and the like, the proper food of animals; nor in which the heavenly gods could eat human food; nor in which beings of the form and formless realms could take solid food at all — a human eating an animal's food, or a god eating a human's, being no more fitting than a person eating what a dog eats, since each realm's own food is calibrated to its own being, the higher realms sustained instead by the very joy of meditative absorption, having long since abandoned scent, taste, and the corresponding faculties of nose and tongue altogether. The lecture illustrates this with a story from the Buddha's own time: King Bimbisāra, imprisoned by his own son Ajātaśatru and left to starve, wondered silently why the Buddha did not come to rescue him; the Buddha, perceiving this thought, sent Maudgalyāyana by supernatural power to explain that he had already done everything within his power to help, and that what remained was Bimbisāra's own karma to bear, which Maudgalyāyana should now help him accept through teaching. Ravenously hungry, Bimbisāra asked Maudgalyāyana which realm's food was most delicious, and Maudgalyāyana described in turn what the Four Heavenly Kings ate, what Śakra's own retinue ate, and what beings throughout the six heavens of the desire realm ate — but Bimbisāra, consumed by longing for that very food, could take in none of the genuine teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness that followed, fixating instead only on the description of the especially fragrant food enjoyed in Vaiśravaṇa's own heavenly realm. Though his accumulated wholesome karma should have carried him to rebirth in Tuṣita heaven, his overwhelming craving for that one remembered food, combined with an intermediate-state being's own nature of feeding on scent alone, drew him instead toward the fragrance he could not stop craving, and he was reborn in that lower heaven instead of the higher one his karma had actually earned — a sobering illustration, the lecture notes, of how a mind's final overwhelming craving at the moment of death can redirect even karma that had already, in principle, secured a better destination. The gate of attainment closes with a seventh and final example: there is no place or time in which a being could obtain a human body without first relinquishing whatever body-form it currently holds — a hell-being, for instance, cannot become human while still bound to its hellish form, and must first exhaust that karmic term and be released from it before any new body can be obtained — this relinquishing-before-obtaining principle applying, by analogy, to every possible transition between one body-form and another.

無處無位人趣有情以傍生趣草等飲食以充節會，若諸天眾食人飲食，色無色界食諸段食，無有是處；與此相違，斯有是處。無處無位不捨那落迦所有身形而得人身，如是不捨所餘身形而得餘身，無有是處；捨已方得，斯有是處。如是等類，應當觀察第三處非處門差別之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no place, no circumstance, in which a human being could nourish the body's limbs with an animal's food such as grass, in which the heavenly multitudes could eat human food, or in which the form and formless realms could take solid food — this is not valid; the opposite of this is valid. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one could obtain a human body without relinquishing whatever body-form belongs to the hells, and likewise for obtaining any other body without relinquishing whatever body-form one currently holds — this is not valid; that one obtains a new body only after relinquishing the old is valid. Cases such as these should be observed as illustrating the third gate's own distinguishing characteristic.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats King Bimbisāra's story as one of this fascicle's most quietly devastating illustrations, precisely because nothing supernatural or villainous causes his misfortune — only his own uncontrolled craving, arising at the single worst possible moment, redirects karma that had already, by his own genuine goodness, secured him a better rebirth. He draws the practical lesson explicitly: a dying mind's final, overwhelming preoccupation carries enormous weight in determining what comes next, which is exactly why traditional guidance for the dying emphasizes keeping the mind fixed on wholesome objects, rather than allowing hunger, pain, or unfulfilled longing to seize the very last moments of attention. He notes the closing point about relinquishing one body before obtaining another as a small but important corrective to any sentimental picture of rebirth as a smooth, gradual drift from one life to the next — a hell-being's own karmic term must run its full course, with genuine finality, before anything new can begin, exactly as no new body can be obtained while still bound to an old one.

8. Section: The Fourth Gate Opens — Elements That Never Betray Their Own Nature, and Why a Greedy Mind Cannot Feel Free

The treatise turns to its fourth and final gate, the gate of active manifestation, working through it with a fresh set of examples grounded in how things actually behave when active. There is no place or time in which earth could relinquish its own characteristic of solidity and take on the characteristic of another element instead — earth stays hard, water stays moist, fire stays warm, and wind stays mobile, each element invariably holding to its own characteristic rather than ever surrendering it, this being valid, and the same reasoning extends, by analogy, to water, fire, and wind in turn. There is no place or time in which someone born in the desire realm, without having attained the heavenly eye, could nonetheless see the forms belonging to the heavens — an ordinary person's eye sees only what belongs to the human realm, this being valid, and the same reasoning extends to the other faculties: without the heavenly ear, heavenly sounds likewise cannot be heard. There is no place or time in which someone whose mind remains covered by greed, whose greed has not yet been severed, could nonetheless feel genuinely free of attachment toward wealth and material gain — only someone free of such covering greed, or who has already severed it, can feel that genuine freedom, this being valid, and the very same reasoning extends to hatred and delusion: wherever the corresponding affliction still covers the mind and remains unsevered, the corresponding freedom from anger or confusion, when the triggering circumstance actually arises, simply cannot occur.

Chinese Source

云何第四處非處門差別？謂無處無位地捨自相成餘界相，無有是處；不捨自相，斯有是處。如地，如是餘大種，如應當知。無處無位生長欲界不得天眼見諸天色，無有是處；見人中色，斯有是處。如是餘根，如應當知。無處無位有貪愛者，貪愛覆蔽、貪愛未斷，而於財利心離染著，無有是處；與此相違，斯有是處。如是瞋、癡，隨應當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

What distinguishes the fourth gate of the valid and the invalid? There is no place, no circumstance, in which earth could relinquish its own characteristic and take on the characteristic of another element — this is not valid; that it does not relinquish its own characteristic is valid. As with earth, so too, by analogy, should the remaining great elements be understood. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one born and matured in the desire realm, without the heavenly eye, could see the forms of the heavens — this is not valid; that one sees the forms of the human realm is valid. As with this, so too, by analogy, should the remaining faculties be understood. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one whose mind is covered by greed, whose greed remains unsevered, could nonetheless be free of attachment toward wealth and gain — this is not valid; the opposite of this is valid. As with this, so too, by analogy, should hatred and delusion be understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourth gate's opening examples as circling back, quite deliberately, to territory the very first gate already covered — elements holding to their own nature, faculties limited to their own proper domain — but now examined specifically through the lens of active manifestation rather than mere capacity, a difference he characterizes as the difference between what something could in principle do and what it is actually doing right now. He highlights the greed example as the fascicle's own transition point from physical and perceptual impossibility toward a directly practical, psychological application: an affliction that has not actually been severed does not simply lie dormant and harmless, but actively covers the mind and actively prevents the corresponding freedom, whatever a person might sincerely wish or claim about their own inner state. He notes that this same reasoning is about to be extended even further, into the specific conditions required for genuinely practicing the path itself — a topic that will lead, a little further on, into one of this fascicle's richest discussions, on what it actually means to see the Buddha.

9. Section: No Genuine Meditation Without First Subduing Affliction, and What It Truly Means to See the Buddha

The fourth gate continues: there is no place or time in which someone could genuinely cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness while still entangled by the active manifestation of greed and every other root and secondary affliction — only once such afflictions have been suppressed, or their arising otherwise prevented, can this cultivation genuinely proceed, this being valid, and the very same principle extends, by analogy, to every other factor belonging to enlightenment, from the four right efforts through the noble eightfold path itself: none of these can genuinely be cultivated while affliction remains actively manifest, precisely because such cultivation itself functions as the means by which afflictions come to be severed in the first place. A further example turns directly to what it means to truly see the Buddha: there is no place or time in which someone who has not relinquished contentious views, contentious desires, and a mind still given to contention — none of which the Buddha ever sanctions — could nonetheless genuinely behold the Buddha face to face; only by relinquishing these, or by the specific manner of seeing the Buddha that the Buddha himself does sanction, is such genuine seeing possible. The lecture explains that contention arises from three sources: clinging to particular views and doctrines, in dispute with those who hold different ones; craving directed at particular objects, disputed with those who would also possess them; and an already-defiled mind acting out through bodily, verbal, or mental misconduct provoked by resentment at being slighted — and that anyone still caught up in these three, even a nominal disciple sitting at the Buddha's own feet, cannot be said to have genuinely realized dependent origination's own true nature, and hence cannot be said to have genuinely seen the Buddha at all, exactly as both the early scriptures and the Mahāyāna alike affirm: to see dependent origination is to see the Dharma, and to see the Dharma is to see the Buddha — never a matter of merely observing his physical form or bodily sound.

Chinese Source

無處無位不斷貪等一切煩惱、隨煩惱纏，修四念住，無有是處；與此相違，斯有是處。如修念住，如是所餘菩提分法，當知亦爾。無處無位於如來所不捨諍見、諍欲、諍心，若不開許，而能正面覩於如來，無有是處；若捨若許，斯有是處。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is no place, no circumstance, in which one could cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness while not having severed greed and every other root and secondary affliction currently manifest — this is not valid; the opposite of this is valid. As with the foundations of mindfulness, so too should the remaining factors of enlightenment be understood. There is no place, no circumstance, in which one who has not relinquished contentious views, contentious desires, and a contentious mind in the Buddha's presence, and whom the Buddha has not sanctioned, could genuinely behold the Buddha face to face — this is not valid; that one relinquishes these, or is sanctioned by the manner the Buddha permits, is valid.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the bodhipakṣa-dharma example as a precise, practical warning against a common misunderstanding: meditation is not a technique that works regardless of one's underlying mental state, but something that itself requires affliction to already be held in check before it can function at all — trying to cultivate the four foundations of mindfulness while greed or anger is actively manifest is, on this treatise's own logic, simply not possible, however much effort is exerted. He treats the three sources of contention — view, desire, and an already-defiled mind's reactive misconduct — as worth distinguishing carefully in one's own experience, since each calls for a slightly different remedy: settling a disputed opinion is not the same task as releasing a competitive craving, which is again not the same task as simply cooling down a provoked temper. He flags the closing citation — to see dependent origination is to see the Dharma, and to see the Dharma is to see the Buddha — as the very same maxim this fascicle's own predecessor invoked when first introducing dependent origination, now returning here to anchor an entirely different, and unusually rich, discussion about what "seeing the Buddha" genuinely requires.

10. Section: Five Verses on Seeing the Buddha, and a Gas-Station Attendant Who Had Never Seen a Monk

The lecture unpacks the "seeing the Buddha" discussion further by citing the Diamond Sūtra's own famous warning: "If one seeks to see me through physical form, or to seek me through sound, that person walks a wrong path and cannot see the Tathāgata" — mistaking the Buddha's thirty-two excellent physical marks, or his sixty kinds of beautiful voice, for the Buddha himself, rather than for the genuine liberating wisdom those marks merely accompany. Five verses from this treatise's own earlier discussion of wisdom gained through reflection unpack exactly what kind of person can, and cannot, be said to genuinely see the Buddha. The first describes an ordinary person still gripped by craving, who sees the Buddha's physical marks and concludes, correctly enough, "this must be a buddha" — yet remains someone who, the moment an unfulfilled craving or a persuasive false teacher arrives, can still be led away into wrong views or even into slandering the Three Jewels, since recognizing the Buddha's outward form is not the same as having genuinely realized anything at all. The second describes a meditator, even one trained in non-Buddhist traditions, who through the heavenly eye perceives the Buddha inwardly from a great distance and thinks, "this is the Buddha" — yet, lacking any actual grounding in genuine teaching, remains just as susceptible to being swayed by a more persuasive outside doctrine, meditative attainment alone proving no more reliable a foundation than ordinary craving-driven recognition. The third describes someone with no meditative attainment at all, who nonetheless sees the Buddha's physical body with ordinary eyes and recognizes him as the Buddha, yet who, lacking any inner realization to anchor that recognition, remains equally susceptible to later being swayed away by outside voices. The fourth describes someone blocked by especially heavy karmic obstruction, who has neither the inner meditative perception nor even the outer physical perception to recognize the Buddha at all — the most impoverished of the four failed conditions. The lecture pauses here to note how genuinely rare even the ordinary "outer seeing" described in the third verse can be, recalling a story of a young attendant at a gas station who, when a customer casually referred to the monastic beside her as "miss," admitted she had simply never seen a Buddhist monk before in her entire life — a small but telling illustration that mere physical exposure to genuine teaching cannot be taken for granted even now, let alone the far deeper kind of seeing these verses are actually concerned with.

Chinese Source

若以色量我　以音聲尋我　欲貪所執持　彼不能知我。若於內了知　於外不能見　由內果觀察　彼音聲所引。若於內無知　於外而能見　由外果觀察　亦音聲所引。若於內無知　於外不能見　彼普障愚夫　亦音聲所引。若於內了知　於外亦能見　英雄出離慧　非音聲所引。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one takes my measure through physical form, and seeks me through sound, one who is held by craving cannot truly know me. If one knows inwardly but cannot see outwardly, one is led by sound even while observing an inward result. If one does not know inwardly yet can see outwardly, one is likewise led by sound even while observing an outward result. If one neither knows inwardly nor can see outwardly, such a thoroughly obstructed, foolish being is likewise led by sound. If one knows inwardly and can also see outwardly, that hero of liberating wisdom is not led by sound.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five verses as an unusually precise diagnostic tool, distinguishing four separate ways that seeing the Buddha's own outward or inward form can fail to constitute genuinely "seeing" him at all — craving-driven recognition, ungrounded meditative perception, ordinary sightless-of-anything-deeper recognition, and total obstruction — each one sharing the same underlying vulnerability of being swayable by a sufficiently persuasive outside voice, since none of them rest on any actual realized wisdom of their own. He treats the gas-station story as a small, almost humorous corrective against complacency: it is easy to assume that recognizing a Buddhist monk on sight is the most basic possible level of religious literacy, yet even this ordinary threshold cannot be taken for granted in every place and every generation, however commonplace it may feel to a longtime practitioner. He notes that only the fifth verse's own "hero" — someone with both genuine inward realization and outward perception together — is described as truly unswayable, setting up the fascicle's next story as an illustration of exactly this kind of unshakeable resolve.

11. Section: The Dying Monk Who Asked to Be Carried Outside, and Reached Arhatship Before He Died

The lecture illustrates the fifth verse's own genuine "hero" with a story from the Buddha's own time. A monk of unshakeable faith in the Three Jewels, though he had not yet attained any of the noble fruits, fell gravely ill; rather than continue suffering without being able to practice, he asked his attendants to carry him outside, intending to end his own life, insisting all the while that his faith in the Dharma would never waver regardless. Two devas, observing this, discussed his situation — one lamenting that his suffering had driven him to attempt suicide, the other predicting that he would in fact attain the noble path — and reported what they had seen to the Buddha; when the Buddha's own disciples asked whether this was really so, the Buddha confirmed it and sent them to see for themselves. As he was carried outward, the dying monk poured his full remaining strength into contemplating the Buddha's own teaching on impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, and in that concentrated effort swiftly passed through the first, second, third, and fourth fruits in immediate succession, attaining arhatship, and died an arhat shortly afterward. When the disciples returned and asked the Buddha why he had not intervened to stop the suicide, the Buddha explained his own actual criterion plainly: he intervenes to stop such an act, and teaches the person the Dharma instead, whenever that person has no real hope of attaining arhatship and would otherwise simply carry unresolved karma into a further round of suffering — but when someone genuinely has the capacity to attain nirvāṇa in that very effort, there is no need to intervene at all, since that person already possesses what is needed to achieve liberation. The lecture is careful to note that this is not, in any way, an endorsement of suicide as a spiritual method — the monk's own extraordinary outcome depended entirely on a rare, unwavering faith already cultivated to something like the threshold of genuine sagehood, sharpened rather than shaken by the direct confrontation with his own body's imminent end, a combination the Buddha's own careful discernment alone could judge, and not a template for anyone to imitate.

Chinese Source

觀見如來，略由二種。一、由勝義，二、由世俗。由勝義者，謂諸賢聖，除斷、調伏、超越欲貪，得聖慧眼。彼由如是聖慧眼故，於內證解如來法身。由世俗者，謂諸異生，或以天眼、或以肉眼觀見如來色身。

Ru-Yi Translation

Beholding the Tathāgata is, in brief, of two kinds. First, through ultimate truth; second, through conventional truth. Through ultimate truth: the noble ones, having eliminated, subdued, and transcended craving, attain the noble wisdom-eye, and through that noble wisdom-eye inwardly realize and understand the Tathāgata's own dharma-body. Through conventional truth: ordinary beings, whether through the heavenly eye or the fleshly eye, behold the Tathāgata's physical form.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own explicit criterion for when he intervenes — no real hope of arhatship, versus a genuine and immediate capacity for it — as the story's own single most important safeguard against misreading it as some kind of endorsement: the outcome depended entirely on a discernment only the Buddha himself possessed at that moment, not on any general principle that ending one's life in the grip of illness reliably leads to liberation. He notes that the monk's own faith, described as already unwavering before his illness ever struck, functioned here as exactly the reservoir of practice the fifth verse's "hero" draws on, sharpened rather than shattered by the extremity of his circumstance — a reminder that unshakeable faith is not a feeling cultivated in comfort, but something proven precisely under the hardest possible pressure. He closes by connecting this back to the treatise's own twofold summary of what "seeing the Buddha" ultimately means: ultimate seeing, through a noble wisdom-eye earned by genuinely transcending craving, and conventional seeing, through an ordinary eye that may or may not be reliable — the dying monk's own final moments being, in effect, his passage from the second kind of seeing into the first.
