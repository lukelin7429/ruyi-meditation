Fascicle 23 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十三 · Restraint of the Faculties, in Depth

Guarding the Senses, Six Steps at a Time

Fascicle 23 continues the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation's long account of the fourteen path-provisions, picking up exactly where fascicle 22 left off: restraint of the faculties, the fifth provision, and moderation in eating, the sixth — both given far more careful, technical treatment here than their brief mentions in fascicle 21.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 23 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Restraint of the Faculties Reopens: Six Steps to Guarding the Senses

Fascicle 23 opens directly onto the fifth of the fourteen provisions, restraint of the faculties — a topic already introduced briefly in fascicle 21, but now unpacked through its own six-step structure: closely guarding the sense-gates, maintaining and sustaining mindfulness, protecting the mind itself, resting in equanimity, and finally applying restraint to the mind-faculty specifically.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之二……云何根律儀？謂如有一，能善安住密護根門，防守正念，常委正念，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the third chapter, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, part two. …What is restraint of the faculties? It is like one who is able to skillfully abide in closely guarding the sense-gates, maintaining mindfulness, sustaining mindfulness — and so on, to be explained at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi orients the reader to where this fascicle sits: the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples' first yogic process covers spiritual lineage, entry, and setting out toward liberation, and setting out toward liberation itself breaks into five installments across fascicles 21 through 25 — self-perfection, perfection by others, and the wish for the good in fascicle 21; mundane and supramundane detachment together with moral discipline in fascicle 22; restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating here in fascicle 23; wakeful practice and clear awareness in fascicle 24; and good companionship, hearing and reflecting on the Dharma, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the adornments of renunciation in fascicle 25. This fascicle's task is narrower but deeper than fascicle 21's brief treatment of the same topic: restraint of the faculties unpacks into six concrete steps. Closely guarding the sense-gates addresses the basic problem directly — contact between any of the six senses and their objects easily triggers craving toward what is pleasant, aversion toward what is unpleasant, or confusion toward what is neither, unless the mind is actively guarded against being pulled in by any of the three. Maintaining mindfulness means cultivating, through sustained hearing, reflection, and meditative cultivation, the actual mindfulness capable of accomplishing that guarding — mindfulness itself functioning as a kind of protective gatekeeper. Sustaining mindfulness means keeping that mindfulness continuously present, not just occasionally recalled but thoroughly, uninterruptedly maintained. Protecting the mind means using mindfulness specifically to prevent incorrect attention from arising in the sixth consciousness. Resting in equanimity means immediately relinquishing craving, aversion, or confusion the moment any of the three arises — either through wise, deliberate relinquishment, or through a neutral equanimity that simply declines to follow the afflictive impulse either way. And restraint applied to the mind-faculty itself means never grasping at any object's general appearance or alluring details in a way that lets unwholesome thought seep through and disturb the mind. Together, these six steps are what "restraint of the faculties" actually consists in.

2. Closely Guarding the Sense-Gates, and Obtaining the Mindfulness That Guards Them

The treatise details the first two steps: closely guarding the sense-gates is simply the umbrella term for all five remaining steps working together, while maintaining mindfulness explains specifically how genuine protective mindfulness is obtained in the first place — through sustained hearing, reflection, and meditative cultivation of the Dharma.

Chinese Source

云何名為密護根門？謂防守正念，常委正念，廣說乃至防護意根，及正修行意根律儀。如是名為密護根門。云何名為防守正念？謂如有一，密護根門增上力故，攝受多聞、思惟、修習。由聞思修增上力故，獲得正念。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called closely guarding the sense-gates? It is maintaining mindfulness, sustaining mindfulness — and so on at length, down to protecting the mind-faculty and correctly practicing restraint of the mind-faculty. This is called closely guarding the sense-gates. What is called maintaining mindfulness? It is that one, through the power of closely guarding the sense-gates, takes up extensive learning, reflection, and cultivation. Through the power of learning, reflection, and cultivation, one obtains mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's structure here is deliberately circular in a productive way: "closely guarding the sense-gates" is simply the name for the whole six-step process taken together, so the second step, "maintaining mindfulness," immediately begins unpacking what that guarding actually requires — starting with mindfulness's own origin. Genuine protective mindfulness is not simply willed into existence; it arises specifically through the sustained work of hearing the Dharma, reflecting on its meaning, and cultivating it in meditation, each stage reinforcing the last. The underlying logic, Master Cháng Bǎi explains, is that the six sense-faculties themselves become the very basis on which this threefold cultivation operates — a mind not yet guarded at all cannot sustain the hearing and reflection needed to develop mindfulness in the first place, so the process must begin modestly and build on itself, guarding leading to more capacity for hearing and reflection, which in turn produces the very mindfulness needed to guard more effectively still.
