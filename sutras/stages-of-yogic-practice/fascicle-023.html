Fascicle 23 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十三 · Restraint of the Faculties, in Depth

Guarding the Senses, Six Steps at a Time

Fascicle 23 continues the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation's long account of the fourteen path-provisions, picking up exactly where fascicle 22 left off: restraint of the faculties, the fifth provision, and moderation in eating, the sixth — both given far more careful, technical treatment here than their brief mentions in fascicle 21. Fascicle 23 is now complete.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 23 complete Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 23 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Restraint of the Faculties Reopens: Six Steps to Guarding the Senses

Fascicle 23 opens directly onto the fifth of the fourteen provisions, restraint of the faculties — a topic already introduced briefly in fascicle 21, but now unpacked through its own six-step structure: closely guarding the sense-gates, maintaining and sustaining mindfulness, protecting the mind itself, resting in equanimity, and finally applying restraint to the mind-faculty specifically.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之二……云何根律儀？謂如有一，能善安住密護根門，防守正念，常委正念，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the third chapter, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, part two. …What is restraint of the faculties? It is like one who is able to skillfully abide in closely guarding the sense-gates, maintaining mindfulness, sustaining mindfulness — and so on, to be explained at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi orients the reader to where this fascicle sits: the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples' first yogic process covers spiritual lineage, entry, and setting out toward liberation, and setting out toward liberation itself breaks into five installments across fascicles 21 through 25 — self-perfection, perfection by others, and the wish for the good in fascicle 21; mundane and supramundane detachment together with moral discipline in fascicle 22; restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating here in fascicle 23; wakeful practice and clear awareness in fascicle 24; and good companionship, hearing and reflecting on the Dharma, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the adornments of renunciation in fascicle 25. This fascicle's task is narrower but deeper than fascicle 21's brief treatment of the same topic: restraint of the faculties unpacks into six concrete steps. Closely guarding the sense-gates addresses the basic problem directly — contact between any of the six senses and their objects easily triggers craving toward what is pleasant, aversion toward what is unpleasant, or confusion toward what is neither, unless the mind is actively guarded against being pulled in by any of the three. Maintaining mindfulness means cultivating, through sustained hearing, reflection, and meditative cultivation, the actual mindfulness capable of accomplishing that guarding — mindfulness itself functioning as a kind of protective gatekeeper. Sustaining mindfulness means keeping that mindfulness continuously present, not just occasionally recalled but thoroughly, uninterruptedly maintained. Protecting the mind means using mindfulness specifically to prevent incorrect attention from arising in the sixth consciousness. Resting in equanimity means immediately relinquishing craving, aversion, or confusion the moment any of the three arises — either through wise, deliberate relinquishment, or through a neutral equanimity that simply declines to follow the afflictive impulse either way. And restraint applied to the mind-faculty itself means never grasping at any object's general appearance or alluring details in a way that lets unwholesome thought seep through and disturb the mind. Together, these six steps are what "restraint of the faculties" actually consists in.

2. Closely Guarding the Sense-Gates, and Obtaining the Mindfulness That Guards Them

The treatise details the first two steps: closely guarding the sense-gates is simply the umbrella term for all five remaining steps working together, while maintaining mindfulness explains specifically how genuine protective mindfulness is obtained in the first place — through sustained hearing, reflection, and meditative cultivation of the Dharma.

Chinese Source

云何名為密護根門？謂防守正念，常委正念，廣說乃至防護意根，及正修行意根律儀。如是名為密護根門。云何名為防守正念？謂如有一，密護根門增上力故，攝受多聞、思惟、修習。由聞思修增上力故，獲得正念。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called closely guarding the sense-gates? It is maintaining mindfulness, sustaining mindfulness — and so on at length, down to protecting the mind-faculty and correctly practicing restraint of the mind-faculty. This is called closely guarding the sense-gates. What is called maintaining mindfulness? It is that one, through the power of closely guarding the sense-gates, takes up extensive learning, reflection, and cultivation. Through the power of learning, reflection, and cultivation, one obtains mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's structure here is deliberately circular in a productive way: "closely guarding the sense-gates" is simply the name for the whole six-step process taken together, so the second step, "maintaining mindfulness," immediately begins unpacking what that guarding actually requires — starting with mindfulness's own origin. Genuine protective mindfulness is not simply willed into existence; it arises specifically through the sustained work of hearing the Dharma, reflecting on its meaning, and cultivating it in meditation, each stage reinforcing the last. The underlying logic, Master Cháng Bǎi explains, is that the six sense-faculties themselves become the very basis on which this threefold cultivation operates — a mind not yet guarded at all cannot sustain the hearing and reflection needed to develop mindfulness in the first place, so the process must begin modestly and build on itself, guarding leading to more capacity for hearing and reflection, which in turn produces the very mindfulness needed to guard more effectively still.

3. The Four-Part Discipline Behind Mindfulness, and Sustaining It Without Interruption

Maintaining mindfulness closes with a fourfold discipline — faith, aspiration, effort, and skillful means — that actually carries a practitioner from hearing the teaching to genuinely embodying it. The third step, sustaining mindfulness, then insists this mindfulness must never be occasional: it needs to be both unbroken and applied with real thoroughness.

Chinese Source

為欲令此所得正念無忘失故，能趣證故，不失壞故。於時時中，即於多聞、若思、若修，正作瑜伽，正勤修習，不息加行、不離加行。如是由此多聞思修所集成念，於時時中，善能防守正聞思修瑜伽作用。如是名為防守正念。……云何名為常委正念？謂於此念恆常所作、委細所作。當知此中，恆常所作，名無間作；委細所作，名殷重作。即於如是無間所作、殷重所作，總說名為常委正念。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order that this mindfulness once obtained not be forgotten, that it lead to genuine realization, and that it not be lost or destroyed — from time to time, with regard to learning, reflection, and cultivation, one correctly engages in yogic practice, correctly and diligently cultivates it, never resting from the effort and never abandoning it. Thus, through the mindfulness accumulated by such learning, reflection, and cultivation, one is, from time to time, well able to guard the function of correct hearing, reflection, and cultivation. This is called maintaining mindfulness. …What is called sustaining mindfulness? It is that this mindfulness is exercised constantly, and exercised thoroughly. It should be understood that exercising it constantly is called uninterrupted practice, and exercising it thoroughly is called earnest practice. This uninterrupted and earnest practice together are called, in sum, sustaining mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the treatise's own fourfold account of genuine "yogic practice" to explain how mindfulness, once obtained, is actually kept from fading: faith comes first, a deep confidence in what the Dharma teaches (impermanence, selflessness, nirvāṇa's peace); aspiration follows naturally from that confidence, a genuine wish to cultivate the wholesome states faith has opened one to; effort is aspiration acted on, disciplined exertion pursued day and night without wavering; and skillful means is the actual method by which that effort is sustained and applied correctly. Faith produces aspiration, aspiration produces effort, and effort, given the right means, becomes durable practice rather than a single burst of enthusiasm that fades.

Sustaining mindfulness then adds a second requirement beyond simply obtaining mindfulness: it must be maintained both without interruption and with real thoroughness — not a mindfulness recalled occasionally when convenient, but one continuously present and applied with genuine care to every phenomenon observed, exactly the way a practitioner would want no gap at all in noticing impermanence, suffering, and selflessness as they actually show up moment to moment. Once mindfulness is genuinely sustained this way, it gains the settled strength needed to subdue whatever afflictive reaction the six sense-objects might otherwise provoke.

4. Protecting the Mind Itself: A Precise Map of How a Single Glance Becomes Craving

The fourth step, protecting the mind, gets the treatise's most technical treatment yet — a precise account of exactly how ordinary perception slides into affliction, moment by moment, as the five sense-consciousnesses hand off to a discriminating sixth consciousness that can tip toward craving or aversion before a practitioner even notices.

Chinese Source

謂眼色為緣生眼識，眼識無間生分別意識；由此分別意識，於可愛色色將生染著，於不可愛色色將生憎恚。即由如是念增上力，能防護此非理分別起煩惱意，令其不生所有煩惱。如是耳鼻舌身廣說，當知亦爾。……意法為緣生意識，即此意識有與非理分別俱行，能起煩惱；由此意識，於可愛色法將生染著，於不可愛色法將生憎恚。亦由如是念增上力，能防護此非理分別起煩惱意，令其不生所有煩惱。如是名為念防護意。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is that the eye and form, as condition, give rise to eye-consciousness; immediately following eye-consciousness, discriminating mental-consciousness arises. Through this discriminating mental-consciousness, one is about to become attached to an agreeable form, or about to feel hatred toward a disagreeable form. It is precisely through the power of mindfulness that one is able to guard against this afflictive mind arising from unwise discrimination, preventing any affliction from arising. So too, at length, should it be understood regarding the ear, nose, tongue, and body. …Mind and mental object, as condition, give rise to mental-consciousness; this very mental-consciousness, conjoined with unwise discrimination, is able to give rise to affliction. Through this mental-consciousness, one is about to become attached to an agreeable mental object, or about to feel hatred toward a disagreeable one. It is likewise through the power of mindfulness that one is able to guard against this afflictive mind arising from unwise discrimination, preventing any affliction from arising. This is called protecting the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the treatise's classic five-moment account of how contact with a sense-object unfolds: a first, purely receptive moment simply registering the object with no judgment at all; a second moment of the sixth consciousness "seeking," beginning to investigate what was just registered; a third moment of "determining," settling what the object actually is; a fourth moment where that determination tips toward either a defiled or undefiled response — this is exactly where craving or aversion first takes root; and a fifth, "outflowing" moment in which the initial sense-consciousness itself, following the sixth consciousness's lead, continues wholesomely or unwholesomely in a similar vein. Only the first moment belongs to bare sense-consciousness itself; the discrimination that actually generates craving or aversion belongs entirely to the sixth consciousness accompanying it. This is why the treatise's language is so precise — "form" named twice in the Chinese, once for the object as bare sense-consciousness first registers it, uncategorized and nameless, and again for that same object once the sixth consciousness has judged it "agreeable" or "disagreeable" and given it a name.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise then extends this same analysis to a mental consciousness operating entirely on its own — what tradition calls the "lone-running" mental consciousness, active without any of the five senses currently engaged at all, occurring in ordinary undirected mind-wandering, in dreams, in deep meditative absorption, or in states of genuine mental disturbance. Reflecting on a person one is fond of, growing more pleased the longer one dwells on the thought, or ruminating on someone who caused offense, growing angrier with every repetition, are both instances of exactly this — affliction generated purely by unwise mental discrimination, with no fresh sense-contact triggering it at all. Protecting the mind means applying that same guarding mindfulness here too, whether affliction arises through the five senses or through thought alone, catching the discriminating impulse before it can fully form into craving or aversion.

5. Resting in Equanimity: Two Ways to Release What Has Already Stirred

The fifth step addresses what happens the moment craving, aversion, or confusion has actually begun to stir despite everything already covered — releasing it through one of two methods, a wisdom-guided relinquishment or a simple, non-judgmental non-following.

Chinese Source

云何名為行平等位？平等位者，謂或善捨、或無記捨。由彼於此非理分別起煩惱意善防護已，正行善捨、無記捨中，由是說名行平等位。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called resting in equanimity? Equanimity means either wholesome relinquishment or neutral relinquishment. Once one has skillfully guarded against this afflictive mind arising from unwise discrimination, and correctly rests within wholesome relinquishment or neutral relinquishment, this is called resting in equanimity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two methods clearly. "Wholesome relinquishment" actively applies wisdom in the moment — recognizing, through correct attention, that the object is not what unwise discrimination made it out to be (contemplating the body's actual impurity rather than an idealized image, or recognizing that attraction and aversion toward the same object shift entirely with one's own mood, proving the reaction was never really about the object at all) — genuinely composed, he notes, of the four factors non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion, and effort. "Neutral relinquishment" works differently, simply declining to follow the stirring impulse at all, neither judging it nor engaging it, corresponding to a feeling that is neither pleasant nor unpleasant — a method Master Cháng Bǎi notes is used extensively in the Theravāda meditation tradition. Both methods accomplish the same end from different directions: a mind that neither grasps nor pushes away, settled in genuine equanimity the moment the six senses meet their objects.

6. Two Aspects Unpacked in Full: What "Not Grasping the Mark" Actually Means, Moment by Moment

The treatise now returns to steps four and five in far greater technical depth, distilling them into two aspects — skillfully guarding against affliction, and genuinely resting in equanimity — and then, using the eye as its worked example, precisely defines what "not grasping the general mark" and "not grasping the alluring details" mean at the level of the five-moment cognitive process already introduced.

Chinese Source

謂於色聲香味觸法，不取其相，不取隨好，終不依彼發生諸惡不善尋思，令心流漏。……由是二相，故能於此非理分別起煩惱意能善防護。……云何於眼所識色中不取其相？言取相者，謂於眼識所行色中，由眼識故取所行相，是名於眼所識色中執取其相。……若能遠離如是眼識所行境相，是名於眼所識色中不取其相。……云何於眼所識色中不取隨好？取隨好者，謂即於眼所識色中，眼識無間俱生分別意識，執取所行境相，或能起貪、或能起瞋、或能起癡，是名於眼所識色中執取隨好。若能遠離此所行相，於此所緣不生意識，是名於眼所識色中不取隨好。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding forms, sounds, scents, tastes, tactile objects, and mental objects: not grasping their general mark, not grasping their alluring details — never, on that basis, giving rise to unwholesome, improper trains of thought that let the mind leak out into affliction. …It is through these two aspects that one is able to skillfully guard against this afflictive mind arising from unwise discrimination. …What is not grasping the general mark in a form known by the eye? Grasping the mark means that, within the form that is eye-consciousness's own domain, one grasps, by means of eye-consciousness, the mark of that domain — this is called grasping the mark within a form known by the eye. …If one is able to be free of this domain-object as eye-consciousness presents it, this is called not grasping the mark within a form known by the eye. …What is not grasping the alluring details in a form known by the eye? Grasping the alluring details means that, within a form known by the eye, the discriminating mental-consciousness that arises immediately following eye-consciousness grasps at that domain-object, and is thereby able to give rise to craving, or to hatred, or to delusion — this is called grasping the alluring details within a form known by the eye. If one is able to be free of this domain-object, such that no [afflicted] mental-consciousness arises with regard to it, this is called not grasping the alluring details within a form known by the eye.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi condenses the whole of restraint into two aspects working together: skillfully guarding against affliction (not grasping either the general mark or the alluring details, and, should mindfulness lapse or affliction prove unusually strong, immediately falling back on the full six-step restraint already covered) and genuinely resting in equanimity (the wholesome or neutral relinquishment just described) — both aspects applying identically across all six sense-faculties, eye through mind, each substituting its own object and consciousness into the same underlying pattern.

Working through the eye as the model case, Master Cháng Bǎi clarifies exactly where "grasping" enters the process already mapped in the five-moment account: "grasping the general mark" happens not at the bare, non-judgmental first moment of eye-consciousness itself, but through the mental-consciousness that treats what eye-consciousness merely registered as something definite and real — form here covering, in the treatise's own threefold analysis, a color or quality that can be displayed (blue, yellow, and the like), a shape (long, short, round, square), and an expressive form conveying inner intention (a gesture, a posture, an outstretched hand). "Grasping the alluring details" happens one step further in, at the very moment the sixth consciousness's determination tips toward "agreeable" or "disagreeable" and craving, hatred, or delusion actually begins to form. Not grasping either, in each case, simply means being free of that particular domain-object exactly at the point where affliction would otherwise take hold — cutting the process off, in effect, before the general impression can ever ripen into a defiled reaction. Master Cháng Bǎi cites a verse from the *Saṃyukta Āgama* making the stakes explicit: losing mindfulness the instant the eye sees a form lets an "agreeable mark" take hold, binding the mind, breeding craving and aversion, and keeping nirvāṇa permanently out of reach — while mindfulness that refuses to grasp the mark at all draws a practitioner steadily closer to it instead.

7. A Second Kind of Grasping: Familiar Objects, Four Driving Forces, and Why Grasping Itself Isn't Always the Problem

The treatise names a second category of grasping — not the unfamiliar object encountered for the first time, but the already-familiar one, drawn in by four specific forces. It then makes an important qualification: grasping the mark or the details is not, in itself, automatically a fault. What matters is whether it actually leads to affliction.

Chinese Source

復有餘類執取其相、執取隨好。言取相者，謂色境界在可見處，能生作意正現在前，眼見眾色。……取隨好者，謂即色境在可見處……即隨所聞名句文身，為其增上為依為住，如是士夫補特伽羅，隨其所聞，種種分別眼所識色。……又此取相及取隨好，或有由此因緣、由此依處、由此增上，發生種種惡不善法，令心流漏；或有由此因緣、由此依處、由此增上，不生種種惡不善法，令心流漏。……謂諸貪欲，及貪所起諸身惡行、諸語惡行、諸意惡行；若諸瞋恚、若諸愚癡，及二所起諸身惡行、諸語惡行、諸意惡行。是名種種惡不善法。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is, further, a second kind that grasps the general mark and grasps the alluring details. Grasping the general mark means that a visible object, present within sight, gives rise to attention that is now directly engaged, and the eye sees the many forms present. …Grasping the alluring details means that, this visible object being present within sight … resting on, taking as its basis, whatever names, phrases, and syllables were previously heard about it, such a person, in accordance with what was heard, variously discriminates the form known by the eye. …This grasping of the general mark and the alluring details may, through this cause, this basis, this governing condition, give rise to various unwholesome states, letting the mind leak out into affliction; or it may, through this same cause, basis, and governing condition, not give rise to any unwholesome states at all. …It is craving, and the bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct arising from craving; it is hatred, and it is delusion, together with the bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct arising from these two. This is called various unwholesome states.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains this second kind of grasping applies specifically to objects already familiar, drawn in by one or more of four forces: desire (a straightforward wish to look, already present before contact occurs), memory (recalling something and deliberately seeking it out again), the object's own intensity (a sound too loud, a smell too strong, a dish too enticingly plated to ignore, regardless of one's actual intention — precisely the mechanism advertising exploits), and habituation (a practiced cook automatically sampling another kitchen's noodles, a coffee connoisseur automatically evaluating a new café's brew, simply because long practice has made that kind of attention second nature). Grasping the alluring details, in this second category, means letting previously-heard descriptions actively shape how the object is now perceived — tasting a dish and confirming, point by point, that it matches what one had been told about it in advance. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that familiar objects, according to Kuiji's commentary, may not require the full five-moment sequence at all — memory and recognition can jump straight to the "determining" moment, skipping the initial bare registration and investigation a genuinely novel object requires.

Crucially, the treatise then clarifies that grasping itself — of either kind — is not inherently a fault. The same act of attending closely to an object's mark and details can either generate craving, hatred, and delusion (and the bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct that follows from them) or can serve a genuinely wholesome purpose instead — Master Cháng Bǎi gives the example of deliberately, repeatedly attending to the body's actual impurity as a contemplative practice, using the very same grasping mechanism to weaken attachment rather than feed it. The real criterion is simply whether a given instance of grasping leads the mind into affliction or not; where it does, the remedy is straightforward — turn away from that object's mark and details entirely, exactly as one would refuse to listen further to gossip already stirring up anger, rather than continuing to investigate every detail of who-said-what.

8. Knowing What to Look At and What Not To: Old Age and Sickness Versus Entertainment

The treatise draws a sharp, practical line: some sights are worth deliberately attending to precisely because they awaken genuine insight, while others should be actively avoided because they only ever feed affliction — old age, sickness, and physical frailty belonging to the first category, entertainment and performance to the second.

Chinese Source

若於其眼所識色中，應策眼根……即便於彼作意策發。如是策發，令不雜染。……若於其眼所識色中，不應策發所有眼根……即便於彼遍一切種而不策發。不策發故，令不雜染。……謂於一類所應觀色，如諸所有衰老朽邁上氣者身、傴僂憑杖戰掉者身、或諸疾苦重病者身，乃至廣說。又於所識色不應策發眼根者，謂於一類不應觀色，如諸妓樂戲笑歡娛，或餘遊戲所作歌舞音樂等事，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding forms known by the eye, where the eye faculty should be roused … one applies attention and rouses it accordingly there. Rousing it in this way, one keeps the mind free of defilement. …Regarding forms known by the eye, where the eye faculty should not be roused … one, in every such case, does not rouse it at all. By not rousing it, one keeps the mind free of defilement. …As for the kind of forms one should observe: bodies aged, decrepit, frail, short of breath; bodies bent, leaning on a staff, trembling; or bodies afflicted with serious illness — and so on at length. As for forms the eye faculty should not be roused toward: the kind one should not observe, such as courtesans' entertainment, laughter and revelry, or other performances of song and dance and music — and so on at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a genuinely practical guide to where a practitioner's attention should and shouldn't go. Deliberately observing the elderly, the frail, the sick — bent backs, trembling limbs, labored breathing, serious illness in any of its many forms — is actively encouraged, since these sights are precisely what awaken the felt recognition of aging, sickness, and death that motivates genuine practice; this, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, is exactly why the Buddha himself left the palace in the first place, and exactly the insight no comfortable, sheltered life can otherwise provide. Deliberately avoiding entertainment, performance, and revelry, by contrast, protects against sights that stir up nothing but craving and distraction, with no compensating insight to offer in return. The same principle — actively engaging what awakens wisdom, actively avoiding what only feeds affliction — applies identically across all six senses, not the eye alone.

9. A Five-Part Summary: Who Protects, What Is Protected, From What, and How

Having covered restraint of the faculties in full technical detail, the treatise offers its own compact five-part summary: identifying who does the protecting, what is being protected, what it is being protected from, and how that protection is actually carried out.

Chinese Source

此略義者，謂若能防護、若所防護、若從防護、若如防護、若正防護。如是一切，總略為一，名根律儀。……謂防守正念，及所修習常委正念，是能防護。……謂防護眼根，防護耳鼻舌身意根，是所防護。……謂從可愛、不可愛色，廣說乃至從其可愛、不可愛法而正防護。……謂不取相，不取隨好。若依是處發生種種惡不善法，令心流漏，即於此處修行律儀。防守根故，名修律儀。如是防護。

Ru-Yi Translation

This summary meaning is: who protects; what is protected; from what one protects; how one protects; and correct protection itself. All of this together, in brief, is called restraint of the faculties. …It is guarding mindfulness and the sustained mindfulness cultivated from it — this is who protects. …It is guarding the eye faculty, guarding the ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind faculties — this is what is protected. …It is from agreeable and disagreeable forms, and so on at length, from agreeable and disagreeable mental objects, that one correctly protects — this is from what one protects. …It is not grasping the general mark, not grasping the alluring details; and, should various unwholesome states nonetheless arise in dependence on these, letting the mind leak into affliction, then practicing restraint precisely there — guarding the faculty in this way is called practicing restraint. This is how one protects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this five-part frame as a tidy recap of everything already covered: mindfulness itself — both the mindfulness first obtained through guarding the sense-gates and the sustained, thorough mindfulness that follows — is what actually does the protecting; the six sense-faculties, eye through mind, are what gets protected; agreeable and disagreeable objects across all six senses are what the protection guards against; and the method is the twofold approach already detailed at length — not grasping the general mark or the alluring details in the first place, and, should affliction arise anyway, immediately practicing restraint right at that point rather than letting it continue. A fifth element, correct protection itself, remains to be explained.

10. Milestone: Restraint at Two Depths — Suppressing Affliction Versus Uprooting It for Good

The five-part summary closes with correct protection itself, and a second, three-part summary quickly recaps the same ground. The treatise then draws its most important distinction yet: restraint of the faculties comes in two depths entirely — one attained through discernment alone, which can keep affliction from manifesting but leaves its underlying seeds fully intact, and one attained through genuine meditative cultivation, which destroys those seeds for good.

Chinese Source

謂由正念，防護於意，行平等位，是名正防護。……又根律儀略有二種：一者、思擇力所攝，二者、修習力所攝。思擇力所攝根律儀者，謂於境界深見過患，不能於此所有過患除遣斷滅。修習力所攝根律儀者，謂於境界深見過患，亦能於此所有過患除遣斷滅。……又由思擇力所攝根律儀故，於所緣境，令煩惱纏不復生起、不復現前；而於依附所依隨眠，不能斷除、不能永拔。由修習力所攝根律儀故，於所緣境，煩惱隨眠不復生起、不復現前；一切時分，依附所依所有隨眠，亦能斷除、亦能永拔。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is through correct mindfulness protecting the mind, resting in equanimity — this is called correct protection. …Restraint of the faculties, in brief, is of two kinds: that comprised within the power of discernment, and that comprised within the power of cultivation. Restraint of the faculties comprised within the power of discernment means that, seeing deeply the faults inherent in objects, one is nonetheless unable to remove and eliminate all such faults entirely. Restraint of the faculties comprised within the power of cultivation means that, seeing deeply the faults inherent in objects, one is also able to remove and eliminate all such faults entirely. …Through restraint of the faculties comprised within the power of discernment, with regard to a given object, the fetters of affliction no longer arise, no longer manifest — yet the underlying tendencies, resting on their own basis, cannot be cut off, cannot be permanently uprooted. Through restraint of the faculties comprised within the power of cultivation, with regard to a given object, the underlying tendencies of affliction no longer arise, no longer manifest — and, at all times, the underlying tendencies resting on their own basis are also cut off, also permanently uprooted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the five-part summary with correct protection itself — mindfulness safeguarding the sixth consciousness so that it settles into equanimity — then notes the treatise immediately offers a second, tidier three-part version of the same material: the means of protection (the same fourfold sequence — guarding, sustaining, not grasping, and falling back on restraint), what is protected (the six sense-objects), and correct protection (identical to the fifth element just given). Both summaries, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, describe exactly the same practice; the treatise is simply offering two different ways of organizing it for memorization.

The far more consequential distinction follows immediately: restraint of the faculties can be accomplished at two genuinely different depths. Restraint attained purely through the "power of discernment" — reflective insight, prior to genuine meditative attainment — can see an object's faults clearly enough to keep affliction from actually manifesting in that moment, but cannot touch the underlying tendency (anuśaya) still dormant in the storehouse consciousness; given the right conditions later, that tendency can still resurface. Restraint attained through the "power of cultivation" — the fruit of genuine concentration paired with insight — goes further still, actually severing that underlying tendency at the root, so it can never resurface regardless of what conditions arise afterward. Master Cháng Bǎi ties this directly back to the seed-in-storage-versus-seed-burnt-by-fire image already used in fascicle 22 to distinguish mundane from supramundane detachment: discernment alone merely keeps the grain dry and dormant, while genuine cultivation burns it so nothing can ever sprout from it again — the very same distinction, now applied specifically to how deeply the senses can actually be restrained.

11. Milestone: Restraint of the Faculties Closes — Moderation in Eating Opens

The treatise closes the discernment-versus-cultivation distinction by mapping each onto its proper stage of the path — one belonging to the path of accumulating provisions, the other to the ground of genuine detachment — and with that, restraint of the faculties, the fifth provision, closes. The sixth provision, moderation in eating, opens immediately with the classic five contemplations recited before a meal.

Chinese Source

當知此中，思擇力所攝根律儀，是資糧道所攝；修習力所攝根律儀，當知墮在離欲地攝。……云何名為於食知量？謂如有一，由正思擇食於所食，不為倡蕩、不為憍逸、不為飾好、不為端嚴，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that restraint of the faculties comprised within the power of discernment belongs to the path of provisions; restraint of the faculties comprised within the power of cultivation should be understood as belonging to the ground of detachment. …What is called moderation in eating? It is that one, through correct discernment, eats what is eaten — not for wanton pleasure, not for pride and indulgence, not to enhance one's beauty, not for physical elegance — and so on at length, as already stated.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes restraint of the faculties by placing each of its two depths on its proper rung of the path: discernment-based restraint, still relying on reflective conviction about desire's dangers rather than direct meditative power, belongs to the "path of provisions" — the gathering stage before genuine concentration is reached; cultivation-based restraint, resting on at least access concentration and genuine insight practice (unattractiveness meditation, selflessness contemplation), belongs to the "ground of detachment" proper, where affliction's seeds are actually destroyed. A beginning practitioner cannot leap straight to the second; understanding a principle instantly is possible, but embodying it takes gradual practice — which is exactly why an ordinary person can know all the right ideas about affliction and still be overtaken by it the moment the senses meet a compelling object, since knowledge and hearing alone, without sustained reflection and cultivation, do not yet carry real power against it. With this, restraint of the faculties — the fifth of the fourteen provisions — closes.

Moderation in eating, the sixth provision, opens directly with the same "five contemplations before eating" already introduced briefly in fascicle 21 (weighing the effort behind the food, examining one's own worthiness to receive it, guarding the mind against craving, treating food as medicine for the body's frailty, and eating only to accomplish the path) — never for indulgence, pride, vanity, or any motive unrelated to genuine practice.

12. Contemplating Food's Faults: From Appetizing to Repulsive in Three Swallows

The treatise details the first of moderation's contemplative practices: examining, before eating, the faults inherent in ordinary food itself — faults of consumption, of transformation, and of the pursuit required to obtain it in the first place. It opens with consumption's own faults, tracing exactly how appetizing food becomes repulsive the moment it is actually chewed and swallowed.

Chinese Source

正思擇者，如以妙慧等隨觀察段食過患。見過患已，深生厭惡，然後吞咽。……謂即於此所食段食，或觀受用種類過患、或觀變異種類過患、或觀追求種類過患。……謂如有一，將欲食時，所受段食色香味觸皆悉圓滿，甚為精妙。從此無間進至口中，牙齒咀嚼，津唾浸爛，涎液纏裹，轉入咽喉。爾時此食先曾所有悅意妙相一切皆捨，次後轉成可惡穢相，當轉異時，狀如變吐。……謂此飲食既噉食已，一分銷變，至中夜分或後夜分，於其身中，便能生起養育增長血肉筋脈骨髓皮等非一眾多種種品類諸不淨物。次後一分變成便穢，變已趣下展轉流出。

Ru-Yi Translation

Correct discernment means observing, with excellent wisdom, either the full range or some part of the faults inherent in solid food. Having seen these faults, one gives rise to deep revulsion, and only then swallows. …With regard to this very food being eaten, one observes the faults of its consumption, or the faults of its transformation, or the faults of the pursuit required to obtain it. …It is like one who, about to eat, finds the food received — its color, aroma, taste, and texture — entirely complete, truly excellent and refined. Yet the moment it enters the mouth, chewed by the teeth, soaked and softened by saliva, wrapped in mucus, and passed down into the throat, every one of its previously delightful, excellent qualities is entirely abandoned, transforming instead into something repulsive and filthy — becoming, at the moment of this transformation, indistinguishable from vomit. …This food, once eaten, is in part transformed: by the middle or the last watch of the night, within the body, it gives rise to and nourishes blood, flesh, sinew, marrow, bone, skin, and many other kinds of impure substances. Another part is transformed into excrement, which then passes downward and is expelled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this contemplation as a deliberate corrective to ordinary craving: before eating, a practitioner examines, in full or in part, three categories of fault — the faults of consumption itself, of the food's subsequent transformation in the body, and of whatever was required to obtain it — and, having genuinely seen these faults, eats without the craving that would otherwise accompany the meal. The faults of consumption are traced in vivid, almost startling detail: food that looks, smells, and tastes wonderful the instant before eating becomes, within moments of actual chewing and swallowing, something no one would ever willingly look at again, no different in principle from what would come back up if vomited — precisely the sequence a practitioner is meant to hold in mind before the very first bite, so that even the most beautifully prepared meal never becomes an occasion for real attachment. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this teaching is not meant for someone already struggling with disordered eating, and cites an alternative practice from Nanyue Huisi, Zhiyi's own teacher, for exactly that circumstance: visualizing the food as a heavenly offering served in vessels of gold and jewels, first offered to the buddhas and all beings, only then eaten — a way of restoring appetite rather than suppressing it.

The faults of transformation continue the same unflinching honesty: what is swallowed becomes, over the following hours, partly absorbed into the body's own tissues — blood, flesh, sinew, marrow, bone, skin — themselves already regarded as impure substances in the treatise's own analysis of the body, and partly expelled as waste. Master Cháng Bǎi's point throughout is not disgust for its own sake, but a clear-eyed remedy for craving: seeing plainly what food actually is and actually becomes makes it far harder to eat from anything other than genuine, undistorted necessity.

13. Transformation's Remaining Faults, and the Real Cost of Simply Obtaining Food

Transformation's faults close with waste itself and the many illnesses diet can cause; the third fault then turns outward, to everything a practitioner must endure just to obtain food in the first place — braving harsh weather, laboring hard, and the anxiety of both failing to acquire enough and, having acquired it, fearing its loss.

Chinese Source

由是日日數應洗淨。或手、或足、或餘支節誤觸著時，若自若他皆生厭惡。又由此緣，發生身中多種疾病。……如是等類無量疾病，由飲食故，身中生起。或由所食不平和故，於其身中不銷而住。是名飲食變異種類所有過患。……謂如有一，為食因緣，寒時為寒之所逼惱，熱時為熱之所逼惱，種種策勵劬勞勤苦。……如是策勵劬勞勤苦，方求之時，所作事業若不諧遂；由是因緣，愁憂焦惱、拊胸傷歎、悲泣迷悶，何乃我功唐捐無果？……謂所作業若得諧遂，為護因緣起大憂慮：勿我財寶當為王賊之所侵奪，或火焚燒，或水漂蕩，或宿惡作當令滅壞，或現非理作業方便當令散失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of this, one must wash daily — should a hand, a foot, or any other limb accidentally touch [waste], both oneself and others feel revulsion. This same cause also gives rise to manifold illness within the body. …Such countless illnesses arise in the body because of food. Or, because what was eaten was poorly balanced, it remains undigested within the body. This is called the fault of transformation in food. …It is like one who, for the sake of food, is afflicted by cold in cold weather and afflicted by heat in hot weather, exerting oneself and toiling with great hardship. …Having toiled and exerted oneself in this way, seeking food, should the undertaking not succeed, one becomes sorrowful, anxious, and distressed, beating one's chest and lamenting, weeping and despondent: "Why has all my effort come to nothing?" …Should the undertaking succeed, one then gives rise, in order to protect it, to great anxiety: may my wealth not be seized by kings or bandits, not burned by fire, not swept away by flood, not ruined by some past unwholesome deed, not dissipated through some present unwise transaction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the faults of transformation with the plain fact of needing to wash after using the toilet, and the sheer number of illnesses diet can cause — the treatise lists roughly fifteen specific ailments by name (skin eruptions, various forms of eczema and leprosy, respiratory trouble, coughing, blistering, indigestion-related reflux, diabetes, epilepsy, chills and fever, jaundice, blood disorders, and swelling), all offered as evidence that even something as basic as eating carries real physical risk — a sobering counterweight to any illusion that food is simply a source of pleasure.

The third and final category, the faults of pursuit, turns to everything required just to obtain food in the first place: farming, herding, trade, accounting, clerical work, craft — whatever livelihood a person pursues, all of it demanding real endurance of cold and heat and hard labor, all of it liable to fail despite genuine effort, producing exactly the grief and frustration anyone who has worked hard for something and come up empty already knows. And even genuine success brings its own anxiety: wealth once obtained must then be protected — from theft, fire, flood, the lingering consequences of past unwholesome karma, or simple present-day misjudgment — an endless, low-grade worry that never fully resolves. Master Cháng Bǎi's point throughout this entire threefold analysis is cumulative: food that looks effortless on the plate in fact carries the full weight of labor, risk, and anxiety behind it, worth remembering precisely so that eating is approached with genuine gratitude and moderation rather than either indulgence or indifference.

14. Milestone: From Broken Families to War Itself — The Pursuit's Final Faults, and a Deathbed Vision of Encroaching Dark

The remaining four faults of pursuing food escalate sharply: families and friendships genuinely destroyed by disputes over wealth, kings waging literal war out of sheer insatiability, soldiers maimed or killed carrying out that war, and, worst of all, the unwholesome deeds committed simply to obtain a living — closing with one of the treatise's most striking images, a dying person watching darkness swallow the mountains exactly as their own future rebirth grows correspondingly darker.

Chinese Source

謂諸世間，為食因緣多起鬥諍。父子、母女、兄弟、朋友尚為飲食互相非毀，況非親里為食因緣而不展轉更相鬥訟。……以其手足塊刀杖等互相加害。是名於食能壞親愛所作過患。……謂諸國王剎帝利種，位登灌頂，亦於自國王都聚落不住喜足，俱帥兵戈，互相征討。……種種戈仗傷害其身，或便致死，或等死苦。是名於食無有厭足所作過患。……謂如一類，為王所使，討固牢城，因遭種種極熱脂油、熱牛糞汁及鎔銅鐵而相注灑，或被戈仗傷害其身，或便致死，或等死苦。是名因食不得自在所作過患。……謂如有一，為食因緣，造作積集身諸惡行。如身惡行，語、意亦爾。……臨命終時，為諸重病苦所逼切。由先所作諸身語意種種惡行增上力故，於日後分，見有諸山、或諸山峰垂影懸覆、近覆、極覆。便作是念：我自昔來依身語意所造諸業，唯罪非福。若有其趣，諸造惡者當生其中，我今定往。如是悔已，尋即捨命。既捨命已，隨業差別生諸惡趣。謂那落迦、傍生、餓鬼。

Ru-Yi Translation

In the world, disputes over food frequently arise: even fathers and sons, mothers and daughters, brothers, and friends slander one another over food — how much more so those who are not kin at all. …[Great families] harm one another with their hands, feet, stones, knives, and clubs. This is called the fault of destroying affection. …Kings of the warrior caste, having ascended the throne, remain unsatisfied even with their own kingdoms, cities, and villages, and lead their armies to wage war against one another … wounding one another's bodies with every kind of weapon, sometimes to death, sometimes to a suffering equal to death. This is called the fault of insatiability. …It is like one sent by a king to besiege a fortified city, who then suffers scalding oil, boiling dung, or molten copper and iron poured down upon them, or is wounded by weapons, sometimes to death, sometimes to a suffering equal to death. This is called the fault of losing one's freedom because of food. …It is like one who, for the sake of food, commits and accumulates bodily misconduct — and just as with bodily misconduct, so too with verbal and mental misconduct. …At the time of death, oppressed by the pain of grave illness, through the power of the bodily, verbal, and mental misconduct previously committed, one sees, as at the sun's late afternoon, the shadows of mountains or mountain peaks beginning to fall — first partially covering, then drawing nearer, then covering everything entirely. One then thinks: "Everything I have done through body, speech, and mind, from long ago until now, has been nothing but wrongdoing, never merit. If such destinations exist, all who have done wrong are destined for them — I too shall surely go there now." Having thought this with regret, one's life then comes to an end. Having died, one is reborn, according to the particular nature of one's karma, into one of the unfortunate realms — hell, the animal realm, or the realm of hungry ghosts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the escalation deliberately. The fault of destroying affection shows even close family turning on each other over money and food — a plain, universal observation ("people die for wealth, birds die for food," as the saying goes). The fault of insatiability scales the same dynamic up to nations: kings already ruling entire kingdoms still wage war for more, driving the vivid battle imagery the treatise describes in full period detail — conch-horns and drums rallying troops, chariots, cavalry, war-elephants, and infantry clashing in chaos, swords and spears inflicting wounds that kill outright or leave survivors wishing they had died. The fault of losing one's freedom follows the ordinary soldier conscripted into that same war, subjected to horrific violence entirely outside their own choosing — boiling oil, molten metal, and worse. The sixth and final fault, unwholesome conduct, names the deepest cost of all: committing genuinely evil acts, through body, speech, and mind alike, simply to secure a livelihood.

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on the treatise's own description of this sixth fault's consequence, since it is among the most vivid deathbed images anywhere in the text: a person whose life was built on wrongdoing, dying, watches what looks like an ordinary late-afternoon sunset — except the mountain shadows keep advancing, first covering only a peak's tip, then creeping further down the slope, then finally swallowing the light entirely, moving, as Master Cháng Bǎi puts it, "from brightness toward darkness." Someone who died well, by contrast, experiences the reverse: darkness receding toward ever-greater light. Watching this darkness close in, the dying person recognizes with sudden clarity that a lifetime's conduct has been almost entirely unwholesome, and that a lower rebirth now genuinely awaits — a recognition that arrives, devastatingly, only once it is already too late to act on it. With this sixth and final fault, the entire threefold analysis of food's dangers — consumption, transformation, and pursuit — closes.

15. A Small Genuine Benefit: Eating Not to Survive, but to Practice

Having catalogued food's dangers in full, the treatise turns to its one genuine benefit — sustaining the body — while immediately cautioning against settling for that benefit alone. Merely staying alive is not yet the point; only using that sustained body to actually cultivate the path makes eating a genuinely excellent act.

Chinese Source

又此段食有少勝利。……謂即此身由食而住，依食而立，非無有食。……若唯修此身暫住行，非為妙行；若於如是身暫時住而生喜足，非妙喜足，亦非領受飲食所作圓滿無罪功德勝利。若不唯修身暫住行，亦不唯於身暫時住而生喜足，而即依此暫時住身，修集梵行令得圓滿，乃為妙行，亦妙喜足。又能領受飲食所作圓滿無罪功德勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

This solid food, further, has a small genuine benefit. …It is that this very body abides through food, is established through food, and cannot exist without food. …If one only sustains the body's temporary existence, this is not excellent conduct; if one merely finds contentment in the body's temporary existence, this is not excellent contentment, nor the full, blameless benefit that receiving food can accomplish. If one does not merely sustain the body's temporary existence, nor merely find contentment in it, but instead, resting on this temporarily sustained body, brings pure conduct to fulfillment — this is excellent conduct, and excellent contentment. It also achieves the full, blameless benefit that receiving food can accomplish.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own threefold account of how a being's life is sustained elsewhere — day-to-day physical sustenance, the full span of a natural lifespan, and, further out, favorable future rebirths flowing from wholesome deeds — to clarify exactly what kind of "sustaining" eating alone provides: only the first, most immediate kind. Merely eating to survive one more day, however necessary, is not yet "excellent conduct," and simple physical contentment is not yet "excellent contentment" — both remain incomplete unless the sustained body is actually put to use cultivating discipline, concentration, and wisdom. Only when eating serves that larger purpose — carrying a practitioner, in this very life, toward liberation, or at least toward a favorable future rebirth through sustained wholesome practice — does the act of eating itself become genuinely excellent, and does receiving food actually accomplish the "full, blameless benefit" the treatise describes.

16. Eating Like It Were Your Own Child's Flesh: Gratitude, Purity, and Freedom From Resentment

The treatise now turns to the proper inner attitude while actually receiving and eating food — reflecting deeply on the hardship behind every donor's gift (illustrated with the treatise's own stark parable of a family forced to eat a child's flesh to survive), approaching almsgivers' homes with a mind as clear as moonlight, and remaining free of both pride and resentment regardless of what is actually given.

Chinese Source

若於如是遍一切種段食過患圓滿知已，以正思擇，深見過患，而求出離。為求如是食出離故，如子肉想，食於段食。應作是念：彼諸施主，甚大艱難積集財寶，具受廣大追求所作種種過患。由悲愍故，求勝果故，如割皮肉及以刺血，而相惠施。……當隨月喻往施主家，盪滌身心，安住慚愧，遠離憍傲，不自高舉，不輕懱他。如自獲得所有利養，心生喜悅，如是於他所得利養，心亦喜悅。……豈有出家往詣他所，要望他施非不惠施，要望他敬非不恭敬，要多非少，要妙非麤，要當速疾而非遲緩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having fully understood every kind of fault inherent in solid food, one, through correct discernment, sees these faults deeply and seeks to be free of them. In order to seek this freedom, one eats solid food as though it were the flesh of one's own child. One should reflect: those benefactors, with the greatest difficulty, accumulated this wealth, undergoing every manner of fault in its pursuit. Out of compassion, and seeking an excellent result, they give it as though cutting their own flesh and drawing their own blood. …One should approach a benefactor's home as the moon [approaches its fullness] — cleansing body and mind, abiding in a sense of shame and conscience, free of arrogance, neither exalting oneself nor disdaining others. Just as one feels joy upon receiving support oneself, so too should one feel joy when another receives it. …How could one who has gone forth, visiting another's home, insist that they must give and not withhold, must show respect and not fail to, must give much and not little, must give something fine and not something coarse, must give quickly and not slowly?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces the treatise's own striking image for how a monastic should regard alms: eating "as though it were the flesh of one's own child." He recounts the parable behind it — a family fleeing famine, food exhausted, each parent in turn begging to sacrifice their own flesh rather than let the others starve, until the child himself insists on being the one, reasoning that his parents could always have another child but he could never have new parents; neither parent can bear to eat what the other has given without deep sorrow, yet eat they must, simply to survive. The point, Master Cháng Bǎi explains, is that a donor's gift costs just as much — every one of the labor-faults, dangers, and anxieties already catalogued at length lie behind whatever food actually reaches a monastic's bowl, offered out of genuine compassion the way a parent offers their own flesh.

The moon simile that follows sets the standard for how a monastic should actually approach a donor's home: cleansed in body and mind (proper deportment, no ulterior motive of gain or praise), carrying a genuine sense of shame at not yet having completed the path oneself, entirely free of the kind of arrogance Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates with a cautionary story of a monk who demanded his own lay brother kneel and venerate him as one of the "Three Jewels," and was promptly thrown out for it — since an ordained person who has not yet attained the path is, strictly speaking, only a nominal member of the saṅgha, in no position to demand anything. Genuine equanimity extends to others' fortune as well: rejoicing exactly as much when a fellow practitioner receives generous support as when one receives it oneself. And it extends, finally, to freedom from any sense of entitlement at all — never expecting a donor's generosity, respect, abundance, quality, or promptness as though owed, since almsgiving remains, by its very nature, a gift freely given rather than a debt collected.

17. Milestone: Freedom From Resentment Completes, and the Four Kinds of Food That Sustain a Being

The fivefold reflection on eating closes with genuine equanimity regardless of what is given, and with knowing exactly how much to eat — enough to sustain life without indulging craving. The treatise then names the doctrine underlying all of this: four kinds of food sustain any being, of which this entire provision concerns only the first, solid food, defined in full period detail.

Chinese Source

應作是心，往施主家：設不惠施，終不於彼起怨害心及瞋恚心而相嫌恨。……又我應依所食段食，發起如是如是正行，及於其量如實了達。謂我命根由此不滅，又於此食不苦耽著，纔能隨順攝受梵行。……云何所食？謂四種食：一者、段食，二者、觸食，三者、意等思食，四者、識食。今此義中意說段食。此復云何？謂餅、麨、飯、羹、臛、糜、粥、酥油、糖、蜜、魚、肉、葅鮓、乳、酪、生酥、薑、鹽、酢等種種品類，和雜為摶，段段吞食；故名段食。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should approach a benefactor's home with this resolve: even should no gift be given at all, one will never, on that account, give rise to resentment or ill will. …I should, in dependence on this solid food I eat, arouse conduct of exactly this kind, and understand its proper measure exactly as it is — knowing that my life-faculty is sustained by it, yet not clinging to it with any suffering-inducing attachment, so that it may serve only to support pure conduct. …What is eaten? There are four kinds of food: solid food; contact as food; volition and intention as food; and consciousness as food. In the present context, solid food alone is meant. What is this? Cakes, roasted grain flour, rice, soup, meat broth, gruel, ghee, sugar, honey, fish, meat, pickled preserves, milk, yogurt, fresh cream, ginger, salt, vinegar, and various other kinds, mixed together into a mouthful and swallowed piece by piece — this is why it is called solid food.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes freedom from resentment with a plain warning: harboring ill will toward a stingy donor plants exactly the karmic seed for an unfortunate rebirth, so equanimity here is not merely good manners but genuinely protective. He illustrates the underlying attitude with a well-known story: a wealthy householder tested Buddhist monks against non-Buddhist ascetics by feeding both groups lavishly, then meagerly, at two separate gatherings — the monks responded identically both times, simply expressing contentment and leaving, while the ascetics praised the first feast extravagantly and stormed off offended at the second, convincing the householder and onlookers alike that freedom from craving, not asceticism for its own sake, was the real mark of genuine practice. Knowing the proper measure, the fifth reflection, means eating enough to sustain life and support practice — neither so little that the body weakens, nor so much that it becomes an indulgence in its own right.

Master Cháng Bǎi then introduces the treatise's classic fourfold account of what actually sustains any living being: solid food (the physical food this entire provision concerns); contact as food (sensory and mental engagement itself — anything from reading a newspaper to any pleasurable experience — which sustains a being through the joy and ease it provides); volition as food (hope and aspiration themselves, the will to keep living toward some cherished outcome); and consciousness as food (the storehouse consciousness itself, sustained and nourished by the other three, continuing to hold the body together). Only the first of these four — solid food, defined here through a vivid catalogue of period foods ranging from roasted grain flour and rice to meat, fish, pickled preserves, dairy, and seasonings, all mixed and swallowed mouthful by mouthful — falls within this provision's scope; Master Cháng Bǎi notes the early tradition included meat and fish where the later Mahāyāna emphasis on compassion moved toward vegetarianism, though the treatise's own analysis here applies regardless of what specific foods are actually eaten.

18. Not Eating to Fuel Indulgence: The First of Four Wrong Motives

Having closed the long reflection on correct discernment before eating, the treatise turns to four specific wrong motives named back in its opening verse. The first — eating in order to fuel an evening of sensual indulgence — is illustrated as a genuine cautionary case: someone eating well specifically to have the energy for revelry later that night.

Chinese Source

云何名為不為倡蕩？謂如有一樂受欲者，為受諸欲食於所食。彼作是思：我食所食，令身飽滿、令身充悅。過日晚時至於夜分，當與姝妙嚴飾女人共為嬉戲，歡娛受樂，倡掉縱逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called not eating for the sake of wanton pleasure? It is like one who delights in sensual pleasure, eating for the sake of experiencing those pleasures. Such a person thinks: "I eat this food so that my body will be full and content. When the day passes into evening and night falls, I shall enjoy myself with beautifully adorned women, delighting in pleasure, indulgent and unrestrained." …Wanton pleasure means that, within this noble Dharma and discipline, one who indulges desire is driven by craving and lust into every kind of unwholesome, improper thought; eating for this reason leaves all the faculties agitated, the mind restless, uneasy, and never at peace. Eating for this purpose is called eating for the sake of wanton pleasure. …The many learned noble disciples, however, through the power of discernment, see these faults deeply, know well how to be free of them, and eat accordingly — not as those driven by desire eat. This is why it is called not eating for the sake of wanton pleasure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this first wrong motive as a caution against the most obvious form of self-deception around food: eating well specifically to fuel an evening of indulgence afterward, rather than to support genuine practice. The unwholesome thoughts this motive stirs up — restlessness, unease, a mind that cannot settle — are exactly the opposite of what moderation in eating is meant to cultivate. A genuinely learned disciple, by contrast, has already seen through desire's costs clearly enough that food simply does not carry this charge; the same meal, eaten with a different intention entirely, produces none of the agitation the first kind of eater invites.

19. Three More Wrong Motives: Competitive Pride, Grooming, and Vanity

The treatise names three further wrong motives for eating, all variations on the same underlying theme: eating well specifically to fuel physical competition and the vanity that follows it — described in vivid period detail, from wrestling and archery contests to elaborate bathing, perfuming, and dressing rituals.

Chinese Source

謂如有一樂受欲者，為受諸欲食於所食。彼作是思：我今宜應多食所食、飽食所食，隨力隨能食噉肥膩，增房補益，色香味具精妙飲食。過今夜分至於明日，於角武事當有力能。……依如是等諸角武事，當得勇健，膚體充實，長夜無病，久時少壯，不速衰老，壽命長遠，能多噉食，數數食已能正銷化，除諸疾患。如是為於無病憍逸、少壯憍逸、長壽憍逸而食所食。……既角武已，復作是思：我應沐浴。便以種種清淨香水沐浴其身；沐浴身已，梳理其髮；梳理髮已，種種妙香用塗其身；既塗身已，復以種種上妙衣服、種種華鬘、種種嚴具莊飾其身。此中沐浴、理髮、塗香，名為飾好；既飾好已，復以種種上妙衣服、華鬘、嚴具莊飾其身，名為端嚴。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like one who delights in sensual pleasure, eating for the sake of experiencing those pleasures. Such a person thinks: "I should now eat much, eat until full, eating rich and fatty foods to the limit of my strength and capacity, excellent food complete in color, aroma, and taste. When this night passes into tomorrow, I shall have the strength for feats of contest." …Through such feats of contest, one gains vigor and strength, a firm and full physique, freedom from illness over a long span, lasting youthfulness, no premature aging, a long lifespan, the capacity to eat abundantly, and food that digests properly each time, free of any ailment. This is eating for the sake of pride in freedom from illness, pride in lasting youth, and pride in longevity. …Having competed, one further thinks: "I should bathe." One then bathes the body with various kinds of pure, fragrant water; having bathed, one combs and arranges the hair; having done so, one applies various fine fragrances to the body; and, having done so, one further adorns the body with various fine garments, garlands, and ornaments. Bathing, combing the hair, and applying fragrance are called grooming; adorning the body afterward with fine garments, garlands, and ornaments is called elegance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three motives — pride, grooming, and elegance — as variations building on the same underlying vanity. Eating "for pride" means eating specifically to fuel competitive athletic feats — the treatise names an extensive catalogue of period contests (massage and conditioning, ball games, weight-lifting, high jumping, a form of wrestling similar to taekwondo, arm-wrestling, sword and spear combat, archery, discus and rope-throwing) — all pursued to remain vigorous, youthful, and long-lived, which the treatise sorts into three specific prides: pride in freedom from illness, pride in lasting youth, and pride in longevity. Eating "for grooming" and "for elegance" follow the same competitive display outward into appearance itself: bathing, combing, and perfuming the body count as grooming; dressing in fine clothing and ornaments afterward counts as elegance — the entire sequence, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, describing an entirely ordinary worldly relationship to food and appearance that a genuine practitioner is meant to recognize and set aside, not because competition or personal care are inherently wrong, but because none of it serves the actual purpose eating is meant to serve.

20. Milestone: The Four Wrong Motives Close — Eating Only to Sustain the Body

The treatise closes its account of eating for pride, grooming, and elegance by tracing the whole cycle to its natural, repeating conclusion, then contrasts it with a learned disciple's resolve to abandon it entirely. It then opens the treatise's own positive account of correct motivation, beginning with the simplest: eating only so the body continues to live.

Chinese Source

彼既如是憍逸、飾好、身端嚴已，於日中分或日後分，臨欲食時，饑渴并至。於諸飲食極生希欲、極欣、極樂，不見過患，不知出離，隨得隨食。……諸有多聞聖弟子眾，以思擇力，深見過患，善知出離，而食所食；非如前說諸受欲者食於所食。唯作是念：我今習近所不應習、所應斷食，為欲永斷如是食故。……云何名為為身安住食於所食？謂飲食已壽命得存，非不飲食壽命存故，名身安住。我今受此所有飲食，壽命得存，當不夭沒；由是因緣，身得安住，能修正行，永斷諸食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus indulged in pride, grooming, and elegance, when midday or late afternoon arrives and the time to eat draws near, hunger and thirst both set in. One then feels intense craving for food, intense delight in obtaining it, intense pleasure in eating it — seeing no fault in any of this, knowing nothing of being free of it, eating whatever is obtained the moment it is obtained. …The many learned noble disciples, however, through the power of discernment, see these faults deeply, know well how to be free of them, and eat accordingly — not as those driven by desire eat. They think only this: "I shall now grow familiar with what should not be practiced, and abandon this kind of eating, precisely in order to be permanently free of it." …What is called eating for the sake of the body's sustained existence? It is that, having eaten, life is preserved — whereas without eating, life could not be preserved; this is called the body's sustained existence. I now receive this food so that my life may be preserved and not cut short prematurely; because of this, the body remains sustained, able to cultivate correct practice, and, in time, to be permanently free of the need for any of these forms of food at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces the full, self-perpetuating cycle the treatise describes: a life organized around pride, grooming, and elegance produces, meal after meal, exactly the same craving, delight, and unreflective consumption, day after day, with no awareness that any of it could be otherwise. A learned disciple's response is not merely to eat differently on a given occasion but to resolve, deliberately, to abandon this entire pattern for good — recognizing it, once truly seen, as something to feel genuine revulsion toward rather than something to indulge more carefully. With this contrast drawn, the four wrong motives close, and the treatise turns to its own positive account: the first and simplest correct motivation is simply keeping the body alive, since without food life cannot continue at all — a body sustained is a body still capable of practicing and, eventually, of transcending the need for food altogether.

21. Eating for Temporary Support: Nourishment That Helps Versus Nourishment That Harms

The treatise's next correct motivation, eating for temporary support, opens a genuinely practical distinction: two kinds of nourishment, one that causes illness, attachment, or physical and mental obstruction, and one that avoids all three — recognizing that not all food, even food eaten with the right general intention, actually serves a practitioner equally well.

Chinese Source

謂略說有二種存養。一、有艱難存養，二、無艱難存養。……謂受如是所有飲食，數增饑羸，困苦重病。……或以非法追求飲食，非以正法。……得已，染愛、耽嗜、饕餮、迷悶、堅執、湎著受用。……或有食已，令身沈重，無所堪能，不任修斷；或有食已，令心遲鈍，不速得定；或有食已，令入出息來往艱難；或有食已，令心數為惛沈睡眠之所纏擾。如是名為有艱難存養。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, there are two kinds of nourishment: nourishment that is difficult, and nourishment that is not difficult. …It is that, receiving such food, one grows repeatedly weaker and hungrier, afflicted with distress and grave illness. …Or, food sought through improper rather than proper means. …Once obtained, it is received with attachment, craving, gluttony, confusion, rigid grasping, and complete absorption. …Or, having eaten, the body becomes heavy, incapable, unfit for cultivation; or, having eaten, the mind grows dull, unable to quickly attain concentration; or, having eaten, the in-breath and out-breath become labored; or, having eaten, the mind is repeatedly entangled by dullness and drowsiness. This is called nourishment that is difficult.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through "difficult nourishment"'s three faces: physical harm (food that weakens the body and breeds illness rather than sustaining it); improper acquisition and attached consumption (obtained through the same deceptive means already catalogued in fascicle 22 — flattery, false claims, staged appearances, badgering, or comparison-shaming — and then eaten with escalating degrees of craving the treatise names in six steps: simple attachment, habitual craving, outright gluttony, an unreflective refusal to weigh any cost or benefit, rigid possessiveness that refuses to share, and finally complete absorption that lets no consideration interrupt the indulgence); and physical or mental obstruction (food that leaves the body too heavy to practice, the mind too dull to concentrate, the breath labored, or drowsiness constantly encroaching — junk food, heavy or greasy meals, and sugar-laden food offered as period-appropriate examples of each). Any food producing even one of these three effects counts as "difficult" nourishment, regardless of how sincerely it was eaten.

22. Five Remaining Reasons to Eat: From Simple Hunger to Healing the Body Already Harmed

The treatise closes "temporary support" with unproblematic nourishment's mirror-positive qualities, then unpacks, one at a time, the remaining correct motivations for eating first summarized briefly back in fascicle 21: removing hunger and thirst, supporting pure conduct, healing illness already caused by past mistakes in eating, and preventing new illness from arising.

Chinese Source

如是受用，身無沈重，有所堪能，堪任修斷；令心速疾得三摩地；令入出息無有艱難；令心不為惛沈睡眠之所纏擾。如是名為無艱難存養。……若受飲食，為除饑渴、為攝梵行、為斷故受、為令新受當不更生、為當存養力樂無罪安隱而住，如是習近無罪無染所有存養，而自存活。……謂至食時，多生饑渴，氣力虛羸，希望飲食。為欲息此饑渴纏逼、氣力虛羸，知量而食。……謂如其量受諸飲食，由是因緣，修善品者……身無沈重，有所堪能，堪任修斷；令心速疾得三摩地……由是速疾有力有能得所未得、觸所未觸、證所未證。……謂如有一，由過去世食不知量、食所匪宜、不銷而食；由是因緣，於其身中生起種種身諸疾病……為欲息除如是疾病，及為息除從此因緣所生苦受，習近種種良醫所說饒益所宜隨順醫藥，及受種種悅意飲食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eaten in this way, the body does not grow heavy, remains capable, fit for cultivation; the mind swiftly attains concentration; the in-breath and out-breath are not labored; the mind is not entangled by dullness and drowsiness. This is called nourishment that is not difficult. …If one eats in order to remove hunger and thirst, to support pure conduct, to heal past illness, to prevent new illness from arising, and to preserve the strength, ease, and blameless, peaceful abiding needed — cultivating, in this way, nourishment free of fault and free of defilement — one sustains one's own life properly. …When mealtime arrives, great hunger and thirst commonly arise, strength grows weak, and one longs for food. In order to put an end to this affliction of hunger and thirst and this weakness, one eats according to the proper measure. …Eating according to the proper measure, for this reason, one who cultivates wholesome practice … the body does not grow heavy, remains capable, fit for cultivation; the mind swiftly attains concentration … and, through this, swiftly gains the strength and capacity to attain what has not been attained, to touch what has not been touched, to realize what has not been realized. …It is like one who, through not knowing the proper measure in past eating, eating what was unsuitable, or eating before what was already eaten had digested, thereby gives rise to various bodily illnesses … in order to put an end to such illness, and to the painful sensation arising from it, one draws near to whatever beneficial and suitable medicine a good physician prescribes, and to whatever pleasing food is appropriate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes "temporary support" by naming its positive counterpart point for point — food that leaves the body light and capable, the mind swiftly concentrated, the breath unlabored, and drowsiness at bay — then draws the sharp practical conclusion: nourishment obtained and eaten this way is genuinely blameless and undefiled, while nourishment causing illness, attachment, or obstruction is genuinely faulty and tainted, regardless of how sincere the underlying intention was. He then works through the five remaining correct motivations for eating in turn. Removing hunger and thirst means eating precisely enough — by the proper measure — to prevent the weakness and preoccupation hunger itself would otherwise cause, sufficient to carry a practitioner through to the next appropriate mealtime without further craving. Supporting pure conduct means eating with the specific aim of remaining fit to study, reflect, and meditate — food chosen and measured so the body stays light, the mind concentrates readily, breathing stays easy, and drowsiness stays away, precisely so that stream-entry and the higher fruits, not yet attained, become swiftly attainable. Healing past illness addresses a specific practical situation: someone who has already made themselves sick through poor past eating — overeating, unsuitable food, or eating before the last meal had properly digested — now eats and takes medicine specifically to recover, following a competent physician's actual guidance rather than guesswork.

23. Milestone: Every Motivation for Eating Closes, and Two Summaries Distill the Whole Teaching

The treatise closes preventing new illness and the fifth remaining motivation — preserving strength, ease, blamelessness, and peaceful abiding, itself unpacked into five components — bringing the entire long teaching on correct discernment before eating to its formal close. Two summaries then distill everything covered: what is eaten and how it should be eaten, and, more pointedly, four underlying purposes eating should always serve.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，由現在世安樂無病、氣力具足，不非量食……令於未來食住身中成不銷病，或於身中當生隨一身諸疾病……如是名為為令新受當不更生受諸飲食。……謂飲食已，壽命得存，是名存養。若除饑羸，是名為力。若斷故受，新受不生，是名為樂。若以正法追求飲食，不染、不愛，乃至廣說而受用之，是名無罪。若受食已，身無沈重，有所堪能，堪任修斷……如是名為安隱而住。是故說言：由正思擇食於所食，不為倡蕩、不為憍逸、不為飾好、不為端嚴，乃至廣說，是名廣辯於食知量。……謂若所受食，若如是食，當知總名此中略義。……復次，應知此中略義，謂為攝受對治，為遠離欲樂行邊，為遠離自苦行邊，為攝受梵行，受諸飲食。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like one who, presently at ease and free of illness, with strength fully intact, does not eat beyond measure … so that, in the future, undigested food lodging in the body does not become a source of illness, nor gives rise to any of the various bodily ailments already named. This is called eating so that new illness will not arise. …Having eaten, life is preserved — this is called preservation. Removing hunger and weakness is called strength. Healing past illness and preventing new illness from arising is called ease. Seeking food through proper means, and receiving it without attachment or craving — as explained at length — is called blamelessness. Having eaten, the body is not heavy, remains capable, fit for cultivation — this is called peaceful abiding. It is therefore said: through correct discernment in eating what is eaten — not for wanton pleasure, not for pride, not for grooming, not for elegance, and so on at length — this is called the full explanation of moderation in eating. …What is received as food, and how it is eaten — this, in total, should be understood as the summary meaning here. …Furthermore, the summary meaning here should be understood as: eating in order to counteract affliction, in order to avoid the extreme of indulgence, in order to avoid the extreme of self-mortification, and in order to support pure conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes preventing new illness simply: someone currently healthy still eats within proper measure precisely so that overeating or careless eating doesn't create the very illnesses already catalogued. The fifth remaining motivation, preserving strength, ease, blamelessness, and peaceful abiding, unpacks into five precisely defined components: preservation (life itself continuing), strength (freedom from hunger-induced weakness), ease (both healing old illness and preventing new), blamelessness (properly sourced food, eaten without any of the six degrees of craving), and peaceful abiding (a body and mind genuinely fit for practice). With this fifth motivation named, the entire extended teaching on correct discernment before eating — reflection on food's dangers, its one small benefit, the proper attitude toward it, the four wrong motives to avoid, and now the six correct motivations to embrace — formally closes.

Master Cháng Bǎi then walks through the two summaries offered to help this material be remembered. The first simply distinguishes what is eaten (solid food, in all its period varieties) from how it is eaten (the entire apparatus of correct discernment just covered, condensed to a single phrase). The second, more pointed summary reduces the entire teaching to four underlying purposes any act of eating should serve: counteracting the various afflictions and faults eating can otherwise provoke; avoiding the extreme of indulgence; avoiding the opposite extreme of self-mortification; and, above all, supporting pure conduct — the same "middle way" already familiar from the treatise's account of detachment itself, now applied specifically to the humble, daily act of eating a meal.

24. The Second Summary Matched Back to the Earlier Teaching

Chinese Source

謂如說言：由正思擇食於所食。……云何為遠離欲樂行邊受諸飲食？謂如說言：不為倡蕩、不為憍逸、不為飾好、不為端嚴食於所食。……云何為遠離自苦行邊受諸飲食？謂如說言：為除饑渴、為斷故受、為令新受當不更生、為當存養若力若樂食於所食。……云何為攝受梵行受諸飲食？謂如說言：為攝梵行、為得無罪安隱而住食於所食。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is said: through correct discernment in eating what is eaten. …What is it to eat in order to avoid the extreme of indulgence? It is said: not for wanton pleasure, not for pride, not for grooming, not for elegance. …What is it to eat in order to avoid the extreme of self-mortification? It is said: to remove hunger and thirst, to break old illness, to prevent new illness from arising, and to preserve strength and ease. …What is it to eat in order to support pure conduct? It is said: to support pure conduct, and to attain blameless, peaceful abiding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi matches the second summary's four purposes back to language already given in full: counteracting affliction is simply correct discernment itself, applied to whatever fault food might otherwise cause. Avoiding indulgence is matched to the four wrong motives already rejected — eating for spectacle, pride, grooming, or fine appearance. Avoiding self-mortification — the opposite extreme, starving or neglecting the body under some notion that discomfort itself is virtuous — is matched to the positive motivations already covered: removing hunger, healing old illness, preventing new illness, and preserving strength and ease. Supporting pure conduct is matched to the two motivations most directly tied to practice: sustaining the capacity for study and meditation, and attaining a peaceful, blameless state fit for the path. Nothing new is added here; the second summary is simply a second lens on material already fully unpacked, useful precisely because it groups the same sixteen or so phrases by underlying purpose rather than by sequence.

25. A Third Summary: Balanced and Unbalanced Eating

Chinese Source

復次，應知此中略義，謂有二種：一、無所食，二、有所食。無所食者，謂一切種都無所食；無所食故，即便夭沒。有所食者，有其二種：一、平等食，二、不平等食。平等食者，謂非極少食、非極多食、非不宜食、非不消食、非染汙食。不平等食者，謂或極少食、或極多食、或不宜食、或不消食、或染汙食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the summary meaning here should be understood as twofold: not eating at all, and eating. Not eating at all means eating nothing whatsoever in any manner; through not eating at all, one simply perishes. Eating is of two kinds: balanced eating and unbalanced eating. Balanced eating means not eating too little, not eating too much, not eating what is unsuitable, not eating before what came before has digested, and not eating in a defiled manner. Unbalanced eating means eating too little, or too much, or unsuitably, or without digestion, or in a defiled manner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces a third and final summary, organized around a stark first division: not eating at all, which simply ends in death and is dismissed in a single line, versus eating, which is the only real option for a being still embodied in the desire realm. Eating itself then splits into the balanced and the unbalanced, each defined by the same five qualities in opposite directions — quantity too little, quantity too much, unsuitable food, undigested food eaten again too soon, and defiled food sought or eaten with attachment. He then walks back through why each of the five balanced qualities matters, largely by pointing to teaching already given: eating too little leaves the body weak and hungry, with little chance of actually sustaining life; eating too much sits like a heavy stone in the stomach, past what the body can digest in time; eating what is undigested or unsuitable lodges in the body and becomes the seed of illness already catalogued; and defiled eating — food sought wrongly or eaten with craving, infatuation, or gluttony — carries all six degrees of attachment described earlier. Taken together, balanced eating in all five respects, and the avoidance of unbalanced eating in all five respects, is what the treatise calls "even application in eating."

26. Milestone: Moderation in Eating Closes, and Fascicle 23 Reaches Its End

The treatise finishes matching every one of the sixteen phrases on eating back to the five balanced qualities, dwells at length on why defiled eating is genuinely blameworthy and disturbs meditative stability, and then closes the entire teaching on moderation in eating with a formal summary — both the sixteen-part detailed version and the three-part brief version — bringing fascicle 23 to its true end.

Chinese Source

若復說言：為斷故受、為令新受當不更生。由此遮止不消而食、食所匪宜。若復說言：為當存養、為當得力。由此顯示不極少食、不極多食。若復說言：為當得樂。由此顯示消已而食，及食所宜。若復說言：為當無罪安隱而住。由此顯示無染汙食。所以者何？若以非法追求飲食，得已染愛，如前廣說而受用之，名染汙食，亦名有罪。若於善品勤修習者……由彼諸惡不善尋思，令心流漏、令心相續，隨順、趣向、臨入而轉，由是因緣，不安隱住。此安隱住復有二種：一者、遠離所食極多，由是因緣，身無沈重，有所堪能，堪任修斷，如前廣說。二者、於食不生味著，由是因緣，遠離諸惡尋思擾動不安隱住。是故如此一切諸句，皆為宣示開顯於食平等所作。如是名為廣略宣說於食知量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, it is said: to break old illness, and to prevent new illness from arising — by this, eating before digestion and eating what is unsuitable are rejected. It is said: to preserve life, and to attain strength — by this, eating too little and eating too much are rejected. It is said: to attain ease — by this, eating only after digestion, and eating what is suitable, are revealed. It is said: to attain blameless, peaceful abiding — by this, defiled eating is revealed. Why is this so? If one seeks food unlawfully and, having obtained it, eats it with attachment and craving as explained at length before, this is called defiled eating, and is also called blameworthy. If one who diligently cultivates wholesome states … through unwholesome deliberation, the mind leaks and continues, following, tending, and turning toward it as one is about to enter concentration — by this cause, one does not abide peacefully. This peaceful abiding is itself twofold: first, avoiding eating too much, by which the body is not heavy and remains capable, fit for cultivation, as explained at length before; second, not developing a taste for food, by which one avoids the disturbance and unrest that unwholesome deliberation about food would otherwise bring. Therefore, all these phrases together are set forth to reveal even application in eating. This is called the broad and brief teaching on moderation in eating.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi finishes matching the remaining phrases to the five balanced qualities: healing old illness and preventing new illness reject eating before digestion and eating unsuitable food; preserving life and attaining strength reject both extremes of quantity; attaining ease reveals the positive counterpart — eating only after proper digestion, and eating what actually suits the body; and attaining blameless, peaceful abiding reveals the rejection of defiled eating specifically.

He then dwells on why defiled eating is genuinely blameworthy, in two ways. First, food sought or eaten with the six degrees of craving already described is simply blameworthy in itself. Second — and this is the part he lingers on — even a practitioner seriously engaged in meditation can be derailed by unwholesome thoughts about food arising during sitting: a sudden craving for a particular meal, or scheming how to obtain it, is enough to pull the mind out of concentration just as it approaches stillness or insight. He illustrates this with two stories he tells often. The elder Guangqin is remembered for saying that skipping one evening meal while the mind spends the whole night planning tomorrow's food is no different from having eaten many meals already — each craving-thought is itself a kind of eating, so genuine restraint means not even thinking about food, not merely not consuming it. And the arhat Upagupta, famous for guiding an enormous number of students to awakening, is remembered for curing one gluttonous disciple by having him chew and swallow his meal, then spit it back into a second bowl, then offering him that bowl to eat again — the disciple's revulsion at his own half-digested food broke his attachment to eating on the spot, after which he quickly settled into concentration and insight and reached the path. Genuine peaceful abiding, Cháng Bǎi concludes, has exactly two supports: not overeating, so the body stays light and capable, and not clinging to food's taste, so the mind stays free of the restless craving-thoughts that would otherwise disturb it.

With this, the entire teaching on moderation in eating formally closes — and with it, fascicle 23 itself reaches the end of its source text. Maitreya's own closing formula names both versions offered throughout: the detailed, sixteen-phrase account (correct discernment; not for spectacle, pride, grooming, or fine appearance; to sustain the body; for temporary support; to remove hunger and thirst; to support pure conduct; to break old illness; to prevent new illness; to preserve life; to gain strength; to attain ease; to be blameless; to abide peacefully) and the compressed three-part summary (what is eaten and how; the four underlying purposes of counteracting affliction, avoiding indulgence, avoiding self-mortification, and supporting pure conduct; and balanced versus unbalanced eating in all five respects). Restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating — provisions five and six of the fourteen provisions for the path — have now both been unpacked in far greater depth than fascicle 21's original brief mention promised, exactly as fascicle 22's own forecast anticipated.
