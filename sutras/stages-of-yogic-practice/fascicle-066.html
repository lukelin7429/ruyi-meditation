Fascicle 66 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十六 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection, Continued

Four Foods and the Life Faculty That Sustain Every Being

Continuing directly from fascicle 65, Maitreya resumes the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection at its eleventh of twenty-seven topics, the dharmas that sustain a being's own continuity — coarse food, contact food, volitional food, and consciousness food, together with the life faculty itself — before opening why the Buddha, though seven causes together sustain the conditioned aggregates, chose to speak of beings as sustained by food in particular.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 66 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Coarse Food, Absent Only from the Great Hells

This second part of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 65, addressing altogether twenty-nine dharmas grouped into twenty-seven distinct topics, of which the first ten have already been explained. The eleventh topic now opens: the dharmas that sustain a being's own continuity, established through five marks — coarse food, contact food, volitional food, consciousness food, and the life faculty. Coarse food refers to the ordinary meals a being of the desire realm actually eats, composed of smell, taste, and touch, which transform and decay once swallowed and thereby nourish the faculties; contact food refers to whatever soft, smooth, warm, or cool sensation the six consciousnesses encounter through contact with their objects, giving rise to joy that likewise nourishes the faculties, as when total absorption in a play or a performance leaves a person unaware of hunger for an entire day; volitional food refers to the sixth consciousness's own hopeful longing toward a desired object, sustaining life the way a traveler's hope of soon reaching water and food keeps their body from collapsing even before any actual nourishment is received; and consciousness food refers to the eighth consciousness itself, which, strengthened by the power of the other three foods, continues to hold and sustain every faculty in turn. The life faculty refers to a further capacity, resting on the very seed that gives rise to the eighth consciousness, which carries forward, without interruption, the specific functioning proper to a given rebirth-class as shaped by past karmic action, its own duration fixed and provisionally designated the life faculty. Regarding the distribution of coarse food specifically: it is found, in its ordinary, tangible form, throughout all five destinies of the desire realm, humans, gods, hell-beings, hungry ghosts, and animals alike, with only the great hells themselves genuinely lacking it altogether, since beings born even into the lesser, more scattered hells still retain a subtle form of coarse food, a faint inner movement of wind within the body's own organs sustaining them a while longer, coarse food in its fullest sense being entirely absent only from the great hells' own most severe suffering.

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中思所成慧地之二。復次，由五相故，建立任持諸法差別。一、段食，二、觸食，三、意思食，四、識食，五、命根。若麤段食，於欲界五趣中皆現可得。此於一分各別那落迦，非大那落迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second part of the Compendium of Decisive Discernment's Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection. Furthermore, by five marks, the dharmas that sustain a being are established: coarse food, contact food, volitional food, consciousness food, and the life faculty. Coarse food is presently found throughout the five destinies of the desire realm. It is found in a portion of the lesser hells individually, though not in the great hells.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the careful distinction between the great hells, entirely without coarse food, and the lesser hells, which still retain a subtle trace of it in the form of a faint inner wind, as a genuinely precise piece of cosmology rather than a mere aside, reminding the reader that even within a single broad category like "hell," this treatise resists flattening real and significant differences of degree. He highlights the everyday illustration of a person absorbed in a performance and unaware of hunger for a whole day as an unusually vivid and immediately recognizable example of contact food actually nourishing a being, grounding what could otherwise seem a purely technical classification in ordinary human experience.

2. Section: What Sustains and What Is Sustained, Opening a Question About Seven Causes

The remaining three foods, together with the life faculty, are by contrast found universally throughout all three realms without exception. It is precisely upon the ceaselessly flowing aggregates of form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness that the provisional name of a being is placed, and it is for this reason that the World-Honored One taught that these same dharmas, coarse food, contact food, volitional food, consciousness food, and the life faculty together, sustain a being and keep them from falling apart — what does the sustaining is these five dharmas, while what is sustained is simply this same ever-flowing collection of the five aggregates themselves. A further topic then opens, resolving an apparent difficulty: if, in fact, seven distinct causes together sustain the conditioned aggregates and keep them from falling apart, why did the World-Honored One speak only of a being's own continuity as resting on food in particular? These seven causes are: arising itself, since it is only once the aggregates have actually arisen that any continuity at all becomes possible, there being no such thing as continuity belonging to what has never arisen in the first place; the life faculty; food itself; and mastery of mind, together with several further causes yet to be named.

Chinese Source

餘食及命，遍三界中皆現可得。由於諸行假立有情，是故世尊，說此諸法任持有情，令住不壞。問：有七因緣，任持諸行令住不壞，何故世尊但說有情由食而住？何等為七？一、生是諸行住因。由諸行生，方得有住，無有無生而有住者。二、命根。三、食。

Ru-Yi Translation

The remaining foods and the life faculty are found throughout all three realms. It is upon the aggregates that a being is provisionally named, and so the World-Honored One taught that these dharmas sustain a being and keep them from falling apart. Question: if seven causes sustain the aggregates and keep them from falling apart, why did the World-Honored One speak only of beings resting on food? What are the seven? First, arising, since continuity is only possible once the aggregates have arisen. Second, the life faculty. Third, food.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the clean division between what sustains, the four foods and the life faculty, and what is sustained, the five aggregates provisionally called a being, as a natural extension of this entire ground's own recurring insistence that a being is never anything more than these same causally arising processes, now examined specifically through the lens of what actually keeps that process going moment to moment. He notes the question just opened, why the Buddha singled out food alone from among seven genuine sustaining causes, as a promising invitation to look more closely at food's own particular significance among these seven, likely because it is the cause a practitioner can most directly observe and work with in ordinary life.

3. Section: Five Reasons the Buddha Singled Out Food, from Easy Grasp to the Four Foundations of Mindfulness

The remaining four causes are then completed: mastery of mind, whereby one possessed of both deep concentration and the supernatural knowledge of the domain may, through this same mastery, actually extend the life faculty itself, dwelling for a full eon or even longer; the coming together of causes and conditions, whereby wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral dharmas alike continue in unbroken succession for exactly as long as the very conditions sustaining life, food, clothing, shelter, and medicine among them, remain unexhausted; shared and unshared karmic action, whereby past deeds, wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral, continue to draw forth both the aggregates themselves and the surrounding world they inhabit, this same karmic action distinguished into what is shared with others, such as mountains and rivers, and what is not, such as one's own house, and further into what is shared even within one's own body, such as the outwardly visible face others can see, and what is entirely unshared, such as the inner faculties whose private sensations only oneself can ever feel; and freedom from obstruction, whereby the aggregates, once arisen, continue in unbroken, self-similar succession for as long as no destructive condition, disease, fire, or the like, actually intervenes to bring that succession to an end. Though all seven of these causes together genuinely sustain the aggregates, the four foods remain the primary cause among them, sustaining life through many different channels at once, and the Buddha singled out food in particular for five further reasons: because even a fool or an infant, without any instruction whatsoever, immediately grasps hunger and thirst and knows to eat and drink, unlike these other, subtler causes; because food alone can restore weakened faculties and elements and actually cure illness; because even a being destined for great longevity will die before their time if deprived of food; and because contemplating the four foods skillfully opens the way into practicing the four foundations of mindfulness — coarse food contemplated as though it were starving parents forced, in direst need, to eat their own child's flesh, so that all relish for it falls away; contact food contemplated as though it were a skinned cow, its raw flesh tormented by insects and thorns, so that craving for any of the three feelings ceases; volitional food contemplated as though it were a great fire raging through a city, to be avoided at all costs lest one fall into it and surely perish, so that craving for future existence is cut off; and consciousness food contemplated as though it were a captured thief pierced through by three hundred spears, so that consciousness food itself is finally severed, and with it name-and-form, and with that severance, no further existence is ever again received.

Chinese Source

答：雖由如是七種因緣，諸行得住，然此四食是諸行住多分因緣，由種種門，能令諸行相續而住。又此諸食，能令有情相續而住，易取易入，乃至愚夫嬰兒等類亦能隨覺，非所餘法。又此諸食，能令羸損諸根大種皆得增益，又令疾病亦得除愈，非所餘法。又有長壽諸有情類，若不得食，非時中夭。又此諸食，令易入道，能修身等四種念住。謂一切有情食所住故。是故由此五種因緣，世尊但說一切有情由食而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

Answer: though all seven causes sustain the aggregates, the four foods are the primary cause, sustaining them through many channels. These foods also let beings continue easily, grasped even by a fool or infant. They restore weakened faculties and elements and cure illness. Even long-lived beings die prematurely without them. And they make it easy to enter the path by cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness. For these five reasons, the World-Honored One spoke of all beings as sustained by food.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four contemplative similes given here, starving parents driven to eat their own child, a skinned cow tormented by insects, a raging fire threatening the unwary, a thief pierced by three hundred spears, as among the most deliberately shocking imagery anywhere in this treatise, each one calibrated to sever a specific, deeply rooted craving by making its true cost impossible to look away from. He highlights the everyday reason given first, that even a fool or an infant instinctively grasps hunger and thirst without any teaching at all, as this passage's own explanation for why the Buddha chose food as his teaching's own starting point, beginning wherever a listener's own experience already stands rather than requiring abstract doctrine to be understood first.

4. Section: Where the Life Faculty Alone Sustains, and What Kind of Form Is Food

A further question then opens: for what reason did the Buddha also teach that the aggregates are sustained by the life faculty specifically? The answer is that there exist certain places where food is never lacking at all, and where seeking food presents no difficulty whatsoever, and in such places a being is sustained purely by the power of the life faculty itself, abiding for exactly as long as their own karmically determined lifespan allows — this describing every realm apart from the hungry ghosts, animals, and human beings, namely the gods of the desire realm, the hell-beings, and the gods of the form and formless realms alike, the heavenly gods sustained by the sheer power of their own great merit, the hell-beings bound by their own karmic suffering until it is exhausted, and the gods of the form and formless realms nourished by the very joy of meditative absorption rather than needing coarse food at all. It is for this reason that the World-Honored One, speaking specifically of these same places, taught that beings there are sustained by the life faculty. A further topic then carefully distinguishes coarse food in particular: this same coarse food, one should say, is subsumed under the sense-fields of smell, taste, and touch.

Chinese Source

何緣復說依止命根諸行得住？謂有是處，曾無飲食有所闕乏，非求飲食有所艱難，於彼處所，唯由命根勢力而住，如其所感壽量而住。是故世尊依彼處所，說諸有情由命根故，諸行得住。復次，此中段食，當言香、味、觸處所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

For what reason is it also said that the aggregates are sustained by the life faculty? Because there are places where food is never lacking and never difficult to obtain, and there beings are sustained purely by the power of the life faculty, abiding for as long as their own destined lifespan. Hence the World-Honored One, speaking of those places, taught that beings there are sustained by the life faculty. Furthermore, this same coarse food should be said to be subsumed under the sense-fields of smell, taste, and touch.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of food and the life faculty as two complementary explanations for the very same underlying fact, that some beings' continuity is sustained through active daily effort while others' is sustained purely through the passive unfolding of their own accumulated karma, the Buddha choosing whichever explanation actually fits the audience and realm being addressed rather than insisting on one single account for every being everywhere. He notes the gods of the form and formless realms, nourished by meditative joy rather than any coarse food at all, as a fitting reminder of a theme already explored at length in the previous fascicle, that even sustenance itself takes an entirely different form once craving for the coarser pleasures of the desire realm has been left behind.

5. Section: Swallowing Is Contact Food, Digesting Well Is Coarse Food

The reason coarse food is subsumed under smell, taste, and touch alone, and never under form or sound, is that smell, taste, and touch are precisely what actually transform through digestion, nourishing the faculties when this transformation proceeds properly and harming the body when it does not, whereas form and sound possess no comparable power to nourish or diminish anything at all through such transformation, a being never truly consuming a food's own color but only its smell, taste, and texture. A further distinction then follows regarding the very moment food should properly be called coarse food: at the moment of swallowing itself, when a portion of food brings the mind delight and every faculty a sense of ease, this is not yet called coarse food at all, but only contact food; only once that same food has actually been received and peacefully digested, genuinely increasing the body's own ease, does the moment of digestion itself deserve the name coarse food — and food that has fully ripened through digestion yet fails to nourish the faculties in this way, even though genuinely transformed, still does not count as coarse food. A four-cornered analysis then distinguishes a mere food-item from genuine food more carefully still: some food-items are not genuine food at all, namely whatever portion of food fails to actually nourish the faculties and elements; some genuine food is not a mere food-item, namely contact, volition, and consciousness, each of which nourishes the faculties and elements without being any such portioned substance; some genuine food is also a food-item, namely whatever portion of food does nourish the faculties and elements, this being coarse food in its full and proper sense; and some things are neither a food-item nor genuine food at all, namely contact, volition, or consciousness that fails to nourish the faculties and elements in any way.

Chinese Source

由香、味、觸，若正消變，便能長養；不正消變，乃為損減。色等餘法無有長養、損減消變，是故說彼非段食性。若諸段物，於吞咽時，令心歡喜，諸根悅豫，當於爾時不名段食，但名觸食。若受用已，安隱消變，增長安樂，於消變時乃名段食。問：若有段物，亦是食耶？設是食者，亦段物耶？答：如其所應，當作四句。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because smell, taste, and touch, when properly transformed, nourish, and when improperly transformed, harm, while form and the rest possess no such power to nourish or diminish through transformation, they are not coarse food. When a portion of food brings delight and ease at the moment of swallowing, this is not yet coarse food but only contact food. Only once peacefully digested and genuinely increasing the body's own ease does the moment of digestion deserve the name coarse food. Question: is every food-item genuine food, and is every genuine food a food-item? Answer: this should be answered through four distinct cases, as appropriate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the careful distinction between the mere pleasure of swallowing, still only contact food, and the true nourishment of digestion, genuine coarse food, as a precise piece of physiological observation any reader can verify directly against their own experience, the immediate delight of a first bite never itself guaranteeing the food will actually agree with the body afterward. He highlights the four-cornered analysis distinguishing a food-item from genuine food as a further example of this treatise's own recurring insistence on separating a thing's outward form from its actual function, a portion of food that fails to nourish being, in the end, no more genuinely "food" than a name without the reality behind it.

6. Section: Closing the Four Foods, Opening What Ripens and What Is Born of Ripening

This same fourfold analysis extends, in just the same way and as appropriate, to each of the remaining foods, contact, volitional food, and consciousness food alike, each admitting of its own four cases in turn — this closing the entire account of the dharmas that sustain a being. A further, twelfth topic then opens: dharmas possessing a ripened result yet to come, dharmas without any such ripened result yet to come, the ripened result itself, and dharmas born of that same ripening — every one of these should be properly understood. Dharmas possessing a ripened result yet to come refer to whatever outflow and whatever dharma still bound by outflow, namely the five aggregates as objects of grasping, retain their own power, remain undamaged, and have not yet been either exhausted by some future experience or fully consumed by a present one. Dharmas without any ripened result yet to come refer, by contrast, to whatever is entirely free of outflow, to neutral dharmas, to wholesome or unwholesome dharmas whose own outflow has already been severed, and to whatever ripened result has already fully ripened and run its course. The ripened result itself refers to the very last moment of mind at the time of death, together with the moment, immediately following and without interruption, that first establishes the continuity of the next rebirth, and every moment of mind abiding in its own basic nature from that point onward — this same continuous result, according to the Great Vehicle's own teaching, being none other than the ālaya-consciousness itself, the true master of retribution and the genuine ripened result in its fullest sense. And dharmas born of that same ripening refer to the many and varied states belonging to the six evolving consciousnesses, each arising in turn from the very seeds held within this same ālaya-consciousness.

Chinese Source

如是所餘觸乃至識，隨其所應，皆作四句。復次，若有異熟法、無異熟法、若異熟法、若異熟生法，皆應了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The remaining foods, from contact through consciousness, should likewise each be answered through four cases, as appropriate. Furthermore, dharmas possessing a ripened result yet to come, dharmas without one, the ripened result itself, and dharmas born of ripening — all of these should be properly understood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fascicle's own swift transition, from the intimate, bodily concern of what actually nourishes a being to the far more abstract question of karmic ripening across an entire lifetime and beyond, as characteristic of this treatise's own habit of moving fluidly between the immediate and the cosmic without ever losing the thread connecting them. He highlights the identification of the ālaya-consciousness as the true ripened result, the very continuity spanning a being's last moment of one life and the first moment of the next, as a valuable reminder that karmic ripening, however abstractly described in Abhidharma terms, always ultimately traces back to this same single, continuously flowing consciousness already so carefully explained throughout the treatise's own earlier fascicles.

7. Section: Strong or Weak, Damaged or Undamaged, Already Spent or Not Yet Spent

Regarding dharmas still possessing a ripened result yet to come: briefly, this refers to outflow itself and to whatever is bound by outflow, provided each retains its own power, remains undamaged, and has not yet had its result fully consumed. Retaining power means being unwholesome, the desire realm's own coarse afflictions of craving, hostility, and the like being genuinely capable of provoking further unwholesome deeds and drawing forth the suffering fruit of birth and death, whereas the far subtler afflictions bound up with obscuring neutrality, such as the innate self-love, self-view, self-delusion, and self-conceit conjoined with the seventh consciousness, or the refined craving for form and formlessness belonging to the higher realms, are said to lack this same power, being too faint to draw forth such a fruit on their own. Remaining undamaged means not yet having been severed by either of the two paths capable of removing craving, the mundane path of meditative absorption, which can suppress affliction's own manifestation without uprooting its seed, or the transcendent path of the noble ones, which alone can sever that seed entirely; whatever has already been cut off by either of these paths is instead called damaged. And not yet having had its result fully consumed means either that a future ripened result has not yet come to pass at all, or that a present ripened result is only now actually unfolding, as opposed to whatever ripened result already belongs entirely to the past, its fruit already exhausted and nothing further left to receive. Only a dharma satisfying all three of these conditions together, retaining power, remaining undamaged, and not yet having been fully consumed, is properly called a dharma possessing a ripened result yet to come; every dharma failing to satisfy them, whether entirely free of outflow, morally neutral, wholesome or unwholesome yet already severed of its own outflow, or a ripened result already fully matured and past, is instead called a dharma without any ripened result yet to come.

Chinese Source

略說有異熟法，謂漏及有漏，彼要有力、不被損害、受用未盡，當知是名有異熟法。於諸漏中，若不善者，說名有力；有覆無記，說名無力。於有漏中，若善不善，說名有力；餘名無力。若漏有漏，為世出世二離欲道之所斷者，名被損害；與此相違，名不損害。若過去世，其異熟果已成熟者，名受用已盡。若未來世，當與異熟果者；若現在世，其異熟果正現前者；名受用未盡。若諸無漏、無記、有漏若善不善，有漏已斷，若異熟果先已成熟，如是皆名無異熟法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly, dharmas possessing a ripened result yet to come are outflow and what is bound by outflow, retaining power, remaining undamaged, and not yet having their result fully consumed. Among afflictions, the unwholesome retain power; the obscuring neutral lack it. Among what is bound by outflow, the wholesome or unwholesome retain power; the rest lack it. Whatever is severed by either the mundane or transcendent path of removing craving is damaged; otherwise, undamaged. A ripened result already matured in the past has been fully consumed; one yet to come, or presently unfolding, has not. Whatever is free of outflow, neutral, already severed, or already fully matured is a dharma without any ripened result yet to come.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the distinction between the desire realm's own coarse, obviously destructive afflictions, genuinely "strong" in their power to draw forth suffering, and the far subtler obscuring afflictions such as innate self-love bound up with the seventh consciousness, merely "weak," as a valuable corrective against assuming that only the most dramatic, easily noticed afflictions actually matter, when in truth these quieter, more deeply buried ones can persist for a very long time simply because they are too faint to draw urgent attention to themselves. He treats the careful threefold test given here, retaining power, remaining undamaged, and not yet consumed, as this treatise's own precise way of specifying exactly when an affliction can still be said to threaten a future result and when it genuinely no longer can, a distinction with real practical value for any practitioner trying to assess their own remaining work.

8. Section: The Last Moment of Dying Is Always Neutral — The Ālaya as the True Ripened Result

A further topic then opens: ripening and dharmas born of ripening, examined this time through the lens of mind itself. The very last moment of mind at the time of death is itself the ripened result. A dying being's own experience may, in the moments leading up to this point, grow progressively brighter if wholesome deeds predominate in that life, or progressively darker if unwholesome deeds predominate, yet the very last moment of consciousness itself, the instant it actually departs the body, is neither wholesome nor unwholesome but purely neutral in nature, this same neutral, ripened consciousness being none other than the ālaya-consciousness itself. The moment that immediately and without any interruption establishes the continuity of the next rebirth is likewise itself the ripened result, comprising both the very first moment of the intermediate state between death and rebirth and the very first moment of the new life that follows it, this same uninterrupted continuity being drawn forth purely by the power of past karmic action and thus properly called ripening.

Chinese Source

又臨終時最後念心，是異熟法。若結生相續無間之心，亦是異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the very last moment of mind at the time of death is itself a ripened dharma. The moment that establishes the continuity of the next rebirth without interruption is likewise itself ripening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the observation that a dying being's very last moment of consciousness is always neutral, regardless of how bright or dark the experience leading up to it may have felt, as a genuinely striking piece of Abhidharma psychology, suggesting that the drama of a wholesome or unwholesome life finally gives way, in its very last instant, to something calm and undifferentiated beneath both. He highlights the identification of this same neutral final moment with the ālaya-consciousness itself as the thread tying together everything this treatise has said about that consciousness across many earlier fascicles, the ālaya now shown to be present not only throughout an ordinary waking life but precisely at the single most decisive transition any being ever passes through.

9. Section: Even After Meditating into a Higher Realm, the Ālaya Stays Tied to This One

From this same moment onward, every mind abiding simply in its own basic nature throughout the remainder of that life is likewise itself ripening, excluding only wholesome mind, defiled mind, and whatever neutral mind arises specifically through deliberate effort, such as the mind engaged in ordinary bodily comportment, in skillful craft, or in supernatural transformation, together with the seventh consciousness's own defiled activity — every other moment of mind, quietly sustaining the body without any such deliberate coloring, belongs to this same self-abiding ripening consciousness, already shown elsewhere, through the many converging lines of reasoning establishing the ālaya-consciousness's own existence, to be this very consciousness continuously at work beneath the whole of ordinary experience. A further and final case then closes this same topic: even once a being's own mind has become free of craving for the desire realm, cultivating concentration and thereby transforming the sixth consciousness into a wholesome or neutral mind belonging to the form or formless realm above, this same being's own ripening consciousness, apart from whatever wholesome or deliberately effortful neutral mind now belongs to that higher level, nonetheless continues, exactly as before, to remain bound to and sustain the very same desire-realm body it has always inhabited — meditative attainment transforming the sixth consciousness's own present activity, but never in itself relocating the ripening consciousness to any new realm at all, this same steadfast continuity likewise properly called ripening.

Chinese Source

從此已後，所有一切自性住心，皆是異熟。除善、染汙，及除加行無記之心，所餘皆名自性住心。若心離欲，猶故隨轉。除下地善及與加行無記之心，當知此心亦是異熟。

Ru-Yi Translation

From this point onward, every mind abiding in its own basic nature is likewise ripening, excluding wholesome mind, defiled mind, and neutral mind arising through deliberate effort. If the mind becomes free of craving yet the ripening consciousness still continues as before, apart from the lower realm's wholesome mind and deliberately effortful neutral mind, one should know that this too is ripening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the observation that the ālaya-consciousness remains tied to the desire-realm body even after a meditator's own sixth consciousness has ascended, through concentration, into a form-realm or formless-realm state of mind, as a genuinely important clarification against a natural but mistaken assumption, that deep meditative attainment alone somehow relocates a being to a higher realm outright. He highlights this same steady, unmoved continuity of the ripening consciousness beneath whatever transformation the sixth consciousness undergoes as a fitting final note to this entire discussion, the ālaya's own quiet, background persistence throughout an entire life, and even through the most exalted meditative states reached within it, being exactly the kind of continuity this whole topic has set out to describe.

10. Section: Neither Painful Nor Pleasant — The Feeling Belonging to Ripening Itself

This same ripening consciousness, wherever it is found throughout the three realms, should always be said to be purely neutral in its own nature, obscuring nothing at all. Dharmas born of this same ripening, by contrast, refer to whatever wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral dharma among the six evolving consciousnesses arises from its own seed held within the ripening consciousness, excluding only whatever seed has already been severed by some path of removing craving or belongs to some attainment not yet actually reached — every dharma so arising, one should know, is properly called a dharma born of ripening. A further topic then opens, examining this same distinction now through the lens of feeling itself: the very last moment of mind at death, together with the first and second moments immediately establishing the continuity of the next rebirth, are, throughout the three realms without exception, accompanied only by a feeling neither painful nor pleasant, this same neutral feeling alone belonging to ripening in the strictest sense; every other feeling that follows is instead born of that same ripening. Beings born in hell, and a portion of those born as hungry ghosts or animals, experience continuous pain and sorrow born of ripening in this way; beings born as humans or as gods of the desire realm experience no fixed pattern at all, sometimes pain and sorrow, sometimes joy and pleasure, and sometimes only a feeling neither painful nor pleasant, depending entirely on the karma that shaped their birth; beings born in the first or second meditative absorption experience only joy and pleasure born of ripening, continuously; beings born in the third absorption experience only pleasure, continuously; and beings born in the fourth absorption or above experience only the neutral feeling, continuously, and nothing else.

Chinese Source

又此異熟於一切處，當言唯是無覆無記。若從一切種子異熟，除其已斷未得之法，餘自種子為因所生，若善、不善、或復無記，如是一切，當知皆名異熟生法。復次，一切處最後沒心，及隨初第二相續心，於三界中，當知唯有非苦樂受。餘苦樂受，應知皆是異熟所生。生那落迦諸有情類，異熟無間，有異熟生苦憂相續。若生人中及欲界天諸有情類，無有決定。若生初二靜慮，異熟無間，唯異熟生喜受相續。若生第三靜慮，唯異熟生樂受相續。若生第四靜慮已上，唯有異熟不苦不樂受。

Ru-Yi Translation

This ripening consciousness, everywhere in the three realms, is purely neutral. Dharmas arising from every seed of ripening, excluding what is severed or not yet attained, whether wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral, are all called dharmas born of ripening. Furthermore, the last moment at death and the first two moments establishing rebirth are, throughout the three realms, accompanied only by neutral feeling; every other feeling is born of ripening. Hell-beings experience continuous pain and sorrow. Humans and desire-realm gods have no fixed pattern. Those born in the first or second absorption experience only joy and pleasure. The third, only pleasure. The fourth and above, only neutral feeling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the graduated progression traced here, joy and pleasure together in the first two absorptions, pleasure alone in the third, and neutral feeling alone from the fourth onward, as a precise map of how a meditator's own inner experience quietly refines itself with each successive stage, excitement giving way to steadier pleasure, and steadier pleasure in turn giving way to a stillness beyond either. He highlights the unpredictable mixture of pain, pleasure, and neutrality belonging to ordinary human and desire-realm-god existence, in sharp contrast to the fixed and singular feeling belonging to each of the higher absorptions, as a fitting reminder of exactly what makes the desire realm so unsettled compared to the ordered, gradually purified experience found progressively higher up.

11. Section: Desirable Ripening throughout the Form Realm, and White Karma's Own White Result

It should accordingly be understood that this same feeling, born of ripening, is what is meant by ripening in this further sense: since it is embraced by vast and expansive joy and pleasure, the joy of the first absorption born of having left the desire realm behind, the joy of the second born of settled concentration itself, and the subtle pleasure of the third born of having left even joy behind, all three are called desirable ripening; and even the fourth absorption's own equanimity, neither painful nor pleasant yet still the very fruit of wholesome karmic action actually presenting itself, likewise nourishes body and mind and is for this reason also called desirable ripening. Whatever stands opposed to this, by contrast, should be known as undesirable ripening. A further topic then opens, distinguishing among the very causes producing such ripening as they relate to feeling: white, white-ripening karmic action, namely the unmoving karmic action cultivated through the meditative absorptions and formless attainments of the form and formless realms, yields a wholly neutral, undefiled ripened result whose every seed of feeling belongs, without exception, to what is desirable and pleasing — such karmic action being called white because both its cause and its ripened result alike are pure, and it should accordingly be understood that whatever result it yields is uniformly desirable and uniformly pleasing.

Chinese Source

是故當知，即此受於彼名異熟生。廣大喜樂所攝受故，彼諸善業不苦不樂正現前時，亦名可愛異熟。與此相違，當知名不可愛異熟。復次，白白異熟業所得無覆無記異熟果，一向可愛受種子所攝受故，當知一向可愛、一向可意。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that this feeling is what is meant by ripening in this sense. Embraced by vast joy and pleasure, and likewise the fourth absorption's own neutral feeling actually arising as wholesome karma's fruit, both are called desirable ripening. Whatever is contrary to this is undesirable ripening. Furthermore, white, white-ripening karmic action yields a neutral, undefiled ripened result whose feeling-seeds are uniformly desirable, and should be known as uniformly desirable and pleasing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the inclusion of the fourth absorption's own equanimity among desirable ripening, despite being neither painful nor pleasant in the ordinary sense, as an important reminder that "desirable" here tracks whether a result is wholesome karma's own genuine fruit rather than whether it feels dramatically pleasurable, a subtler but no less real form of benefit than the more obvious joy of the lower absorptions. He treats the naming of white, white-ripening karmic action, pure in both cause and result together, as this passage's own elegant shorthand for exactly the kind of unmoving, meditation-born karma capable of producing an entirely undefiled and pleasing rebirth, setting up a natural contrast with whatever darker or mixed karmic causes are likely to be introduced next.

12. Section: Black Karma, and the Mixed Karma of a Wavering Heart

Black, black-ripening karmic action stands opposed to this same white karmic action just described, referring to the unwholesome deeds that draw forth the miserable destinies, both their shared and particular results alike, this same black cause yielding a wholly undesirable, wholly displeasing, and wholly unwelcome black result in turn. A further kind of karmic action then opens: mixed black-and-white karma, itself established in four distinct ways. Considered through the seeds of feeling that follow it, such action carries both a white seed and a black seed at once, and so yields both a pleasant and a painful feeling in turn, as when a wholesome deed is carried out through some unwholesome means, scolding another out of genuine care yet still tinged with irritation, or an unwholesome deed is carried out through some wholesome-seeming means, giving generously yet still hoping, underneath, to gain some advantage from the recipient in return. Considered through the very category of rebirth into which it leads, this same mixed action is established wherever both painful and pleasant results are found together within a single destination, this describing every realm apart from hell below and the form and formless heavens above, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, and the gods of the desire realm all experiencing this same mixture of pain and pleasure in turn. And considered through the particular matter at hand, such action is seen wherever a single person's own intention shifts from benefiting another to no longer wishing to benefit them within the very same undertaking, moment to moment, or wherever, as in stealing from the wealthy only to give to the poor, an unwholesome act and a wholesome one are bound up together within one single deed.

Chinese Source

黑黑異熟業，當知與此相違。黑白俱異熟業，二種種子所隨逐故，所得異熟果，當知亦有二種異熟生受。又黑白業，由生類差別建立。謂於是處黑白俱有，即此處業，總立黑白。又由事差別建立。謂如有一，隨於一事，於一時間起利益心而現在前；即於此事，復於一時不利益心而現在前；或奪他物而行惠施。

Ru-Yi Translation

Black, black-ripening karmic action should be known as the opposite of white karma. Mixed black-and-white karmic action, followed by two kinds of seed, yields a ripened result likewise accompanied by two kinds of feeling. Such mixed action is also established by the very category of rebirth, wherever both black and white results are found together in one place. It is further established by the particular matter at hand, as when one's intention to benefit another shifts, within the same undertaking, to no longer wishing to benefit them, or as when one robs another's wealth in order to give it away in charity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the example of stealing from the wealthy to give to the poor as a genuinely provocative test case for this entire fourfold scheme, forcing the reader to confront directly that even an act popularly celebrated as heroic remains, on this treatise's own accounting, a mixture of black and white rather than purely white, since the means themselves were never made wholesome merely by the nobility of the end. He highlights the everyday example of scolding someone out of genuine care while still betraying real irritation as an equally sharp illustration much closer to ordinary life, most people's own well-intentioned actions carrying at least some trace of this same mixture rather than the pure, single-colored karma described in the two extreme cases just before it.

13. Section: Even Righteous Anger Still Counts as Black — Closing on Ripening, Opening Cause and Result

Mixed karmic action is established, in its fourth sense, through its own very nature, as when a single person, having agreed to benefit a particular being, nonetheless goes on, through some further circumstance, to actually cause that same being harm — as when someone, encountering a person of truly cruel and violent conduct, gives rise to a thought conjoined with genuine anger, disapproving deeply of that same person's evil deeds. One should know that this very thought, precisely because it is conjoined with anger, falls into the black portion, even though the underlying disapproval of evil, arising from a genuinely wholesome impulse, falls into the white portion — it is for this reason that such an act is called mixed, black and white together; though such righteous indignation may seem reasonable enough on its own terms, being conjoined with hatred still places it squarely within the black portion, the truly wholesome response instead belonging to a capable bodhisattva, who is able to guide even the most evil of beings through skillful means alone, without ever needing anger to arise at all. Every other case of this same kind should be understood in just the same way. This closes the account of dharmas possessing and lacking a ripened result. A thirteenth topic then opens: dharmas having a cause and result, and dharmas without one, established across twelve distinct ways in turn. Regarding the first: whatever wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral dharma retains its own unsevered seed, capable of giving rise to itself once again, is, through this very generative cause, called a dharma having a cause.

Chinese Source

又由自性，建立如是黑白俱業。譬如有一，於極暴虐作惡人所，發生瞋恚俱行之思，不喜彼惡。當知此思瞋俱行故，墮黑分中；不喜樂彼惡俱行故，墮白分中。是故此業說名黑白。如是所餘種類亦爾。復次，若善、不善、無記諸法所有種子，未被損害，彼一切法皆由能生生起因故，名有因法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Mixed karma is also established through its own nature, as when one, encountering a person of cruel conduct, gives rise to a thought conjoined with anger, disapproving of that evil. This thought, being conjoined with anger, falls into the black portion; the disapproval itself, being wholesome, falls into the white portion — hence such action is called mixed. Every remaining case should be understood likewise. Furthermore, whatever wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral dharma retains its own unsevered seed, capable of giving rise to itself, is thereby called a dharma having a cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the careful insistence that even righteous anger at genuine cruelty still counts as black karma, however justified it may feel, as one of this treatise's most demanding ethical teachings, refusing any exception for anger simply because its object happens to deserve disapproval. He holds up the bodhisattva's own capacity to guide evildoers through skillful means alone, without anger ever arising at all, as the treatise's own real aspiration here, a standard few ordinary practitioners could meet at once but one worth keeping clearly in view as the direction genuine practice moves toward, even while the twelvefold analysis of cause and result now opening turns to altogether more technical ground.

14. Section: Womb, Moisture, and Egg — Why Every Kind of Birth Has Its Own Cause

Regarding the second establishment: whatever karmic action and affliction a being has previously accumulated, once its ripened result actually matures anywhere throughout the three realms, this same ripened result, drawn forth precisely by that same karmic action and affliction serving as its inducing cause, is likewise called a dharma having a cause. Regarding the third: a being born from the womb depends on three conditions all actually present together as its own inducing cause, the mother's own body being healthy and free of any obstruction within the birth canal and standing at the proper time of fertility, the father and mother actually coming together in union, and the intermediate being between death and rebirth actually presenting itself at that very moment — a being so born from the womb should likewise be known as a dharma having a cause; and in just the same way, a being born from moisture, coming together through the coincidence of warmth and dampness, and a being born from an egg, developing hidden within its shell until it finally breaks free, are each likewise dharmas having a cause. Regarding the fourth: the six consciousness-bodies, arising from the eye and form all the way through the mind and mental objects, each serving as the dominant condition and the very same cooperative cause for the consciousness that depends on it, are likewise called dharmas having a cause. And regarding the fifth: mind and its mental factors, arising together in the very same moment and mutually serving, each for the other, as a shared cooperative cause, are likewise called dharmas having a cause.

Chinese Source

又先所作諸業煩惱，於三界中異熟果熟；此異熟果，由業煩惱引發因故，名有因法。又由三處正現在前引發因故，生胎生中，當知此亦名有因法。濕和合故，生濕生中；卵㲉藏故，生卵㲉中，當知亦爾。又六識身，以從眼色乃至意法，為增上緣同事因故，名有因法。又有俱生諸心心所，互為展轉同事因故，名有因法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever karmic action and affliction previously accumulated ripens throughout the three realms is, being drawn forth by that karmic action and affliction as its inducing cause, likewise called a dharma having a cause. A being born from the womb, depending on three conditions actually present as its inducing cause, is likewise a dharma having a cause; so too a being born from moisture or from an egg. The six consciousness-bodies, arising from the eye and form through the mind and mental objects as their cooperative cause, are likewise dharmas having a cause. Mind and mental factors, arising together and mutually serving as cooperative cause, are likewise dharmas having a cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three conditions required for birth from the womb, a healthy and fertile mother, the parents' actual union, and the intermediate being's own presence at the decisive moment, as a genuinely careful piece of classical embryology folded directly into this treatise's own account of causation, insisting that even so intimate and seemingly private an event as conception is, like everything else examined in this section, thoroughly conditioned rather than simply given. He highlights the extension of this same principle even to moisture-born and egg-born beings as a reminder that this treatise's own notion of "having a cause" applies with equal rigor across every mode of birth recognized in traditional cosmology, not merely the most familiar human case.

15. Section: Fruitless Toil and Fruitful Toil, Twelve Ways of Having a Cause

The remaining seven establishments complete this same twelvefold list. Wholesome dharmas are shown to have a cause through the very reverse of the three conditions given for the unwholesome, drawing close to good friends, hearing the true Dharma, and reflecting properly upon it. A defiled dwelling of mind gives rise to wrong effort, fruitless toil, and a sorrowful abiding as its own result, as when certain non-Buddhist practitioners, mistaking the four absorptions or the four formless attainments for liberation itself, subject themselves to extreme self-mortification, eating filth or keeping the vows of an ox or a dog, believing this can burn away past unwholesome karma, when in truth their toil bears no fruit at all and leaves them only in continued sorrow — this same wrong effort, fruitless toil, and sorrow being drawn forth by that same defiled dwelling as their inducing cause. An undefiled dwelling of mind, by contrast, gives rise to right effort, fruitful toil, and a joyful abiding, proper view guiding a mind free of defilement toward genuine calm-abiding and insight, this same right effort, fruitful toil, and joy likewise drawn forth by that undefiled dwelling as their cause. The mundane path that seeks release from craving further gives rise to the four meditative absorptions and the four formless attainments as its own result; the very attainment of these same absorptions and formless states in turn gives rise to actual rebirth in the higher realms they correspond to; the mundane path itself gives rise to the transcendent path built upon it; and the transcendent path, belonging to the noble eightfold path, gives rise finally to the very attainment of nirvana. By all such marks together, dharmas having a cause are established.

Chinese Source

當知與此相違三種引發因故，一切善法，名有因法。又染汙住，生邪精進，無果劬勞，生憂苦住，彼由引發因故，名有因法。又不染汙住，生正精進，有果劬勞，生喜樂住，彼由引發因故，名有因法。又世間道趣於離欲，及能引發靜慮、無色，彼由引發因故，名有因法。又出世法聖道所攝，能證涅槃；彼證涅槃，由引發因故，名有因法。由如是等所說諸相，當知建立有因諸法。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the reverse of these three causes, all wholesome dharmas are dharmas having a cause. A defiled dwelling gives rise to wrong effort, fruitless toil, and sorrow, drawn forth by that dwelling as cause. An undefiled dwelling gives rise to right effort, fruitful toil, and joy, likewise drawn forth as their cause. The mundane path toward freedom from craving gives rise to the absorptions and formless attainments as its cause. The transcendent path, belonging to the noble path, gives rise to the attainment of nirvana as its cause. By all such marks, dharmas having a cause are established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of fruitless toil born of a defiled dwelling and fruitful toil born of an undefiled one as a compact summary of this entire fascicle's own recurring warning against mistaking sincere effort for correct method, self-mortification and genuine cultivation alike requiring real exertion, yet only one of the two actually leading anywhere. He highlights the closing chain, mundane absorption giving rise to rebirth, mundane path giving rise to transcendent path, and transcendent path giving rise finally to nirvana itself, as a fitting overview of the entire Buddhist path compressed into a single twelvefold list, nothing in this progression, not even the most exalted attainment, ever arising without its own proper cause behind it.

16. Section: Five Kinds of Result — Equal-Flow, Ripened, and Dominant

A further topic then opens: dharmas having a result, distinguished across five further kinds. The very generative cause already described, whereby unwholesome or wholesome practice, once undertaken, tends to increase further unwholesome or wholesome practice in turn, yields what is called an equal-flow result, the result resembling its own cause in kind, exactly as habitual killing in one life is said to bring about a shortened lifespan in the next, a resemblance only loosely called equal-flow since the two are not, strictly speaking, of the very same moral nature. Whatever karmic action and affliction draws a being toward a future existence, once it actually and specifically ripens into that future life's own result across any of the three realms, unwholesome deeds ripening into the miserable destinies, wholesome deeds into a favorable rebirth within the desire realm, and unmoving meditative karma into the form or formless realms above, yields what is called a ripened result. And the many and varied conditions already described as inducing causes, the three conditions of the womb, the coincidence of warmth and dampness, the shelter of an egg, the eye and form and their fellow sense-conditions, mind and its co-arising mental factors, and the two contrasting triads of drawing close to bad friends or to good ones, each in turn giving rise to its own particular result, birth itself, the six consciousnesses, co-arising mental activity, or unwholesome and wholesome dharmas respectively, together yield what is called a dominant result.

Chinese Source

復次，此中能生生起因法，彼由各別等流果故，名有果法。若諸後有業及煩惱，彼由各別異熟果故，名有果法。若有三處正現在前，若濕和合正現在前，若卵㲉藏，若眼色等，若彼俱生諸心心法，若近惡友等、若近善友等二種三法，如是一切，皆由各別增上果故，名有果法。

Ru-Yi Translation

The generative cause already described, by its own particular equal-flow result, is a dharma having a result. Karmic action and affliction drawing a being toward future existence, by its own particular ripened result, is likewise a dharma having a result. The three conditions of the womb, the coincidence of warmth and dampness, the shelter of an egg, the eye and form and the rest, co-arising mind and mental factors, and the two triads of bad and good friends, all by their own particular dominant result, are dharmas having a result.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the careful qualification placed on equal-flow result, resembling its own cause only loosely rather than sharing its precise moral nature, as with killing leading merely to a shortened lifespan rather than to some further unwholesome deed exactly like the original act, a reminder that this classical fivefold scheme of results is applied here with real precision rather than treated as a set of loose folk analogies. He treats the sheer breadth of causes gathered together under a single dominant result, everything from conception to sense-perception to the company one keeps, as this passage's own demonstration that dominance is the most encompassing of the five results, capturing virtually any influence exerting real force on an outcome without itself falling into one of the four more specific categories.

17. Section: Only the Transcendent Path Truly Liberates — Closing Cause and Result, Opening Conditioned Arising

A final distinction closes the fifth and last kind of result: only the transcendent path, actually reaching the ultimate liberation from bondage, is properly called a liberation result in the fullest sense; the mundane path, though it may lead to rebirth in a higher realm, is not called a liberation result in this same ultimate sense, since it can only suppress the manifest activity of affliction belonging to a lower level without ever severing its seed, whereas the transcendent path alone genuinely severs every afflictive seed throughout the three realms and thereby actually attains nirvana — this being the very distinction between these two paths. By all such marks together, one should know, the distinctions among dharmas having a result, equal-flow, ripened, dominant, personal-effort, and liberation, are fully established. Dharmas without a cause and without a result should, by direct contrast, be understood simply as their own reversal. A further, fourteenth topic then opens: conditionally arisen dharmas, referring to every conditioned dharma as possessing no ruling self and no independent agent behind it at all, exactly as already explained at great length elsewhere in this treatise's own earlier account of skillful understanding of dependent origination — arising from no fixed cause of its own, resting on the coming together of many conditions, originally not existing and only then coming to be, and, having come to be, inevitably dispersing and ceasing once again.

Chinese Source

謂由究竟離繫果故，名有果法。若世間道，非由究竟離繫果故，名有果法。當知是名二道差別。由如是等所說相故，當知建立有果諸法差別之相。與此相違，應知建立非有因法、非有果法。復次，緣生法者，謂無主宰、無作者法。如前意地決擇緣起善巧中，已廣分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Only through ultimate liberation from bondage is a dharma having a result truly named; the mundane path, not reaching this ultimate liberation, is named otherwise — this is the distinction between the two paths. By these marks, the distinctions among dharmas having a result are fully established. By direct contrast, dharmas without a cause and without a result are established. Furthermore, conditionally arisen dharmas possess no ruling self and no independent agent, exactly as already explained at length in the earlier discussion of skillful understanding of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp line drawn here between the mundane path's merely temporary suppression and the transcendent path's genuine severance as this entire long section's own final and most important payoff, every one of the twelve causes and five results just catalogued ultimately mattering only insofar as it helps clarify exactly which practices actually lead to lasting freedom and which, however impressive their outward results, do not. He notes the swift, almost perfunctory treatment given to conditionally arisen dharmas here, essentially pointing back to a much fuller discussion already given elsewhere in the treatise, as a sign that this fascicle intends to move briskly through this topic before turning to its own next major concern.

18. Section: Five Reasons a Dharma Is Called "Inner"

A further, fifteenth topic then opens: inner dharmas, established briefly through five distinct reasons, each explaining why a given dharma comes to be called inner in the first place. The first is provisional designation, as when the world's own eight conventional descriptors, a being's name, birth-category, lineage, food, experience of pleasure and pain, lifespan, duration, and outer limit of that lifespan, are all provisionally applied to the aggregates as objects of grasping. The second is mistaken attachment, as when a being grasps at these same aggregates as a self or gives rise to the conceit of self upon them, this same self-grasping itself dividing further into an innate form, woven automatically into experience from beginningless time without needing any external wrong teaching to arise, and a discriminated form, arising only once some external wrong teaching or wrong reasoning has actually been encountered. The third is dominant power, as when the five sense faculties, through their own directing and surpassing power, actually give rise to the very differences among their corresponding external objects of form, sound, and the rest, which those same faculties then go on to receive and use. The fourth is the holding of seeds, as when a single consciousness, called in the Great Vehicle's own teaching the ālaya-consciousness, is said to hold within it the seeds of every wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral dharma alike, the lesser vehicles instead attributing this same function to the six faculties, their own supporting basis, and the six consciousnesses together, having never posited any further ālaya-consciousness of their own. And the fifth is simply the event itself, as when the five kinds of pure form together with mind, the seventh consciousness, and the six evolving consciousnesses are each, quite directly, causally arisen events in their own right.

Chinese Source

復次，略由五因，當知建立內法差別。由此因故，說名為內。何等為五？謂假名故、妄執故、增上故、攝受種子故、事故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, briefly, by five causes, the distinctions among inner dharmas are established, and it is by these same causes that they are called inner. What are the five? Through provisional designation, mistaken attachment, dominant power, the holding of seeds, and the event itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fivefold analysis of what makes something "inner" as a fitting continuation of the treatise's own long-running project of showing that even the most basic, seemingly self-evident categories, inner versus outer chief among them, are never simple givens but each rest on their own distinct and analyzable basis. He highlights the contrast between the Great Vehicle's single ālaya-consciousness, holding every seed at once, and the lesser vehicles' own more distributed account, spread across the six faculties, their support, and the six consciousnesses together, as a clear and concrete illustration of exactly the kind of doctrinal divergence this treatise elsewhere handles with real care rather than simply picking one side and ignoring the other.

19. Section: Even the Visible Eyeball Is Still Called Inner

Each of these five reasons is explained in turn. Provisional designation refers specifically to the world's own eight conventional descriptors applied to the aggregates as objects of grasping: such and such a name, such and such a class of birth, such and such a lineage, such and such food, such and such an experience of pleasure or pain, such and such a lifespan, such and such a duration, and such and such an outer limit to that lifespan, every one of these merely assigned in this way rather than belonging to the aggregates' own true nature. Mistaken attachment refers to grasping at these same internally transformed aggregates, never any truly external self, as a self or as belonging to a self, an attachment that itself divides into an innate form, woven automatically into the seventh consciousness's own constant engagement with the eighth, or intermittently into the sixth consciousness's own engagement with the aggregates as a whole or in part, and a discriminated form, arising in the sixth consciousness alone only once some external wrong teaching concerning the aggregates or concerning a self has actually been encountered and believed. Dominant power refers to the five sense faculties' own directing and surpassing capacity to give rise to their own corresponding objects of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, which those same faculties then go on to receive and use. The holding of seeds refers, in the Great Vehicle's own teaching, to the single ālaya-consciousness holding within it every seed of every wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral dharma at once. And the event itself refers simply to the five kinds of pure form together with mind, the seventh consciousness, and the six evolving consciousnesses, each a causally arisen event in its own right. A further point then extends this same account of provisional designation still further: even the coarse sense-organs themselves, the very visible eyeball and its fellow organs supporting each subtle faculty, though technically classed among the processed, external objects a faculty perceives rather than among the faculties themselves, are nonetheless also called inner, precisely because they are held and appropriated within the very same body by the ālaya-consciousness itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the extension of "inner" even to the coarse, visible sense-organs, technically classified as external objects yet still called inner because held within the body by the ālaya-consciousness, as a genuinely careful piece of boundary-drawing, showing that "inner" and "outer" here track something more precise than mere physical location or ordinary visibility. He highlights the parallel drawn between innate self-attachment, woven automatically into the seventh consciousness's own constant relationship with the eighth, and discriminated self-attachment, arising only after encountering some external wrong teaching, as a welcome return to a distinction already carefully established earlier in this treatise's own extended refutation of a real self, now applied specifically to explain why the aggregates themselves come to be called inner in the first place.

20. Section: Form and Name — Why the Five Aggregates Divide This Way

A further, sixteenth topic then opens: the five aggregates should be understood as subsumed entirely under form and name. Why is this so? The aggregate of form is called form precisely because it can be found increasing at this or that location, thereby possessing its own spatial presence, and because a mere touch of the hand or a passing stone is enough to damage and destroy it, thereby possessing its own vulnerability to contact. The remaining four aggregates, by contrast, are called name precisely because they depend on the very power of assigned, designated names to direct themselves toward their own objects, mental consciousness in particular relying on such names to discriminate and engage with whatever it encounters, and it is for this reason that these four formless aggregates together are called name. A further, seventeenth topic then opens: dharmas that are grasped and appropriated, and dharmas that are not, established through five marks. First, only form itself, and never mind or any mental factor, is properly called grasped. Second, among form specifically, only the inner faculties together with the coarser organs directly supporting them are called grasped, excluding whatever external form remains unattached to any faculty at all. Third, mind and mental factors continuously holding and never abandoning a given form is what makes that form grasped, excluding hair, nails, and other such parts no longer actually held, together with the entire body once life has departed it at death. And fourth, grasped form itself varies and changes through four further causal conditions.

The five aggregates should be understood as subsumed under form and name. Why? Because the aggregate of form is found increasing at particular locations, and is damaged by mere touch, it is called form. Because the remaining four aggregates depend on the power of designated names to direct themselves toward their objects, they are called name. Furthermore, briefly, by five marks, grasped and ungrasped dharmas are established. First, only form is called grasped. Second, among form, only the inner faculties and their supporting organs are called grasped. Third, form continuously held by mind and mental factors is called grasped. Fourth, grasped form varies through four causal conditions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the clean division of the five aggregates into form and name as a useful compression of everything this treatise has said about the aggregates across many earlier fascicles into just two words, form standing for whatever occupies space and can be damaged, name standing for whatever instead depends on language's own power to direct attention toward an object. He highlights the precise boundary drawn around what counts as genuinely grasped form, excluding shed hair, cut nails, and the body once life has departed, as a fittingly exact piece of definition, showing that appropriation by the ālaya-consciousness, not mere physical attachment or resemblance, is what actually determines whether a given form belongs to a living being's own grasped body at all.

21. Section: Four Ways the Grasped Body Itself Changes

These four causal conditions are: being pressed upon by external form, whereby harmful external conditions of earth, water, fire, or wind actually presenting themselves, flood, storm, or fire among them, cause the grasped body to give rise to pain, sorrow, and distress, while beneficial external conditions actually presenting themselves, such as drinking pure water or breathing clean air, cause that same body to give rise instead to pleasure, ease, and genuine benefit; the mutual imbalance of the internal elements, whereby an excess of the earth element leads to obesity, an excess of the water element to swelling, an excess of the fire element to inflammation rising within the body, and an excess of the wind element to conditions such as gout or difficulty breathing; the frequent manifestation of afflictions such as craving and hostility, whereby, much as traditional medicine likewise teaches, excessive joy harms the heart, anger harms the liver, worry and rumination harm the spleen, grief harms the lungs, and fear harms the kidneys, each such emotion, driven by an underlying affliction, leaving its own mark on the body over time; and the object of careful reflection itself, referring to the sixth consciousness's own threefold way of engaging its object, whether guided by proper reasoning, by improper reasoning, or by neither, proper reasoning neither exaggerating the body as permanent, blissful, a genuine self, and pure, nor denying its own conditioned reality outright, and thereby genuinely benefiting it, while improper reasoning instead indulges precisely these same distorted views and thereby gives rise to the very afflictions that harm it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the third cause, afflictive emotion actually leaving its mark on specific bodily organs, as a striking point of convergence between this treatise's own Abhidharma psychology and the correlations long taught in traditional Chinese medicine, joy harming the heart, anger the liver, worry the spleen, grief the lungs, and fear the kidneys, suggesting that different medical traditions arrived independently at strikingly similar observations about the mind's own power over the body. He treats the fourth cause, the sixth consciousness's own proper or improper reasoning about the body, as this passage's own reminder that even purely mental reflection, entirely apart from any external contact or internal imbalance, is itself already enough to genuinely help or harm the very body being reflected upon.
