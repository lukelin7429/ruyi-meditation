Fascicle 73 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十三 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

The Five Things Turned Over Again

This is the second part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, continuing the Chapter on the Real that fascicle 72 opened. A second summary verse takes up the five things of the Great Vehicle — characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct knowledge — from nine further angles: how they are sorted against the four noble truths, how they include one another, the difference between their inexpressible nature and their spoken nature, grasper and grasped, their relation to the view of self and to worldly existence, the four kinds of reality, the four searches and the four knowledges of things as they are, the hidden intent behind what the Buddha said, and the order in which the five are set out.

1. Section: A Second Verse for the Five Things, and How They Are Sorted Against the Four Truths

This is the second part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. It continues the determination of the Chapter on the Real begun in the previous fascicle, a chapter broad enough that it will run on through roughly the first third of the fascicle after this one. Two sets of summary verses organize what follows: two verses determining the five things, of which the first was given in the previous fascicle and the second is given here, and three verses determining the three natures, of which the first is given here and the remaining two later. The verse says: investigation, own nature, grasping; the view of self, existence, the world; reality, search, and the knowledge of things as they are; hidden intent, and order. Investigation means investigating how the four truths and the five things include one another, and then how the five things include one another, set out in four alternatives. Own nature means determining the difference between the inexpressible nature of the five things and the nature they have in speech. Grasping means determining, among the five things, the difference between what grasps, what is grasped, and what grasping ranges over. The view of self, existence, and the world mean determining how the five things stand to the view of a real self, to the three kinds of existence, and to the world — this being the gate of continued wandering. Reality means determining how the four kinds of reality stand to the five things — this being the gate of return. Search and the knowledge of things as they are mean determining how the four searches and the four knowledges of things as they are stand to the five things, these being the means of entering the real. Hidden intent means determining, by way of the five things, what the Buddha meant beneath what he said. And order means determining why the five things are set out in the order they are. Of these five things, how many are included in the truths? Characteristic is included in all four truths as conventionally set out — conventional setting out being the marking off of one thing from another by words and terms, so that the four truths so marked off are the four conventionally set out truths. Within characteristic, the characteristic of characteristic and the characteristic of name belong to the truth of suffering; the characteristic of discrimination belongs to suffering, to its origin, and to the truth of the path insofar as that path still has outflow; the characteristic of correct knowledge belongs to the uncontaminated truth of the path; and the characteristic of suchness belongs to the truth of cessation. A name is included in one truth, the truth of suffering, since it is by clinging to names that beings raise the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity, and so bring suffering upon themselves. Discrimination is included in three truths, cessation excepted: in suffering, in its origin, and in the path with outflow — the path here meaning the discrimination belonging to the stages of accumulating the provisions for liberation and of penetrating decisively, not the uncontaminated path; and cessation is excepted because nirvāṇa has no discrimination in it. Suchness is included in the four truths as not conventionally set out, being the nature of things, apart from words and letters, without characteristics, known only by realizing it, and so not able to be set out as cause and effect. And correct knowledge is included in the truth of the path that takes as its object both the truths as conventionally set out and the truth not so set out, since fundamental knowledge takes the truth not conventionally set out, while the knowledge subsequently attained can take both, and either way both are the uncontaminated truth of the path.

Further, the summary verse says: investigation, own nature, grasping; the view of self, existence, the world; reality, search, and the knowledge of things as they are; hidden intent, and order. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi puts this verdict side by side with the one given in the previous fascicle, where three of the five were said to be included in the conventionally set out truths, characteristic both included and not included, and suchness included in the truth not so set out. The difference, he explains, lies in how widely characteristic is being read: taken narrowly, the characteristics of characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge fall under the four conventionally set out truths while the characteristic of suchness does not, which is why the earlier passage said characteristic is both included and not; taken as it is here, all four truths conventionally set out are said to include it. On the terse claim that a name belongs to the truth of suffering, he quotes the commentary's reason without softening it: it is from provisional names that the four inversions arise.

2. Section: How the Five Include One Another, and Whether Discrimination Must Always Join Name to Thing

Are all characteristics names? And are all names characteristics? All names are characteristics, but there are characteristics that are not names, namely the four characteristics other than the characteristic of name. This is answered in the manner that affirms the latter half of the question only: where the first term is wide and the second narrow, one grants that whatever falls under the narrow term falls under the wide, while denying the converse. Characteristic is wide and name narrow, and so all names are characteristics while not all characteristics are names. The rest should be understood in the same way, each according to its case: all discriminations are characteristics, with the four characteristics other than the characteristic of discrimination not being discrimination; suchness is not apart from characteristic, being the emptiness of characteristics, while the four characteristics other than the characteristic of suchness are not suchness; and correct knowledge is the characteristic that is without inversion, while the four characteristics other than the characteristic of correct knowledge are not correct knowledge. The same manner of answering holds when name is set against the remaining three, and discrimination against the last two, and suchness against correct knowledge — for suchness can always be what correct knowledge takes as object, while there is suchness that is not correct knowledge, since correct knowledge is an uncontaminated conditioned thing that does the knowing and suchness is unconditioned and is what is known. Again, are all characteristics the characteristic of characteristic, and is the characteristic of characteristic always a characteristic? Every characteristic of characteristic is a characteristic, but there are characteristics that are not the characteristic of characteristic, namely the four beginning with the characteristic of name — for the characteristics of name and of discrimination are only image-characteristics, suchness has no characteristic, and correct knowledge is apart from characteristics, so none of these can be called the characteristic of characteristic. Then a harder question. When one discriminates the characteristic of characteristic, must the characteristic of name in every case be joined with it and depended upon for the discrimination to arise? And when one discriminates the characteristic of name, must the characteristic of characteristic in every case be joined with it and depended upon? Four alternatives should be made. There is discriminating the characteristic of characteristic without the characteristic of name being joined and depended upon, which happens in two ways: when the discrimination does not know the name that belongs to the characteristic, as with the discrimination of cattle and sheep and of infants, who cannot yet handle words and so do not take up a characteristic by way of its name — what the Basic Section called discrimination without characteristics; and when the latent tendency of names has already been pulled out from characteristics, which is the domain of bodhisattvas from the first ground onward, in whom the seeds of clinging to phenomena perfumed by names have been severed, so that when their subsequently attained knowledge takes up characteristics it does not cling to them by way of names. There is discriminating the characteristic of name without the characteristic of characteristic being joined and depended upon, when the discrimination does not know the thing that the name refers to — knowing the word and not the reality, as a foreigner newly arrived who has learned a local term for something he has never seen and cannot picture. The alternative contrary to the above is the alternative in which both hold: discriminating by taking the characteristic through the name and the name through the characteristic, knowing both the word that expresses and the thing it rests on, which is the ordinary person's condition — seeing a teacup and taking up its name and its characteristic together, characteristic, name, and discrimination all three meeting in one act. And excluding all the characteristics just spoken of is the alternative in which neither holds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi records two readings of the fourth alternative, where neither name nor characteristic is joined. One commentary takes it for non-discriminating knowledge, in which name and characteristic are dismissed together, which requires the fundamental non-discriminating knowledge to have been realized. The other takes it for the pervasive imputation that runs between name and characteristic, in which one settles that a certain word must name a certain thing — the shared vocabulary of earth, water, fire, wind, sun, moon, mountains, and rivers — reasoning from names already understood to meanings not yet understood, or from a thing seen to the word for it, and imputing throughout that this word and this meaning have such a nature of their own.

3. Section: Four Alternatives on Contemplating Suchness and Beholding It

If one contemplates suchness, is one thereby beholding suchness? And if one beholds suchness, is one thereby contemplating it? Four alternatives should be made. Here contemplating has discriminating attention as its substance and belongs to the stage where characteristics are still present, while beholding has non-discriminating wisdom as its substance and belongs to the stage where they are not. There is contemplating suchness without beholding suchness: namely, contemplating suchness by an attention in accordance with reason that is still included under discrimination, whereby one sees only the characteristic of suchness and does not see the real suchness — this holding from the first arising of the aspiration all the way up to the highest worldly state, before one has reached the stage of true penetration, and holding again after penetration whenever one turns attention to the suchness of the truths as conventionally set out. In both cases suchness is approached carrying a characteristic, and so there is contemplation but no beholding. There is beholding suchness without contemplating suchness: namely, at the moment of penetrating suchness, when by the ultimate one contemplates its characteristic. Here the noble one's non-discriminating wisdom takes hold of the substance of suchness directly, unlike the preparatory stage which contemplates suchness by seizing a characteristic, and unlike the subsequently attained knowledge which contemplates the conventionally set out suchness by way of words; and the word contemplate is used here, the commentators explain, only as another name for that which does the knowing — as the defiled faculty of mind, once uncontaminated, may still be called mind although it no longer deliberates in the defiled way. There is contemplating suchness that is also beholding suchness: namely, after penetration, continuing to contemplate the suchness that is not conventionally set out. This is the subsequently attained knowledge that arises in the wake of the first direct realization, doubling back upon what was beheld there; it is called contemplating because it still bears an image-characteristic raised by searching, and it is called beholding because, being led forth by non-discriminating knowledge, its object closely resembles the real suchness. And here lies its difference from the first alternative, whose object, the conventionally set out suchness, does not resemble the real suchness and so earns the name contemplating alone. And there is neither contemplating suchness nor beholding it: namely, contemplating characteristics by an attention other than the one that reason draws forth — the ordinary mind, or a subsequently attained knowledge turned elsewhere, attending to the characteristics of things other than suchness altogether.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi gathers several commentators on this set, which they call the four alternatives with suchness for their theme, to be followed shortly by a matching set with characteristic for its theme. The cleanest formulation he passes on divides the four by faculty: the first belongs to the preparatory knowledge before the grounds and to the subsequently attained knowledge when it takes the conventionally set out as object; the second to fundamental knowledge itself; the third to the subsequently attained knowledge when it takes the suchness not conventionally set out; and the fourth to every other attention. To the natural question of why the suchness taken in the first is called conventionally set out and the suchness taken in the third is not, the answer given is that the third arises immediately out of fundamental knowledge and so keeps its resemblance to the original, whereas the first does not resemble the original and is named accordingly.

4. Section: The Same Four Alternatives Read from the Side of Characteristic, and Why No Thing's Own Nature Can Be Spoken

If one contemplates a characteristic, is one thereby beholding that characteristic? And if one beholds a characteristic, is one thereby contemplating it? Four alternatives should be made, and each is simply one of the four just given, read now from the side of characteristic rather than of suchness. There is contemplating a characteristic without beholding it, which is the second alternative above — for when the practitioner takes hold of the substance of suchness directly by fundamental knowledge and contemplates its ultimate aspect free of any characteristic, that is what contemplating a characteristic means here, while his not taking up suchness by seizing a characteristic is what not beholding it means. There is beholding a characteristic without contemplating it, which is the first alternative above — for the ordinary person's preparatory knowledge attending to the characteristic of suchness in accordance with reason, and the noble one's subsequently attained knowledge attending to the conventionally set out suchness through words, both approach suchness carrying a characteristic, which is what beholding a characteristic means, while neither contemplates the ultimate free of characteristics. There is contemplating a characteristic that is also beholding it, which is the fourth alternative above — for one who does not contemplate suchness contemplates the characteristics of other things, and one who does not behold suchness beholds the characteristics of other things; and the word characteristic in these last two alternatives means not the characteristic of suchness but the whole field of objects bearing characteristics outside it. And there is neither contemplating a characteristic nor beholding it, which is the third alternative above — for the subsequently attained knowledge that after penetration goes on contemplating the suchness not conventionally set out is neither contemplating nor beholding the characteristics of anything else. Then a new topic opens, the own nature of the five things. Do these five things include all phenomena, or not? They do. They include everything conditioned and unconditioned: characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge are conditioned and belong to the nature that arises in dependence on other things, and suchness is unconditioned and belongs to the perfectly accomplished nature — while everything set up by speech belongs to the nature that is pervasively imputed, has neither substance nor characteristic, is like the horns of a hare, and is therefore not included among the five at all. And with what should it be said that all those phenomena have as their own nature? The own nature of things cannot be spoken. Every conditioned and unconditioned thing has an own nature apart from names and words. What the ordinary person recognizes through speech and concept is the product of pervasive imputation; speech and concept are not the same as the dependently arisen characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge that conditions have produced, nor the same as unconditioned suchness. They are one's own consciousness and discrimination and nothing more, and in substance and characteristic they simply do not exist — a phantom appearance and an empty word, like taking a rope in a dark room for a snake. So the spoken nature of the five things is the pervasively imputed nature and has neither substance nor characteristic, which is why it is said that the own nature of things cannot be spoken; but their inexpressible nature is not nothing, the dependently arisen being illusory but present and the perfectly accomplished being an emptiness that is not a void, and one who knows the first to be non-existent and these two to be not non-existent has entered the middle way of the three natures.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi passes on a commentator's patient argument for why no thing's own nature can be spoken: speech can only ever say what is shared. Say "blue," and though it rules out yellow and red and white, the one word covers every blue bamboo, root, stalk, branch, and leaf there is. Narrow it to "blue leaf," and though it rules out root and stalk and branch, the one word still covers leaves that are each unlike every other, as faces are unlike faces, so that no shared name can pick out the particular leaf before you. Go further and the leaf is a mass of subtle particles, none of which is a blue leaf, so the word does not fit them either; and those particles are each unlike the others, so that even a single one has an own nature that cannot be spoken. As it is with form, so with mind, with mental factors, with what is neither, and with the unconditioned. What speech offers is a provisional shared aspect; take that for something real and you are in the pervasively imputed.

5. Section: How to Look at Things That Cannot Be Spoken — the Conjurer's Elephants

If the own nature of things cannot be spoken, how then should one look at the characteristics of those things? Like the appearance of a conjured display, which is not wholly non-existent. Just as a conjured display has the nature of a conjured display while having no nature of elephant, horse, chariot, or infantry, of jewel, pearl, gold, or silver, so it is here. The treatise's fuller telling of this simile runs thus: a skilled conjurer or his apprentice stands at a crossroads, heaps up tiles and pebbles, grass and leaves and sticks, and produces from them all manner of conjured works — the body of an elephant, of a horse, of a chariot, of infantry, jewels, pearls, beryl, conch, jade, coral, and every kind of wealth and grain and storehouse. Beings who are dull and know nothing, seeing and hearing this, think: what I see here is a real elephant, a real horse, real jewels and storehouses; and they cling firmly to it as they saw and heard it, and say, only this is true and everything else is foolishness — and they will have to look again later. But beings who are not dull, who know something, seeing and hearing the same conjured works upon those tiles and pebbles and grass and leaves, think: what I see here is no real elephant, no real horse, no real jewels or storehouses; there is only a conjured appearance deceiving the eye, upon which the notion of an elephant's body arises, or notions of its various kinds, and so on for the notions of every sort of wealth and grain and storehouse. They do not cling firmly to it as they saw and heard, saying only this is true and all else foolishness; and when they do speak of it, they speak in order to convey the sense of the matter — and they will not have to look again later. In just this way, the substance of these things offers nothing to be found except name and characteristic; there is nothing to be found of an own nature or of differences, set up and made to appear. A characteristic, by way of the illusory characteristic and by way of the name for the characteristic, seems to be there, but the own nature of characteristic is really not to be found. As with characteristic, so should it be understood of name and the own nature of name, of discrimination and the own nature of discrimination, and of suchness and the own nature of suchness — for even suchness, which is what stands revealed when the absence of own nature in characteristic and in arising is penetrated, seems to be there by way of the word suchness, while the spoken nature of suchness is really not to be found. And correct knowledge, by way of the word correct knowledge, seems to be there, while the own nature of correct knowledge is really not to be found. Why so? Because in every case a nature that follows upon words is not established. All things conditioned and unconditioned have no nature such as words assign them; the real substance of everything is apart from words, and the spoken nature is in every case unreal. The ordinary person, recognizing things by his own subjective construction, cannot help imputing pervasively, producing concepts and names, and then clinging to them as really existing — a real self and real phenomena — while what he recognizes is not the state of the thing at all but only the phantom and the empty word his own naming produced, the world of his own mental activity and nothing more.

Master Cháng Bǎi asks why correct knowledge is given a line of its own when the treatise could have swept the last four together after characteristic, and offers a reading beyond the translator's habit of style. That hinge deserved its own sentence. 6. Section: Two Positions Refuted — Names Fitted to Things, and Things Produced by Names

Suppose someone holds that the own natures of characteristics are established, and that the provisional names are assigned so as to match their measure exactly, this provisional naming being founded upon the characteristic. Then before a name has been provisionally assigned to a characteristic, there ought to be the corresponding awareness of it, just as there is once the name has been assigned. If a name were truly fitted to a thing, then on first seeing some new product one ought to be able to say what it is called — and plainly one cannot, before it has been named. Further, since the names assigned to a single characteristic are many, and since a single name carries various senses, there ought to be many different substances. One woman may be a housewife at home, a wife to her husband, a mother to her children, a teacher at her school, and the chairwoman of a charity — five names; and each name in turn carries several senses. If names fitted substances, five different sets of aggregates ought to be standing there, and a different one again for each sense a name bears, whereas in fact however many names she has and however many senses those names carry, they all refer to one person. Therefore that names are founded upon characteristics does not stand to reason. Suppose instead someone holds that characteristics are established after names, the own nature of a characteristic arising by the force of the name. Then before the name has been provisionally assigned to that characteristic, it ought to have no own nature; and since it does not exist, the provisional name ought not to exist either — so that both are ruined together. Say "ox," and if the word is what makes the ox's substance arise, then before the word was assigned there was no ox; and with no ox there, there was nothing upon which to assign the word ox. Further, since provisional names are many and carry various senses, there ought to be many different substances — many substances present at once wherever one characteristic bears several names, and different substances jumbled together within a single name that bears several senses, which contradicts what anyone can see. Further, there is the fault of dependence on another, since those characteristics would be provisionally established merely in dependence on something else. Therefore that everything provisionally named is as its own nature is named does not stand to reason. It is like the various conjured kinds a conjurer raises: just as a conjurer produces all sorts of conjured persons and creatures — men, women, elephants, horses, bears, brown bears, and the rest — and those kinds do not have real substance answering to the appearance they wear.

7. Section: Two More Positions Refuted — a Nature Born from the Meeting of Name and Thing, and a Name That Lights Things Up

In just this way, these characteristics do not have real substance answering to their names; it should be understood as the simile has it. Suppose someone holds that apart from that characteristic and that name, an own nature is born from the meeting of the two. Then that own nature ought to appear and be found either in the characteristics, or in the name, or somewhere between the two — and it is not found. This position is raised in order to escape the two faults just exposed, that names are founded on characteristics and that characteristics are established after names; but the escape fails, because whatever is born of the meeting of two must be findable in one or the other or in between, and since characteristic and name are each already unfindable, their meeting is unfindable too. Therefore this reckoning does not stand to reason. And for this reason, a nature that follows upon words has, in every case, no existence whatever — which is to say that everything words point to is finally empty. Suppose instead someone holds that names are able to light up own natures. This too does not stand to reason. For whether a characteristic is taken up before the provisional name is assigned or not taken up, either way there is a fault. If the characteristic is taken up first and the name assigned afterward, then nothing is lit up, since the own nature was already there before the naming. And if the name is provisionally assigned without the characteristic having been taken up, a name with no thing behind it does not stand to reason. Further, as was said before, since the names assigned are many and carry various senses, there would then have to be many different substances, which is a grave fault. And further, the simile of illumination is not a parallel case, so the position does not stand to reason. What makes it not parallel is this: the causal condition that illuminates makes no distinction among the things it lights, and the same holds for the many sorts of illumination — a lamp in a room lights the vase and lights the table and everything else in the room without discriminating, and so it is with sun, moon, jewels, and stars. But the causal condition that takes something up is not like that. A name used to indicate mind cannot at the same time indicate form; a name used to indicate form takes up the characteristic of form and no other. A name, then, is not able to make manifest the own natures of all things whatever, and so it is not the condition by which all things whatever are taken up. On that reasoning the simile does not resemble the position it is offered to support.

8. Section: Objections Answered — Saying the Unsayable, and Whether the Conjuring Simile Really Fits

An objection: within what cannot be spoken, the very words "cannot be spoken" are right here to be had; therefore your saying that the nature of things cannot be spoken does not stand to reason. And a second: as for the various conjured shapes a conjurer produces, though those shapes are not as their nature appears, still there are the various acts of conjuring that produce them, and these are as their own nature is — the conjurer's cloth is real — and therefore the simile is not a parallel case either. The answer: at the very moment the thesis was set up, the words "cannot be spoken" were themselves already ruled out. For the thesis was that in every case a nature that follows upon words is not established, and that plainly covers this phrase along with all the rest; within what cannot be spoken, even the words "cannot be spoken" are not established either. So one may not point to the phrase as though it stood outside the claim it makes. As for the simile: it is in order to make this sense known that similes and the like are provisionally set up, and so it is not a case of non-resemblance. Though what is provisionally named is not as that name has it, the meaning that cannot be spoken is not therefore non-existent. Conventional things have no real substance of their own, and though names are provisionally assigned to them, they are not as those names have them, exactly as a conjured display has no real substance; while the inexpressible sense of the real, the principle of suchness, is not unreal at all. So the simile carries both halves of the thesis at once — that a nature following upon words does not exist, and that the nature apart from words is not nothing. A further objection: if those characteristics and things come to be when a provisional name is assigned and do not come to be when none is assigned, then the simile would indeed be parallel and your reckoning of the unsayable would stand to reason; but if that is not how it is, your reckoning of the unsayable is thrown away for nothing. For a conjured hare exists when the conjurer produces it and not otherwise, whereas conventional things are there whether one speaks of them or not; and if they are always there, they can always be spoken of. The answer: in this way, because of the eight discriminations previously raised, three kinds of thing arise in the present world, as the Basic Section has already described their characteristics — the discriminations of own nature, of difference, and of comprehensive grasping producing the six faculties and six objects that elaboration rests upon; the discriminations of self and of what belongs to self producing the view of self and conceit; and the discriminations of the pleasing, the displeasing, and what runs contrary to both producing greed, hatred, and delusion. And because of these three kinds of thing so produced, discrimination arises again. By this principle the defiled phenomena continue one after another without a break, and it is by this that the simile does resemble. And when what discrimination has provisionally set up is cut off, the defiled phenomena can all cease along with it, and the wisdom of the noble ones is realized. Since this is directly known, the reckoning of the unsayable is not thrown away for nothing.

9. Section: If One Person Wakes, Does the World Vanish?

An objection: if at that moment everything provisionally set up by discrimination is entirely cut off, then at that same moment the characteristics and things are swept away with it. If so, then as soon as any single person attains the wisdom of a noble one, all the sentient and insentient beings, all the inner and outer things included under characteristic, name, and discrimination ought to perish forever — as when a conjurer stops conjuring and the conjured show is gone. The answer: characteristics and the rest of these things arise either with uncommon discrimination as their cause or with common discrimination as their cause. One's own resultant body is produced by the perfuming of one's own discrimination and not by any perfuming shared with others; the receptacle world is produced by the shared discrimination of beings dwelling together. If a thing has arisen from uncommon discrimination, then for the one who is without discrimination, it too ceases along with him: his own faculties, objects, and consciousness, and whatever of the surrounding world is not shared with others, no longer appear. But if a thing has arisen from common discrimination, then although his discrimination is gone, it does not perish forever, being sustained by the discrimination of others. Were it not so, the discrimination of others would have no fruit at all. Mountains and rivers and the great earth stand on, held up by those who have not yet awakened and who still cling firmly to the world their naming presents as real. And though they do not perish, one who has attained purity has, with regard to those very things, a right view that is pure: he knows as it is that what the shared discrimination presents cannot be found as it is spoken, and that a receptacle world arisen from conditions is like an illusion, like a conjured show. It is as when many practitioners of contemplation, upon one and the same thing, arrive at various resolutions and different ways of seeing through the knowledge of a concentrated mind; so it is here as well. For a bodhisattva who has gained mastery of mind, and a śrāvaka, a solitary realizer, or even an ordinary person who has attained absorption, can by the force of resolve make an object appear as they wish, turning the great earth into water or into fire; one who has attained a superior power of concentration finds that the moment he directs attention, the meanings of the teaching stand before him, impermanence and suffering and emptiness and non-self appearing distinctly as the mind turns to them; and one who has attained non-discriminating knowledge finds that while that knowledge is present, no meanings appear at all and no objective characteristic shows itself. Seeing this range in what a concentrated mind presents, one who has attained purity knows as it is that outside the mind there is no real referent, and that the world of beings and the receptacle world alike are what one's own uncommon or common discrimination presents, exactly like a conjured display.

10. Section: What Grasps and What Is Grasped, and Three Fields That Grasping Ranges Over

Of these five things, how many are what is grasped and how many are what grasps? Three are what is grasped — characteristic, name, and suchness, which are only ever the objects that mind and mental factors take up. Discrimination and correct knowledge are both what grasps and what is grasped: discrimination grasps characteristic and name and can itself be grasped by a discriminating mind, and correct knowledge grasps suchness while the characteristic of correct knowledge can itself become the object of a discriminating mind. This is why the previous fascicle could say that discrimination is what emptiness and wishlessness range over and that correct knowledge is what emptiness ranges over: though both are on the side of grasping, at the causal stage they can also be taken up by a contemplating mind. Of these five things, how many fields should be known that grasping ranges over? In brief there are three: the field with words and with characteristics that grasping ranges over; the field without words but with characteristics that grasping ranges over; and the field with neither words nor characteristics that grasping ranges over. Of these, the first is the field that grasping ranges over for one in whom speech is presently awake — one at home in language, who takes up a characteristic by way of its name, as hearing "mountain" he takes up what a mountain is. The second is the field that grasping ranges over for one in whom speech is only latent — an infant, or an ox or sheep, who does not command language, and yet by the force of the seeds of speech takes up characteristics all the same, as an infant sees a person without being able to say the word. And the third is the field that grasping ranges over for one who has separated from the latency of speech — the noble one, in whom the seeds of the afflictions of speech have been severed, at the moment fundamental knowledge realizes suchness, apart from words and apart from characteristics. Read another way: the first two are grasping at the level of conventional truth, both being still within language, whether spoken or merely latent, and both still discriminating and clinging. The last is grasping at the level of ultimate truth. And further, there is the grasping subsequently attained by one who has separated from the latency of speech, which grasps across everything included under both truths as the field it ranges over. That is to say, since the knowledge that is at once worldly and transcendent ranges over the truths as conventionally set out, that knowledge is established as using both truths for the field it ranges over. And these two graspings should be known to be established as worldly and transcendent by two causes: by what has been attained before and what has not been attained before, and by depending on speech and not depending on speech. The subsequently attained knowledge takes speech for its object and reviews the four conventionally set out truths that worldly knowledge had examined before, and in that respect it is what was attained before, depends on speech, has words and characteristics, and is called worldly; yet when it arises no seeds of affliction accompany it, and it is not produced by the elaborations of naming and does not cling to speech, and in that respect it is what was not attained before, does not depend on speech, and is called transcendent.

11. Section: Grasping Without Characteristics — What Causes It, What It Is, and How Anyone Knows It Has Happened

An objection: grasping with characteristics is what the world holds in common, while grasping without characteristics is not held in common. What cause and what condition make it grasping without characteristics? To have no cause and no condition does not stand to reason. The answer: the fruit of grasping that the perfuming of conventional naming produces is grasping with characteristics; it is held in common by the world and it makes for defilement. The fruit of grasping that the perfuming of ultimate knowledge and vision produces is grasping without characteristics; it is not held in common and it makes for purity. Therefore both of these have a cause and a condition. Conventional naming perfumes the storehouse consciousness with seeds of naming, and when conditions meet, those seeds bring out objects and the mind takes them for real, which is the whole of the world's condition and sinks it into the defilements of affliction, action, and rebirth. The perfuming of ultimate knowledge and vision is the seed laid down by long hearing of the teaching, which counters the storehouse consciousness; and by contemplating the sixteen aspects of the four truths and then the four searches and four knowledges of things as they are, by cultivating and repeatedly cultivating the knowledge whose object is suchness, one passes in a single uninterrupted moment from the highest worldly state into direct realization, and there is grasping without characteristics. It is like this: if the eye has the fault of a cataract or the like, then the film-marks of hairs and wheels appear before it and can be found; and if it has no such fault, they cannot be found, and there is only the taking of the thing's own nature without inversion. Empty space holds no hairs or wheels, and yet the diseased eye sees them there, or sees two moons where there is one — which is exactly the ordinary person's condition, every characteristic he opens his eyes upon being taken as real. Cure the disease and the phantom marks are simply not there, and space is seen to be what it always was. A further question: if within the realm without characteristics one grasps a characteristic, that is not grasping without characteristics; and if one grasps nothing at all, that too fails to be grasping without characteristics. In that case, why is it called grasping without characteristics? The answer: because the latency of speech has already been left behind, this grasping, though it does take the realm without characteristics, does not take a characteristic, and so becomes grasping without characteristics. And if there is no acquiring, how does it come to be grasping at all? Though it does not acquire the various differences among characteristics, adding nothing to them, it does take what is without characteristics, and so it comes to be grasping. And if there is no acquiring and nothing added, how can the shape of this grasping be known? Because it takes the ultimate and takes what is without characteristics, the shape it has is that the characteristics of the five kinds of thing do not appear at all — mind, mental factors, form, the formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned, the five sorts of object that in concentration are made to appear as images and are here swept away. Then why not simply allow that grasping has ceased and become nothing? Because in what has ceased into nothing there is no sense of practice to be done, and one who cultivates contemplation does not practice upon a mere nothing. And in that case, how is its shape realized and known? 12. Section: Why It Cannot Be Reported, How Something with Characteristics Causes Something Without, and Which Knowledge Actually Severs the Afflictions

In that case, why does one not report what one has realized just as one realized it? Because this inward realization is not a place where names can find their footing. What is realized when the real is realized is not the ground on which naming stands; it is without characteristics, the path of words cut off and the place of mental activity ended — like a mute person tasting something, who knows perfectly well and cannot say. A further objection: if there was previously no knowledge of what is without characteristics, then, there being none, there was also no repeated practice of such knowledge; and having never been practiced, the knowledge of what is without characteristics has no cause and ought not to arise at all. The answer: what has characteristics can also be a cause of what is without them, since it accords with it. As worldly knowledge serves as the condition from which transcendent knowledge arises, as knowledge with outflow serves as the condition from which uncontaminated knowledge arises, and as an absorption with mind serves as the condition from which the absorption without mind arises, so it is here as well. At the stages of preparatory effort the practitioner's knowledge is worldly throughout, right up to the highest worldly state, and from that single moment he enters non-discriminating knowledge without interval; one first attains an absorption with mind and only from there cultivates the absorption of cessation. The cause meant here is not a seed but a drawing-forth: one present activity accords with another and so draws it out. A further objection: the knowledges of suffering and the rest the Blessed One declared to be the cause of attaining purity. But if the knowledge of suffering and the rest discriminates suffering and the rest within the truths of suffering and the rest, it must have characteristics; and if it does not discriminate, then the knowledges of suffering and the rest do not exist at all — and if they do not exist, how can final purity be attained? The answer: it is by the enhancing force of the knowledge without characteristics that, with regard to the truths, a supremely purified discriminating knowledge, at once worldly and transcendent, arises; and that is what it means to say the afflictions to be abandoned have been abandoned. That knowledge without characteristics is the cause of the knowledges of suffering and the rest, and it is what actually severs the afflictions to be abandoned; the name of the fruit is provisionally assigned to this cause, and so it is provisionally called the knowledge of suffering and the rest. Therefore there is no fault. The sixteen aspects contemplated at the stage of hearing and reflecting are still a grasping with characteristics and do not by themselves make anyone pure; the afflictions are severed when the knowledge without characteristics is realized, and only afterward does the subsequently attained knowledge return to the four truths and their sixteen aspects, now finally pure because the severing already happened. And one more: it was said earlier that what is grasped is the fruit of what grasps; of what, then, should this grasping be called the fruit? These two are the fruit of one another in turn. The six faculties grasp and the six objects are grasped, so the objects are the fruit of the faculties; but an object also draws the faculty into taking it, and so the faculty is equally the fruit of the object. Grasper and grasped hold to each other, and neither stands without the other.

13. Section: Sweeping Away the Five Kinds of Object, and Why Nirvāṇa Is Not Mere Extinction

An objection: if the objects one knows are impermanent, accumulated, continuous, immeasurable, and for the most part not directly seen, how can one who cultivates contemplation take them as his object and bring about their ceasing? The answer: by the enhancing force of hearing and reflecting upon them, he attains concentration; and by that cause the images of the five kinds of object appear within the concentration — form, mind, mental factors, the formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned — and he takes just those things as his objective sphere, and by sweeping them away they come to cease. The order matters: the strength of hearing and reflecting brings concentration, concentration brings the object directly before him, and only then, contemplating the suchness of what stands before him, does he sweep it away; when he succeeds, those images no longer appear. And when the five kinds of knowable object have been swept away, what characteristic should this be said to have? It has the unsurpassed turning of the basis, unconditioned nirvāṇa, for its characteristic — unsurpassed because nothing goes beyond it, a turning of the basis because the whole body-and-world that consciousness had been presenting is turned into something uncontrived. What then is nirvāṇa? It is the purity of the sphere of phenomena, the sense in which the afflictions and the mass of suffering are stilled forever; it is not the sense of being extinguished into nothing. But if it is only the eternal stilling of the afflictions and the mass of suffering that is called nirvāṇa, on what grounds is it not the sense of extinction into nothing? Because it is as with the water element outside: only by leaving turbidity behind does it attain clarity, and it is not that when turbidity is left behind there is no clarity. And as with true gold: only by leaving hardness behind does it attain malleability, and it is not that when hardness is left behind there is no malleability. And as with empty space: only when the obscuration of clouds and mist is stilled does it attain purity, and it is not that when they are gone its purity is also nowhere to be found. The principle here should be known to be the same. What then is called the purity of the sphere of phenomena? It means that by cultivating correct knowledge one removes all characteristics forever and realizes suchness. It is like a man who, in the midst of a dream, sees his own body being swept away and drowned in a great torrent. Wishing to cross that torrent, he rouses great effort; and just because he roused great effort, he suddenly wakes; and having woken, he sees nothing whatever of that torrent. The principle by which characteristics are removed should be known to be the same. The rousing of effort is the cultivation of moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom; the waking is the moment after the highest worldly state when non-discriminating knowledge is realized; and what is then seen to have never been there is the whole of it — faculties, objects, consciousness, the receptacle world, grasper and grasped, and speech.

14. Section: Severing and Sweeping Away Happen at Once, and the Five Stages of the Turn to Characteristiclessness

A question: are the characteristics swept away at the very moment the latency of speech is being severed, or only after the severing is complete? The answer: the time of severing and the time of sweeping away are exactly equal, on the principle of the two ends of a balance, where one end goes down as the other goes up, and neither waits for the other. And it is as with a painted image, where the moment the pigments are ruined the figure vanishes with them. And as when the fault of a cataract or the like is healed, the marks of hairs and wheels are swept away with it, the time of healing and the time of sweeping away being exactly equal. The principle here should be known to be the same. Then: how does one who cultivates contemplation sweep away the characteristics of the object he takes? By a rightly concentrated mind he first examines carefully the images of the objects he knows; and afterward, by the force of attention directed to the ultimate, he turns away from what has characteristics and turns to what is without them. That is, established at least in the access concentration and ordinarily, for a bodhisattva, in the fourth absorption, he looks closely at the image standing before him; and then, attending to it as suchness, in a single moment he realizes the suchness that is the emptiness of person and of phenomena, and in that same moment lets go of the object's characteristic and arrives at the characteristicless. As the verse has it: when in the object nothing whatever is attained by knowledge, then one abides in consciousness only, the twin appearance of grasper and grasped being gone. And this turn to characteristiclessness has, further, five stages: the stage of a small portion, the stage of full pervasion, the stage that still wavers, the stage that still requires effort, and the stage of completion. In the first, the bodhisattva before the grounds, from the stage of forbearance onward, has not yet realized characteristicless suchness but has gained a superior conviction and accords with a portion of its sense. In the second, the bodhisattva of the first ground realizes the real nature of things by non-discriminating knowledge and can contemplate suchness as of one taste in all phenomena. In the third, the noble bodhisattva through the first six grounds does directly realize suchness, but cultivates it with intervals, still interrupted by what has characteristics and still moved by effort. In the fourth, the bodhisattva of the seventh ground realizes suchness without characteristics but still with applied effort. And in the fifth, from the eighth ground onward, where there is no more applied effort and no more exertion, realization proceeds spontaneously, on to the completion of the third incalculable eon and buddhahood itself.
