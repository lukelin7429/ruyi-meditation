Fascicle 73 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十三 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

The Five Things Turned Over Again

This is the second part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, continuing the Chapter on the Real that fascicle 72 opened. A second summary verse takes up the five things of the Great Vehicle — characteristic, name, discrimination, suchness, and correct knowledge — from nine further angles: how they are sorted against the four noble truths, how they include one another, the difference between their inexpressible nature and their spoken nature, grasper and grasped, their relation to the view of self and to worldly existence, the four kinds of reality, the four searches and the four knowledges of things as they are, the hidden intent behind what the Buddha said, and the order in which the five are set out. Along the way come the conjurer's elephants at the crossroads, the four positions on how names and things are related and why each fails, the question of whether one person's awakening would end the world, grasping without characteristics and how anyone knows it has happened, and why nirvāṇa is not extinction. The fascicle then opens the three natures, sorting ten kinds of imputation, the five subtle clingings, and the five reasons a fool takes what a word points to for something real — coming to rest exactly where the source lecture-transcript ends.

1. Section: A Second Verse for the Five Things, and How They Are Sorted Against the Four Truths

This is the second part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. It continues the determination of the Chapter on the Real begun in the previous fascicle, a chapter broad enough that it will run on through roughly the first third of the fascicle after this one. Two sets of summary verses organize what follows: two verses determining the five things, of which the first was given in the previous fascicle and the second is given here, and three verses determining the three natures, of which the first is given here and the remaining two later. The verse says: investigation, own nature, grasping; the view of self, existence, the world; reality, search, and the knowledge of things as they are; hidden intent, and order. Investigation means investigating how the four truths and the five things include one another, and then how the five things include one another, set out in four alternatives. Own nature means determining the difference between the inexpressible nature of the five things and the nature they have in speech. Grasping means determining, among the five things, the difference between what grasps, what is grasped, and what grasping ranges over. The view of self, existence, and the world mean determining how the five things stand to the view of a real self, to the three kinds of existence, and to the world — this being the gate of continued wandering. Reality means determining how the four kinds of reality stand to the five things — this being the gate of return. Search and the knowledge of things as they are mean determining how the four searches and the four knowledges of things as they are stand to the five things, these being the means of entering the real. Hidden intent means determining, by way of the five things, what the Buddha meant beneath what he said. And order means determining why the five things are set out in the order they are. Of these five things, how many are included in the truths? Characteristic is included in all four truths as conventionally set out — conventional setting out being the marking off of one thing from another by words and terms, so that the four truths so marked off are the four conventionally set out truths. Within characteristic, the characteristic of characteristic and the characteristic of name belong to the truth of suffering; the characteristic of discrimination belongs to suffering, to its origin, and to the truth of the path insofar as that path still has outflow; the characteristic of correct knowledge belongs to the uncontaminated truth of the path; and the characteristic of suchness belongs to the truth of cessation. A name is included in one truth, the truth of suffering, since it is by clinging to names that beings raise the four inversions of permanence, pleasure, self, and purity, and so bring suffering upon themselves. Discrimination is included in three truths, cessation excepted: in suffering, in its origin, and in the path with outflow — the path here meaning the discrimination belonging to the stages of accumulating the provisions for liberation and of penetrating decisively, not the uncontaminated path; and cessation is excepted because nirvāṇa has no discrimination in it. Suchness is included in the four truths as not conventionally set out, being the nature of things, apart from words and letters, without characteristics, known only by realizing it, and so not able to be set out as cause and effect. And correct knowledge is included in the truth of the path that takes as its object both the truths as conventionally set out and the truth not so set out, since fundamental knowledge takes the truth not conventionally set out, while the knowledge subsequently attained can take both, and either way both are the uncontaminated truth of the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi puts this verdict side by side with the one given in the previous fascicle, where three of the five were said to be included in the conventionally set out truths, characteristic both included and not included, and suchness included in the truth not so set out. The difference, he explains, lies in how widely characteristic is being read: taken narrowly, the characteristics of characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge fall under the four conventionally set out truths while the characteristic of suchness does not, which is why the earlier passage said characteristic is both included and not; taken as it is here, all four truths conventionally set out are said to include it. On the terse claim that a name belongs to the truth of suffering, he quotes the commentary's reason without softening it: it is from provisional names that the four inversions arise.

2. Section: How the Five Include One Another, and Whether Discrimination Must Always Join Name to Thing

Are all characteristics names? And are all names characteristics? All names are characteristics, but there are characteristics that are not names, namely the four characteristics other than the characteristic of name. This is answered in the manner that affirms the latter half of the question only: where the first term is wide and the second narrow, one grants that whatever falls under the narrow term falls under the wide, while denying the converse. Characteristic is wide and name narrow, and so all names are characteristics while not all characteristics are names. The rest should be understood in the same way, each according to its case: all discriminations are characteristics, with the four characteristics other than the characteristic of discrimination not being discrimination; suchness is not apart from characteristic, being the emptiness of characteristics, while the four characteristics other than the characteristic of suchness are not suchness; and correct knowledge is the characteristic that is without inversion, while the four characteristics other than the characteristic of correct knowledge are not correct knowledge. The same manner of answering holds when name is set against the remaining three, and discrimination against the last two, and suchness against correct knowledge — for suchness can always be what correct knowledge takes as object, while there is suchness that is not correct knowledge, since correct knowledge is an uncontaminated conditioned thing that does the knowing and suchness is unconditioned and is what is known. Again, are all characteristics the characteristic of characteristic, and is the characteristic of characteristic always a characteristic? Every characteristic of characteristic is a characteristic, but there are characteristics that are not the characteristic of characteristic, namely the four beginning with the characteristic of name — for the characteristics of name and of discrimination are only image-characteristics, suchness has no characteristic, and correct knowledge is apart from characteristics, so none of these can be called the characteristic of characteristic. Then a harder question. When one discriminates the characteristic of characteristic, must the characteristic of name in every case be joined with it and depended upon for the discrimination to arise? And when one discriminates the characteristic of name, must the characteristic of characteristic in every case be joined with it and depended upon? Four alternatives should be made. There is discriminating the characteristic of characteristic without the characteristic of name being joined and depended upon, which happens in two ways: when the discrimination does not know the name that belongs to the characteristic, as with the discrimination of cattle and sheep and of infants, who cannot yet handle words and so do not take up a characteristic by way of its name — what the Basic Section called discrimination without characteristics; and when the latent tendency of names has already been pulled out from characteristics, which is the domain of bodhisattvas from the first ground onward, in whom the seeds of clinging to phenomena perfumed by names have been severed, so that when their subsequently attained knowledge takes up characteristics it does not cling to them by way of names. There is discriminating the characteristic of name without the characteristic of characteristic being joined and depended upon, when the discrimination does not know the thing that the name refers to — knowing the word and not the reality, as a foreigner newly arrived who has learned a local term for something he has never seen and cannot picture. The alternative contrary to the above is the alternative in which both hold: discriminating by taking the characteristic through the name and the name through the characteristic, knowing both the word that expresses and the thing it rests on, which is the ordinary person's condition — seeing a teacup and taking up its name and its characteristic together, characteristic, name, and discrimination all three meeting in one act. And excluding all the characteristics just spoken of is the alternative in which neither holds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi records two readings of the fourth alternative, where neither name nor characteristic is joined. One commentary takes it for non-discriminating knowledge, in which name and characteristic are dismissed together, which requires the fundamental non-discriminating knowledge to have been realized. The other takes it for the pervasive imputation that runs between name and characteristic, in which one settles that a certain word must name a certain thing — the shared vocabulary of earth, water, fire, wind, sun, moon, mountains, and rivers — reasoning from names already understood to meanings not yet understood, or from a thing seen to the word for it, and imputing throughout that this word and this meaning have such a nature of their own.

3. Section: Four Alternatives on Contemplating Suchness and Beholding It

If one contemplates suchness, is one thereby beholding suchness? And if one beholds suchness, is one thereby contemplating it? Four alternatives should be made. Here contemplating has discriminating attention as its substance and belongs to the stage where characteristics are still present, while beholding has non-discriminating wisdom as its substance and belongs to the stage where they are not. There is contemplating suchness without beholding suchness: namely, contemplating suchness by an attention in accordance with reason that is still included under discrimination, whereby one sees only the characteristic of suchness and does not see the real suchness — this holding from the first arising of the aspiration all the way up to the highest worldly state, before one has reached the stage of true penetration, and holding again after penetration whenever one turns attention to the suchness of the truths as conventionally set out. In both cases suchness is approached carrying a characteristic, and so there is contemplation but no beholding. There is beholding suchness without contemplating suchness: namely, at the moment of penetrating suchness, when by the ultimate one contemplates its characteristic. Here the noble one's non-discriminating wisdom takes hold of the substance of suchness directly, unlike the preparatory stage which contemplates suchness by seizing a characteristic, and unlike the subsequently attained knowledge which contemplates the conventionally set out suchness by way of words; and the word contemplate is used here, the commentators explain, only as another name for that which does the knowing — as the defiled faculty of mind, once uncontaminated, may still be called mind although it no longer deliberates in the defiled way. There is contemplating suchness that is also beholding suchness: namely, after penetration, continuing to contemplate the suchness that is not conventionally set out. This is the subsequently attained knowledge that arises in the wake of the first direct realization, doubling back upon what was beheld there; it is called contemplating because it still bears an image-characteristic raised by searching, and it is called beholding because, being led forth by non-discriminating knowledge, its object closely resembles the real suchness. And here lies its difference from the first alternative, whose object, the conventionally set out suchness, does not resemble the real suchness and so earns the name contemplating alone. And there is neither contemplating suchness nor beholding it: namely, contemplating characteristics by an attention other than the one that reason draws forth — the ordinary mind, or a subsequently attained knowledge turned elsewhere, attending to the characteristics of things other than suchness altogether.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi gathers several commentators on this set, which they call the four alternatives with suchness for their theme, to be followed shortly by a matching set with characteristic for its theme. The cleanest formulation he passes on divides the four by faculty: the first belongs to the preparatory knowledge before the grounds and to the subsequently attained knowledge when it takes the conventionally set out as object; the second to fundamental knowledge itself; the third to the subsequently attained knowledge when it takes the suchness not conventionally set out; and the fourth to every other attention. To the natural question of why the suchness taken in the first is called conventionally set out and the suchness taken in the third is not, the answer given is that the third arises immediately out of fundamental knowledge and so keeps its resemblance to the original, whereas the first does not resemble the original and is named accordingly.

4. Section: The Same Four Alternatives Read from the Side of Characteristic, and Why No Thing's Own Nature Can Be Spoken

If one contemplates a characteristic, is one thereby beholding that characteristic? And if one beholds a characteristic, is one thereby contemplating it? Four alternatives should be made, and each is simply one of the four just given, read now from the side of characteristic rather than of suchness. There is contemplating a characteristic without beholding it, which is the second alternative above — for when the practitioner takes hold of the substance of suchness directly by fundamental knowledge and contemplates its ultimate aspect free of any characteristic, that is what contemplating a characteristic means here, while his not taking up suchness by seizing a characteristic is what not beholding it means. There is beholding a characteristic without contemplating it, which is the first alternative above — for the ordinary person's preparatory knowledge attending to the characteristic of suchness in accordance with reason, and the noble one's subsequently attained knowledge attending to the conventionally set out suchness through words, both approach suchness carrying a characteristic, which is what beholding a characteristic means, while neither contemplates the ultimate free of characteristics. There is contemplating a characteristic that is also beholding it, which is the fourth alternative above — for one who does not contemplate suchness contemplates the characteristics of other things, and one who does not behold suchness beholds the characteristics of other things; and the word characteristic in these last two alternatives means not the characteristic of suchness but the whole field of objects bearing characteristics outside it. And there is neither contemplating a characteristic nor beholding it, which is the third alternative above — for the subsequently attained knowledge that after penetration goes on contemplating the suchness not conventionally set out is neither contemplating nor beholding the characteristics of anything else. Then a new topic opens, the own nature of the five things. Do these five things include all phenomena, or not? They do. They include everything conditioned and unconditioned: characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge are conditioned and belong to the nature that arises in dependence on other things, and suchness is unconditioned and belongs to the perfectly accomplished nature — while everything set up by speech belongs to the nature that is pervasively imputed, has neither substance nor characteristic, is like the horns of a hare, and is therefore not included among the five at all. And with what should it be said that all those phenomena have as their own nature? The own nature of things cannot be spoken. Every conditioned and unconditioned thing has an own nature apart from names and words. What the ordinary person recognizes through speech and concept is the product of pervasive imputation; speech and concept are not the same as the dependently arisen characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge that conditions have produced, nor the same as unconditioned suchness. They are one's own consciousness and discrimination and nothing more, and in substance and characteristic they simply do not exist — a phantom appearance and an empty word, like taking a rope in a dark room for a snake. So the spoken nature of the five things is the pervasively imputed nature and has neither substance nor characteristic, which is why it is said that the own nature of things cannot be spoken; but their inexpressible nature is not nothing, the dependently arisen being illusory but present and the perfectly accomplished being an emptiness that is not a void, and one who knows the first to be non-existent and these two to be not non-existent has entered the middle way of the three natures.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi passes on a commentator's patient argument for why no thing's own nature can be spoken: speech can only ever say what is shared. Say "blue," and though it rules out yellow and red and white, the one word covers every blue bamboo, root, stalk, branch, and leaf there is. Narrow it to "blue leaf," and though it rules out root and stalk and branch, the one word still covers leaves that are each unlike every other, as faces are unlike faces, so that no shared name can pick out the particular leaf before you. Go further and the leaf is a mass of subtle particles, none of which is a blue leaf, so the word does not fit them either; and those particles are each unlike the others, so that even a single one has an own nature that cannot be spoken. As it is with form, so with mind, with mental factors, with what is neither, and with the unconditioned. What speech offers is a provisional shared aspect; take that for something real and you are in the pervasively imputed.

5. Section: How to Look at Things That Cannot Be Spoken — the Conjurer's Elephants

If the own nature of things cannot be spoken, how then should one look at the characteristics of those things? Like the appearance of a conjured display, which is not wholly non-existent. Just as a conjured display has the nature of a conjured display while having no nature of elephant, horse, chariot, or infantry, of jewel, pearl, gold, or silver, so it is here. The treatise's fuller telling of this simile runs thus: a skilled conjurer or his apprentice stands at a crossroads, heaps up tiles and pebbles, grass and leaves and sticks, and produces from them all manner of conjured works — the body of an elephant, of a horse, of a chariot, of infantry, jewels, pearls, beryl, conch, jade, coral, and every kind of wealth and grain and storehouse. Beings who are dull and know nothing, seeing and hearing this, think: what I see here is a real elephant, a real horse, real jewels and storehouses; and they cling firmly to it as they saw and heard it, and say, only this is true and everything else is foolishness — and they will have to look again later. But beings who are not dull, who know something, seeing and hearing the same conjured works upon those tiles and pebbles and grass and leaves, think: what I see here is no real elephant, no real horse, no real jewels or storehouses; there is only a conjured appearance deceiving the eye, upon which the notion of an elephant's body arises, or notions of its various kinds, and so on for the notions of every sort of wealth and grain and storehouse. They do not cling firmly to it as they saw and heard, saying only this is true and all else foolishness; and when they do speak of it, they speak in order to convey the sense of the matter — and they will not have to look again later. In just this way, the substance of these things offers nothing to be found except name and characteristic; there is nothing to be found of an own nature or of differences, set up and made to appear. A characteristic, by way of the illusory characteristic and by way of the name for the characteristic, seems to be there, but the own nature of characteristic is really not to be found. As with characteristic, so should it be understood of name and the own nature of name, of discrimination and the own nature of discrimination, and of suchness and the own nature of suchness — for even suchness, which is what stands revealed when the absence of own nature in characteristic and in arising is penetrated, seems to be there by way of the word suchness, while the spoken nature of suchness is really not to be found. And correct knowledge, by way of the word correct knowledge, seems to be there, while the own nature of correct knowledge is really not to be found. Why so? Because in every case a nature that follows upon words is not established. All things conditioned and unconditioned have no nature such as words assign them; the real substance of everything is apart from words, and the spoken nature is in every case unreal. The ordinary person, recognizing things by his own subjective construction, cannot help imputing pervasively, producing concepts and names, and then clinging to them as really existing — a real self and real phenomena — while what he recognizes is not the state of the thing at all but only the phantom and the empty word his own naming produced, the world of his own mental activity and nothing more.

Master Cháng Bǎi asks why correct knowledge is given a line of its own when the treatise could have swept the last four together after characteristic, and offers a reading beyond the translator's habit of style. That hinge deserved its own sentence. 6. Section: Two Positions Refuted — Names Fitted to Things, and Things Produced by Names

Suppose someone holds that the own natures of characteristics are established, and that the provisional names are assigned so as to match their measure exactly, this provisional naming being founded upon the characteristic. Then before a name has been provisionally assigned to a characteristic, there ought to be the corresponding awareness of it, just as there is once the name has been assigned. If a name were truly fitted to a thing, then on first seeing some new product one ought to be able to say what it is called — and plainly one cannot, before it has been named. Further, since the names assigned to a single characteristic are many, and since a single name carries various senses, there ought to be many different substances. One woman may be a housewife at home, a wife to her husband, a mother to her children, a teacher at her school, and the chairwoman of a charity — five names; and each name in turn carries several senses. If names fitted substances, five different sets of aggregates ought to be standing there, and a different one again for each sense a name bears, whereas in fact however many names she has and however many senses those names carry, they all refer to one person. Therefore that names are founded upon characteristics does not stand to reason. Suppose instead someone holds that characteristics are established after names, the own nature of a characteristic arising by the force of the name. Then before the name has been provisionally assigned to that characteristic, it ought to have no own nature; and since it does not exist, the provisional name ought not to exist either — so that both are ruined together. Say "ox," and if the word is what makes the ox's substance arise, then before the word was assigned there was no ox; and with no ox there, there was nothing upon which to assign the word ox. Further, since provisional names are many and carry various senses, there ought to be many different substances — many substances present at once wherever one characteristic bears several names, and different substances jumbled together within a single name that bears several senses, which contradicts what anyone can see. Further, there is the fault of dependence on another, since those characteristics would be provisionally established merely in dependence on something else. Therefore that everything provisionally named is as its own nature is named does not stand to reason. It is like the various conjured kinds a conjurer raises: just as a conjurer produces all sorts of conjured persons and creatures — men, women, elephants, horses, bears, brown bears, and the rest — and those kinds do not have real substance answering to the appearance they wear.

7. Section: Two More Positions Refuted — a Nature Born from the Meeting of Name and Thing, and a Name That Lights Things Up

In just this way, these characteristics do not have real substance answering to their names; it should be understood as the simile has it. Suppose someone holds that apart from that characteristic and that name, an own nature is born from the meeting of the two. Then that own nature ought to appear and be found either in the characteristics, or in the name, or somewhere between the two — and it is not found. This position is raised in order to escape the two faults just exposed, that names are founded on characteristics and that characteristics are established after names; but the escape fails, because whatever is born of the meeting of two must be findable in one or the other or in between, and since characteristic and name are each already unfindable, their meeting is unfindable too. Therefore this reckoning does not stand to reason. And for this reason, a nature that follows upon words has, in every case, no existence whatever — which is to say that everything words point to is finally empty. Suppose instead someone holds that names are able to light up own natures. This too does not stand to reason. For whether a characteristic is taken up before the provisional name is assigned or not taken up, either way there is a fault. If the characteristic is taken up first and the name assigned afterward, then nothing is lit up, since the own nature was already there before the naming. And if the name is provisionally assigned without the characteristic having been taken up, a name with no thing behind it does not stand to reason. Further, as was said before, since the names assigned are many and carry various senses, there would then have to be many different substances, which is a grave fault. And further, the simile of illumination is not a parallel case, so the position does not stand to reason. What makes it not parallel is this: the causal condition that illuminates makes no distinction among the things it lights, and the same holds for the many sorts of illumination — a lamp in a room lights the vase and lights the table and everything else in the room without discriminating, and so it is with sun, moon, jewels, and stars. But the causal condition that takes something up is not like that. A name used to indicate mind cannot at the same time indicate form; a name used to indicate form takes up the characteristic of form and no other. A name, then, is not able to make manifest the own natures of all things whatever, and so it is not the condition by which all things whatever are taken up. On that reasoning the simile does not resemble the position it is offered to support.

8. Section: Objections Answered — Saying the Unsayable, and Whether the Conjuring Simile Really Fits

An objection: within what cannot be spoken, the very words "cannot be spoken" are right here to be had; therefore your saying that the nature of things cannot be spoken does not stand to reason. And a second: as for the various conjured shapes a conjurer produces, though those shapes are not as their nature appears, still there are the various acts of conjuring that produce them, and these are as their own nature is — the conjurer's cloth is real — and therefore the simile is not a parallel case either. The answer: at the very moment the thesis was set up, the words "cannot be spoken" were themselves already ruled out. For the thesis was that in every case a nature that follows upon words is not established, and that plainly covers this phrase along with all the rest; within what cannot be spoken, even the words "cannot be spoken" are not established either. So one may not point to the phrase as though it stood outside the claim it makes. As for the simile: it is in order to make this sense known that similes and the like are provisionally set up, and so it is not a case of non-resemblance. Though what is provisionally named is not as that name has it, the meaning that cannot be spoken is not therefore non-existent. Conventional things have no real substance of their own, and though names are provisionally assigned to them, they are not as those names have them, exactly as a conjured display has no real substance; while the inexpressible sense of the real, the principle of suchness, is not unreal at all. So the simile carries both halves of the thesis at once — that a nature following upon words does not exist, and that the nature apart from words is not nothing. A further objection: if those characteristics and things come to be when a provisional name is assigned and do not come to be when none is assigned, then the simile would indeed be parallel and your reckoning of the unsayable would stand to reason; but if that is not how it is, your reckoning of the unsayable is thrown away for nothing. For a conjured hare exists when the conjurer produces it and not otherwise, whereas conventional things are there whether one speaks of them or not; and if they are always there, they can always be spoken of. The answer: in this way, because of the eight discriminations previously raised, three kinds of thing arise in the present world, as the Basic Section has already described their characteristics — the discriminations of own nature, of difference, and of comprehensive grasping producing the six faculties and six objects that elaboration rests upon; the discriminations of self and of what belongs to self producing the view of self and conceit; and the discriminations of the pleasing, the displeasing, and what runs contrary to both producing greed, hatred, and delusion. And because of these three kinds of thing so produced, discrimination arises again. By this principle the defiled phenomena continue one after another without a break, and it is by this that the simile does resemble. And when what discrimination has provisionally set up is cut off, the defiled phenomena can all cease along with it, and the wisdom of the noble ones is realized. Since this is directly known, the reckoning of the unsayable is not thrown away for nothing.

9. Section: If One Person Wakes, Does the World Vanish?

An objection: if at that moment everything provisionally set up by discrimination is entirely cut off, then at that same moment the characteristics and things are swept away with it. If so, then as soon as any single person attains the wisdom of a noble one, all the sentient and insentient beings, all the inner and outer things included under characteristic, name, and discrimination ought to perish forever — as when a conjurer stops conjuring and the conjured show is gone. The answer: characteristics and the rest of these things arise either with uncommon discrimination as their cause or with common discrimination as their cause. One's own resultant body is produced by the perfuming of one's own discrimination and not by any perfuming shared with others; the receptacle world is produced by the shared discrimination of beings dwelling together. If a thing has arisen from uncommon discrimination, then for the one who is without discrimination, it too ceases along with him: his own faculties, objects, and consciousness, and whatever of the surrounding world is not shared with others, no longer appear. But if a thing has arisen from common discrimination, then although his discrimination is gone, it does not perish forever, being sustained by the discrimination of others. Were it not so, the discrimination of others would have no fruit at all. Mountains and rivers and the great earth stand on, held up by those who have not yet awakened and who still cling firmly to the world their naming presents as real. And though they do not perish, one who has attained purity has, with regard to those very things, a right view that is pure: he knows as it is that what the shared discrimination presents cannot be found as it is spoken, and that a receptacle world arisen from conditions is like an illusion, like a conjured show. It is as when many practitioners of contemplation, upon one and the same thing, arrive at various resolutions and different ways of seeing through the knowledge of a concentrated mind; so it is here as well. For a bodhisattva who has gained mastery of mind, and a śrāvaka, a solitary realizer, or even an ordinary person who has attained absorption, can by the force of resolve make an object appear as they wish, turning the great earth into water or into fire; one who has attained a superior power of concentration finds that the moment he directs attention, the meanings of the teaching stand before him, impermanence and suffering and emptiness and non-self appearing distinctly as the mind turns to them; and one who has attained non-discriminating knowledge finds that while that knowledge is present, no meanings appear at all and no objective characteristic shows itself. Seeing this range in what a concentrated mind presents, one who has attained purity knows as it is that outside the mind there is no real referent, and that the world of beings and the receptacle world alike are what one's own uncommon or common discrimination presents, exactly like a conjured display.

10. Section: What Grasps and What Is Grasped, and Three Fields That Grasping Ranges Over

Of these five things, how many are what is grasped and how many are what grasps? Three are what is grasped — characteristic, name, and suchness, which are only ever the objects that mind and mental factors take up. Discrimination and correct knowledge are both what grasps and what is grasped: discrimination grasps characteristic and name and can itself be grasped by a discriminating mind, and correct knowledge grasps suchness while the characteristic of correct knowledge can itself become the object of a discriminating mind. This is why the previous fascicle could say that discrimination is what emptiness and wishlessness range over and that correct knowledge is what emptiness ranges over: though both are on the side of grasping, at the causal stage they can also be taken up by a contemplating mind. Of these five things, how many fields should be known that grasping ranges over? In brief there are three: the field with words and with characteristics that grasping ranges over; the field without words but with characteristics that grasping ranges over; and the field with neither words nor characteristics that grasping ranges over. Of these, the first is the field that grasping ranges over for one in whom speech is presently awake — one at home in language, who takes up a characteristic by way of its name, as hearing "mountain" he takes up what a mountain is. The second is the field that grasping ranges over for one in whom speech is only latent — an infant, or an ox or sheep, who does not command language, and yet by the force of the seeds of speech takes up characteristics all the same, as an infant sees a person without being able to say the word. And the third is the field that grasping ranges over for one who has separated from the latency of speech — the noble one, in whom the seeds of the afflictions of speech have been severed, at the moment fundamental knowledge realizes suchness, apart from words and apart from characteristics. Read another way: the first two are grasping at the level of conventional truth, both being still within language, whether spoken or merely latent, and both still discriminating and clinging. The last is grasping at the level of ultimate truth. And further, there is the grasping subsequently attained by one who has separated from the latency of speech, which grasps across everything included under both truths as the field it ranges over. That is to say, since the knowledge that is at once worldly and transcendent ranges over the truths as conventionally set out, that knowledge is established as using both truths for the field it ranges over. And these two graspings should be known to be established as worldly and transcendent by two causes: by what has been attained before and what has not been attained before, and by depending on speech and not depending on speech. The subsequently attained knowledge takes speech for its object and reviews the four conventionally set out truths that worldly knowledge had examined before, and in that respect it is what was attained before, depends on speech, has words and characteristics, and is called worldly; yet when it arises no seeds of affliction accompany it, and it is not produced by the elaborations of naming and does not cling to speech, and in that respect it is what was not attained before, does not depend on speech, and is called transcendent.

11. Section: Grasping Without Characteristics — What Causes It, What It Is, and How Anyone Knows It Has Happened

An objection: grasping with characteristics is what the world holds in common, while grasping without characteristics is not held in common. What cause and what condition make it grasping without characteristics? To have no cause and no condition does not stand to reason. The answer: the fruit of grasping that the perfuming of conventional naming produces is grasping with characteristics; it is held in common by the world and it makes for defilement. The fruit of grasping that the perfuming of ultimate knowledge and vision produces is grasping without characteristics; it is not held in common and it makes for purity. Therefore both of these have a cause and a condition. Conventional naming perfumes the storehouse consciousness with seeds of naming, and when conditions meet, those seeds bring out objects and the mind takes them for real, which is the whole of the world's condition and sinks it into the defilements of affliction, action, and rebirth. The perfuming of ultimate knowledge and vision is the seed laid down by long hearing of the teaching, which counters the storehouse consciousness; and by contemplating the sixteen aspects of the four truths and then the four searches and four knowledges of things as they are, by cultivating and repeatedly cultivating the knowledge whose object is suchness, one passes in a single uninterrupted moment from the highest worldly state into direct realization, and there is grasping without characteristics. It is like this: if the eye has the fault of a cataract or the like, then the film-marks of hairs and wheels appear before it and can be found; and if it has no such fault, they cannot be found, and there is only the taking of the thing's own nature without inversion. Empty space holds no hairs or wheels, and yet the diseased eye sees them there, or sees two moons where there is one — which is exactly the ordinary person's condition, every characteristic he opens his eyes upon being taken as real. Cure the disease and the phantom marks are simply not there, and space is seen to be what it always was. A further question: if within the realm without characteristics one grasps a characteristic, that is not grasping without characteristics; and if one grasps nothing at all, that too fails to be grasping without characteristics. In that case, why is it called grasping without characteristics? The answer: because the latency of speech has already been left behind, this grasping, though it does take the realm without characteristics, does not take a characteristic, and so becomes grasping without characteristics. And if there is no acquiring, how does it come to be grasping at all? Though it does not acquire the various differences among characteristics, adding nothing to them, it does take what is without characteristics, and so it comes to be grasping. And if there is no acquiring and nothing added, how can the shape of this grasping be known? Because it takes the ultimate and takes what is without characteristics, the shape it has is that the characteristics of the five kinds of thing do not appear at all — mind, mental factors, form, the formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned, the five sorts of object that in concentration are made to appear as images and are here swept away. Then why not simply allow that grasping has ceased and become nothing? Because in what has ceased into nothing there is no sense of practice to be done, and one who cultivates contemplation does not practice upon a mere nothing. And in that case, how is its shape realized and known? 12. Section: Why It Cannot Be Reported, How Something with Characteristics Causes Something Without, and Which Knowledge Actually Severs the Afflictions

In that case, why does one not report what one has realized just as one realized it? Because this inward realization is not a place where names can find their footing. What is realized when the real is realized is not the ground on which naming stands; it is without characteristics, the path of words cut off and the place of mental activity ended — like a mute person tasting something, who knows perfectly well and cannot say. A further objection: if there was previously no knowledge of what is without characteristics, then, there being none, there was also no repeated practice of such knowledge; and having never been practiced, the knowledge of what is without characteristics has no cause and ought not to arise at all. The answer: what has characteristics can also be a cause of what is without them, since it accords with it. As worldly knowledge serves as the condition from which transcendent knowledge arises, as knowledge with outflow serves as the condition from which uncontaminated knowledge arises, and as an absorption with mind serves as the condition from which the absorption without mind arises, so it is here as well. At the stages of preparatory effort the practitioner's knowledge is worldly throughout, right up to the highest worldly state, and from that single moment he enters non-discriminating knowledge without interval; one first attains an absorption with mind and only from there cultivates the absorption of cessation. The cause meant here is not a seed but a drawing-forth: one present activity accords with another and so draws it out. A further objection: the knowledges of suffering and the rest the Blessed One declared to be the cause of attaining purity. But if the knowledge of suffering and the rest discriminates suffering and the rest within the truths of suffering and the rest, it must have characteristics; and if it does not discriminate, then the knowledges of suffering and the rest do not exist at all — and if they do not exist, how can final purity be attained? The answer: it is by the enhancing force of the knowledge without characteristics that, with regard to the truths, a supremely purified discriminating knowledge, at once worldly and transcendent, arises; and that is what it means to say the afflictions to be abandoned have been abandoned. That knowledge without characteristics is the cause of the knowledges of suffering and the rest, and it is what actually severs the afflictions to be abandoned; the name of the fruit is provisionally assigned to this cause, and so it is provisionally called the knowledge of suffering and the rest. Therefore there is no fault. The sixteen aspects contemplated at the stage of hearing and reflecting are still a grasping with characteristics and do not by themselves make anyone pure; the afflictions are severed when the knowledge without characteristics is realized, and only afterward does the subsequently attained knowledge return to the four truths and their sixteen aspects, now finally pure because the severing already happened. And one more: it was said earlier that what is grasped is the fruit of what grasps; of what, then, should this grasping be called the fruit? These two are the fruit of one another in turn. The six faculties grasp and the six objects are grasped, so the objects are the fruit of the faculties; but an object also draws the faculty into taking it, and so the faculty is equally the fruit of the object. Grasper and grasped hold to each other, and neither stands without the other.

13. Section: Sweeping Away the Five Kinds of Object, and Why Nirvāṇa Is Not Mere Extinction

An objection: if the objects one knows are impermanent, accumulated, continuous, immeasurable, and for the most part not directly seen, how can one who cultivates contemplation take them as his object and bring about their ceasing? The answer: by the enhancing force of hearing and reflecting upon them, he attains concentration; and by that cause the images of the five kinds of object appear within the concentration — form, mind, mental factors, the formations dissociated from mind, and the unconditioned — and he takes just those things as his objective sphere, and by sweeping them away they come to cease. The order matters: the strength of hearing and reflecting brings concentration, concentration brings the object directly before him, and only then, contemplating the suchness of what stands before him, does he sweep it away; when he succeeds, those images no longer appear. And when the five kinds of knowable object have been swept away, what characteristic should this be said to have? It has the unsurpassed turning of the basis, unconditioned nirvāṇa, for its characteristic — unsurpassed because nothing goes beyond it, a turning of the basis because the whole body-and-world that consciousness had been presenting is turned into something uncontrived. What then is nirvāṇa? It is the purity of the sphere of phenomena, the sense in which the afflictions and the mass of suffering are stilled forever; it is not the sense of being extinguished into nothing. But if it is only the eternal stilling of the afflictions and the mass of suffering that is called nirvāṇa, on what grounds is it not the sense of extinction into nothing? Because it is as with the water element outside: only by leaving turbidity behind does it attain clarity, and it is not that when turbidity is left behind there is no clarity. And as with true gold: only by leaving hardness behind does it attain malleability, and it is not that when hardness is left behind there is no malleability. And as with empty space: only when the obscuration of clouds and mist is stilled does it attain purity, and it is not that when they are gone its purity is also nowhere to be found. The principle here should be known to be the same. What then is called the purity of the sphere of phenomena? It means that by cultivating correct knowledge one removes all characteristics forever and realizes suchness. It is like a man who, in the midst of a dream, sees his own body being swept away and drowned in a great torrent. Wishing to cross that torrent, he rouses great effort; and just because he roused great effort, he suddenly wakes; and having woken, he sees nothing whatever of that torrent. The principle by which characteristics are removed should be known to be the same. The rousing of effort is the cultivation of moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom; the waking is the moment after the highest worldly state when non-discriminating knowledge is realized; and what is then seen to have never been there is the whole of it — faculties, objects, consciousness, the receptacle world, grasper and grasped, and speech.

14. Section: Severing and Sweeping Away Happen at Once, and the Five Stages of the Turn to Characteristiclessness

A question: are the characteristics swept away at the very moment the latency of speech is being severed, or only after the severing is complete? The answer: the time of severing and the time of sweeping away are exactly equal, on the principle of the two ends of a balance, where one end goes down as the other goes up, and neither waits for the other. And it is as with a painted image, where the moment the pigments are ruined the figure vanishes with them. And as when the fault of a cataract or the like is healed, the marks of hairs and wheels are swept away with it, the time of healing and the time of sweeping away being exactly equal. The principle here should be known to be the same. Then: how does one who cultivates contemplation sweep away the characteristics of the object he takes? By a rightly concentrated mind he first examines carefully the images of the objects he knows; and afterward, by the force of attention directed to the ultimate, he turns away from what has characteristics and turns to what is without them. That is, established at least in the access concentration and ordinarily, for a bodhisattva, in the fourth absorption, he looks closely at the image standing before him; and then, attending to it as suchness, in a single moment he realizes the suchness that is the emptiness of person and of phenomena, and in that same moment lets go of the object's characteristic and arrives at the characteristicless. As the verse has it: when in the object nothing whatever is attained by knowledge, then one abides in consciousness only, the twin appearance of grasper and grasped being gone. And this turn to characteristiclessness has, further, five stages: the stage of a small portion, the stage of full pervasion, the stage that still wavers, the stage that still requires effort, and the stage of completion. In the first, the bodhisattva before the grounds, from the stage of forbearance onward, has not yet realized characteristicless suchness but has gained a superior conviction and accords with a portion of its sense. In the second, the bodhisattva of the first ground realizes the real nature of things by non-discriminating knowledge and can contemplate suchness as of one taste in all phenomena. In the third, the noble bodhisattva through the first six grounds does directly realize suchness, but cultivates it with intervals, still interrupted by what has characteristics and still moved by effort. In the fourth, the bodhisattva of the seventh ground realizes suchness without characteristics but still with applied effort. And in the fifth, from the eighth ground onward, where there is no more applied effort and no more exertion, realization proceeds spontaneously, on to the completion of the third incalculable eon and buddhahood itself.

15. Section: What Completion Looks Like, Why the Two Vehicles Rest on the Great Vehicle, and the Heaviness in Skin, Flesh, and Heart

What then is the characteristic of this completion? Because one is no longer intruded upon and adulterated by any affliction or any calamity, and is the final untroubled and purified support, it is called completion. And this same completion is what a thoroughly purified real meaning ranges over, what all direct perception ranges over, and what all mastery ranges over. Three things are left behind here and three answer to them: leaving the afflictions behind is what makes it the ranging of the real meaning, leaving suffering behind is what makes it the ranging of direct perception, and leaving heaviness behind is what makes it the ranging of mastery — the manifest activity, the seeds, and the residual traces of both the obstruction of the afflictions and the obstruction to what is knowable being wholly severed. How many vehicles are established upon this completion, and by what time is it realized? Three vehicles are established, according to the difference of three kinds of faculty in those who realize it. All noble ones are distinguished by the unconditioned, and though the unconditioned they realize is the same, the vehicles are established by the difference in the faculties of those who realize, in the depth of the non-discriminating knowledge attained, in the narrowness or breadth of what is taken as object, and in the presence or absence of compassion. But those two vehicles take the vehicle of unsurpassed complete awakening as their root — for a person of the śrāvaka or solitary-realizer vehicle must rely on a buddha's appearing in the world and setting out the true teaching before he can complete his own turning of the basis. And since for those two vehicles the differences of condition and the ripening are not fixed, the length of time for realization is not fixed either: a śrāvaka may take as little as three lives and as long as sixty eons, a solitary realizer as little as four lives and as long as a hundred. But the last vehicle can be realized only after three incalculable great eons, on account of the difference among three kinds of heaviness to be severed. What are the three? First, the heaviness lodged in the skin, of the class of the unpleasant in the lower destinies. By severing it one does not go to the lower destinies, and when one cultivates preparatory effort one is no longer intruded upon by anything unpleasant. This is what the bodhisattva severs on completing the first incalculable eon and entering the path of seeing at the first ground. Second, the heaviness lodged in the flesh, of the class of the obstruction of the afflictions. By severing it even the subtlest afflictions of every kind no longer become active, though the latent tendencies have not yet all been destroyed. This is severed on completing the second incalculable eon and entering the eighth ground. Third, the heaviness lodged in the heart, of the class of the obstruction to what is knowable. By severing it one destroys forever every latent tendency there is, and unobstructed knowledge proceeds with mastery throughout the whole field of the knowable. This is severed in the adamantine concentration at the end of the third incalculable eon, when the seeds of both obstructions are finally cut, and it is the knowledge of all aspects itself.

16. Section: How Each Vehicle Is Established, and the Two Ways of Ruining It — a Fever One Refuses to Cure and a Fever One Denies Having

Further, how is the śrāvaka vehicle established? By three causes: by manifestation, by vow, and by nature. By manifestation means that, according to the strength of what each sort of being is to be trained by, the Tathāgata manifests a manifested śrāvaka — for buddhas and bodhisattvas, meeting beings who can be liberated through the form of a śrāvaka, take that form in order to liberate them. By vow means that there is a person who has already made a vow directed to the śrāvaka vehicle, and just so he is established as a śrāvaka; the vow is one he does not change. By nature means that there is a person whose compassion has been thin and weak from the beginning and who is deeply afraid of things that involve suffering. By these two causes he does not greatly delight in benefiting others, and does not for the sake of that willingly stay in birth and death. Because he abides in this nature, he is established as a śrāvaka. And further by awakening to that nature, which means that within the truths as conventionally set out he for the most part cultivates a practice governed by fear — the mind filled with the thought that the three realms give no rest, are as a burning house, are full of suffering and much to be feared — delighting only in abiding in unconditioned nirvāṇa; and by this cause he attains completion. As with the śrāvaka vehicle, so with the solitary realizer: his compassion too is thin, he too fears the mass of suffering, he too does not delight in benefiting others; the difference is that he appears in an age without a buddha and realizes awakening there. And by the three causes contrary to the above, the bodhisattva should be known — whose compassion is strong, who abides in neither birth and death nor nirvāṇa, and who has a powerful wish for the welfare and ease of beings; who knows birth and death as it is and so has neither the ordinary person's fault of being stained by it nor the two vehicles' fault of recoiling from it, and knows nirvāṇa as it is and so has neither the ordinary person's fault of dreading it nor the two vehicles' fault of longing for it. Further, how does a śrāvaka ruin the true teaching and the discipline? There are śrāvakas who reckon that what is called quiescence is merely the absence of the burning of the afflictions, and who conceive a great fear of it, thinking: I shall be cut off, I shall be destroyed forever, I shall not exist. It is like a man afflicted with a fever who has no way of recognizing what being free of it would be like, and who supposes that when the illness is cured his whole body will perish with it, and so becomes afraid and thinks, I would rather not be rid of this fever — that is what is called ruin. By this simile, the śrāvaka who ruins the teaching should be known to be the same. Further, how does a bodhisattva ruin the Great Vehicle? There are bodhisattvas who, hearing that all phenomena are profoundly without nature, cling to the view that the burning of the afflictions is of itself originally non-existent, and suppose that they have no grave sickness of birth and death. It is like a man who, with a fever arisen in his own body, declares that he has no illness, and so cannot be freed from that fever — that is what is called ruin. By this simile, the bodhisattva who ruins the Great Vehicle should be known to be the same. Phenomena are indeed empty of any substance; but the afflictions, the inversions, and the clinging that beings have rehearsed from beginningless time are extremely thick, and unless one hears, reflects, and cultivates, and works at moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom until greed, hatred, and delusion are put out, the afflictions do not cease and the grave sickness of birth and death cannot stop.

17. Section: The Five Against the View of Self, the Four Realities, the Four Searches, and the Four Knowledges of Things as They Are

Of these five things, how many are the view of a real self and how many are not? Characteristic runs through both, since the characteristics of characteristic, of name, and of discrimination belong to it, while the characteristics of suchness and of correct knowledge do not. Two are the view of a real self, namely name and discrimination, both belonging to the ordinary person's false discrimination and joined to the view of self. One is not, namely correct knowledge, which is the purified knowledge that there is no self and nothing belonging to a self, and is not joined to the view of self. And of suchness neither can be said, since suchness is the real emptiness of all phenomena and stands as it is whether or not the view of a real self is present, so that it can be called neither joined to it nor unjoined. As with the view of a real self, so should it be understood of existence and of the world. Existence here means existence in the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless: characteristic runs through both, its conditioned and outflowing side belonging to existence and its unconditioned and uncontaminated side not; name and discrimination belong to existence throughout; correct knowledge does not; and of suchness neither can be said. And so with the world: characteristic runs through the worldly and the transcendent, name and discrimination are worldly, correct knowledge is transcendent, and of suchness neither can be said. Of these five things and the four realities, which things here include how many realities? The reality established by the world and the reality established by reasoning are included in three of the things — in characteristic, name, and discrimination — for both still stand within false discrimination, taking up things by name and characteristic, and neither has left the pervasively imputed behind. The reality that the knowledge purified of the obstruction of the afflictions ranges over and the reality that the knowledge purified of the obstruction to what is knowable ranges over are included in two — in correct knowledge and suchness — the first being the four noble truths as the uncontaminated knowledge of śrāvakas and solitary realizers ranges over them, the second being the selflessness of phenomena as the non-discriminating knowledge of bodhisattvas and buddhas ranges over it, once they have entered the selflessness of phenomena and become thoroughly purified in it, holding the inexpressible nature and the provisionally spoken nature of all phenomena to be perfectly equal. Of these five things and the four searches, which things include how many searches? Discrimination joined to attention in accordance with reason includes all four; discrimination not so joined includes none of them. And of these five things and the four kinds of thorough knowledge of things as they are, which things include how many? All of them are included in correct knowledge alone.

18. Section: The Hidden Intent Behind "All Things Are Without Two" and "All Things Are Without Own Nature"

With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that all phenomena are without two? Just with reference to these five things as set out: by their conventional nature they are said to be without own nature, and by their several particular characteristics they are said to have own nature. The nature that words assign is not there, while the inexpressible nature that each of the five carries in its own particular shape is not nothing; so all phenomena are neither existent nor non-existent, and that is what being without two means. With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that all phenomena are without own nature? Three kinds of absence of own nature are taught according to the capacity of those to be trained: absence of own nature in respect of characteristic, absence of own nature in respect of arising, and absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate. What is the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic? It is the conventionally spoken nature of all phenomena. The pervasively imputed has no substance and therefore no characteristic; it is set up as a characteristic by provisional naming and not by any characteristic of its own, and so is called the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic. What is the absence of own nature in respect of arising? All conditioned things are produced by a coming together of conditions and exist by the force of conditions, not of themselves; therefore it is called the absence of own nature in respect of arising. Mind and mental factors arise from four conditions and form from two; and precisely because arising has no own nature, conditions are needed at all — had it one, it would need none. What is the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate? It is that which the aspect of real meaning has left behind — that is, what is left behind when the pervasively imputed appearance of being existent and of being non-existent is removed — and by reference to the ultimate this is said to be without own nature. It is like a monk cultivating contemplation who forms a provisional resolve upon a great heap of bones and cannot then sweep it away, and in whom the notion that this heap of bones is without own nature in respect of the ultimate runs on without interruption. He has contemplated the skeleton until bones fill the room, the monastery, the village, the country, the whole earth, and then finds he cannot dismiss what long practice has made present; so he reflects that this heap of bones, though it covers the earth, is only what his own mind provisionally set up, that outside mind there is no thing — and tracing the contemplation back to the mind that made it, the appearance of bones is gone. Thus should the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate be known. Among these five things, it is not by the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic that they are said to be without own nature, since none of the five is the pervasively imputed; it is by the absence of own nature in respect of arising and by the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate that they are said, each according to its case, to be without own nature. That is, characteristic, name, discrimination, and correct knowledge are all so by both of those two, arising from conditions and being conventional rather than ultimate. Suchness is not said to be without own nature by any absence of own nature at all, for suchness is how things already are, present whether or not the ultimate has been realized. Therefore the Blessed One, with this hidden intent, spoke thus in verse: I teach one truth, and there is no second.

19. Section: Unborn, Like Space, Like an Illusion, Impermanent, Painful, Empty, and Without Self — Which Absence of Own Nature Each Formula Rests On

With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that all phenomena are unborn and unceasing, quiescent from the beginning, and of their own nature nirvāṇa? He said this with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic. As the scripture explains it: if a thing has no characteristic of its own whatever, then there is no arising; if there is no arising, there is no ceasing; if there is neither arising nor ceasing, then it is quiescent from the beginning; and if it is quiescent from the beginning, it is of its own nature nirvāṇa, with not the least portion left over that could be made to pass into nirvāṇa. With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that all phenomena are equal to space? This too he said with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic — for what is provisionally set up in words has neither substance nor characteristic, just as space has none, and the scripture likens it to a flower seen in the air. With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that all phenomena are like an illusion and the rest? He said this with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of arising and the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate — the conditioned arising only from conditions, and the conventional dependently arisen no longer appearing once suchness is realized; and the scripture accordingly likens the absence of own nature in respect of arising, and one portion of the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate, to a conjured image. With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that contemplating form and so on through consciousness, wholly or in part, one finds them impermanent? He said this with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic. Why so? Because wishing to say that in such contemplation nothing permanent is to be found, he said that in such contemplation they are impermanent. To be impermanent just is for the permanent not to be there; and to hold the five aggregates permanent is the pervasively imputed, which is why this formula rests on the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic. With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that contemplating form and so on through consciousness, wholly or in part, one finds them all painful? He said this with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of arising and the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate. The five appropriated aggregates do not arise causelessly but from a coming together of conditions, and having the character of oppression they are painful in their very substance; and further, when the ultimate is realized their characteristics no longer appear, so that clinging to the conventional dependently arisen as really existing is itself painful. With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that contemplating those same aggregates one finds them all empty? Just with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of arising and the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate, phenomena having left the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic behind — that is, the emptiness spoken of here is not the emptiness of the pervasively imputed, which has neither substance nor characteristic at all, but an emptiness contemplated upon the dependently arisen itself: empty of arising by itself, and empty in that when the ultimate is realized faculties, objects, consciousness, grasper and grasped, the receptacle world, and speech no longer appear. And as their being empty is spoken of by way of their having left something behind, so their being without self is spoken of by way of their being of a different aspect, and this should be known to work the same way — the five aggregates in their own shape simply not being the permanent, single, controlling self that imputation supposes, and so differing in aspect from it.

20. Section: Six More Sayings, and the Ones That Rest on No Absence of Own Nature at All

With what hidden intent did the Blessed One say that when form and so on through consciousness are contemplated in accordance with reason and thought over carefully, one comes at last to see that they do not appear as existing? He said this with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic — for form and the rest have no nature such as words assign, and to impute a real own nature to them through speech is to look for a flower in the air, which is what not appearing as existing means. With what hidden intent did he say that they appear falsely and unreally? He said this with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of arising and the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate — the dependently arisen being empty and unreal, appearing from conditions, present without being real, and arising by nothing of its own; and further, when suchness is realized, the conventional dependently arisen does not appear at all. With what hidden intent did he say this: therefore one should now know this place, where the eye is forever stilled and separated from the notion of form, and so on until the mind is forever stilled and separated from the notion of phenomena? He said this without reference to any absence of own nature whatsoever. This is spoken of suchness itself, where grasper and grasped have both been stilled and neither can be found; and the three absences of own nature belong to the path of preparation, whereas in the contemplation of suchness one makes no contemplation of characteristiclessness or of non-arising at all. With what hidden intent did he say that, by that, in every place one dismisses every notion, so that even Śakra and the other gods cannot know upon what basis such a one has entered absorption? He said this too without reference to any absence of own nature — the absorption meant being the real one that takes suchness for its condition, so that the gods, with all their divine sight and their knowledge of others' minds, search for where such a monk has entered and cannot find it, and bow instead, saying: homage to the supreme among men; I cannot know upon what you rest your meditation. With what hidden intent did he say that in a form cognized by the eye, and so on to a phenomenon cognized by the mind, that answers to a place of joy or sorrow or equanimity, there is no truth, no reality, no absence of inversion, and no non-inversion — and yet also say that there is a transcendent truth of the noble ones? He said this both with reference to all the absences of own nature and without reference to any absence of own nature. That in these there is no truth and no reality is said with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of characteristic; that there is no absence of inversion is said with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of arising, against those who hold that things arise of themselves and count that view uninverted; that there is no non-inversion is said with reference to the absence of own nature in respect of the ultimate, against those who take the pleasant fortunes of gods and men for nirvāṇa; while the transcendent truth of the noble ones is suchness, and is spoken of on no absence of own nature at all. 21. Section: Why the Five Stand in That Order, and the Opening of the Three Natures

Of these five things, on what grounds is characteristic established first and correct knowledge established last? Because if there were no thing, it would not stand to reason to set up a name for it, and so in this order a name is set up. And because there is a name, an own nature is set up and differences are set up, and so in this order discrimination is set up. And because of discrimination, one discriminates now a characteristic, now a name, now both together. By these three things the defiled side is displayed in its complete order — a being's whole condition in birth and death being just this, that it takes up a characteristic, fixes a name on it, and discriminates about it inwardly, so that affliction, action, and suffering never stop coming. Only from there does it become possible to cultivate the purified side: namely, contemplating the suchness of all those defiled things, and by correct knowledge being able to contemplate rightly, one attains purity. One who wishes to know the real should further cultivate contemplation upon the three natures. The summary verse says: a general statement, then distinguishing them one by one; what each takes as object, the differences, what they rest upon; also the subtle clinging; and the imputed nature that follows names and the rest. What then are called the three natures? First, the nature that is pervasively imputed; second, the nature that arises in dependence on other things; third, the nature that is perfectly accomplished. What is the nature that is pervasively imputed? It is the nature established by following speech and relying on provisional naming. What is set up in words has neither substance nor characteristic, and to cling to it as really having an own nature is the pervasively imputed. What is the nature that arises in dependence on other things? It is the nature produced from a coming together of conditions — mind and mental factors arising from four conditions and form from two, and every one of the ninety-four conditioned things, mind, mental factors, form, and the formations dissociated from mind, arising this way; as the scripture puts it, all phenomena have arising from conditions as their nature, this being, that is; this arising, that arises, ignorance conditioning formations and so on to the massing of the whole aggregate of suffering. And what is the nature that is perfectly accomplished? It is the suchness of phenomena, which is what the knowledge of the noble ones ranges over, the objective sphere of that knowledge, what that knowledge takes as object; and further, it is able to bring one to the attainment of purity, able to release one from every bond of characteristics and every bond of heaviness, and able also to draw forth every good quality.

22. Section: What Each of the Three Natures Is Known Through, and Five Kinds of Imputation

Through what should the pervasively imputed nature be known as its condition? Through the binding together of characteristic and name. Beings settle that a name matches the substance of a thing, that name and characteristic belong together fixedly, that the thing is as the name has it, or that the name lights the thing up; and out of this the pervasively imputed nature arises. The commentators list five ways the imputing runs: imputing a meaning from a name, as when one hears an unfamiliar word and casts about for what it must refer to; imputing a name from a meaning, as when one sees an animal one cannot name and casts about for its word; imputing a name from other names, as when a term is explained by terms already understood; imputing a meaning from other meanings, as when something known is used to guess at something unknown, which is most of what the sciences do; and imputing both from both, taking a name whose meaning is settled and a meaning whose name is settled and reasoning from them to what is not yet settled, which is most of what learned disputes consist of. Whether speech is presently awake, as in one at home in language, or only latent, as in an infant, both are included under this binding together of characteristic and name, and with it as condition beings are made to cling to the spoken nature of everything as real. Through what should the nature that arises in dependence on other things be known as its condition? Through the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature. Beings raise the pervasively imputed out of the binding of characteristic and name; that clinging perfumes the storehouse consciousness into seeds; the seeds, meeting their conditions, become active again as discrimination and clinging, and so as affliction; from affliction comes action, and from action the fruit of birth and death. So the whole dependently arisen array in its defiled aspect is what clinging produces, and it is by way of the clinging mind that this nature is known. Through what should the perfectly accomplished nature be known as its condition? Through the pervasively imputed nature being, within the nature that arises in dependence on other things, finally unreal. To know that the spoken nature is not real within the dependently arisen, that the own natures and differences that words set up are nowhere to be found — that is how this nature is known. In another scripture the Blessed One said that this nature should be known through not clinging to the pervasively imputed nature; but that was said with reference to the attainment of purity, not with reference to characteristics. Here, in the present sense, it should be understood as said with reference to characteristics — spoken, that is, for one who has not yet attained the purified non-discriminating knowledge, so that the perfectly accomplished may be understood through its characteristic and lead him toward the uncontaminated. Then: how many kinds does the pervasively imputed nature have? In brief, five: imputing the meaning as an own nature, imputing the name as an own nature, imputing the defiled as an own nature, imputing the purified as an own nature, and imputing what is neither defiled nor purified as an own nature — that is, imputing what a name refers to; imputing the name itself, not the thing; imputing the five aggregates as they stand joined to greed and the other defilements; imputing them as they stand joined to non-greed and the other wholesome states; and imputing the phenomena that are indeterminate, being neither.

23. Section: Four Ways of Imputing a Meaning, and Two Ways of Imputing a Name

What is imputing the meaning as an own nature? There are four kinds: imputing an own characteristic, imputing a characteristic of difference, imputing the characteristic of what is grasped, and imputing the characteristic of what grasps. Imputing an own characteristic means imputing that this thing is the own nature of form, and so on to imputing that this thing is the own nature of consciousness; that this thing is the own nature of the eye, and so on to imputing that this thing is the own nature of a phenomenon. It is the mind, working from names, settling that the objects, the consciousnesses, and the faculties really are the substances their names announce. Imputing a characteristic of difference means imputing that this form is agreeable, that this form is disagreeable, that this form is neither agreeable nor disagreeable; that this form is visible, that this form is invisible; that this form is resistant, that this form is unresistant; that this form has outflow, that this form is without outflow; that this form is conditioned, that this form is unconditioned — and by reasoning of this sort, imputing all the differences this form has. Taking the own nature of form as the place it rests on, one goes on to sort out its varieties and then holds those varieties to be real. As with form, so should it be understood of the remaining aggregates, of all the sense fields, and of the rest. Imputing the characteristic of what is grasped means imputing that this form is what the eye grasps, and this what the ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind grasp — the visible and resistant being assigned to the eye, the invisible and resistant to ear, nose, tongue, and body, and the invisible and unresistant form included in the sphere of phenomena to the mind. And further, imputing that this feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are what a desire-realm mind grasps, that these are what a form-realm mind grasps, that these are what a formless-realm mind grasps, and that these are what an unbound mind grasps. Imputing the characteristic of what grasps means imputing that this form is what grasps form, and that this form is what grasps sound, smell, taste, touch, and phenomena — that is, imputing the clear faculty of the eye, itself a form, as the grasper of visible form, and the faculties of ear, nose, tongue, and body as graspers of sound, smell, taste, and touch, and the faculty of mind as grasper of phenomena. And further, imputing that this feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are what grasps form, and that these are what grasps sound, smell, taste, touch, and phenomena — feeling and the rest that arise in dependence on the eye grasping visible and resistant form just as the eye does, and those that arise in dependence on ear and so on to mind grasping sound, smell, taste, touch, and phenomena. What is imputing the name as an own nature? There are two kinds: without difference and with difference. Without difference means imputing the names that belong to all phenomena alike — aggregate, element, sense field, and terms of that sort, which run across everything and do not mark off which phenomenon is meant. With difference means imputing that this is named form, this is named feeling, this is named perception, this is named formation, this is named consciousness — and terms of that sort, the separate names belonging severally to phenomena immeasurable and innumerable in their differences.

24. Section: Imputing the Defiled, the Purified, and the Indeterminate, and Five Further Ways the Imputing Runs

What is imputing the defiled as an own nature? It means imputing that this form has greed, has hatred, has delusion, cannot get free of the bondage of greed, hatred, and delusion, and further is unaccompanied by faith and all the wholesome states — form here taken as it appears when the mind is greedy over an agreeable object, angry over a disagreeable one, and dull over one that is neither, so that greed shows in the look of the eyes, hatred in the set of the face, delusion in the words spoken, and the whole of bodily and verbal action comes out defiled. And further, imputing that this feeling, this perception, this formation, this consciousness have greed, have hatred, have delusion, cannot get free of that bondage, and are unaccompanied by faith and all the wholesome states — pleasant feeling joined to greed, painful feeling to hatred, neutral feeling to delusion, and perception, volition, and the mind itself defiled along with them. What is imputing the purified as an own nature? Its characteristic should be known as the contrary of the above: aggregates without greed, hatred, or delusion, free of that bondage, and accompanied by faith and all the wholesome states. This is still the purity that hearing, reflecting, and worldly cultivation understand, not yet free of imputation, which is why it too is called an imputing. What is imputing what is neither defiled nor purified as an own nature? It means imputing that this form is what is grasped and this is what grasps; that this feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are what is grasped and this is what grasps — an imputing that concerns only the functions of grasping and being grasped, aimed neither at the afflictions nor at what counters them. And further, within all indeterminate phenomena, imputing whatever indeterminate phenomena there are: the fruit that past action ripens, the four postures of walking, standing, sitting, and lying, the crafts and occupations by which a living is made, and the manifestations that a supernormal power produces from an indeterminate mind — as when one imputes a real own nature to someone's walking or scratching his head, without imputing him defiled or purified. Further, it should be known that the pervasively imputed nature has five more kinds: imputing a meaning by way of a name, imputing a name by way of a meaning, imputing a name by way of a name, imputing a meaning by way of a meaning, and imputing both by way of both. What is imputing a meaning by way of a name? It means imputing that this name for a thing that is form has a real nature of form behind it, and that these names for things that are feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness have real natures of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness behind them. One knows the word without knowing what it refers to, and reasons from the word to a substance: hearing "ox" without knowing an ox, one casts about for what an ox must be, and afterward, meeting the thing, takes it up under the word one learned. What is imputing a name by way of a meaning? It means imputing that this thing is named form, or is not named form; that this thing is named feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, or is not so named. One has met the thing without knowing its word, and then, learning the word, fits it onto what one had already seen — as one who sees an animal he cannot name and later asks, and thereafter names that kind on sight. What is imputing a name by way of a name? It means that, not understanding the thing that is form, one discriminates the name of form and raises an imputation upon it; and not understanding the things that are feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, one discriminates their names and raises imputations upon them. 25. Section: Where the Clinging Rests — Five Kinds Driven by Effort and Two Kinds Made of Naming

What is imputing a meaning by way of a meaning? It means that, not understanding the name of form, one discriminates the thing that is form by way of what one does not name, and raises an imputation upon it; and not understanding the names of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, one discriminates those things by way of what one does not name, and raises imputations upon them. One touches something hot without knowing what it is called, and having met fire before and known fire to be hot, reckons that there must be fire here — using one meaning to reach for another with no name in hand. This is the small child's way of seeing, and the way of direct perception before words arrive: taking up the meaning without taking up the name. What is imputing both by way of both? It means imputing that this thing is the own nature of form and naming it form; that this thing is the own nature of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness and naming it feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. Here name and thing are already matched, and the matching is what generates the imputing: knowing what a sheep looks like and knowing that this kind is called a sheep, one sees an animal resembling a sheep and settles that it must be called a sheep and must be shaped just so. Further, the clinging to the pervasively imputed nature should be known to be, in brief, of two kinds: clinging that comes with effort, and clinging that comes with the assigning of names. The clinging that comes with effort should be known to have five kinds again: through the effort of craving; through the effort of hatred; through the effort of bringing together; through the effort of parting; and through the effort of indifferently going along. That is, greed raised toward an agreeable object; hatred raised toward a disagreeable one; the pushing to be joined with what one wants that greed produces; the pushing to be rid of what one does not want that hatred produces; and the going along with an object that is neither, in a mind of indifference. All five are the operation of volition, which makes the mind do something and so earns the name effort. And the clinging that comes with the assigning of names should be known to have two kinds: what is not made of words, and what is made of words. What is not made of words means clinging in the form: what is this thing? how does this thing come about? what is this thing in particular? and how does that come about? The first casts about for the substance of what is seen, the second for the cause by which it came to be, the third for its varieties, the fourth for the cause of those varieties. As when one first meets an unfamiliar appliance and asks what it is, what it was designed to do, what other kinds there are that do the same work, and which kind is used for which purpose. None of this says outright that this thing is form or this thing is feeling, and so it is not made of words — yet the thinking cannot proceed apart from naming at all, and so it is still counted as an assigning of names. What is made of words means clinging in the form: this is this thing, and this thing is thus — form, or so on to consciousness, or conditioned, or unconditioned, or permanent, or impermanent, or wholesome, or unwholesome, or indeterminate, and the like.

26. Section: Five Subtle Clingings, and Three Reasons the Fool Takes What a Word Points To for Something Real

Further, subtle clinging should be known to be of five kinds: clinging to the permanent in what is impermanent; clinging to the pleasant in what is painful; clinging to the pure in what is impure; clinging to a self in what has no self; and clinging, within all characteristics, to the pervasively imputed nature. Not knowing that the momentary aggregates are impermanent, one takes them as lasting; not knowing that painful feeling is the suffering of pain, pleasant feeling the suffering of change, and neutral feeling the suffering of conditioned existence, one takes them all for happiness, so that even the fortunes of gods and men in the desire realm, which carry all three sufferings, and the fruits of the form and formless realms, which carry the last two, are held to be pleasant; not knowing the body to be impure, one takes it as clean; not knowing the aggregates to be without self and without anything belonging to a self, one takes them as self and one's own; and within every characteristic that mental consciousness takes as object, one holds the nature that words set up to be real. The first four are clinging to a self in the person, the last is clinging to a self in phenomena; all arise from being confused about suchness, and all arise spontaneously and are therefore extremely hard to notice, which is why they are called subtle. Further, by five causes it should be known that the fool, following names and following speech, clings to what those words point to as having a real own nature. How so? Suppose someone asks: what does this thing have as its own nature? And he answers: this thing has the own nature of form, not the name of form. Or he answers: this thing has the own nature of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, not the names of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness. He is holding the name and the thing named to be different — a fire that burns is not the word "fire" spoken with the mouth or entertained in the mind, since neither of those burns anything. That is the first cause. Further, dwelling alone in a quiet place and diligently examining the own characteristics and shared characteristics of things, he searches out that this thing is the characteristic of form, not the name of form; or searches out that this thing is the characteristic of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, not their names. In concentration he takes up the shape of the thing itself — the hardness, wetness, warmth, and motion of the body's elements, its running pus and blood, the felt quality of a pleasant feeling, the shape that perception seizes, the motion of the mind's fashioning, the discerning of consciousness, or their shared marks of impermanence and non-self — and not the words for any of them. That is the second cause. Further, when he seeks the characteristic of form in this thing that is form and cannot get it, displeasure arises; not so when he seeks the name of form and cannot get it. Or when he seeks the characteristics of feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness in these things and cannot get them, displeasure arises; not so when he seeks their names and cannot get them. What we actually want is the thing, not the word; and yet, perfumed from beginningless time by naming, the meaning we get is always one that arrives bound to a name — as when a man is told a card is being sent him and feels nothing, since he has only the word, and is delighted only when told it is a credit card without limit and he grasps what the word points to. That is the third cause. In all three the man knows perfectly well that the thing differs from its name, and clings to the thing and not the word — and still cannot reach the thing apart from the word, and so still, following names and following speech, clings to what words point to as having a real own nature.

27. Section: Does a Word Run Upon Something or Upon Nothing, and How Names Bind the Mind

Further, speech runs upon names and a name runs upon a meaning. Here, so the opponent holds, if a name makes manifest the meaning that is an own characteristic, it does not make manifest the meaning that is a characteristic of difference, nor the meaning that is the characteristic of what is grasped, nor the meaning that is the characteristic of what grasps; or if a name makes manifest the meaning that is the characteristic of what grasps, it does not make manifest the others down to the own characteristic. Say "flower" and one does not thereby know a blue flower from a yellow one; say "eye" and one names what grasps and not the visible form grasped. And, he continues, if that same name ran upon the meaning that is an own characteristic and also upon the meaning that is the characteristic of what grasps, then all these other names, each understood separately and running upon its own meaning, could not be had at all. This does not stand to reason. For then, among the names that run each upon its separate meaning, if a name runs upon the meaning that is an own characteristic, and so on to a name that runs upon the meaning that is the characteristic of what grasps — does this name run upon something that exists, or upon something that does not? That it runs upon something that exists does not stand to reason; why it does not was already argued in the examination of the five things — for before a name is assigned there is no corresponding awareness, so the name does not match the substance; and one thing carries many names without having many substances; and one name is applied to other meanings besides, so that the substance would have to be as indeterminate as the name. And if it runs upon something that does not exist, then it follows that this name runs upon a meaning that has no characteristic — and that is where the reasoning arrives. And if it runs upon a meaning that has no characteristic, then this non-existent meaning can only make manifest what one has oneself added; and to take up what one has added is precisely to cling. Therefore that the fool, following names and following speech, clings to an own nature in what those words point to, is a conclusion that stands. Further, all fools are bound by naming within all characteristics, and so it should be known that, following names and following speech, they falsely cling to an own nature in what those words point to. How is it known that they are bound by naming within all characteristics? By reasoning and by scripture. How by reasoning? Because apart from naming no delight is to be had in things, whereas where naming accompanies them delight is to be had in things — the cook who is praised for the dish grows happy and stops feeling the labor, and without the praise feels no particular joy. That is one reason. Further, because the two arise in dependence on each other. Why so? Because with a thing as its support a name comes to be, and with a name as its support a thing can come to be. That is: the world must have a thing to depend on before the discrimination of naming arises, and does not raise such discrimination where there is no thing; so with a thing as support, a name comes to be. And as when one who cultivates absorption, inwardly absorbed, directs attention and thinks according to a name held in mind, and just so an image of the same kind as the thing to be known arises, running forward by that means and standing present before him; so with a name as support, a thing can come to be. One attends to the word "corpse," and the shape of a corpse appears in the mind, and when what appears there matches closely enough what the eye would see, the contemplation of the impure can begin upon it.

28. Section: The Verse on Being Bound by Words, and Why Knowing There Is No Self Is Not Yet Being Free of It

Further, when one cultivates the counteragent to naming: if one settles the mind in the realm without characteristics, no characteristic whatever appears before it; and if one does not settle the mind in the realm without characteristics, then it does not go as one wishes, and the characteristics carry the mind off and turn it about. By this reasoning it should be known that within characteristics, naming is a bond. And how is it known by scripture? As the Blessed One said: the fool who thinks in ignorance is bound by words within characteristics; the Sage is released from the bond of words and has mastery within characteristics. One who dwells in a purified seeing abides in true knowledge; he attains nothing of an own nature, and does not see what that own nature was resting on. Because the true knowledge is purified, he is called truly luminous; because he is joined to neither of the two clingings, he is called the one without two. To be bound by words is to be one in whom the eyes have not yet been washed, whose every act of thinking takes up a characteristic by way of a name and who cannot awaken to the inexpressible nature of what he knows. To dwell in a purified seeing is for the noble bodhisattva to abide in the real knowledge, to penetrate that the natures which naming sets up are all unfindable, and no longer to see the characteristic that naming rested on as real — for as when a rope in the dark appears as a snake, one who realizes the nature of consciousness has not only the notion of the snake gone but the notion of the rope swept away with it. And the two clingings are these: to hold the natures that words provisionally set up to be really there is the clinging that adds; to deny that there is anything at all where words rested, dismissing the dependently arisen and the perfectly accomplished together, is the clinging that takes away. The Buddha is apart from both, and therefore is called the one without two, and therefore is said to hold the middle, and therefore is called unsurpassed. Further, it is as with an ordinary person who understands well that there is no self within the aggregates: though he contemplates that the self set up within the aggregates is only provisionally there, it is not the case that clinging to a self does not go on arising in him, since the latent tendency to it has not been cut off for good. The principle here should be known to be the same. Naming binds in exactly this way — one may reflect on selflessness in accordance with reason and still be bound within characteristics, because the seeds of naming remain. And yet that is not a counsel of despair: diligent hearing, reflecting, and cultivating of the twofold emptiness of person and phenomena, attention that accords with suchness, does loosen the bond, and one who keeps at it will one day reach the field with neither words nor characteristics and realize the suchness that is apart from words.

Further, it is as with an ordinary person who understands well that there is no self within the aggregates: though he contemplates that the self set up within the aggregates is only provisionally there, it is not the case that clinging to a self does not go on arising in him, since the latent tendency to it has not been cut off for good. The principle here should be known to be the same.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi gathers the three reasons the chapter has given for saying that naming binds. First, without naming there is no delight to be had in things and with it there is, which is why an ordinary person cannot know anything apart from words. Second, thing and name arise supporting each other — the world names what it meets, and one in absorption meets what he has named. Third, a noble one settled in the characteristicless realm finds no characteristic before him, while one who cannot settle there is carried off by them. The closing analogy is the one he wants remembered: a person can understand selflessness perfectly and still find the sense of self arising, because understanding is not the same as having severed the latency — and the same holds for the bond of names. But the fascicle does not end in resignation; it ends where the practice does, with the reminder that reflection accordant with suchness genuinely thins the bond.
