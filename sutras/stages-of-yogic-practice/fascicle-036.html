The Causes and Fruits That Equip a Bodhisattva to Help

Continuing the Ground of the Bodhisattva's chapter on self-and-other-benefit, this fascicle turns to three pairs of cause and fruit — karmic ripening, merit, and wisdom — that together equip a bodhisattva with the actual capacity to benefit both self and others, before the chapter closes and the treatise opens its account of true meaning.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 36 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Three Causes and Fruits Behind a Bodhisattva's Capacity to Help — Eight Marks of Ripening

Self-and-other-benefit's fourth category asks a practical question: what actually equips a bodhisattva to help at all? The answer comes in three pairs of cause and fruit — karmic ripening, merit, and wisdom — opening with eight concrete marks of a ripened result: long life, fine appearance, noble birth, authority, credibility, influence, an easy rapport with people, and great strength.

Chinese Source

略說應知三因三果。何等為三？一者、異熟因、異熟果，二者、福因、福果，三者、智因、智果。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, one should know there are three causes and three fruits. What are the three? First, the cause and fruit of karmic ripening; second, the cause and fruit of merit; third, the cause and fruit of wisdom.

Chinese Source

云何異熟？謂略有八。一者、壽量具足，二者、形色具足，三者、族姓具足，四者、自在具足，五者、信言具足，六者、大勢具足，七者、人性具足，八者、大力具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is karmic ripening? In brief, there are eight kinds: complete longevity; complete fineness of form; complete nobility of birth; complete authority; complete credibility; complete greatness of influence; complete rapport with people; and complete strength.

Chinese Source

眾所信奉，斷訟取則，不行諂誑、偽斗稱等，所受寄物終不差違，於諸有情言無虛妄；以是緣故，凡有所說無不信受，是名菩薩信言具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Trusted and deferred to by all, taken as the standard when settling disputes, never resorting to flattery or deceit, false measures or false scales; anything entrusted to their keeping is never mishandled; their word to any being is never false — for this reason, whatever they say is universally believed. This is called a bodhisattva's completeness of credibility.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this section as answering a question easy to overlook: good intentions alone do not make someone an effective bodhisattva — a person also needs the actual worldly standing to be listened to, trusted, and followed. The eight marks of karmic ripening supply exactly this: long life (giving enough time to cultivate and to teach — he recounts the Buddha's own choice of an eighty-year lifespan, and the tradition's account of his final illness from a poisoned mushroom dish he insisted on eating alone so his attendants would be spared); fine appearance (people are simply more inclined to approach and trust someone pleasant to look at); noble birth (inherited social standing that opens doors ordinary origins would keep closed); great authority (wealth, allies, and capable associates that make ambitious projects to help others actually feasible); and credibility, illustrated at length — a bodhisattva known never to use rigged scales in trade, never to falsify a document, never to mishandle an object placed in their care, becomes the person a community naturally turns to for fair arbitration. He contrasts this with a cautionary story of an unlettered monk who entrusted temple-building funds to a lay patron without any written record, was denied the money back, and was beaten to death pursuing the matter through the local magistrate — proof, he notes, of how much ordinary human affairs still run on documented trust rather than character alone, and how rare and valuable a reputation like the bodhisattva's actually is. The remaining marks — great influence, an easy rapport with people, and great strength — will complete the set as the chapter continues, together forming not a list of unrelated blessings but a coherent toolkit: exactly the worldly capital a bodhisattva needs in hand before compassion alone can actually accomplish anything at scale.

2. The Remaining Marks of Ripening, and What Actually Produces Each One

The eight marks of ripening close with influence, an easy rapport with others, and physical vigor — then the treatise turns to what actually produces each of the eight, tracing every worldly advantage back to a specific, deliberate prior cause rather than treating it as unearned luck.

Chinese Source

有大名稱，流聞世間。所謂具足勇健精進、剛毅、敏捷、審悉善戒，種種技藝工巧業處，展轉妙解，出過餘人……是名菩薩大勢具足……為性少疾，或全無病，有大堪能，是名菩薩大力具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Possessing great renown, spread throughout the world — endowed with courage, diligence, resolve, and sharpness, keeping the precepts thoroughly, and surpassing others in every craft and skill … this is called a bodhisattva's completeness of influence … by nature rarely ill, or entirely free of illness, possessing great capability — this is called a bodhisattva's completeness of strength.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩於諸眾生不加傷害，遠離一切傷害意樂，是名菩薩壽量具足因……惠施光明鮮淨衣物，是名菩薩形色具足因……於諸眾生捨離憍慢，是名菩薩族姓具足因……所言誠諦，亦不好習乖離、麤獷不相應語，是名菩薩信言具足因……攝持當來種種功德於自身中，發弘誓願、供養三寶及諸尊長，是名菩薩大勢具足因……於諸眾生以身供事……用能增長身心勢力餅、飯、糜等種種飲食施諸眾生，是名菩薩大力具足因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Never harming any being, entirely free of any wish to cause harm — this is the cause of long life … giving light and clean, fresh clothing — this is the cause of fine appearance … relinquishing pride toward every being — this is the cause of noble birth … truthful speech, never divisive or coarse — this is the cause of credibility … gathering merit for the future, raising the great vow, making offerings to the three treasures and one's elders — this is the cause of great influence … personally serving other beings' lawful undertakings … giving nourishing food that strengthens body and mind — this is the cause of great strength.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats influence as compounding out of ordinary practical virtues rather than charisma alone — genuine diligence, unshaken resolve, quick and capable action, careful deliberation, and thorough precept-keeping, together with real competence across the classical five fields of knowledge, naturally earn a reputation that spreads on its own. He passes briefly over the treatise's own note that a bodhisattva's rapport-with-others is tied, in this classical Indian framework, to a male body specifically — glossed as the culturally-conditioned assumption that a male rebirth carries fewer social obstacles and greater physical capacity in the world this text was composed within — noting the treatise's own two applications of this teaching: encouraging a woman who genuinely wishes to be reborn male toward that aspiration, and, more strikingly, a bodhisattva using skillful means to protect a man's virility when threatened by past unwholesome karma, or a man seeking to restore it, rather than treating the matter with indifference. He then walks through the causal pairing for the remaining marks, each traced to a specific virtue already practiced: not harming beings produces long life; giving light and fresh clothing produces fine appearance (illustrated with the simple image of a streetlamp lit in a dark alley, letting travelers pass safely without fear); relinquishing pride produces noble birth, since a person free of arrogance naturally draws others toward them; truthful, ungossiping, gentle speech produces credibility; accumulated merit and genuine devotion toward one's teachers produces influence; and simply showing up to personally help with others' lawful work, alongside providing genuinely nourishing food, produces physical strength and vigor. He stresses the thread running through all eight: nothing here is arbitrary reward — each worldly advantage traces to a specific, previously cultivated virtue, making the entire list less a catalogue of blessings than a precise causal ledger.

3. Three Conditions That Make These Causes Actually Grow, and What Each Ripening Mark Is Good For

Beyond the eight specific causes, three further conditions determine how much they actually grow — purity of mind, of practice, and of the field they're planted in — after which the treatise names exactly what each of the eight ripening marks is functionally good for, closing the entire karmic-ripening analysis.

Chinese Source

又此諸因略由三緣而得增長，能感圓滿增上廣大異熟令起……一、心清淨，二、加行清淨，三、田清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

These causes, in brief, grow through three further conditions, able to bring about a ripening that is complete, ever-increasing, and vast: first, purity of mind; second, purity of practice; third, purity of the field.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩壽量具足故，能於長時修習善品，依自他利積集增長無量善根，是名菩薩壽量具足果……若諸菩薩形色具足故，大眾愛樂；眾愛樂故，咸共歸仰……凡所發言無不聽用，是名菩薩形色具足果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of complete longevity, a bodhisattva is able to cultivate wholesome practices over a long span of time, accumulating boundless wholesome roots for the benefit of self and others alike — this is the fruit of complete longevity … because of fine appearance, the multitude delights in and takes refuge in such a bodhisattva; and because of this, whatever they say is universally heeded — this is the fruit of fine appearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the three growth-conditions as what separates a passing good deed from a cause that compounds into something vast: purity of mind is genuine, settled confidence in awakening itself, deepening through sustained reflection on the path (the treatise specifies a bodhisattva already resting in the first ground's own "purified superior intention," whose reliance no longer depends on external conditions at all); purity of practice is that same resolve carried out with sustained, careful, uninterrupted effort — never abandoning practice once begun, and actively encouraging others toward the same practice whether they've started it yet or not; and purity of the field is simply what results when both of the first two are genuinely present, since a mind this settled and a practice this sustained naturally becomes the kind of "field" worth planting further merit in, for oneself and others alike. He then closes the ripening section by naming each mark's actual function: long life gives simply the time needed for sustained cultivation; fine appearance draws people close enough to actually listen, so that a bodhisattva's teaching lands rather than being ignored — the remaining six marks (noble birth, authority, credibility, influence, rapport, and strength) each supplying, in their own way, the practical means to be heard, trusted, and followed once that initial audience is won. With the entire eightfold ripening analysis — its marks, its individual causes, its three conditions for growth, and its functions — now complete, the treatise turns next to merit's own cause and fruit.

4. Milestone: Power Without Skill Fails, Skill Without Power Fails — And Why a Bodhisattva's Wandering Is Never Wasted

The ripening fruits close the eightfold analysis, then the treatise sharpens its point: having the worldly capacity to help is not enough on its own, and neither is having the skill to guide others without the capacity to back it — both must be present together. From there follows a striking claim: unlike an ordinary being's aimless wandering through birth and death, a bodhisattva's own cycling through the same birth and death is never actually wasted.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩自有力能，不善安處所化有情，彼於自事不隨順作；如是於他所作利益不名熾盛、不名隨順……若諸菩薩自無力能，而善安處所化有情……不名能作他利益事……是故菩薩要具二事，方於有情所作利益名為熾盛、名為隨順。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a bodhisattva has the capacity but is not skilled at properly settling those to be guided, so that they do not follow through with what benefits them, then the benefit rendered to others cannot be called flourishing or genuinely realized … if a bodhisattva is skilled at properly settling those to be guided but lacks the capacity … this too cannot be called genuinely benefiting others … for this reason, a bodhisattva must possess both together before benefiting beings can be called flourishing and genuinely realized.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩安住八種異熟果中，能使有情利益安樂，是故一切有情所處空無義利無始生死，菩薩處之，能令不空，有大義利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas, abiding in these eight ripened fruits, are able to bring beings benefit and happiness — where ordinary beings' beginningless wandering through birth and death is empty, devoid of genuine meaning — a bodhisattva's own presence within that very cycle is never empty, but carries great and genuine meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two-failure-modes teaching as a corrective against two opposite but equally common mistakes: a wealthy, capable, well-connected bodhisattva who nonetheless lacks the skill in the four gathering methods (giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, cooperation) to actually settle a student in genuine practice ends up with resources but no real result to show for them; conversely, a struggling beginner-bodhisattva who has genuinely mastered the art of gathering others skillfully but lacks the worldly means — wealth, standing, influence — to actually follow through finds their good methods stall for lack of material or social backing. Neither capacity alone counts as genuine other-benefit; only their combination does. He then draws out what he considers the chapter's single most striking claim: every ordinary being's endless cycling through birth after birth accomplishes, strictly speaking, nothing at all — no benefit to self, no benefit to others, simply repetition without a genuine point — while a bodhisattva's own continued presence in that identical cycle of birth and death is never empty in this way, since even an ordinary-looking life event, for a bodhisattva, functions toward liberation for both self and others at once. The claim is not that a bodhisattva escapes saṃsāra faster, but that a bodhisattva's saṃsāra is a fundamentally different kind of thing to be in, precisely because of the ripening fruits and gathering skills just described working together.

5. Merit and Wisdom's Own Cause and Fruit — Sorting the Six Perfections into Three Clean Categories

Karmic ripening closed, the treatise turns to the second and third of its three pairs — merit and wisdom — sorting the six perfections with unusual precision: three belong purely to merit, one purely to wisdom, and two straddle both categories depending on how they are actually practiced.

Chinese Source

云何為福？云何為智？謂略說福，即是三種波羅蜜多。一、施波羅蜜多，二、戒波羅蜜多，三、忍波羅蜜多。智唯一種波羅蜜多。謂慧波羅蜜多。精進、靜慮波羅蜜多，應知通二分。一者、福分，二者、智分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is merit? What is wisdom? In brief, merit is three perfections: giving, precepts, and patience. Wisdom is only one perfection: the perfection of wisdom itself. Diligence and concentration, one should know, belong to both divisions — partly to merit, and partly to wisdom.

Chinese Source

若依精進修行布施、受護淨戒，及修慈等四種無量，如是等類所有精進，名為福分。若依精進習聞思修所成三慧，修蘊善巧、修界善巧、修處善巧、修緣起善巧、修處非處善巧，修能觀察苦為真苦、集為真集、滅為真滅、道為真道……如是等類所有精進，名為智分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Diligence exercised in practicing giving, upholding and guarding pure precepts, and cultivating loving-kindness and the other three immeasurables — this kind of diligence is called the portion belonging to merit. Diligence exercised in cultivating the wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and meditation, in skillfulness regarding the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, and valid reasoning, and in observing suffering as truly suffering, origin as truly origin, cessation as truly cessation, and the path as truly the path — this kind of diligence is called the portion belonging to wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this three-way division is one reasonable classification among several found across the broader literature — some texts treat the first five perfections collectively as merely preparatory ("blind" without wisdom to guide them), reserving genuine insight for wisdom alone; this treatise instead draws a cleaner internal line, assigning giving, precepts, and patience to merit outright, wisdom alone to wisdom, and splitting diligence and concentration by how each is actually exercised rather than by which perfection they belong to nominally. Diligence applied to giving, precept-keeping, or cultivating the four immeasurables (loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity) counts as merit's own diligence; diligence applied to study and reflection across the five topics of Buddhist analysis (the aggregates, sense-bases, elements, dependent origination, and valid-versus-invalid reasoning) or to direct contemplation of the four noble truths counts as wisdom's own diligence — the same single quality of sustained effort, sorted entirely by its actual object rather than by any fixed label. He notes the same logic will apply to concentration once the text takes it up: cultivating the four immeasurables in meditation belongs to merit, while resting in the three gates to liberation (emptiness, marklessness, and wishlessness) belongs to wisdom — a single meditative practice capable of serving either purpose depending on what it actually rests upon.

6. Milestone: Merit and Wisdom Complete — Merit Is Most Excellent, Wisdom Is Unsurpassed, Neither Alone Suffices

Closing the merit-and-wisdom analysis, the treatise names three causes that produce both, describes each one's distinct fruit, and delivers a carefully balanced final verdict: merit is the more excellent of the two, wisdom the more unsurpassed — yet lacking either one, however excellent the other, makes buddhahood entirely impossible.

Chinese Source

略說應知福因、智因總有三種。一者、於福於智，能得、能住、能增長欲；二者、於福於智，善能隨順無違背緣；三者、於福於智，先已串習。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, one should know there are, in total, three causes for both merit and wisdom: first, the wish to attain, sustain, and increase them; second, favorable conditions genuinely free of obstruction; third, prior habituation with them.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩依止福故，雖復長時流轉生死，不為極苦之所損惱；又隨所欲能攝眾生，為作義利……依止智故，所攝受福是正非邪；又能起作種種無量善巧事業，乃至究竟當證無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on merit, bodhisattvas, though cycling through birth and death for a long time, are never afflicted by the most extreme suffering, and are able, as they wish, to gather beings and accomplish genuine benefit for them … resting on wisdom, the merit thereby gathered is correct rather than deviant, and one is able to undertake boundless skillful works, ultimately realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Chinese Source

應知此中若異熟體、若異熟因、若異熟果，如是一切皆依於福，從福所生；福復依智，從智所起……是故二種，於證無上正等菩提雖俱是勝；而於其中福為最勝，智為無上。若諸菩薩於福、於智隨闕一種，決定不能證於無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that the ripened body, its cause, and its fruit — all of this rests upon merit, arising from merit; and merit in turn rests upon wisdom, arising from wisdom … therefore, though both are excellent for realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening, merit is the more excellent of the two, and wisdom the more unsurpassed. Should a bodhisattva lack either merit or wisdom, awakening can never, under any circumstance, be realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three causes as simple but genuinely necessary: sheer wish (illustrated by the Buddha's own famous reply to a blind monk he personally helped thread a needle — asked why a fully awakened being would still bother with such a small merit, the Buddha answered that his own hunger for merit was never actually satisfied, a standard ordinary practitioners, whose merit is still thin, should take seriously rather than dismiss as false modesty); genuinely favorable, unobstructed conditions (good company and correct understanding rather than a misguided teacher insisting merit alone suffices, or wisdom alone suffices, when both are in fact required); and sustained habituation, since repeated practice is what actually compounds a small beginning into something vast. He then draws out the two fruits' distinct character: merit functions as a kind of buffer, ensuring that even a bodhisattva's rare missteps in the cycle of birth and death never result in truly extreme suffering, while also supplying the actual means (already covered at length as the eight marks of ripening) to gather and benefit others; wisdom functions as a rudder, ensuring the merit accumulated is genuinely well-directed rather than squandered on indulgence, and opening the door to boundless further skillful undertaking all the way to awakening itself. He lingers on the treatise's own carefully hedged final verdict — merit called "most excellent," wisdom called "unsurpassed" — reading this not as declaring one secretly superior to the other but as praising each in the register where it genuinely excels: merit as the more abundant, generative quality (like a broad, fertile field), wisdom as the rarer, decisive one (like the single seed that determines what actually grows there) — and stresses the treatise's unambiguous bottom line that follows: lacking either one, however excellent the other, closes off awakening entirely. **This closes the entire "three causes, three fruits" framework (karmic ripening, merit, and wisdom) — and with it, the fourth of self-and-other-benefit's ten categories.**

7. Milestone: This-Life and Next-Life Benefit — Four Things a Bodhisattva Rightly Enjoys in the Present

Self-and-other-benefit's fifth category turns to time itself, distinguishing benefit realized in this very life from benefit deferred to a future one. This-life self-benefit opens with four legitimate things a bodhisattva may enjoy now: rightfully earned wealth properly used, the fruit of past good karma, dhyāna's own restful bliss, and the path to nirvāṇa itself.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩以如正理工巧業處士夫作用積集財物，即於如是所集財物，知量受用……既積集已，不染、不住、不耽、不縛、不悶、不著，亦不堅執，深見過患，了知出離而受用之，是名知量受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas accumulate wealth through lawful skill and honest human effort, and, having accumulated it, make use of it with a sense of proportion … once accumulated, they are not defiled by it, do not dwell on it, do not crave it, are not bound by it, do not grieve over it, are not attached to it, and do not clutch it tightly — seeing deeply into its dangers, and using it with a clear understanding oriented toward renunciation. This is called using it with a sense of proportion.

Chinese Source

又先所造可愛果業異熟果熟，於現法中受用彼果……又諸菩薩於諸靜慮善迴轉者，為欲獲得現法樂住，於現法中依此靜慮；不為成立利他事故，依此靜慮……又諸如來現法涅槃，所有世間及出世間一切能得現法涅槃諸有為法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Also, when the karmic fruit of previously created desirable actions ripens, one enjoys that fruit in this very life … also, bodhisattvas already skilled in freely entering and leaving the dhyānas may, wishing simply to obtain the bliss of dwelling in the present dhyāna itself, rest in that dhyāna for its own sake, not in order to accomplish some benefit for others … also, the Tathāgatas' own nirvāṇa realized in this very life rests on every mundane and supramundane conditioned factor capable of bringing about that present-life nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this fourfold list as the treatise's own permission slip against an overly severe misreading of renunciation: a bodhisattva may legitimately earn a living through honest skill, enjoy the fruit of past good karma as it ripens, and even rest in dhyāna's own bliss purely for personal refreshment rather than constantly performing usefulness to others — provided wealth especially is used with genuine proportion, the sevenfold list of non-attachments (not defiled, not dwelt on, not craved, not bound, not grieved over, not clutched, not attached) describing wealth handled the way one might handle a venomous but occasionally useful substance, aware of its danger the entire time it is being used well. He notes the specific point about dhyāna deserves attention, since it might seem to contradict the resolve chapter's earlier insistence that a bodhisattva never clings to meditative bliss at the expense of helping others — the resolution is that occasional, proportionate rest in dhyāna's own ease is legitimate self-benefit, exactly parallel to earning and properly enjoying wealth, so long as it does not calcify into the settled avoidance already flagged as a genuine fault. The fourth item, attaining nirvāṇa itself in this very life, closes the list at its highest point: every mundane cause (dhyāna itself) and supramundane cause (the noble path) that together deliver a fully liberated life, still embodied, in the present — the ultimate form this-life self-benefit can actually take.

8. Milestone: The Self-and-Other-Benefit Chapter Closes — What Actually Makes Benefit Final Rather Than Temporary

The chapter's remaining categories close swiftly: next-life benefit mirrors this-life benefit exactly, and both together complete the time-based analysis. The chapter's true climax, though, is its final distinction — between genuinely final benefit and benefit that only looks final — settled by three precise tests: whether it touches reality's own unchanging nature, whether it can ever regress, and whether its fruit ever runs out.

Chinese Source

謂於欲界財寶具足、自體具足若因若果，及諸異生世間清淨若因若果，是不畢竟自利利他……若諸煩惱一切永斷，若諸所有八支聖道，若此為依獲得一切世間善法，是名畢竟自利利他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wealth and bodily endowment within the desire realm, whether as cause or fruit, and the mundane purification of an ordinary being, whether as cause or fruit — these are not genuinely final self-and-other-benefit … the complete and permanent severance of every affliction, the noble eightfold path itself, and every mundane wholesome quality resting upon that path — this is called genuinely final self-and-other-benefit.

Chinese Source

由三因緣，應知畢竟及不畢竟。一、由自性故；二、由退不退故；三、由受用果有盡無盡故……究竟涅槃，名為畢竟；一切有為，名不畢竟……八支聖道無有退故，及受用果無有盡故，名為畢竟；其餘一切善有漏法，由有退故，及受用果有終盡故，名不畢竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

By three causes and conditions, one should know what is genuinely final and what is not: first, by its own nature; second, by whether it can regress or not; third, by whether its enjoyed fruit is exhaustible or not … ultimate nirvāṇa is called genuinely final; every conditioned thing is called not genuinely final … the noble eightfold path never regresses, and its enjoyed fruit is inexhaustible, so it is called genuinely final; every other wholesome but contaminated quality can regress, and its enjoyed fruit eventually runs out, so it is called not genuinely final.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing distinction as the entire self-and-other-benefit chapter's real payoff: wealth properly earned, a good rebirth, even genuine dhyāna attainment are all worth cultivating, and the treatise has spent the whole chapter carefully legitimizing them — but none of them, by their own nature, ever escape being conditioned things, subject to the same three tests that expose their limits. By nature, only nirvāṇa itself touches reality's own unchanging suchness; everything else, however excellent, remains conditioned. By regression, ordinary wholesome deeds and even the contaminated dhyānas can still be lost — a meditator can fall from an attainment, wealth can be squandered, a good rebirth eventually ends — while the noble eightfold path, once genuinely walked, never reverts to its starting point. By exhaustibility, worldly fruit is inherently finite — a fortune is spent, a heavenly rebirth's lifespan eventually runs out — while the path's own fruit, resting on unconditioned reality, keeps compounding without limit. He stresses this is not a devaluation of everything covered earlier in the chapter, only an honest ranking: a bodhisattva should absolutely pursue right livelihood, good karma, and the rest, while never mistaking any of it for the genuinely final benefit only the path itself delivers. **With this three-part test, the entire self-and-other-benefit chapter (自他利品) — all ten of its categories, running from fascicle 35 through the first third of fascicle 36 — is now complete.**

9. Milestone: The Chapter on True Meaning Opens — Two Kinds of Truth Beneath Everything

Self-and-other-benefit complete, the treatise opens the second of the factors of awakening's seven fields — true meaning — with the foundational distinction the rest of the chapter builds on: reality's own unchanging nature (suchness itself), and the full, exhaustive content of every phenomenon within it.

Chinese Source

云何真實義？謂略有二種。一者、依如所有性，諸法真實性。二者、依盡所有性，諸法一切性。如是諸法真實性、一切性，應知總名真實義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is true meaning? In brief, there are two kinds: first, resting on suchness itself, the true nature of all phenomena; second, resting on exhaustive content, the entirety of all phenomena. This true nature and this entirety together are called, in sum, true meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces this pairing as the conceptual foundation the rest of the true-meaning chapter will build on, and flags it as genuinely more difficult terrain than anything covered so far — the treatise's own core Yogâcāra epistemology. Suchness itself (如所有性) names reality's own unchanging nature — emptiness, the way things actually are beneath every conceptual overlay — accessible only to a mind that has directly, non-conceptually realized it, never through ordinary language or thought, since suchness is by definition beyond both; a hearer realizes only the emptiness of a personal self, while a bodhisattva realizes the emptiness of phenomena themselves as well, a distinction that will structure everything from here through the treatise's account of the ten bodhisattva grounds. Exhaustive content (盡所有性), by contrast, names the complete categorial map of everything that exists — the five aggregates, twelve sense-bases, eighteen elements, and every other classificatory scheme the earlier fascicles have already used to analyze experience — not reality's ultimate nature, but reality's full inventory, so to speak. Master Cháng Bǎi cites Venerable Master Tài Xū's own compact formula: the wisdom that penetrates suchness is called "principled wisdom," while the wisdom that maps exhaustive content is called "quantifying wisdom" — two complementary kinds of insight a bodhisattva must cultivate together, neither one alone sufficient, since ultimate nature without knowing its full expression would be incomplete, and a complete inventory without grasping its underlying nature would stay merely descriptive.

10. Milestone: Seven Kinds of Suchness — Reading Reality's Unchanging Nature Through the Four Noble Truths

Suchness itself is unpacked into seven distinct angles of approach — three examining ordinary existence (its flux, its selflessness, its dependence on mind alone) and four mapping directly onto the four noble truths — together showing that reality's single unchanging nature can be reached from any starting point in experience.

Chinese Source

流轉真如，謂一切行無先後性……相真如，指一切法補特伽羅無我性及法無我性……了別真如，謂一切行唯是識性……安立真如，謂我所說諸苦聖諦……邪行真如，謂我所說諸集聖諦……清淨真如，謂我所說諸滅聖諦……正行真如，謂我所說諸道聖諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The suchness of flux: that all conditioned processes have no fixed prior-or-later nature. The suchness of characteristics: the selflessness of the person and the selflessness of phenomena in every dharma. The suchness of cognition: that all conditioned processes are nothing but consciousness itself. The suchness of what is established: what I have taught as the noble truth of suffering. The suchness of erring conduct: what I have taught as the noble truth of origin. The suchness of purification: what I have taught as the noble truth of cessation. The suchness of right conduct: what I have taught as the noble truth of the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all seven as seven different doorways onto a single underlying reality, drawn from the *Discourse Explaining the Profound Secrets*. The suchness of flux observes that every conditioned process — the ceaseless arising and ceasing already established at length back in fascicle 34's account of momentariness — has, ultimately, no fixed "earlier" or "later" moment to be found anywhere in it, cause and effect arising as a single unbroken continuity rather than as genuinely separate, sequential events; seen this way, the raw fact of change itself already points toward suchness, defusing both the view that things arise from nothing and the view that cause and effect are simply unrelated. The suchness of characteristics is the selflessness already familiar from the hearer's path (no fixed self in the person) now joined with the bodhisattva's further realization (no fixed, independent nature in phenomena themselves either) — the two selflessnesses together. The suchness of cognition is the mind-only teaching already introduced in fascicle 34: every experienced object, examined closely enough, is never actually apart from the consciousness experiencing it. The remaining four map suchness directly onto the four noble truths already explored at length in fascicle 34's account of the supramundane path: the suchness of what is established is simply suffering itself, correctly seen; the suchness of erring conduct is craving, the actual cause producing that suffering; the suchness of purification is cessation, suffering's genuine extinguishing; and the suchness of right conduct is the path itself, the thirty-seven factors of awakening that lead there. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the elegant symmetry this reveals: whether approached through ordinary change, through the two selflessnesses, through mind-only, or through the four truths already mastered on the supramundane path, every route arrives at the identical, single suchness — reality has only one true nature, however many doors happen to open onto it.

11. Four Depths of Truth: What the World Agrees On, and What Reason Actually Proves

Beyond suchness and exhaustive content, the treatise names four progressively deeper senses of "true meaning" — starting with what the ordinary world simply agrees on without ever examining, and what careful reasoning, resting on three valid sources of knowledge, can actually establish.

Chinese Source

謂地唯是地，非是火等……以要言之，此即如此，非不如此；是即如是，非不如是。決定勝解所行境事，一切世間從其本際展轉傳來，想自分別共所成立，不由思惟、籌量、觀察，然後方取。是名世間極成真實。

Ru-Yi Translation

Earth is simply earth, not fire, and so on … in short: this is simply this, not otherwise; that is simply that, not otherwise — the object of a firm, settled conviction, handed down since beginningless time throughout the world, established jointly through ordinary conceptual habit, grasped without any prior deliberation, weighing, or examination. This is called world-established truth.

Chinese Source

謂諸智者有道理義……依止現、比及至教量，極善思擇決定智所行、所知事，由證成道理所建立、所施設義。是名道理極成真實。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wise possess reasoned understanding … resting on direct perception, inference, and scriptural authority, they most excellently investigate and settle the object of understanding through the principle of valid demonstration, establishing and articulating its meaning. This is called reason-established truth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats world-established truth as the shallowest and least examined layer — the instant, unreflective certainty that earth is earth, pleasure is pleasure, a house is not a table, inherited purely through a lifetime of unexamined linguistic habit rather than any actual investigation; useful for getting through an ordinary day, but resting on nothing deeper than convention repeated since beginningless time. Reason-established truth goes one level deeper, belonging specifically to eight kinds of naturally sharp or trained thinkers (those with keen native senses, sharp intellect, careful deliberate reasoners, skilled debaters, and philosophically inclined observers among ordinary unenlightened people) who actually examine their conclusions rather than merely inheriting them, resting on three valid means of knowledge: direct perception (what is presently, actually observed), inference (reasoning correctly from the observed to the unobserved, past or future), and scriptural authority (trusting a fully reliable teacher's testimony, transmitted faithfully). He notes this reasoned understanding genuinely improves on world-established truth — a philosopher who investigates impermanence through careful observation and logical inference reaches real insight ordinary unreflective certainty never touches — yet it remains, crucially, one level short of a noble one's own direct realization, since even the sharpest ordinary reasoning still operates through concepts and inference rather than the unmediated seeing the next two depths of truth will describe.

12. Milestone: The Hearer's Purified Truth — Direct Realization of No-Self Through the Four Noble Truths

The third depth belongs only to those who have genuinely walked the path already detailed at length in fascicle 34: hearers and solitary buddhas whose direct, non-conceptual realization of the four noble truths has actually purified the afflictive obstruction, seeing for themselves that only the aggregates exist, and that no self can be found within or apart from them.

Chinese Source

謂一切聲聞、獨覺，若無漏智、若能引無漏智、若無漏後得世間智所行境界，是名煩惱障淨智所行真實。由緣此為境，從煩惱障智得清淨，於當來世無障礙住，是故說名煩惱障淨智所行真實。

Ru-Yi Translation

The object experienced by every hearer's and solitary buddha's uncontaminated wisdom, the wisdom that leads toward it, and the uncontaminated wisdom attained afterward — this is called the truth belonging to purification from the afflictive obstruction. By taking this as their object, they become purified from the afflictive obstruction, and abide without obstruction in every future life — for this reason, it is called the truth belonging to purification from the afflictive obstruction.

Chinese Source

此諦現觀，聲聞、獨覺能觀唯有諸蘊可得，除諸蘊外我不可得。數習緣生諸行生滅相應慧故，數習異蘊補特伽羅無性見故，發生如是聖諦現觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through direct realization of this truth, hearers and solitary buddhas are able to observe that only the aggregates are to be found, with no self to be found apart from them. Through repeated cultivation of the wisdom that corresponds to conditioned processes' own arising and ceasing, and through repeated cultivation of the insight that no self exists distinct from the aggregates, this direct realization of the noble truths comes about.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this entire section as a direct callback to fascicle 34's supramundane path, now reframed as the third of four depths of truth rather than a standalone teaching: the same three-stage wisdom (the preparatory wisdom leading toward realization, the moment of direct, non-conceptual realization itself, and the conceptual wisdom that follows it) delivers the identical insight already covered at length — that searching the five aggregates for a self, whether within them or apart from them, turns up nothing, and that this insight, once genuinely realized rather than merely reasoned toward, actually purifies the afflictions that bind a being to suffering. He notes the crucial limitation the treatise flags here for the first time: this depth of truth, however genuine, still belongs only to hearers and solitary buddhas — it purifies the afflictive obstruction (attachment to a personal self) but leaves entirely untouched a subtler obstruction to complete omniscience, the cognitive obstruction, which clings not to a self but to phenomena themselves as somehow fixed and real — precisely the boundary the treatise's fourth and final depth, reserved for bodhisattvas and buddhas alone, will now cross.

13. Milestone: The Bodhisattva's Purified Truth — Realizing That Phenomena Themselves Have No Fixed Nature

The fourth and deepest truth belongs only to bodhisattvas and buddhas: realization not merely that no self exists within the aggregates, but that phenomena themselves have no fixed nature at all — neither straightforwardly existent as ordinary language describes them, nor simply nonexistent, but something genuinely beyond both extremes.

Chinese Source

謂於所知能礙智故，名所知障。從所知障得解脫智所行境界，當知是名所知障淨智所行真實。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever obstructs wisdom with respect to what is known is called the cognitive obstruction. The object experienced by the wisdom that has become liberated from this cognitive obstruction should be known as the truth belonging to purification from the cognitive obstruction.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩諸佛世尊入法無我，入已善淨，於一切法離言自性、假說自性平等平等無分別智所行境界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas and buddhas enter into the selflessness of phenomena, and, having entered, become thoroughly purified — this is the object of the non-discriminating wisdom that regards every phenomenon's inexpressible nature and its merely designated nature as utterly equal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the cognitive obstruction as a far subtler trap than the afflictive obstruction already purified at the previous depth: not craving or hatred, but the simple, near-universal reflex of taking whatever a name refers to as thereby real — seeing a wall and unreflectively treating "wall" as picking out something genuinely, independently solid, when a being with different karma (the treatise's own example is a spirit for whom the identical wall appears as nothing more than a low mound of earth) would experience the same location entirely differently. This obstruction operates even in someone who has already fully seen through the illusion of a personal self, since it attaches not to "I" but to phenomena themselves — the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases already used throughout the earlier fascicles as objects of analysis, still quietly treated as fixed, mind-independent realities rather than genuinely questioned. Bodhisattvas, beginning at the first ground, and buddhas at complete fulfillment, realize instead that every phenomenon is neither straightforwardly existent the way its ordinary name suggests, nor simply nonexistent as if nothing were there at all — its inexpressible nature (what it actually is, beyond any name) and its merely designated nature (what convention calls it) are seen as "equally equal," neither one privileged over the other, precisely the same non-dual insight the *Diamond Sūtra*'s "all marks are illusory" already pointed toward back in fascicle 34. He stresses why this fourth depth alone reaches full liberation the third could not: a hearer who has purified only the afflictive obstruction can still, in principle, be misled by phenomena's apparent fixity in subtler ways omniscience would need to see through — only realizing phenomena's own selflessness, in addition to the person's, closes that final gap.

16. Milestone: Truth's Outer Limit — Why the Bodhisattva's Purified Truth Admits No Further Refinement

Having named the object of this fourth and deepest truth, the treatise now states, in the strongest terms it has used for any of the four depths, that reflection on the true teaching can go no further than this: this is the foremost object, suchness unsurpassed, and the very outer limit of what can be known.

Chinese Source

如是境界為最第一、真如無上、所知邊際，齊此一切正法思擇皆悉退還，不能越度。

Ru-Yi Translation

This object is the very foremost; it is suchness unsurpassed, the outer limit of the knowable. Up to this point — and no further — does all reflection on the true teaching turn back; it cannot pass beyond.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first clarifies, with a homely illustration, exactly what "phenomena's selflessness" (法無我) means, since the whole claim to being truth's outer limit rests on it. Take an eye: it is really nothing but an assembly of eyelid, eyeball, and other parts — remove them and no separate "eye" is left over. A house is the same: nothing but window, door, and wall standing together; no single part is the house, and if you go looking for "the house itself" apart from its parts, you will never find it. A car, likewise, is nothing without its hood, doors, and windows. Ordinary beings take a mere assembly, give it one name, and then treat the name as if it picked out something genuinely, independently real — that is exactly the imputed, "designated" nature that has no true existence of its own. But this does not mean the house or car is simply nothing at all: through causes and conditions an illusion-like house or car really does appear and function, and to deny that entirely would be nihilism, a denial of cause and effect itself. Neither straightforwardly existent as its name suggests, nor simply nonexistent — this "neither" is precisely the selflessness of phenomena that only this fourth truth reaches.

He then explains why this earns the titles "most supreme" and "truth's outer limit": a hearer or solitary buddha realizes only that no self can be found within or apart from the five aggregates — the selflessness of the person. A bodhisattva or buddha realizes, in addition, that the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases themselves have no findable, independent nature at all — the selflessness of phenomena, which necessarily includes and surpasses the selflessness of the person already covered by the third truth. Since no examination of the true teaching can uncover anything beyond phenomena's own selflessness, this is, quite literally, as far as reflection on reality can go — hence "truth's outer limit," where all further philosophical inquiry simply turns back rather than finding anything more to discover.

17. Milestone: Neither "Existence" Nor "Non-Existence" — The Two Extremes True Reality Must Reject

Having identified the object of the bodhisattva's purified truth, the treatise turns to how it is actually grasped, opening this new topic with the single framework that will structure everything that follows: true reality is revealed only by rejecting two opposite errors, named simply "existence" and "non-existence."

Chinese Source

又安立此真實義相，當知即是無二所顯。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, when this mark of true reality is established in words, it should be understood as precisely what is revealed by the non-dual — by neither of two extremes.

Chinese Source

所言二者，謂有、非有。

Ru-Yi Translation

The "two" spoken of here are: existence, and non-existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that true reality itself is beyond all language — it cannot be spoken or shown — yet buddhas and bodhisattvas, out of compassion, still speak of it so that others can come to the same realization. Words can only do this in one of two ways: by assertion, affirming something positively, or by negation, denying something. Because affirming any positive description would immediately invite a new attachment to that description, the treatise here uses negation only — hence "the non-dual," neither "existence" nor "non-existence," rather than any positive formula.

He situates this within the broader history of how Buddhist thought has approached emptiness. The Ābhidharmikas divided what exists into worldly-existence (ordinary language), provisional-existence (concepts formed through a perceiver's own experience), substance-existence (the seventy-five real underlying constituents, form and mind alike), and ultimate-existence (for them, chiefly the emptiness of a personal self, without extending emptiness to phenomena themselves). The Madhyamaka teaches instead that whatever arises dependently is, in that very arising, empty of any fixed nature — dependent origination and emptiness are inseparable, neither collapsing into nihilism nor smuggling back a hidden substance. Yin Shun's own study of this tradition distinguishes three kinds of "provisional": nominal (a mere word, like "plum," with no actual plum appearing when spoken), apprehended (grasping an aggregate of factors as a unity, as when one's own five aggregates give rise to the sense of "I"), and constituent (roughly the Ābhidharmikas' substance-existence, which Madhyamaka itself declines to accept as ultimately real). Yogācāra masters such as Asaṅga then articulated the three natures — imputed, dependent, and perfected — teaching that the dependent nature (mind-only, arising through causes and conditions) is illusion-like but not nonexistent, that the imputed nature (the fixed identity language seems to promise) simply does not exist, and that seeing the dependent nature free of imputation just is the perfected nature. Whichever school's vocabulary is used, Master Cháng Bǎi observes, all are aiming at the identical target: neither adding a false fixed identity to what merely arises dependently, nor denying that the dependent arising itself occurs at all.

18. "Existence" Analyzed — The Imputed Nature That Is the Root of All Conceptual Proliferation

The treatise now defines the first of the two extremes in its own words: "existence" refers specifically to the imputed nature established through language — and it identifies this as nothing less than the root of every conceptual elaboration the world has ever produced.

Chinese Source

此中有者，謂所安立假說自性，

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, "existence" refers to the imputed nature that has been established in language.

Chinese Source

即是世間長時所執，亦是世間一切分別戲論根本。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is precisely what the world has clung to since beginningless time, and it is the very root of all the world's discriminative conceptual proliferation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that this imputed nature underlies both worldly truth and the conventional truth Buddhist teaching itself must use — form's obstructive quality, the various aggregates and their functions, or on the other side unconditioned states, nirvāṇa, the stages of a noble path, all still spoken of in names. The treatise then lists, in a long enumeration, the sheer range of what counts as this imputed "existence": the five aggregates and the six sense faculties; the four great elements and the six sense objects; the wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral; arising and ceasing; dependent origination; past, present, and future; the conditioned and the unconditioned; this life and another life; sun and moon; whatever is seen, heard, sensed, known, sought, or attained — right down to nirvāṇa itself. Every one of these, however refined or however ordinary, is still a name resting on the deliberation and reflection (尋伺) that produces language in the first place.

The crucial point he draws out is that an ordinary worldly person and a sincere Buddhist practitioner cling to imputed existence in parallel ways, not just one of them: the unenlightened person clings, without examination, to the conventional truth of everyday language as if it were simply real; the person who has studied the Dharma clings additionally to the further concept of "ultimate truth" itself, believing the correct doctrinal description of emptiness to be the same as directly realizing it. Both remain within conceptual elaboration until direct realization actually occurs — the Buddhist student's clinging is a genuine step forward, since it can eventually lead to realization the ordinary person's clinging never approaches, but it is still, for now, imputed existence rather than the reality it points toward.

19. "Non-Existence" Analyzed — The Opposite Extreme of Denying Even Dependent Origination Itself

Having named the first extreme, the treatise turns to its opposite: a subtler error made by those who, having seen through the emptiness of imputed names, swing too far and deny that the dependently arisen things those names rest on exist at all — even denying nirvāṇa itself in the process.

Chinese Source

言非有者，謂即諸色假說自性，乃至涅槃假說自性，無事無相，假說所依，一切都無。

Ru-Yi Translation

"Non-existence" means this: with respect to the imputed nature of form, and so on up to the imputed nature of nirvāṇa, it is asserted that there is no substance and no characteristic whatsoever — that even what the imputation depends on does not exist at all.

Chinese Source

假立言說依彼轉者皆無所有，是名非有。

Ru-Yi Translation

That on the basis of which imputed language actually operates is said to be utterly nonexistent — this is called "non-existence."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to distinguish this from the correct teaching it superficially resembles. It is entirely correct to say that the NAME "teacup" does not pick out any fixed, independently existing thing — that much is simply the emptiness of imputed nature already covered. The error of "non-existence" goes one step further and denies that the dependently-arisen event underlying the name exists in any sense at all — as if there were not even an illusion-like teacup appearing through causes and conditions, mind, and perception. The treatise's own commentary specifies the target precisely: it is the perceiving mind's own act of grasping an image (想) that this view wrongly declares nonexistent, when in fact language is only ever possible because that image-grasping already occurred first.

He stresses why this is a genuine error and not merely an excess of caution: the dependently-arisen thing — the illusion-like cup, the illusion-like body, the illusion-like path of practice — really does function, produce effects, and support liberation, precisely because it is not the same as pure nothingness. To deny it altogether is to fall into severance-emptiness (斷滅空), a nihilism that denies cause and effect themselves, and if pressed to its logical end would mean denying that nirvāṇa itself is ever actually attained. Between the two extremes now named — over-affirming a fixed identity where there is none, and over-denying an illusion-like arising where there truly is one — the treatise is building toward the single correct middle position it will name next.

20. Milestone: "Neither" — The Middle Way That Resolves Both Extremes at Once

With both errors named, the treatise now states its actual position: true reality is reached only by abandoning existence and non-existence together, arriving at what is called the non-dual, the middle way, and the unsurpassed.

Chinese Source

先所說有，今說非有，有及非有二俱遠離，法相所攝真實性事，是名無二。

Ru-Yi Translation

What was earlier called "existence," and what is now called "non-existence" — abandoning both existence and non-existence together, the true reality that belongs to the nature of phenomena is called the non-dual.

Chinese Source

由無二故，說名中道，遠離二邊，亦名無上。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because it is non-dual, it is called the middle way; because it departs from both extremes, it is also called unsurpassed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the two extremes onto the two classical errors of superimposition and denial: "existence" superimposes a fixed, name-matching identity onto something that has none — like mistaking a coiled rope for a snake, adding something that was never there. "Non-existence" denies the illusion-like dependently-arisen thing that genuinely is there — like insisting a rope isn't even a rope-shaped object at all. What remains once both errors are removed is simply the dependent nature seen clearly, without either superimposition or denial — which just is the perfected nature, the true reality that "belongs to the nature of phenomena" (法相所攝真實性事) named in the root verse. Because it rejects both extremes equally rather than merely correcting one with a subtler version of the same error, it is not one view positioned somewhere between two others, but literally beyond the entire dimension on which "existence" and "non-existence" are opposed — hence "unsurpassed," since nothing can go further, and "the middle way," since it leans toward neither side. He notes the resemblance to the Tiantai school's single mind contemplating emptiness, the provisional, and the middle all at once — different vocabularies converging on the same refusal to settle into either extreme.

21. Buddha and Bodhisattva's Differing Degree of Purity in This Same Wisdom

Closing the account of true reality's mark, the treatise draws one final distinction: buddhas and bodhisattvas both realize this same non-dual wisdom, yet only a buddha's realization of it is fully, completely purified.

Chinese Source

佛世尊智於此真實已善清淨，諸菩薩智於此真實學道所顯。

Ru-Yi Translation

The wisdom of the buddhas, the world-honored ones, has become thoroughly purified with respect to this truth. The wisdom of bodhisattvas, with respect to this same truth, is what is revealed by wisdom still in training.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that both a buddha and a bodhisattva from the first ground onward have genuinely attained this same non-discriminating wisdom realizing phenomena's selflessness — the difference between them is entirely one of degree, not of kind. A buddha's wisdom has already exhausted every trace of subtle obstruction and needs nothing further — it is "wisdom beyond training." A bodhisattva's wisdom, however genuine and however far beyond an ordinary being's, still has subtle ignorance not yet exhausted, and so remains "wisdom in training," continuing to deepen through the grounds until it, too, reaches full purification. This closes the treatise's account of true reality's own mark (真實義相); what follows next turns to the wisdom that actually corresponds to this object (境相應慧), opening with the wisdom of resolute conviction (勝解方便) that serves as the bodhisattva's own expedient means toward unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

22. Milestone: Correct View of Birth-and-Death — Neither the Ordinary Being's Craving Nor the Hearer's Aversion

The wisdom that actually corresponds to true reality (境相應慧) now opens with its first expedient: resolute conviction in emptiness (勝解方便), examined first through how a bodhisattva regards birth-and-death itself — neither clinging to it like an ordinary being, nor fleeing from it like a hearer.

Chinese Source

以諸菩薩處於生死，彼彼生中修空勝解，善能成熟一切佛法及諸有情。又能如實了知生死，不於生死以無常等行深心厭離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas dwell within birth-and-death, in each and every life they cultivate resolute conviction in emptiness, thereby becoming able to bring both the buddha-dharma and sentient beings to full maturity. They are also able to truly understand birth-and-death as it actually is, without developing, through contemplating impermanence and the like, a deep-seated aversion toward birth-and-death itself.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩不能如實了知生死，則不能於貪瞋癡等一切煩惱深心棄捨；不能棄捨諸煩惱故，便雜染心受諸生死；由雜染心受生死故，不能成熟一切佛法及諸有情。若諸菩薩於其生死以無常等行深心厭離，是則速疾入般涅槃；彼若速疾入般涅槃，尚不能成熟一切佛法及諸有情，況能證無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas cannot truly understand birth-and-death, they cannot deeply abandon greed, hatred, delusion, and all other afflictions; unable to abandon these afflictions, they undergo birth-and-death with a defiled mind; and undergoing birth-and-death with a defiled mind in this way, they cannot bring the buddha-dharma or sentient beings to maturity. But if bodhisattvas develop, through contemplating impermanence and the like, a deep-seated aversion toward birth-and-death, they will swiftly enter final nirvāṇa — and having swiftly entered final nirvāṇa, they will be unable even to bring the buddha-dharma and sentient beings to maturity, let alone realize unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames "resolute conviction in emptiness" (空勝解) as close, repeated familiarization with reality's true nature — suchness, inexpressible in itself — cultivated life after life precisely because a bodhisattva vows to remain within saṃsāra to liberate beings rather than exit at the first opportunity. He explains that this correct view of birth-and-death is defined against two opposite failures, one belonging to ordinary beings and one to hearers. An ordinary being simply loves birth-and-death and never questions it. A hearer, contemplating suffering, impermanence, emptiness, and no-self, comes to view the three realms as a prison and birth-and-death as an enemy, developing precisely the "deep-seated aversion" this passage warns against. The bodhisattva takes neither path: lacking emptiness-conviction entirely, a bodhisattva cannot let go of greed, hatred, and delusion, and so cycles through birth-and-death with a mind still genuinely defiled — accomplishing nothing for self or others, the wandering wasted. But over-correcting into a hearer's deep aversion to impermanence and suffering carries its own cost: it leads to swiftly entering nirvāṇa before the bodhisattva path's three-incalculable-eon accumulation of merit and wisdom is anywhere near complete, foreclosing buddhahood just as surely as unchecked craving does. Both errors, from opposite directions, cut the bodhisattva path short.

23. Milestone: Correct View of Nirvāṇa — Neither Feared Nor Overly Craved, the Great Expedient Toward Buddhahood

The same resolute conviction in emptiness governs how a bodhisattva regards nirvāṇa itself: neither dreading it as ordinary beings do, nor longing for it so strongly that a hearer's premature exit is repeated on the far side of the equation.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，由習如是空勝解故，則於涅槃不深怖畏，亦於涅槃不多願樂。若諸菩薩深怖涅槃，即便於彼涅槃資糧不能圓滿……若諸菩薩於其涅槃多住願樂，是則速疾入般涅槃；彼若速疾入般涅槃，則便不能成熟佛法及諸有情。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, because bodhisattvas have cultivated this resolute conviction in emptiness, they neither deeply dread nirvāṇa nor excessively long for it. If bodhisattvas deeply dread nirvāṇa, they will be unable to bring the requisites for realizing it to fulfillment ... and if bodhisattvas dwell excessively in longing for nirvāṇa, they will swiftly enter final nirvāṇa — and having swiftly entered final nirvāṇa, they will be unable to bring the buddha-dharma and sentient beings to maturity.

Chinese Source

是大方便，依止最勝空性勝解；是故菩薩修習學道所攝最勝空性勝解，名為能證如來妙智廣大方便。

Ru-Yi Translation

This great expedient means rests on the most supreme resolute conviction in emptiness; therefore a bodhisattva's cultivation, within the scope of the path still in training, of this most supreme conviction in emptiness is called the great expedient means for realizing the Tathāgata's wondrous wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own careful qualification: a bodhisattva is not without any wish at all for nirvāṇa — that would deny the genuine merit of the unconditioned — but does not dwell heavily in that wish, since the bodhisattva chooses to remain among suffering beings rather than withdraw. Nirvāṇa itself, he explains via the treatise's four kinds of stillness (the stilling of suffering, the stilling of affliction — together called nirvāṇa's "benefits," since they simply remove what is wrong — and the stilling of harming others, the stilling into supreme equanimity — called nirvāṇa's "virtues," since they actively correct ordinary faults), inspires in a bodhisattva of genuine pure conviction a visible reverence — the classical image given is that the hairs of the body stand on end and tears fall, simply on hearing nirvāṇa described. Fearing nirvāṇa outright, as ordinary beings instinctively do on hearing it might mean mere annihilation, prevents ever gathering the two-fold provisions (merit and wisdom) that lead toward it; over-craving it, as hearers characteristically do, produces exactly the premature exit already described for birth-and-death's own excessive aversion. The bodhisattva's resolute conviction in emptiness threads exactly between these: recognizing conditioned existence and the unconditioned alike as, in the end, equally without a fixed nature to be craved or feared, which is why this same conviction is named, without qualification, the great expedient by which the Tathāgata's wondrous, unerring wisdom is finally reached. This closes 勝解方便, the first of the three limbs of 境相應慧 (the wisdom corresponding to true reality); the next limb, entering phenomena's selflessness (入法無我), now opens.

24. Entering Phenomena's Selflessness — Free of All Conceptual Discrimination, Yet Never Denying Cause and Effect

The second limb of the wisdom corresponding to true reality describes what it actually feels like, from the inside, to have deeply entered phenomena's selflessness: no discrimination arises at all regarding any phenomenon or its categories, and yet ordinary cause-and-effect is never denied — a bodhisattva simply takes the dependently-arisen event and takes suchness, without ever needing to think "this is merely an event" or "this is merely suchness."

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，由能深入法無我智，於一切法離言自性，如實知已，達無少法及少品類可起分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, bodhisattvas, through their ability to deeply enter the wisdom of phenomena's selflessness, truly understand every phenomenon's inexpressible nature — and having understood it, they realize that not even the slightest phenomenon, nor the slightest category of phenomena, remains capable of giving rise to discrimination.

Chinese Source

唯取其事，唯取真如。不作是念，此是唯事，是唯真如；但行於義。

Ru-Yi Translation

They take only the event, and take only suchness. They do not form the thought "this is merely the event" or "this is merely suchness" — they simply proceed directly in accordance with the meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this wisdom "deep" for a precise reason: it belongs to bodhisattvas alone, not shared even with hearers, and is difficult to understand through hearing or reflection and harder still to actually realize through meditative cultivation. He notes the treatise's own list of eight kinds of discrimination this wisdom sees through — discrimination of a thing's own nature, of its distinguishing features, of a totalizing grasp of it, of "I," of "mine," of what is liked, of what is disliked, and of whatever opposes both — the root causes of every defiled cycling through birth-and-death, all recognized here as ultimately without substance.

But the crucial balance he draws out is that this seeing-through never tips into denying the dependently-arisen event itself: a bodhisattva who has realized phenomena's selflessness still knows to eat when hungry and drink when thirsty, still functions perfectly within cause and effect — only the FIXED, name-matching identity of these events is seen as empty, never the illusion-like events themselves. "Taking only the event, taking only suchness" describes exactly this double honesty: never denying that dependently-arisen things function (the event), and never failing to see that their true nature is empty (suchness). And crucially, an advanced bodhisattva no longer needs to consciously remind themselves of this teaching, the way a practitioner still on the path of preparation must deliberately recall "this is merely an event, this is merely emptiness" to stay on track — this non-discriminating wisdom now simply operates on its own, effortlessly and spontaneously, without any deliberate application of will. This closes 入法無我, the second of the three limbs; the third and final limb, the many benefits actually gained from this attainment (獲多勝利), now opens.

25. Milestone: Supreme Equanimity — the First Benefit of Realizing Phenomena's Selflessness

The first of three benefits flowing from having entered phenomena's selflessness is called supreme equanimity: a bodhisattva who sees every phenomenon as equally empty of both existence and non-existence rests, at every one of the six sense-doors, in the same undisturbed evenness.

Chinese Source

如是菩薩行勝義故，於一切法平等平等。以真如慧如實觀察，於一切處具平等見、具平等心，得最勝捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such a bodhisattva, by practicing in accordance with ultimate meaning, regards every phenomenon as utterly equal. Observing truly with the wisdom of suchness, they possess an equal view and an equal mind toward every sense-field, and thereby attain supreme equanimity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "equal, equal" as a deliberate doubling: neither clinging to a phenomenon's inexpressible nature as if it were simply nonexistent, nor clinging to its designated, name-based nature as if it were simply existent — both extremes equally released, hence the repeated word. Applied at every one of the twelve sense-fields, from the eye to mental objects, this produces neither an added view (no superimposed reality) nor a subtracted view (no denied reality), and a mind that does not become agitated by discrimination — an abiding equanimity resting in still, peaceful joy. He notes this is not the same as the ordinary equanimity available even to an unenlightened being through worldly meditative absorption; only a noble one equipped with the wisdom of suchness earns the name "supreme" for this equanimity, since it rests on genuinely realizing phenomena's own emptiness rather than merely suppressing agitation through concentration alone. This closes the first of the three benefits (得最勝捨); the second benefit — mastery gained through this equanimity in the five fields of knowledge (依修善巧) — opens next.

26. Mastery of the Five Fields, Achieved Without Pride, and Faced Without Fear

The second and third marks of mastery gained through supreme equanimity: skill in the five fields of knowledge comes quickly and is retained with great mindfulness, yet never curdles into pride or stinginess toward students; and whatever must be done, a bodhisattva faces without timidity, backed by an unshakeable resolve the treatise compares to armor.

Chinese Source

於諸善巧速能成辦，得大念力。不因善巧而自貢高，亦於他所無有祕吝。

Ru-Yi Translation

They quickly master these various skills, gaining great mindful power. Yet they do not become conceited because of this skill, nor are they secretive or stingy with it toward others.

Chinese Source

於諸善巧心無怯弱，有所堪能。所行無礙，具足堅固鉀鎧加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward these various skills their mind holds no timidity, and they possess genuine capability. Whatever they undertake proceeds without obstruction, equipped with the firm, armor-like exertion that never wavers.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that mastery here means something quite specific: skill in each of the five fields gradually becomes accomplished, gradually becomes complete, and gradually becomes effortless, paired with a mindful power strong enough to recall, without loss, whatever was studied or said long before. Yet because this mastery rests on the equanimity already gained from realizing phenomena's own emptiness, it produces no conceit — a bodhisattva who has seen that nothing is ultimately attainable has no basis left for feeling superior to those still learning, and so teaches freely rather than guarding knowledge as a private advantage.

He explains the fearless capability that follows as a "firm-power-bearing nature" resting on five grounds: enduring whatever mistreatment difficult beings inflict; vowing to remain in birth-and-death across a long span purely to benefit others; standing unafraid before hostile factions and debating publicly in defense of the teaching; vowing to master every field of training without exception; and a long-habituated resilience built through sustained exposure to Mahāyāna teaching. This connects to a five-fold framework of diligence taught elsewhere in the treatise (armor-like diligence, methodical diligence, undaunted diligence, unshakeable diligence, and insatiable diligence — the same framework already covered in fascicle 35's account of the six perfections' lineage-marks) — the "firm, armor-like exertion" named here corresponds to the first two of these five, the initial vow taken with intense aspiration and the sustained, courageous follow-through that carries it out. This closes the second of three benefits (依修善巧, mastery in the five fields); the third benefit — how a bodhisattva's cycling through birth-and-death is never wasted (不空生死) — opens next.

27. Milestone: Samsara Never Wasted — Four Paradoxical Marks of Genuine Progress

The third and richest benefit describes four paradoxical patterns running through a bodhisattva's every lifetime in saṃsāra: hardship increases their store of awakening rather than depleting it; honor received makes them humbler rather than prouder; deepening wisdom makes them withdraw from debate rather than seek it out; and growing virtue makes them conceal themselves rather than seek recognition.

Chinese Source

是諸菩薩於生死中，如如流轉遭大苦難，如是如是於其無上正等菩提堪能增長。如如獲得尊貴殊勝，如是如是於諸有情憍慢漸減。

Ru-Yi Translation

The more these bodhisattvas, in the midst of birth-and-death, encounter great suffering and hardship as they cycle onward, the more they become capable of increasing their unsurpassed, perfect awakening. And the more distinguished honor and prestige they attain, the more their pride toward other beings correspondingly diminishes.

Chinese Source

如如證得智慧殊勝，如是如是倍於他所難詰、諍訟、諠雜語論、本惑、隨惑、犯禁現行，能數觀察深心棄捨。如如功德展轉增長，如是如是轉覆自善、不求他知、亦不希求利養恭敬。

Ru-Yi Translation

The more distinguished wisdom they realize, the more they repeatedly observe, and deeply abandon any inclination toward, others' hostile questioning, disputation, noisy quarrelsome talk, the manifestation of root afflictions, secondary afflictions, or precept violations. And the more their virtuous qualities progressively increase, the more they conceal their own goodness, neither seeking that others know of it, nor desiring gain or reverence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats each pairing as a direct rebuttal of how these same conditions typically play out for an unenlightened being. Ordinary hardship usually breaks a person down or at best teaches nothing; here, precisely because a bodhisattva pursues both self-benefit and other-benefit without a self to protect, every hardship endured while remaining unshaken becomes fuel that purifies and enlarges the very seed of buddhahood — across countless eons of accumulated hardship, the seed of great awakening grows only because of what was endured, not despite it. Ordinary honor usually inflates a person's sense of self; here, since a bodhisattva's skill in the five fields exists only to benefit beings and views their suffering as no different from their own, greater renown brings no basis for feeling superior — genuine care for others simply leaves no room for looking down on them.

The third pairing he illustrates with a striking story from the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra: once, seventy-seven thousand adherents of other paths gathered together with their teachers in a single assembly to determine, once and for all, the ultimate meaning of reality. They deliberated, weighed, observed, and searched exhaustively — yet in the end reached no shared understanding whatsoever, only countless private, mutually contradictory interpretations, until the gathering dissolved into open quarreling, verbal blows landing "like arrows and spear-thrusts," wounding and enraging one another before finally scattering in complete disarray. Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as the natural fate of any dispute grounded in unexamined views — a fixed self-view, an eternalist view, or a nihilist view — rather than in genuine realization: such debates, however sophisticated, can never converge, because there is no common ground beneath them. A bodhisattva who has actually realized phenomena's selflessness sees this pattern clearly for what it is, and rather than being drawn into ever more skillful argument, deliberately withdraws from that whole mode of engagement — not from any inability to debate, but from clearly seeing where debate of this kind inevitably leads.

The fourth pairing closes with an example he offers from more recent history: the celebrated Tang-dynasty figures Hánshān, Shídé, and Fēnggān of Mount Wǔtái, whose profound realization stayed hidden for years precisely because they neither announced nor sought recognition for it — tradition holds that once such a bodhisattva's true identity becomes known, they promptly depart, since concealment of one's own virtue was never a strategy but simply the shape genuine, desireless goodness naturally takes. This closes the third of three benefits (不空生死), and with it all three benefits of 獲多勝利; the treatise's own closing summary of this entire section (結顯所依) follows next.

28. Milestone: Every Benefit Just Described Rests on a Single Wisdom, Unsurpassed and Unrivaled

The treatise closes its account of entering phenomena's selflessness with a sweeping conclusion: every benefit named — supreme equanimity, mastery of the five fields, and samsara never wasted — rests entirely on one and the same wisdom, and no buddha past, present, or future has ever relied on, or ever will rely on, anything else.

Chinese Source

如是等類菩薩所有眾多勝利是菩提分。隨順菩提，皆依彼智。

Ru-Yi Translation

All such benefits belonging to bodhisattvas as these are factors of awakening. Whatever accords with awakening depends entirely on that same wisdom.

Chinese Source

是故一切已得菩提、當得、今得，皆依彼智。除此更無若劣若勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Therefore, all who have attained awakening in the past, will attain it in the future, or are attaining it now depend entirely on that same wisdom. Apart from this, there is nothing else, whether lesser or greater, that could serve the same role.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws together everything from the opening of 入法無我 onward: freedom from the eight kinds of discrimination, taking only the event and only suchness, supreme equanimity, tireless and prideless mastery of the five fields, fearless capability, and the four paradoxical marks of a saṃsāra that is never wasted — all of it, without exception, is simply what the single wisdom of phenomena's selflessness looks like in action once it becomes second nature. He stresses the force of the closing claim: it is not merely that this wisdom is the best available means to awakening among several options, but that literally nothing else — no lesser substitute, no more advanced alternative — could ever occupy its place, for any buddha of any time. This closes the second of the three limbs of 境相應慧 (入法無我); the third and final limb, actual practice free of conceptual proliferation (修行正行) — how the six perfections themselves are carried out once this wisdom is in place — now opens.

29. Milestone: Practice Free of Conceptual Proliferation — The Foundation for the Six Perfections

The third and final limb of the wisdom corresponding to true reality turns from understanding to actual practice: riding upon the very theory free of conceptual proliferation just described, a bodhisattva undertakes the six perfections for two purposes — maturing their own buddha-qualities, and maturing other beings according to whichever of the three vehicles fits their own lineage.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩乘御如是無戲論理，獲得如是眾多勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, riding upon this very theory free of conceptual proliferation, bodhisattvas obtain all these many benefits just described.

Chinese Source

為自成熟諸佛法故，為成熟他三乘法故，修行正行。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to mature the buddha-dharmas within themselves, and in order to mature the teachings of the three vehicles in others, they carry out genuine practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi defines "conceptual proliferation" (戲論) precisely: any thinking or speech that asserts existence, nonexistence, sameness, or difference about phenomena, driven by discrimination rather than genuine insight — talk that, however much effort goes into it, adds not the slightest increase to wholesome qualities or decrease to unwholesome ones, and so is ultimately idle. A bodhisattva who has deeply entered phenomena's selflessness simply no longer generates this kind of thinking, since phenomena's own inexpressible nature makes such assertions moot from the start — he cites the Āgama's own image of a monk who sees form, hears sound, and cognizes phenomena without ever generating the thought "this exists," "this doesn't," "this is superior," "this is equal," "this is inferior," or "I know, I see."

He explains the treatise's classification of who is matured by which vehicle: beings settled in the hearer lineage are matured through the hearer's vehicle, beings settled in the solitary-buddha lineage through that vehicle, and beings settled in the buddha lineage through the unsurpassed vehicle — each already carrying the corresponding seed of awakening, so a bodhisattva teaches according to what is already present rather than imposing one single path on everyone. With both purposes now stated, the treatise turns to the substance of this practice, beginning with the wholesome qualities a bodhisattva embraces (攝受善法) — the six perfections themselves, taken up one by one starting with giving.

30. Giving, Precepts, and Patience as Practice Free of Conceptual Proliferation

The six perfections now unfold one by one as concrete expressions of this same freedom from conceptual proliferation. Giving shows no attachment to body or wealth, and actively draws misers toward generosity; precepts are guarded through both self-respect and regard for others until guarding becomes second nature; and patience absorbs every kind of harm through five specific contemplations that make forgiveness genuinely effortless rather than merely willed.

Chinese Source

彼於如是修正行時，於自身財遠離貪愛，於諸眾生學離貪愛，能捨身財，唯為利益諸眾生故。

Ru-Yi Translation

As they carry out this genuine practice, they remain free of attachment to their own body and wealth, and train other beings to become free of such attachment as well — able to give up body and wealth solely for the benefit of all beings.

Chinese Source

又能防護、極善防護由身語等修學律儀，性不樂惡，性極賢善。又能忍他一切侵惱，於行惡者能學堪忍，性薄瞋忿，不侵惱他。

Ru-Yi Translation

They are also able to guard, and most excellently guard, the moral discipline they train in through body, speech, and mind, being by nature disinclined toward wrongdoing and by nature deeply virtuous. They are further able to bear every manner of harm and disturbance from others, learning to remain patient even toward those who act wrongly — their anger and resentment naturally thin, so that they never disturb or harm others in turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

On giving, Master Cháng Bǎi notes this perfection is practiced at every stage of the bodhisattva path — from the very first of the ten stages of resolute faith through the very first ground — though with steadily deepening capacity, the way every grade of elementary school studies the same subject at different levels. Neither the body given (any of its parts, or simply one's own labor on another's behalf) nor outer wealth is held with attachment. He highlights a specific skillful device: on encountering someone hopelessly stingy, a bodhisattva does not merely lecture them on generosity, but takes out their own wealth and gives it to that very person to give away — letting them experience the act of giving directly until they gradually become willing on their own; and when someone is already making a great act of giving, a bodhisattva personally goes there to assist with whatever help of body or word is needed, ensuring the request is genuinely fulfilled.

On precepts, he explains the three kinds a bodhisattva trains in — restraint from harm, cultivation of every wholesome practice, and active benefit to beings — practiced through body, speech, and mind together. When a lapse does occur, guarding depends on two forms of conscience: inward shame before oneself, and outward shame before others, both driving prompt confession and correction. Through sustained habituation, this guarding eventually becomes the bodhisattva's own settled disposition, not an innate trait present from birth but a "habituated nature" built through repeated practice — genuine cherishing of every being, as a parent cherishes a child.

On patience, he lays out the treatise's own five contemplations that make it possible to absorb even severe harm from enemy and friend alike, superior and inferior, virtuous and flawed: recollecting one's kinship with all beings across countless past lives (in beginningless rebirth, every man has been one's father and every woman one's mother at some point, so no one's harm is truly a stranger's harm); seeing that the one causing harm is, in truth, only aggregates in motion, with no fixed person there to resent; contemplating impermanence, since both parties are already moment-to-moment different from who they were an instant ago; contemplating suffering, recognizing that beings are already suffering enough without adding to it; and contemplating all beings as one's own family to be embraced rather than opposed. Through sustained cultivation of these five, even a severe, deeply unwelcome harm provokes only the slightest trace of anger, and lesser harms provoke none at all — not suppressed resentment, but genuinely thinned affliction, since no residual grudge is even stored away to resurface later.

31. Diligence's Threefold Purpose Across the Five Fields of Knowledge

The fourth perfection, diligence, is defined here by exactly what it is aimed at: mastering the five fields of knowledge not for their own sake, but to resolve beings' doubts, to genuinely benefit them in concrete ways, and to complete the bodhisattva's own path toward omniscience.

Chinese Source

又能勤修一切明處令其善巧，為斷眾生一切疑難，為惠眾生諸饒益事，為自攝受一切智因。

Ru-Yi Translation

They are also able to diligently cultivate every field of knowledge until skilled in it, in order to resolve every doubt and difficulty of sentient beings, in order to bestow every kind of genuine benefit upon sentient beings, and in order to gather within themselves the cause of omniscience.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the three stated purposes directly onto specific fields among the five already introduced back in fascicle 35's account of the perfections' own lineage-marks: inner teaching and logic exist to resolve beings' doubts and difficulties, since these two fields directly address questions of meaning and valid reasoning; linguistics, medicine, and practical craft exist to bestow concrete benefit, since these equip a bodhisattva to actually help with communication, healing, and livelihood; and all five together, pursued as a complete set, serve the further purpose of gathering the cause of the bodhisattva's own eventual omniscience — no field is optional busywork, each one is diligence aimed with precision at a real need. This closes diligence's own brief treatment; concentration and wisdom, the remaining two of the six perfections, follow next to complete this section.

32. Concentration — Stabilizing the Mind to Purify the Four Immeasurables and Unlock the Five Superknowledges

The fifth perfection, concentration, is defined by four purposes at once: stabilizing the mind internally, purifying the four divine abidings of loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity, unlocking the five superknowledges, accomplishing whatever actually benefits beings, and removing the very fatigue that diligent study of the five fields produces.

Chinese Source

又能於內安住其心令心善定，於心安住常勤修學，為淨修治四種梵住，為能遊戲五種神通，為能成立利眾生事，為欲除遣精勤修學一切善巧所生勞倦。

Ru-Yi Translation

They are also able to settle the mind internally so that it becomes well concentrated, diligently training so that this settled state is constantly maintained — in order to purify and cultivate the four divine abidings, in order to freely exercise the five superknowledges, in order to accomplish what genuinely benefits sentient beings, and in order to dispel the very fatigue produced by diligently mastering every skill.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a bodhisattva's concentration can settle continuously on its object for as long as seven days and nights, with body and mind experiencing a blameless, deeply pleasurable ease throughout — this is what "settling the mind so it becomes well concentrated" means concretely. He details the three ways the four divine abidings are purified, moving from coarser to subtlest: taking countless individual sentient beings as the object (extending loving-kindness, compassion, joy, and equanimity toward beings without pain or pleasure, beings in pain, and beings in pleasure respectively, already taught in an earlier fascicle); taking phenomena alone as the object, seeing that there is only the five aggregates and no fixed being there to love or pity, which is really a contemplation of no-self; and, subtlest of all, taking no fixed object at all — where neither the one cultivating loving-kindness nor any being to extend it toward can be found — a practice capable of yielding the very forbearance regarding the birthless nature of phenomena described earlier in the treatise.

He then lists the five superknowledges by name — the miraculous-ability, the recollection of past lives, the divine ear, the divine eye seeing death and rebirth, and knowledge of others' minds — noting that a bodhisattva's mastery of concentration makes exercising them as natural and unforced as play, hence "freely exercise." Benefiting beings through concentration takes eleven specific forms detailed elsewhere in the treatise's precepts chapter, and the fatigue concentration removes is the ordinary bodily and mental weariness that comes from diligently studying all five fields of knowledge — a concentration free of discrimination that produces the deepest possible stillness and ease. This closes the fifth perfection; wisdom, the sixth and final one, closes out the full set next.

33. Wisdom Completes the Six Perfections, Defined Through the Seven Marks of Genuine Greatness

Wisdom, the sixth and final perfection, is described here as an innate sharpness that matures into genuine transcendent understanding through sustained cultivation — pursued for the sake of one's own eventual final nirvāṇa, which is why a bodhisattva takes up the Great Vehicle in the first place, a vehicle defined by seven distinct marks of greatness that set it apart from the hearer's path.

Chinese Source

又性黠慧成極真智，為極真智常勤修學，為自當來般涅槃故修習大乘。

Ru-Yi Translation

They are also, by nature, sharply intelligent, and this matures into genuinely transcendent wisdom; they diligently train so that this transcendent wisdom continually deepens; and, for the sake of their own future final nirvāṇa, they cultivate the Great Vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this "sharp intelligence" as skill in grasping the exhaustive-content of every phenomenon, worldly and transcendent alike, matured through sustained practice into genuine transcendent wisdom — not yet complete the moment forbearance regarding the birthless nature of phenomena first arises, and so requiring constant further cultivation: hearing and reflection first, then progressing toward a superior aspiration, then actually entering the practice of the six perfections at the first ground, then, across the second great incalculable eon from the first through the seventh ground and the third from the eighth ground to buddhahood, bringing the fruit of practice to full completion — this entire arc is what "cultivating" wisdom means, and its final completion, the ultimate departure from the three realms, is called final nirvāṇa.

He then lays out the seven marks of greatness that earn the bodhisattva's path the name "Great Vehicle," each contrasted implicitly with the hearer's more limited path: greatness of teaching (the Great Vehicle's teachings necessarily include the hearer's teachings within them, not the reverse); greatness of resolve (the vow to liberate countless beings, not oneself alone); greatness of conviction (an unshakeable, powerful confidence in emptiness that no rival teaching can dislodge); greatness of heightened aspiration (from the first ground onward, a uniquely intensified wish to benefit beings, recognizing beings themselves as the very provisions a bodhisattva needs to reach buddhahood, so that compassion and wisdom only continue to expand together); greatness of provisions (an immense accumulation of merit and wisdom gathered across countless lifetimes); greatness of time (cultivated across vastly long eons rather than a handful of lives); and greatness of realization (a complete, unsurpassed awakening exceeding both the hearer's and solitary buddha's own attainments). This closes wisdom, the sixth and final perfection, and with it the entire section on embracing wholesome practice (攝受善法) — all six perfections now complete; benefiting sentient beings (饒益有情), the second of 修行正行's two remaining topics, opens next.

34. Milestone: Benefiting Beings — Four Ways a Bodhisattva Treats the Virtuous, the Flawed, the Hostile, and the Indebting

Having completed the six perfections, the treatise turns to how a bodhisattva actually treats four distinct kinds of people encountered in life: those with genuine virtue, met with reverence; those with faults, met with the deepest compassion aimed at healing rather than judgment; those bearing active hostility, met with patient, undeceiving kindness until the hostility dissolves on its own; and those to whom a debt of gratitude is owed, repaid generously and honestly, even when the means to fully repay are lacking.

Chinese Source

於具功德諸有情所，常樂現前供養恭敬。於具過失諸有情所，常樂現前發起最勝悲心、愍心，隨能隨力，令彼除斷所有過失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward sentient beings possessing genuine virtue, they constantly delight in offering reverence and respect in person. Toward sentient beings possessing faults, they constantly delight in generating the most excellent compassion and sympathy toward them, helping them, to whatever extent their ability allows, to cut off every one of their faults.

Chinese Source

於己有怨諸有情所，常起慈心，隨能隨力，無諂無誑，作彼種種利益安樂，令彼怨者意樂加行所有過失及怨嫌心自然除斷。於己有恩諸有情所，善知恩故，若等若增現前酬報，隨能隨力，如法令其意望滿足。雖無力能，彼若求請，即於彼彼所作事業示現殷重精勤營務，終不頓止彼所悕求。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward sentient beings who hold hostility against them, they constantly generate loving-kindness, and, to whatever extent their ability allows, without flattery and without deception, perform every kind of act that genuinely benefits and brings ease — so that the hostile being's own faults of intention and action, and their very hostility, dissolve of their own accord. Toward sentient beings to whom they owe gratitude, knowing that debt well, they repay it in the present with equal or greater generosity, to whatever extent their ability allows, satisfying that being's hopes in a fitting way. And even lacking the actual ability, if such a being makes a request, they show earnest, diligent effort toward whatever task is asked, never abruptly refusing what is sought.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the five specific virtues that earn a bodhisattva's reverence: faith, moral discipline, learning, generosity, and wisdom — met with actual material offerings alongside genuine greeting, bowing, and welcome, not empty politeness. For those with faults, he sorts beings into three groups corresponding to the three root afflictions: beings without either pain or pleasure tend toward delusion, beings in pain tend toward anger, and beings in pleasure tend toward greed — and the bodhisattva's compassion here is specifically the objectless kind already described under concentration, extending toward all without exception, unshared with hearers, solitary buddhas, or any other path, precisely because it rests on no fixed reference point at all.

Toward the hostile, he stresses a crucial refusal: a bodhisattva never pursues reconciliation through flattery (never simply telling a hostile being whatever they wish to hear) or through deception (never manipulating them toward a false peace) — only genuine, patient benefit, sustained regardless of the cost to the bodhisattva's own comfort, until the hostile being comes to recognize on their own, without being told, that they are dealing with someone of genuine compassion, at which point the hostility simply has nothing left to attach to and dissolves by itself. Toward benefactors, he notes the treatise's careful honesty: when truly unable to repay in full, a bodhisattva does not pretend otherwise or quietly disappear, but visibly continues trying, so the benefactor can see for themselves that any shortfall is a matter of limited means, never limited willingness. This closes benefiting-beings (饒益有情), the second of 修行正行's topics; a brief closing statement seals the entire account of 境相應慧 (the wisdom corresponding to true reality) next.

35. Milestone: Entering the Inexpressible Nature of Phenomena — What Reasoning Alone Can Establish

With the wisdom corresponding to true reality now complete in all three of its limbs, the treatise turns to its third and final topic under the mode of activity of wisdom: the inexpressible nature of phenomena itself (離言法性), opening by asking exactly what reasoning can actually establish about it.

Chinese Source

如是等類，當知名為菩薩乘御無戲論理，依極真智修正加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

All such practices as these should be understood as a bodhisattva riding upon the theory free of conceptual proliferation, cultivating genuine practice on the basis of transcendent wisdom.

Chinese Source

以何道理應知諸法離言自性？

Ru-Yi Translation

By what reasoning should it be understood that phenomena possess an inexpressible nature?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi marks this as closing the treatise's entire account of 境相應慧, the second of 智行門's three parts (真實義相→境相應慧→離言法性) — everything from resolute conviction in emptiness through entering phenomena's selflessness through the six perfections and benefiting the four kinds of beings has been, in the end, one continuous demonstration of what riding on "theory free of conceptual proliferation" actually looks like in a bodhisattva's daily practice. He frames the new question about to be addressed as the treatise shifting from describing this wisdom's effects to actually proving its basis: rather than simply asserting that phenomena are inexpressible, the text will now argue for it through reasoning precise enough to withstand rival objections — first laying out its own Yogācāra position, then refuting alternative views that would treat language as directly picking out reality.

36. Milestone: Why a Name Never Reaches Its Object — The Yogācāra Account of Speech and Mental Image

The treatise's own position opens with a claim that undercuts the ordinary assumption behind every act of speech: whatever name is given to a phenomenon or event, that phenomenon or event does not thereby possess the nature the name asserts — from form through the aggregates, faculties, and elements, all the way to nirvāṇa itself, everything is merely established through imputation, with no independent nature of its own, yet not simply nonexistent apart from that imputation either.

Chinese Source

謂一切法假立自相，或說為色，或說為受，如前廣說乃至涅槃。當知一切唯假建立，非有自性。亦非離彼別有自性，是言所行、是言境界。

Ru-Yi Translation

All phenomena possess only an imputed characteristic — spoken of as form, as feeling, and so on as elaborated before, all the way up to nirvāṇa. It should be understood that all of this is established merely through imputation, and does not possess a nature of its own. Nor, apart from that imputation, is there some separate nature which is the object of speech's own activity, the sphere in which speech operates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks this through the Yogācāra account of exactly what happens between an actual thing and the word used for it. When the mind perceives something — say, a single flower — that flower itself, external to the mind, is one kind of object (what later scholastics call the "remote" object-condition). But the mind never directly touches that external flower; instead, the mind's own faculty of deliberation and reflection (尋伺) generates an internal image of "flower," and it is this mental image (the "immediate" object-condition) that thought and speech actually operate on. The word "flower," in other words, names not the external flower but the mind's own image of it — an image produced by deliberation, not identical to whatever external referent may have prompted it. This is why the text insists a name never reaches all the way to a phenomenon's genuine, independent nature: speech's entire operation begins and ends with a self-generated image, and stops there.

He stresses this cuts both ways, avoiding a trap the passage anticipates: it would be equally wrong to conclude that, since names don't reach reality, there is simply nothing at all beyond the mental image — apart from the imputed name there is no OTHER separate nature waiting to be found by speech, true, but this doesn't mean reality itself is annihilated; it only means reality (the dependent nature's own suchness) sits entirely outside the reach of naming, deliberation, and reflection altogether, accessible only to a noble one's non-discriminating wisdom, never to ordinary conceptual thought. He recalls the classical image of waking from a dream — "within the dream, clearly there were six realms of rebirth; awakened, empty, there is no great universe at all" — the dream's images were never nothing while being dreamed, yet awakening reveals they never touched reality either; ordinary conceptual cognition operates the same way, generating vivid, functional images that never once make contact with things exactly as they are.

37. Milestone: Ultimate Existence — Neither Superimposing a Fixed Nature Nor Denying the Dependent Nature Itself

Having shown that phenomena's imputed nature does not exist as language asserts, the treatise now poses the harder question directly: given this, in what sense do phenomena exist at all? The answer names the single ultimate nature phenomena genuinely possess — reached only by avoiding both superimposition and denial together.

Chinese Source

如是諸法，非有自性如言所說，亦非一切都無所有……云何而有？

Ru-Yi Translation

Phenomena of this kind do not possess a nature exactly as language describes them, yet neither are they utterly nonexistent in every sense ... in what way, then, do they exist?

Chinese Source

謂離增益實無妄執，及離損減實有妄執，如是而有；即是諸法勝義自性。當知唯是無分別智所行境界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Departing from the false clinging of superimposition, which adds a reality that genuinely is not there, and departing from the false clinging of denial, which subtracts a reality that genuinely is there — existing in just this way is precisely phenomena's ultimate own-nature. This should be understood as the object experienced only by non-discriminating wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi returns here to the rope-and-snake image, now sharpened by the mental-image theory just established: seeing a coiled rope in dim light and taking it for a snake is superimposition, adding a snake that was never there — turn on the light, and the snake vanishes, yet the rope itself does not vanish along with it. Denying that the rope-shaped thing is there at all would be denial, subtracting something that genuinely remains. Applied to phenomena generally: the imputed, name-matching "snake" (a phenomenon's fixed identity as ordinary language asserts it) was never real to begin with, so seeing through it is not a loss; but the dependently-arisen "rope" (the phenomenon's own illusion-like, mind-generated arising, still real in exactly that qualified sense) genuinely persists, so denying it would be a real loss — the loss of any basis for distinguishing purified from defiled states at all, since without a real dependent nature there would be nothing left capable of being purified.

He is careful to note that even this account — expressed necessarily in words, in order for the teaching to be transmitted at all — is still itself only ever grasped conceptually until a noble one's actual non-discriminating wisdom directly experiences it; understanding the formula "avoid both extremes" intellectually is a genuine, necessary step, but not yet the same as the wisdom that simply sees this without any residual conceptual scaffolding at all. This closes 理成 (establishing the inexpressible nature through reasoning) and the Yogācāra school's own position (顯自); the treatise now turns to refuting rival views (斥他) that treat language as directly, exactly picking out reality.

38. Milestone: Refuting Rival Views — If a Name Really Matched Its Object, One Thing Would Have Countless, Contradictory Selves

The treatise now turns from establishing its own position to refuting the assumption it just set aside: if a word really did pick out a phenomenon's own fixed, independent nature exactly as asserted, two absurd consequences would immediately follow — one thing would need countless separate natures to match its countless names, and those natures would collide and contradict each other whenever a single thing is named in more than one overlapping way.

Chinese Source

若於諸法諸事隨起言說，即於彼法彼事有自性者，如是一法一事應有眾多自性。何以故？以於一法一事制立眾多假說而詮表故。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, whenever speech arises regarding some phenomenon or event, that very phenomenon or event thereby possessed the nature asserted, then one single phenomenon or event ought to possess countless natures. Why is this? Because countless distinct designations are established to describe one single phenomenon or event.

Chinese Source

亦非眾多假說詮表決定可得。謂隨一假說，於彼法彼事有體、有分、有其自性；非餘假說。是故一切假說若具不具，於一切法、於一切事，皆非有體、有分、有其自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Nor can it be determined that any of the many designations describing one thing is the correct one. That is to say: were it the case that some one particular designation genuinely possessed, for that phenomenon or event, a substance, a component, and a nature of its own that no other designation possessed — then, since every designation, whether complete or partial, applies equally to every phenomenon and every event, none of them could genuinely possess any substance, component, or nature of its own at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the first contradiction, "multiple substances," with an everyday example: one man is simultaneously his father's son, his own son's father, a school principal, and a professor teaching a course on the side — if every name genuinely picked out a separate real substance, this one man would need a son-substance, a father-substance, a principal-substance, and a professor-substance all at once, an absurd multiplication with no way to say which substance is really him. He notes the treatise's own examples from elsewhere: a single lineage is equally called "seed," "realm," and "nature"; a single ignorance is equally called "not-knowing," "delusion," and "darkness" — under the rival assumption, each synonym would demand its own separate underlying substance, which is plainly absurd.

He illustrates the second contradiction, "mixed substances," with the treatise's own example of a cart: call it "cart" and, on the rival assumption, there is a cart-substance; but a cart is nothing but wheel, axle, and frame joined together, so calling it "wheel" would demand a wheel-substance nested somehow inside the very cart-substance the first name required — the two substances would have to overlap and interpenetrate in a way no coherent account of "substance" can permit. The same point applies to the earlier example: one man is son to his father and father to his son at once, so if each name fixed a genuinely separate, walled-off substance, "son" and "father" would have to be simultaneously true and mutually exclusive substances occupying the identical place — a flat contradiction. Master Cháng Bǎi draws the conclusion plainly: language, by its very nature, can never pin down one single, exclusive, fixed substance for anything, precisely because any one thing can always be truthfully named in more than one overlapping way — this is not a strange technicality but the ordinary, everyday behavior of names, which the passage uses to show that treating any name as reaching a thing's true, independent nature was never coherent to begin with. Two more such contradictions remain to be drawn out, continuing to press the same rival assumption from further angles.

39. Milestone: Two More Contradictions — No-Substance and Non-Correspondence Close the Case Against Fixed Naming

Two further contradictions complete the treatise's fourfold refutation. The third exposes a vicious regress: if naming is what supplies a phenomenon's nature, then before that name was ever assigned the phenomenon should have had no nature at all — yet a name cannot be assigned to what doesn't already exist in some sense. The fourth appeals directly to experience: if color already possessed a fixed "color-nature" prior to being named, then simply encountering it should produce the cognition "this is form" even in someone who has never learned the word — but this plainly does not happen.

Chinese Source

又如前說色等諸法，若隨假說有自性者，要先有事，然後隨欲制立假說，先未制立彼假說時，彼法彼事應無自性；若無自性，無事制立假說詮表，不應道理；假說詮表既無所有，彼法彼事隨其假說而有自性，不應道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, regarding form and the other phenomena discussed earlier: if they possessed a nature only insofar as a designation is applied to them, then there must first be the event, and only afterward, as desired, is a designation established for it. But before that designation was ever established, that phenomenon or event ought to have had no nature at all. And if it had no nature, then there would be no event for which to establish a designation describing it — which makes no sense. Since, in that case, the designation describing it would have nothing at all to rest on, to say that the phenomenon or event acquires a nature simply through its designation makes no sense either.

Chinese Source

又若諸色未立假說詮表已前，先有色性，後依色性制立假說攝取色者，是則離色假說詮表，於色想法、於色想事應起色覺，而實不起。如說其色，如是受等如前所說乃至涅槃，應知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moreover, if form already possessed the nature of "form" prior to any designation being established describing it, and a designation were only afterward established, on the basis of that nature, to describe form — then, apart from any designation describing form, the cognition "this is form" ought to arise merely with respect to something conceived of as form, or an event conceived of as form; yet in fact it does not arise. Just as with form, so too with feeling and the rest, up through nirvāṇa as discussed earlier — it should be understood that the same holds true.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Before reaching these final two contradictions, Master Cháng Bǎi rounds out the second one, "mixed substances," with a further observation: names also fail because their scope is never universal but always locally bounded — "house" applies across every kind of dwelling but stops short of "village," which contains many houses yet cannot itself be called a house; "jar" applies across jars but stops short of "bottle"; "army" applies without distinguishing the men, women, veterans, or new recruits who compose it; "forest" applies without ever specifying root, trunk, branch, leaf, flower, or fruit. Every name, in other words, works only within its own local patch and simply retreats the moment its boundary is crossed — proof that no name was ever tracking a thing's genuine, universal substance to begin with, since a genuine substance-word ought to apply, or fail to apply, consistently rather than shifting with arbitrary convention.

He explains the third contradiction, "no substance," as a regress that undoes naming from its own starting point: whoever claims a name confers a phenomenon's nature is implicitly committed to saying that before the name existed, the phenomenon had no nature — but something with no nature at all is simply nothing, and one cannot coherently assign a name to sheer nothing in the first place. The very act of naming something already presupposes some event there to be named, which is precisely what this rival position denies at its own foundation. The fourth contradiction, "non-correspondence," he grounds in ordinary observed fact: mental cognition of "form," "feeling," and so on requires deliberation acting on an internal image built through learned vocabulary — deliberation itself proceeds by "image first, then thought; thought first, then speech" — so if a fixed color-nature genuinely existed prior to and independent of any name, merely encountering that color ought to trigger the cognition "this is form" instantly and universally, without needing the concept "form" learned beforehand at all; but a small child who has never learned the word plainly does not spontaneously form that cognition on contact with color, which shows the cognition depends entirely on the learned name, not on some pre-existing, name-independent nature waiting to be recognized. With all four contradictions established — multiple substances, mixed substances, no substance, and non-correspondence — the treatise closes this line of reasoning: for exactly these reasons, phenomena's own true nature must be understood as inexpressible.

40. Milestone: The Two Ways the Buddha's Teaching Gets Corrupted — Superimposition and Nihilistic Denial

Having established through reasoning that phenomena's nature is inexpressible, the treatise now names the two opposite ways this teaching itself gets corrupted in practice: one kind of person superimposes a fixed reality onto what is actually empty, treating imputed names as if they were real; the other denies even the genuinely-existing dependent nature and the emptiness realized through it, treating the entire teaching as if nothing at all were real.

Chinese Source

有二種人，於佛所說法毗奈耶，俱為失壞。一者、於色等法、於色等事，謂有假說自性自相，於實無事起增益執。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are two kinds of people who equally corrupt the Buddha's teaching of doctrine and discipline. The first, regarding form and other phenomena and events, holds that the imputed nature and characteristic asserted of them genuinely exists — generating a superimposing attachment toward what, in reality, has no such event at all.

Chinese Source

二者、於假說相處、於假說相依離言自性勝義法性，謂一切種皆無所有，於實有事起損減執。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second, regarding the ground on which the imputed characteristic arises and the basis on which it depends — the ultimate, inexpressible nature of phenomena — holds that this is entirely nonexistent in every respect, generating a denying attachment toward what, in reality, genuinely exists.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first kind of person with everything already refuted across the four contradictions — anyone who takes a name to confer a phenomenon's genuine, fixed nature commits exactly this superimposing error, and inherits every absurdity already shown to follow from it. The second kind commits the opposite error, and he is careful to specify exactly what gets denied: not merely the imputed name (which genuinely is empty, and correctly so), but the dependent nature itself — the mind's own image-generating activity, the actual event underlying any designation — along with the perfected nature, the ultimate emptiness a noble one's wisdom directly realizes. Denying these is not a subtler form of correct emptiness-teaching but a distinct and graver error, since it removes the very ground on which purification, cultivation, and the entire path toward awakening would need to stand. Both errors corrupt the teaching, he notes, but not equally — the treatise is about to explain in detail why the second is considered by far the more dangerous of the two.

41. Milestone: "Nihilistic Emptiness" — When Misreading the Profound Sūtras Denies Everything at Once

The treatise now describes a specific and historically real failure mode: a person who hears the Prajñāpāramitā-type sūtras' genuinely profound, deliberately indirect teaching on emptiness, cannot penetrate their real intent, and concludes instead that literally nothing exists at all — not even the dependent arising or ultimate emptiness the teaching was pointing toward — mistaking this total denial for correct insight.

Chinese Source

如有一類，聞說難解大乘相應、空性相應、未極顯了密意趣義甚深經典，不能如實解所說義，起不如理虛妄分別……起如是見，立如是論：一切唯假，是為真實，若作是觀名為正觀。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is a certain kind of person who, upon hearing profound scriptures difficult to understand, connected with the Great Vehicle, connected with emptiness, and expressing a hidden intent not yet fully made explicit, fails to genuinely understand the meaning being taught, and instead gives rise to unreasonable, false discrimination ... generating a view of this kind, and establishing a thesis of this kind: "everything is merely imputation — this alone is true reality; and to view things this way is to view them correctly."

Chinese Source

彼於虛假所依處所實有唯事，撥為非有，是則一切虛假皆無，何當得有一切唯假是為真實。由此道理，彼於真實及以虛假二種俱謗都無所有。由謗真實及虛假故，當知是名最極無者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such a person denies as nonexistent the genuinely-existing event on which imputation itself depends — but if that is denied, then imputation itself becomes entirely nonexistent too, and there could then be no basis at all for asserting "everything is merely imputation" as true. For this reason, such a person denies both true reality and imputed appearance alike as utterly nonexistent — and because both true reality and imputed appearance are denied, this should be recognized as the most extreme form of nihilism.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the scriptures in question as the Prajñāpāramitā corpus's own teaching that "all phenomena are without inherent nature, without arising or ceasing, originally still, and by nature already nirvāṇa" — genuinely correct as far as it goes, since it teaches the three kinds of no-inherent-nature (imputed characteristics lacking inherent nature, dependent arising lacking a nature that arises on its own, and ultimate reality lacking any nature that could be superimposed on it), but stated without the explicit unpacking the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra later supplies. A person unable to hold this careful three-part distinction collapses it into one flat claim — everything, without exception, is mere imputation — and treats their own sweeping denial as the very insight the sūtra was pointing toward.

He draws out the treatise's own devastating rejoinder: the claim "everything is merely imputation" is itself an assertion, which — like every other imputation — must rest on some genuinely existing event to be about; but this person has just denied that any such event exists at all. Their own central thesis therefore pulls the ground out from under itself the instant it is stated, since there remains nothing left for the words "everything is imputation" to even refer to. This is why the treatise judges such a person more radical than an ordinary denier of the imputed name alone — they deny not one but all three natures at once (imputed, dependent, and perfected), earning the sharpest label the text has: "the most extreme nihilist" (最極無者), someone whose emptiness has swallowed emptiness's own ground along with everything else.

42. Milestone: Why Wisdom Practitioners Must Refuse to Debate the Nihilist — and the Buddha's Own Verdict on Which Error Is Worse

The treatise's judgment on this "most extreme nihilism" is severe: no wise fellow-practitioner should even converse or associate with such a person, since the error is self-destructive and contagious to anyone who adopts it. The treatise then cites the Buddha's own verdict on the two errors compared directly — startlingly, holding a view of a fixed self is judged the lesser fault.

Chinese Source

如是無者，一切有智同梵行者不應共語、不應共住。如是無者，能自敗壞，亦壞世間隨彼見者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward such a nihilist, no wise fellow-practitioner should engage in conversation or dwell together with them. Such a nihilist destroys themselves, and also destroys anyone in the world who follows their view.

Chinese Source

世尊依彼密意說言：寧如一類起我見者，不如一類惡取空者。何以故？起我見者，唯於所知境界迷惑，不謗一切所知境界；不由此因墮諸惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The World-Honored One, with this specific intent in mind, said: "Better to be someone who holds a view of self than to be someone who has wrongly grasped emptiness." Why is this? One who holds a view of self is merely confused with respect to the object of knowledge, but does not deny the object of knowledge in every respect — and for this reason does not, on this account, fall into the miserable realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the severity of the treatise's warning in practical terms: a genuine wisdom-practitioner is advised not merely to disagree with the nihilist but to withdraw from engagement entirely, since debating from within a framework that denies all three natures offers no shared ground from which any correction could even land — precisely the same fate, he notes, already illustrated by the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra's story of seventy-seven thousand heretics debating without any prospect of resolution. Associating with or adopting this view carries real consequence: it is said to incur a grave karmic obstruction leading to rebirth in the lower realms for an immense span of time, since denying cause and effect in their entirety removes any incentive to cultivate good or avoid harm at all.

He then unpacks the Buddha's own comparison — elsewhere phrased even more vividly as "better to hold a view of self as large as Mount Sumeru than a view of emptiness as small as a mustard seed" — by explaining exactly why an ordinary self-view is judged the lesser of the two faults. A person who mistakenly clings to a fixed self is confused about only one thing: they misunderstand the true nature of what they perceive, taking dependently-arisen phenomena to add up to a genuine, fixed self. But they do not thereby deny that dependent arising itself occurs, nor that cause and effect are real — they can still recognize good and bad action, still aspire toward liberation, still practice a path, however imperfectly framed. Because this error touches only understanding, not the ground of practice itself, it does not by itself cause a fall into the lower realms the way total nihilistic denial does. The explanation of why the nihilist's error is so much graver still remains to be read in what follows.
