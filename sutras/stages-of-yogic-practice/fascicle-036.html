The Causes and Fruits That Equip a Bodhisattva to Help

Continuing the Ground of the Bodhisattva's chapter on self-and-other-benefit, this fascicle turns to three pairs of cause and fruit — karmic ripening, merit, and wisdom — that together equip a bodhisattva with the actual capacity to benefit both self and others, before the chapter closes and the treatise opens its account of true meaning.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 36 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Three Causes and Fruits Behind a Bodhisattva's Capacity to Help — Eight Marks of Ripening

Self-and-other-benefit's fourth category asks a practical question: what actually equips a bodhisattva to help at all? The answer comes in three pairs of cause and fruit — karmic ripening, merit, and wisdom — opening with eight concrete marks of a ripened result: long life, fine appearance, noble birth, authority, credibility, influence, an easy rapport with people, and great strength.

Chinese Source

略說應知三因三果。何等為三？一者、異熟因、異熟果，二者、福因、福果，三者、智因、智果。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, one should know there are three causes and three fruits. What are the three? First, the cause and fruit of karmic ripening; second, the cause and fruit of merit; third, the cause and fruit of wisdom.

Chinese Source

云何異熟？謂略有八。一者、壽量具足，二者、形色具足，三者、族姓具足，四者、自在具足，五者、信言具足，六者、大勢具足，七者、人性具足，八者、大力具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is karmic ripening? In brief, there are eight kinds: complete longevity; complete fineness of form; complete nobility of birth; complete authority; complete credibility; complete greatness of influence; complete rapport with people; and complete strength.

Chinese Source

眾所信奉，斷訟取則，不行諂誑、偽斗稱等，所受寄物終不差違，於諸有情言無虛妄；以是緣故，凡有所說無不信受，是名菩薩信言具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Trusted and deferred to by all, taken as the standard when settling disputes, never resorting to flattery or deceit, false measures or false scales; anything entrusted to their keeping is never mishandled; their word to any being is never false — for this reason, whatever they say is universally believed. This is called a bodhisattva's completeness of credibility.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this section as answering a question easy to overlook: good intentions alone do not make someone an effective bodhisattva — a person also needs the actual worldly standing to be listened to, trusted, and followed. The eight marks of karmic ripening supply exactly this: long life (giving enough time to cultivate and to teach — he recounts the Buddha's own choice of an eighty-year lifespan, and the tradition's account of his final illness from a poisoned mushroom dish he insisted on eating alone so his attendants would be spared); fine appearance (people are simply more inclined to approach and trust someone pleasant to look at); noble birth (inherited social standing that opens doors ordinary origins would keep closed); great authority (wealth, allies, and capable associates that make ambitious projects to help others actually feasible); and credibility, illustrated at length — a bodhisattva known never to use rigged scales in trade, never to falsify a document, never to mishandle an object placed in their care, becomes the person a community naturally turns to for fair arbitration. He contrasts this with a cautionary story of an unlettered monk who entrusted temple-building funds to a lay patron without any written record, was denied the money back, and was beaten to death pursuing the matter through the local magistrate — proof, he notes, of how much ordinary human affairs still run on documented trust rather than character alone, and how rare and valuable a reputation like the bodhisattva's actually is. The remaining marks — great influence, an easy rapport with people, and great strength — will complete the set as the chapter continues, together forming not a list of unrelated blessings but a coherent toolkit: exactly the worldly capital a bodhisattva needs in hand before compassion alone can actually accomplish anything at scale.

2. The Remaining Marks of Ripening, and What Actually Produces Each One

The eight marks of ripening close with influence, an easy rapport with others, and physical vigor — then the treatise turns to what actually produces each of the eight, tracing every worldly advantage back to a specific, deliberate prior cause rather than treating it as unearned luck.

Chinese Source

有大名稱，流聞世間。所謂具足勇健精進、剛毅、敏捷、審悉善戒，種種技藝工巧業處，展轉妙解，出過餘人……是名菩薩大勢具足……為性少疾，或全無病，有大堪能，是名菩薩大力具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

Possessing great renown, spread throughout the world — endowed with courage, diligence, resolve, and sharpness, keeping the precepts thoroughly, and surpassing others in every craft and skill … this is called a bodhisattva's completeness of influence … by nature rarely ill, or entirely free of illness, possessing great capability — this is called a bodhisattva's completeness of strength.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩於諸眾生不加傷害，遠離一切傷害意樂，是名菩薩壽量具足因……惠施光明鮮淨衣物，是名菩薩形色具足因……於諸眾生捨離憍慢，是名菩薩族姓具足因……所言誠諦，亦不好習乖離、麤獷不相應語，是名菩薩信言具足因……攝持當來種種功德於自身中，發弘誓願、供養三寶及諸尊長，是名菩薩大勢具足因……於諸眾生以身供事……用能增長身心勢力餅、飯、糜等種種飲食施諸眾生，是名菩薩大力具足因。

Ru-Yi Translation

Never harming any being, entirely free of any wish to cause harm — this is the cause of long life … giving light and clean, fresh clothing — this is the cause of fine appearance … relinquishing pride toward every being — this is the cause of noble birth … truthful speech, never divisive or coarse — this is the cause of credibility … gathering merit for the future, raising the great vow, making offerings to the three treasures and one's elders — this is the cause of great influence … personally serving other beings' lawful undertakings … giving nourishing food that strengthens body and mind — this is the cause of great strength.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats influence as compounding out of ordinary practical virtues rather than charisma alone — genuine diligence, unshaken resolve, quick and capable action, careful deliberation, and thorough precept-keeping, together with real competence across the classical five fields of knowledge, naturally earn a reputation that spreads on its own. He passes briefly over the treatise's own note that a bodhisattva's rapport-with-others is tied, in this classical Indian framework, to a male body specifically — glossed as the culturally-conditioned assumption that a male rebirth carries fewer social obstacles and greater physical capacity in the world this text was composed within — noting the treatise's own two applications of this teaching: encouraging a woman who genuinely wishes to be reborn male toward that aspiration, and, more strikingly, a bodhisattva using skillful means to protect a man's virility when threatened by past unwholesome karma, or a man seeking to restore it, rather than treating the matter with indifference. He then walks through the causal pairing for the remaining marks, each traced to a specific virtue already practiced: not harming beings produces long life; giving light and fresh clothing produces fine appearance (illustrated with the simple image of a streetlamp lit in a dark alley, letting travelers pass safely without fear); relinquishing pride produces noble birth, since a person free of arrogance naturally draws others toward them; truthful, ungossiping, gentle speech produces credibility; accumulated merit and genuine devotion toward one's teachers produces influence; and simply showing up to personally help with others' lawful work, alongside providing genuinely nourishing food, produces physical strength and vigor. He stresses the thread running through all eight: nothing here is arbitrary reward — each worldly advantage traces to a specific, previously cultivated virtue, making the entire list less a catalogue of blessings than a precise causal ledger.

3. Three Conditions That Make These Causes Actually Grow, and What Each Ripening Mark Is Good For

Beyond the eight specific causes, three further conditions determine how much they actually grow — purity of mind, of practice, and of the field they're planted in — after which the treatise names exactly what each of the eight ripening marks is functionally good for, closing the entire karmic-ripening analysis.

Chinese Source

又此諸因略由三緣而得增長，能感圓滿增上廣大異熟令起……一、心清淨，二、加行清淨，三、田清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

These causes, in brief, grow through three further conditions, able to bring about a ripening that is complete, ever-increasing, and vast: first, purity of mind; second, purity of practice; third, purity of the field.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩壽量具足故，能於長時修習善品，依自他利積集增長無量善根，是名菩薩壽量具足果……若諸菩薩形色具足故，大眾愛樂；眾愛樂故，咸共歸仰……凡所發言無不聽用，是名菩薩形色具足果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of complete longevity, a bodhisattva is able to cultivate wholesome practices over a long span of time, accumulating boundless wholesome roots for the benefit of self and others alike — this is the fruit of complete longevity … because of fine appearance, the multitude delights in and takes refuge in such a bodhisattva; and because of this, whatever they say is universally heeded — this is the fruit of fine appearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the three growth-conditions as what separates a passing good deed from a cause that compounds into something vast: purity of mind is genuine, settled confidence in awakening itself, deepening through sustained reflection on the path (the treatise specifies a bodhisattva already resting in the first ground's own "purified superior intention," whose reliance no longer depends on external conditions at all); purity of practice is that same resolve carried out with sustained, careful, uninterrupted effort — never abandoning practice once begun, and actively encouraging others toward the same practice whether they've started it yet or not; and purity of the field is simply what results when both of the first two are genuinely present, since a mind this settled and a practice this sustained naturally becomes the kind of "field" worth planting further merit in, for oneself and others alike. He then closes the ripening section by naming each mark's actual function: long life gives simply the time needed for sustained cultivation; fine appearance draws people close enough to actually listen, so that a bodhisattva's teaching lands rather than being ignored — the remaining six marks (noble birth, authority, credibility, influence, rapport, and strength) each supplying, in their own way, the practical means to be heard, trusted, and followed once that initial audience is won. With the entire eightfold ripening analysis — its marks, its individual causes, its three conditions for growth, and its functions — now complete, the treatise turns next to merit's own cause and fruit.

4. Milestone: Power Without Skill Fails, Skill Without Power Fails — And Why a Bodhisattva's Wandering Is Never Wasted

The ripening fruits close the eightfold analysis, then the treatise sharpens its point: having the worldly capacity to help is not enough on its own, and neither is having the skill to guide others without the capacity to back it — both must be present together. From there follows a striking claim: unlike an ordinary being's aimless wandering through birth and death, a bodhisattva's own cycling through the same birth and death is never actually wasted.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩自有力能，不善安處所化有情，彼於自事不隨順作；如是於他所作利益不名熾盛、不名隨順……若諸菩薩自無力能，而善安處所化有情……不名能作他利益事……是故菩薩要具二事，方於有情所作利益名為熾盛、名為隨順。

Ru-Yi Translation

If a bodhisattva has the capacity but is not skilled at properly settling those to be guided, so that they do not follow through with what benefits them, then the benefit rendered to others cannot be called flourishing or genuinely realized … if a bodhisattva is skilled at properly settling those to be guided but lacks the capacity … this too cannot be called genuinely benefiting others … for this reason, a bodhisattva must possess both together before benefiting beings can be called flourishing and genuinely realized.

Chinese Source

由諸菩薩安住八種異熟果中，能使有情利益安樂，是故一切有情所處空無義利無始生死，菩薩處之，能令不空，有大義利。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because bodhisattvas, abiding in these eight ripened fruits, are able to bring beings benefit and happiness — where ordinary beings' beginningless wandering through birth and death is empty, devoid of genuine meaning — a bodhisattva's own presence within that very cycle is never empty, but carries great and genuine meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two-failure-modes teaching as a corrective against two opposite but equally common mistakes: a wealthy, capable, well-connected bodhisattva who nonetheless lacks the skill in the four gathering methods (giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, cooperation) to actually settle a student in genuine practice ends up with resources but no real result to show for them; conversely, a struggling beginner-bodhisattva who has genuinely mastered the art of gathering others skillfully but lacks the worldly means — wealth, standing, influence — to actually follow through finds their good methods stall for lack of material or social backing. Neither capacity alone counts as genuine other-benefit; only their combination does. He then draws out what he considers the chapter's single most striking claim: every ordinary being's endless cycling through birth after birth accomplishes, strictly speaking, nothing at all — no benefit to self, no benefit to others, simply repetition without a genuine point — while a bodhisattva's own continued presence in that identical cycle of birth and death is never empty in this way, since even an ordinary-looking life event, for a bodhisattva, functions toward liberation for both self and others at once. The claim is not that a bodhisattva escapes saṃsāra faster, but that a bodhisattva's saṃsāra is a fundamentally different kind of thing to be in, precisely because of the ripening fruits and gathering skills just described working together.

5. Merit and Wisdom's Own Cause and Fruit — Sorting the Six Perfections into Three Clean Categories

Karmic ripening closed, the treatise turns to the second and third of its three pairs — merit and wisdom — sorting the six perfections with unusual precision: three belong purely to merit, one purely to wisdom, and two straddle both categories depending on how they are actually practiced.

Chinese Source

云何為福？云何為智？謂略說福，即是三種波羅蜜多。一、施波羅蜜多，二、戒波羅蜜多，三、忍波羅蜜多。智唯一種波羅蜜多。謂慧波羅蜜多。精進、靜慮波羅蜜多，應知通二分。一者、福分，二者、智分。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is merit? What is wisdom? In brief, merit is three perfections: giving, precepts, and patience. Wisdom is only one perfection: the perfection of wisdom itself. Diligence and concentration, one should know, belong to both divisions — partly to merit, and partly to wisdom.

Chinese Source

若依精進修行布施、受護淨戒，及修慈等四種無量，如是等類所有精進，名為福分。若依精進習聞思修所成三慧，修蘊善巧、修界善巧、修處善巧、修緣起善巧、修處非處善巧，修能觀察苦為真苦、集為真集、滅為真滅、道為真道……如是等類所有精進，名為智分。

Ru-Yi Translation

Diligence exercised in practicing giving, upholding and guarding pure precepts, and cultivating loving-kindness and the other three immeasurables — this kind of diligence is called the portion belonging to merit. Diligence exercised in cultivating the wisdom born of hearing, reflection, and meditation, in skillfulness regarding the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, and valid reasoning, and in observing suffering as truly suffering, origin as truly origin, cessation as truly cessation, and the path as truly the path — this kind of diligence is called the portion belonging to wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this three-way division is one reasonable classification among several found across the broader literature — some texts treat the first five perfections collectively as merely preparatory ("blind" without wisdom to guide them), reserving genuine insight for wisdom alone; this treatise instead draws a cleaner internal line, assigning giving, precepts, and patience to merit outright, wisdom alone to wisdom, and splitting diligence and concentration by how each is actually exercised rather than by which perfection they belong to nominally. Diligence applied to giving, precept-keeping, or cultivating the four immeasurables (loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity) counts as merit's own diligence; diligence applied to study and reflection across the five topics of Buddhist analysis (the aggregates, sense-bases, elements, dependent origination, and valid-versus-invalid reasoning) or to direct contemplation of the four noble truths counts as wisdom's own diligence — the same single quality of sustained effort, sorted entirely by its actual object rather than by any fixed label. He notes the same logic will apply to concentration once the text takes it up: cultivating the four immeasurables in meditation belongs to merit, while resting in the three gates to liberation (emptiness, marklessness, and wishlessness) belongs to wisdom — a single meditative practice capable of serving either purpose depending on what it actually rests upon.

6. Milestone: Merit and Wisdom Complete — Merit Is Most Excellent, Wisdom Is Unsurpassed, Neither Alone Suffices

Closing the merit-and-wisdom analysis, the treatise names three causes that produce both, describes each one's distinct fruit, and delivers a carefully balanced final verdict: merit is the more excellent of the two, wisdom the more unsurpassed — yet lacking either one, however excellent the other, makes buddhahood entirely impossible.

Chinese Source

略說應知福因、智因總有三種。一者、於福於智，能得、能住、能增長欲；二者、於福於智，善能隨順無違背緣；三者、於福於智，先已串習。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, one should know there are, in total, three causes for both merit and wisdom: first, the wish to attain, sustain, and increase them; second, favorable conditions genuinely free of obstruction; third, prior habituation with them.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩依止福故，雖復長時流轉生死，不為極苦之所損惱；又隨所欲能攝眾生，為作義利……依止智故，所攝受福是正非邪；又能起作種種無量善巧事業，乃至究竟當證無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

Resting on merit, bodhisattvas, though cycling through birth and death for a long time, are never afflicted by the most extreme suffering, and are able, as they wish, to gather beings and accomplish genuine benefit for them … resting on wisdom, the merit thereby gathered is correct rather than deviant, and one is able to undertake boundless skillful works, ultimately realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening.

Chinese Source

應知此中若異熟體、若異熟因、若異熟果，如是一切皆依於福，從福所生；福復依智，從智所起……是故二種，於證無上正等菩提雖俱是勝；而於其中福為最勝，智為無上。若諸菩薩於福、於智隨闕一種，決定不能證於無上正等菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that the ripened body, its cause, and its fruit — all of this rests upon merit, arising from merit; and merit in turn rests upon wisdom, arising from wisdom … therefore, though both are excellent for realizing unsurpassed, perfect awakening, merit is the more excellent of the two, and wisdom the more unsurpassed. Should a bodhisattva lack either merit or wisdom, awakening can never, under any circumstance, be realized.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the three causes as simple but genuinely necessary: sheer wish (illustrated by the Buddha's own famous reply to a blind monk he personally helped thread a needle — asked why a fully awakened being would still bother with such a small merit, the Buddha answered that his own hunger for merit was never actually satisfied, a standard ordinary practitioners, whose merit is still thin, should take seriously rather than dismiss as false modesty); genuinely favorable, unobstructed conditions (good company and correct understanding rather than a misguided teacher insisting merit alone suffices, or wisdom alone suffices, when both are in fact required); and sustained habituation, since repeated practice is what actually compounds a small beginning into something vast. He then draws out the two fruits' distinct character: merit functions as a kind of buffer, ensuring that even a bodhisattva's rare missteps in the cycle of birth and death never result in truly extreme suffering, while also supplying the actual means (already covered at length as the eight marks of ripening) to gather and benefit others; wisdom functions as a rudder, ensuring the merit accumulated is genuinely well-directed rather than squandered on indulgence, and opening the door to boundless further skillful undertaking all the way to awakening itself. He lingers on the treatise's own carefully hedged final verdict — merit called "most excellent," wisdom called "unsurpassed" — reading this not as declaring one secretly superior to the other but as praising each in the register where it genuinely excels: merit as the more abundant, generative quality (like a broad, fertile field), wisdom as the rarer, decisive one (like the single seed that determines what actually grows there) — and stresses the treatise's unambiguous bottom line that follows: lacking either one, however excellent the other, closes off awakening entirely. **This closes the entire "three causes, three fruits" framework (karmic ripening, merit, and wisdom) — and with it, the fourth of self-and-other-benefit's ten categories.**

7. Milestone: This-Life and Next-Life Benefit — Four Things a Bodhisattva Rightly Enjoys in the Present

Self-and-other-benefit's fifth category turns to time itself, distinguishing benefit realized in this very life from benefit deferred to a future one. This-life self-benefit opens with four legitimate things a bodhisattva may enjoy now: rightfully earned wealth properly used, the fruit of past good karma, dhyāna's own restful bliss, and the path to nirvāṇa itself.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩以如正理工巧業處士夫作用積集財物，即於如是所集財物，知量受用……既積集已，不染、不住、不耽、不縛、不悶、不著，亦不堅執，深見過患，了知出離而受用之，是名知量受用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas accumulate wealth through lawful skill and honest human effort, and, having accumulated it, make use of it with a sense of proportion … once accumulated, they are not defiled by it, do not dwell on it, do not crave it, are not bound by it, do not grieve over it, are not attached to it, and do not clutch it tightly — seeing deeply into its dangers, and using it with a clear understanding oriented toward renunciation. This is called using it with a sense of proportion.

Chinese Source

又先所造可愛果業異熟果熟，於現法中受用彼果……又諸菩薩於諸靜慮善迴轉者，為欲獲得現法樂住，於現法中依此靜慮；不為成立利他事故，依此靜慮……又諸如來現法涅槃，所有世間及出世間一切能得現法涅槃諸有為法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Also, when the karmic fruit of previously created desirable actions ripens, one enjoys that fruit in this very life … also, bodhisattvas already skilled in freely entering and leaving the dhyānas may, wishing simply to obtain the bliss of dwelling in the present dhyāna itself, rest in that dhyāna for its own sake, not in order to accomplish some benefit for others … also, the Tathāgatas' own nirvāṇa realized in this very life rests on every mundane and supramundane conditioned factor capable of bringing about that present-life nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this fourfold list as the treatise's own permission slip against an overly severe misreading of renunciation: a bodhisattva may legitimately earn a living through honest skill, enjoy the fruit of past good karma as it ripens, and even rest in dhyāna's own bliss purely for personal refreshment rather than constantly performing usefulness to others — provided wealth especially is used with genuine proportion, the sevenfold list of non-attachments (not defiled, not dwelt on, not craved, not bound, not grieved over, not clutched, not attached) describing wealth handled the way one might handle a venomous but occasionally useful substance, aware of its danger the entire time it is being used well. He notes the specific point about dhyāna deserves attention, since it might seem to contradict the resolve chapter's earlier insistence that a bodhisattva never clings to meditative bliss at the expense of helping others — the resolution is that occasional, proportionate rest in dhyāna's own ease is legitimate self-benefit, exactly parallel to earning and properly enjoying wealth, so long as it does not calcify into the settled avoidance already flagged as a genuine fault. The fourth item, attaining nirvāṇa itself in this very life, closes the list at its highest point: every mundane cause (dhyāna itself) and supramundane cause (the noble path) that together deliver a fully liberated life, still embodied, in the present — the ultimate form this-life self-benefit can actually take.

8. Milestone: The Self-and-Other-Benefit Chapter Closes — What Actually Makes Benefit Final Rather Than Temporary

The chapter's remaining categories close swiftly: next-life benefit mirrors this-life benefit exactly, and both together complete the time-based analysis. The chapter's true climax, though, is its final distinction — between genuinely final benefit and benefit that only looks final — settled by three precise tests: whether it touches reality's own unchanging nature, whether it can ever regress, and whether its fruit ever runs out.

Chinese Source

謂於欲界財寶具足、自體具足若因若果，及諸異生世間清淨若因若果，是不畢竟自利利他……若諸煩惱一切永斷，若諸所有八支聖道，若此為依獲得一切世間善法，是名畢竟自利利他。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wealth and bodily endowment within the desire realm, whether as cause or fruit, and the mundane purification of an ordinary being, whether as cause or fruit — these are not genuinely final self-and-other-benefit … the complete and permanent severance of every affliction, the noble eightfold path itself, and every mundane wholesome quality resting upon that path — this is called genuinely final self-and-other-benefit.

Chinese Source

由三因緣，應知畢竟及不畢竟。一、由自性故；二、由退不退故；三、由受用果有盡無盡故……究竟涅槃，名為畢竟；一切有為，名不畢竟……八支聖道無有退故，及受用果無有盡故，名為畢竟；其餘一切善有漏法，由有退故，及受用果有終盡故，名不畢竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

By three causes and conditions, one should know what is genuinely final and what is not: first, by its own nature; second, by whether it can regress or not; third, by whether its enjoyed fruit is exhaustible or not … ultimate nirvāṇa is called genuinely final; every conditioned thing is called not genuinely final … the noble eightfold path never regresses, and its enjoyed fruit is inexhaustible, so it is called genuinely final; every other wholesome but contaminated quality can regress, and its enjoyed fruit eventually runs out, so it is called not genuinely final.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing distinction as the entire self-and-other-benefit chapter's real payoff: wealth properly earned, a good rebirth, even genuine dhyāna attainment are all worth cultivating, and the treatise has spent the whole chapter carefully legitimizing them — but none of them, by their own nature, ever escape being conditioned things, subject to the same three tests that expose their limits. By nature, only nirvāṇa itself touches reality's own unchanging suchness; everything else, however excellent, remains conditioned. By regression, ordinary wholesome deeds and even the contaminated dhyānas can still be lost — a meditator can fall from an attainment, wealth can be squandered, a good rebirth eventually ends — while the noble eightfold path, once genuinely walked, never reverts to its starting point. By exhaustibility, worldly fruit is inherently finite — a fortune is spent, a heavenly rebirth's lifespan eventually runs out — while the path's own fruit, resting on unconditioned reality, keeps compounding without limit. He stresses this is not a devaluation of everything covered earlier in the chapter, only an honest ranking: a bodhisattva should absolutely pursue right livelihood, good karma, and the rest, while never mistaking any of it for the genuinely final benefit only the path itself delivers. **With this three-part test, the entire self-and-other-benefit chapter (自他利品) — all ten of its categories, running from fascicle 35 through the first third of fascicle 36 — is now complete.**

9. Milestone: The Chapter on True Meaning Opens — Two Kinds of Truth Beneath Everything

Self-and-other-benefit complete, the treatise opens the second of the factors of awakening's seven fields — true meaning — with the foundational distinction the rest of the chapter builds on: reality's own unchanging nature (suchness itself), and the full, exhaustive content of every phenomenon within it.

Chinese Source

云何真實義？謂略有二種。一者、依如所有性，諸法真實性。二者、依盡所有性，諸法一切性。如是諸法真實性、一切性，應知總名真實義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is true meaning? In brief, there are two kinds: first, resting on suchness itself, the true nature of all phenomena; second, resting on exhaustive content, the entirety of all phenomena. This true nature and this entirety together are called, in sum, true meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces this pairing as the conceptual foundation the rest of the true-meaning chapter will build on, and flags it as genuinely more difficult terrain than anything covered so far — the treatise's own core Yogâcāra epistemology. Suchness itself (如所有性) names reality's own unchanging nature — emptiness, the way things actually are beneath every conceptual overlay — accessible only to a mind that has directly, non-conceptually realized it, never through ordinary language or thought, since suchness is by definition beyond both; a hearer realizes only the emptiness of a personal self, while a bodhisattva realizes the emptiness of phenomena themselves as well, a distinction that will structure everything from here through the treatise's account of the ten bodhisattva grounds. Exhaustive content (盡所有性), by contrast, names the complete categorial map of everything that exists — the five aggregates, twelve sense-bases, eighteen elements, and every other classificatory scheme the earlier fascicles have already used to analyze experience — not reality's ultimate nature, but reality's full inventory, so to speak. Master Cháng Bǎi cites Venerable Master Tài Xū's own compact formula: the wisdom that penetrates suchness is called "principled wisdom," while the wisdom that maps exhaustive content is called "quantifying wisdom" — two complementary kinds of insight a bodhisattva must cultivate together, neither one alone sufficient, since ultimate nature without knowing its full expression would be incomplete, and a complete inventory without grasping its underlying nature would stay merely descriptive.

10. Milestone: Seven Kinds of Suchness — Reading Reality's Unchanging Nature Through the Four Noble Truths

Suchness itself is unpacked into seven distinct angles of approach — three examining ordinary existence (its flux, its selflessness, its dependence on mind alone) and four mapping directly onto the four noble truths — together showing that reality's single unchanging nature can be reached from any starting point in experience.

Chinese Source

流轉真如，謂一切行無先後性……相真如，指一切法補特伽羅無我性及法無我性……了別真如，謂一切行唯是識性……安立真如，謂我所說諸苦聖諦……邪行真如，謂我所說諸集聖諦……清淨真如，謂我所說諸滅聖諦……正行真如，謂我所說諸道聖諦。

Ru-Yi Translation

The suchness of flux: that all conditioned processes have no fixed prior-or-later nature. The suchness of characteristics: the selflessness of the person and the selflessness of phenomena in every dharma. The suchness of cognition: that all conditioned processes are nothing but consciousness itself. The suchness of what is established: what I have taught as the noble truth of suffering. The suchness of erring conduct: what I have taught as the noble truth of origin. The suchness of purification: what I have taught as the noble truth of cessation. The suchness of right conduct: what I have taught as the noble truth of the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all seven as seven different doorways onto a single underlying reality, drawn from the *Discourse Explaining the Profound Secrets*. The suchness of flux observes that every conditioned process — the ceaseless arising and ceasing already established at length back in fascicle 34's account of momentariness — has, ultimately, no fixed "earlier" or "later" moment to be found anywhere in it, cause and effect arising as a single unbroken continuity rather than as genuinely separate, sequential events; seen this way, the raw fact of change itself already points toward suchness, defusing both the view that things arise from nothing and the view that cause and effect are simply unrelated. The suchness of characteristics is the selflessness already familiar from the hearer's path (no fixed self in the person) now joined with the bodhisattva's further realization (no fixed, independent nature in phenomena themselves either) — the two selflessnesses together. The suchness of cognition is the mind-only teaching already introduced in fascicle 34: every experienced object, examined closely enough, is never actually apart from the consciousness experiencing it. The remaining four map suchness directly onto the four noble truths already explored at length in fascicle 34's account of the supramundane path: the suchness of what is established is simply suffering itself, correctly seen; the suchness of erring conduct is craving, the actual cause producing that suffering; the suchness of purification is cessation, suffering's genuine extinguishing; and the suchness of right conduct is the path itself, the thirty-seven factors of awakening that lead there. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the elegant symmetry this reveals: whether approached through ordinary change, through the two selflessnesses, through mind-only, or through the four truths already mastered on the supramundane path, every route arrives at the identical, single suchness — reality has only one true nature, however many doors happen to open onto it.
