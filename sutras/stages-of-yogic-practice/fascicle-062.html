Fascicle 62 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十二 · The Ground of Meditative Equipoise Opens

Eighteen Ways the Mind Refuses to Settle

With the decisive discernment of the three grounds with investigation and reflection now complete, Maitreya turns to an entirely new ground: the Ground of Meditative Equipoise, governing every state of concentration from the threshold of the first absorption to cessation itself. The discussion opens not with concentration's own fruits but with its obstacles, eighteen secondary afflictions that keep an otherwise sincere practitioner from ever reaching single-pointedness of mind.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 62 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: A New Ground Opens — Eighteen Obstacles to Concentration

Maitreya marks the transition explicitly: the decisive discernment of the three grounds with investigation and reflection, without investigation yet with reflection alone, and without either, spanning fascicles 58 through 61, has now been fully explained; the decisive discernment of the Ground of Meditative Equipoise, also called the ground of equal engagement, governing every kind of concentration from the threshold of absorption through the four formless attainments to cessation itself, both mundane and transcendent, shall now be explained in turn. The discussion opens with what obstructs this attainment: a being whose mind-stream is repeatedly defiled by many secondary afflictions cannot rightly realize single-pointedness of mind, whether mundane or transcendent. Eighteen such secondary afflictions are named at once: flattery, deceit, pretense, deception, shamelessness toward oneself and shamelessness toward others, lack of faith, laziness, forgetfulness, lack of concentration, wrong wisdom, dullness, coarseness, pressing eagerly forward, casting off the yoke of solitude, insufficient reverence toward the training rules, disregard for renunciant life, and seeking ordination merely to survive rather than for the sake of nirvana.

Chinese Source

如是已說有尋有伺等三地決擇。三摩呬多地決擇，我今當說。謂補特伽羅，多隨煩惱染汙相續，不能正證心一境性。云何名為多隨煩惱？謂有諂、誑、矯、詐、無慚無愧、不信、懈怠、忘念、不定、惡慧、慢緩、猥雜、趣向前行、捨遠離軛、於所學處不甚恭敬、不顧沙門、唯希活命不為涅槃而求出家。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus I have already explained the decisive discernment of the three grounds with investigation and reflection, and so on. I shall now explain the decisive discernment of the Ground of Meditative Equipoise. A being whose mind-stream is repeatedly defiled by many secondary afflictions cannot rightly realize single-pointedness of mind. What are the many secondary afflictions? Flattery, deceit, pretense, deception, shamelessness and lack of a sense of disgrace, faithlessness, laziness, forgetfulness, lack of concentration, wrong wisdom, dullness, coarseness, pressing eagerly forward, casting off the yoke of freedom from company, insufficient reverence for the training rules, disregard for renunciant life, and seeking ordination only to survive rather than for the sake of nirvana.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Maitreya's own opening sentence as this treatise's clearest possible marker of structure, four fascicles of decisive discernment on the three grounds now explicitly closed and a new ground, meditative equipoise, explicitly opened, the same disciplined transition formula used at every previous major boundary. He cross-references fascicle 58's own four-fold classification of secondary afflictions by scope, some arising with every unwholesome thought, some pervading every defiled state across all three realms, some arising individually and exclusively, and some arising in wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral states alike, as the analytic backbone this eighteen-item list draws on, even though the list itself groups shamelessness and lack of a sense of disgrace together as one pair, making eighteen distinct afflictions appear as seventeen numbered items.

2. Section: Flattery, Deceit, and the Face Saved Through Evasion

Each obstructing affliction is then explained in turn, beginning with flattery: a mind bent and indirect, unable to confess its own faults honestly before the Teacher, before the wise, or before fellow practitioners of pure conduct, exactly as those faults actually occurred, a mind the treatise elsewhere in fascicle 68 analyzes into eight distinct modes and seven distinct circumstances of flattery in the desire realm, including concealment-flattery, a mode distinct from covering itself precisely because flattery's own scope runs broader. Second is deceit, falsely displaying virtues one does not actually possess, wishing the wise and one's fellow practitioners to believe one is virtuous when no such virtue is real. Third is pretense: having actually transgressed the fundamental precepts of higher discipline, or breached the Buddha's own rules of proper conduct in daily life, and then, when questioned by another who has seen, heard, or suspected the transgression, deflecting with irrelevant talk or drawing in some other pretext entirely to avoid ever addressing the fault directly, exactly the improper livelihood the Grounds of Śrāvakas describes at length, seeking robes, food, bedding, and medicine through crooked means rather than through the true Dharma.

Chinese Source

云何有諂？謂自有過，而不能於大師、智者同梵行所，如實發露。云何有誑？謂不真實顯己功德。彼實無德，而欲令他諸有智者同梵行等了知有德。云何有矯？謂於增上戒毀犯尸羅，或於軌範毀犯軌範。由見聞疑，他所舉時，遂用餘事假託餘事，或設外言而相誘引，如經廣說。謂由諂、誑增上力故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is flattery? Having faults oneself, yet unable to confess them truthfully before the Teacher, the wise, or one's fellow practitioners of pure conduct. What is deceit? Falsely displaying one's own virtues, when one actually has none, wishing the wise and one's fellow practitioners to know one as virtuous. What is pretense? Transgressing the precepts of higher discipline, or breaching the rules of proper conduct, and when accused by another through what was seen, heard, or suspected, thereupon using some other matter or pretext, or offering deflecting words to draw the questioner away, as extensively explained in the sutras. This arises through the enhancing power of flattery and deceit together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that pretense is explicitly said to arise from flattery and deceit acting together, flattery's own inability to confess a fault honestly combining with deceit's own willingness to fabricate a false appearance, so that pretense is less a third independent affliction than the practical, evasive behavior these first two produce once a transgression has actually been discovered by someone else. He points to the Compiled Topics' own vivid case study of a monk who cultivates a wealthy, well-known benefactor purely for material support, feigning affection and respect he does not feel while quietly delegating all communal duties to more capable companions so as to live in comfortable idleness himself, as this entire eighteen-item list's own way of insisting that these are not abstract categories but recognizable patterns of real monastic behavior.

3. Section: Deception Born of Fear, and Six More Ways the Mind Resists Stillness

Fourth is deception: out of fear that others will discover a transgression, or out of hope for something desired from them, failing to confess even a grave fault and never allowing it to show, insincerely feigning affection, respect, and gentle conduct of body and speech before wise fellow practitioners, precisely the pattern the Compiled Topics describes of a monk who attaches himself to a wealthy, respected benefactor purely for gain while delegating communal labor to others so as to live at ease himself. Fifth and sixth, treated together, are shamelessness toward oneself and shamelessness toward others: feeling no disgrace whether looking inward at one's own conduct or outward at how it appears to others, deliberately transgressing and then, once having transgressed, unable to make amends as the Dharma prescribes, given instead to quarrels, disputes, and contention. Seventh is faithlessness, an unclear heart toward the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, unable to accept the four noble truths in accordance with reality. Eighth is laziness, clinging to sleep and reclining as pleasure, squandering day and night alike, and abandoning every wholesome practice. Ninth is forgetfulness, unable to recall what was done or said long ago, failing to guard the sense faculties, and lacking clear awareness in daily conduct. Tenth is lack of concentration, unable to attain even the most preliminary right application of mind belonging to form-realm absorption, let alone access concentration itself. Eleventh is wrong wisdom, clinging to one's own view as correct, holding to an unequal, mistaken account of causation, such as attributing everything any being experiences to the transformative power of a single sovereign deity, and finding such wrong conviction difficult to relinquish once adopted. A twelfth affliction, dullness, then opens: a mind neither sharp nor clear, unable to rouse or elevate itself, incapable of real accomplishment, and unable even to attend upon wise fellow practitioners with one's own body in service.

Chinese Source

云何有詐？謂怖他故，或復於彼有所希故，雖有犯重而不發露，亦不現行。非實意樂，詐於有智同梵行所，現行親愛、恭敬、軟善身語二業。云何無慚無愧？謂觀於自、或復觀他，無所羞恥。故思毀犯，犯已不能如法出離，好為種種鬥訟違諍。云何不信？謂於佛法僧心不清淨，於苦集滅道生不順解。云何懈怠？謂執睡眠、偃臥為樂，晝夜唐捐，捨眾善品。云何忘念？謂於久遠所作、所說，不能隨念，不令隨憶，不守根門，不正知住。云何不定？謂於定地，下至作意亦不能得。云何惡慧？謂住自見取，執不平等，難捨言論。云何慢緩？謂不捷利，亦不明了。不自超舉，無所能為，不能以身供侍有智同梵行者。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is deception? Out of fear of others, or out of hope for something from them, not confessing even a grave transgression, nor letting it show. Without genuine sincerity, feigning affection, respect, and gentle bodily and verbal conduct before wise fellow practitioners. What is shamelessness toward self and other? Feeling no disgrace when regarding oneself or another, deliberately transgressing, and once having transgressed, unable to make amends as the Dharma prescribes, given to quarrels and disputes. What is faithlessness? An impure heart toward the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha, not accepting the truths of suffering, origination, cessation, and the path in accordance with reality. What is laziness? Clinging to sleep and reclining as pleasure, squandering day and night, abandoning wholesome practices. What is forgetfulness? Unable to recall what was long ago done or said, not guarding the sense faculties, lacking clear awareness. What is lack of concentration? Unable to attain even the most preliminary right application of mind toward the grounds of concentration. What is wrong wisdom? Holding to one's own view, clinging to an unequal account of causation, finding such discourse hard to relinquish. What is dullness? Neither sharp nor clear, unable to rouse oneself, incapable of accomplishment, unable to serve wise fellow practitioners even with one's own body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the tenth affliction's own bar, failing to reach even the most preliminary right application of mind, against the Grounds of Śrāvakas' own account of how calm-abiding first becomes possible, the mind settling quietly on its object without the interruption of dullness or agitation, marking this tenth affliction as blocking concentration at its very earliest gate rather than at some advanced stage. He treats the eleventh affliction's own example, attributing every being's experience to a single sovereign deity's transformative power, as a plainly theistic wrong view the treatise singles out by name precisely because its own false certainty, treating itself as the one truth and every other account as delusion, makes it uniquely resistant to correction once it has taken hold of the mind.

4. Section: Mixing Freely, Pressing Forward, Refusing the Yoke of Solitude

Dullness, the twelfth affliction, is further unpacked: acting with foolish sluggishness is what makes one unsharp; lacking a pure, clear faith toward the Three Jewels, the four truths, and the practice of calm-abiding and insight is what makes one unclear; and being bound up with laziness is what keeps one from ever rising above this state. A thirteenth affliction, coarseness, is then explained in two aspects. The first is a delight in mixing freely and dwelling together with both householders and fellow renunciants, a habit that drags the mind back into longing and grief over past and future wealth and companions, prevents proper reflection on whatever teaching has just been heard, and draws one, upon seeing householders and renunciants mingling in pleasure or witnessing bad company keeping close together, into sympathetic delight and a wish to join in, sharing their joys and their sorrows alike, all of which the text calls, without qualification, the very work of Māra, since it makes withdrawal into solitude and the cultivation of calm-abiding and insight impossible. The second aspect is a delight in mental mixing, indulging again and again in unwholesome trains of thought, desire, malice, harm, and the rest, up through thoughts bound up with one's own family standing, turning them over and scrutinizing them repeatedly. A fourteenth affliction, pressing eagerly forward, names the wish to be first in line whenever offerings of robes or other requisites are presented, whether from the communal stores or from a private donor, and the actual conduct of a monk who always pushes to the very front among his fellow monastics to receive them. A fifteenth affliction, casting off the yoke of solitude, is an unwillingness to embrace austere bedding at the very margin of comfort or a quiet dwelling remote from the company of the unwholesome, abandoning and refusing any wish for the very conditions that would actually support withdrawal and practice.

Chinese Source

謂不捷利等者：所作愚鈍，名不捷利。心無淨信，名不明了。懈怠相應，名不超舉。云何猥雜？謂樂與在家及出家眾共相雜住。又樂尋思諸惡不善欲尋思等，乃至家勢相應尋思，多隨尋思、多隨伺察。云何趣向前行？謂受僧祇、或復別人諸衣服等所有利養，或請僧祇及與別人，皆名趣向。若諸苾芻，於如是事最初前行，故名趣向前行。云何捨遠離軛？謂於遠離邊際臥具、遠離眾惡人所習近似寂靜室，遠棄捨之，不生欲樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Foolish sluggishness in action is called unsharp. A mind without pure faith is called unclear. Being bound up with laziness is called failing to rise above. What is coarseness? Delighting in mixing and dwelling together with both householders and renunciants. Also delighting in dwelling on unwholesome thoughts of desire and the rest, up through thoughts bound up with family standing, repeatedly indulging and scrutinizing them. What is pressing eagerly forward? Receiving robes and other requisites, whether communal or offered by a private donor, and, among monks, always being first to come forward for such things, is called pressing eagerly forward. What is casting off the yoke of solitude? Being unwilling toward austere bedding at the margin of comfort, or a quiet dwelling remote from bad company, abandoning and refusing any wish for it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls the treatise's own bare designation of coarseness's first aspect, mixing socially with householders and renunciants alike, as literally "the work of Māra" one of this fascicle's most severe judgments so far, a plain social habit most people would consider entirely harmless treated instead as a direct obstacle placed by Māra himself between the practitioner and genuine solitude. He cross-references the eight kinds of unwholesome thought named in coarseness's second aspect to their own fuller treatment in the Compiled Topics, fascicle 89, and reads casting off the yoke of solitude as this cluster's own natural conclusion, since refusing uncomfortable lodging and quiet, remote dwellings is simply mixing and pressing-forward's own logical endpoint, a preference for company and comfort over the very conditions solitude requires.

5. Section: The Final Three — Precepts, Renunciant Life, and Why One Ordains at All

A sixteenth affliction, insufficient reverence toward the training rules, is a heart that does not truly honor the precepts the Buddha set forth: when real danger or hardship arises, one would rather abandon the precepts than give up bodily comfort or life itself, seeking physical ease and longevity rather than guarding what has been received. A seventeenth, disregard for renunciant life, means abandoning the training rules altogether and readily falling back, transgressing the precepts and committing unwholesome deeds while an inner rot of greed, hatred, and delusion continues unchecked, up to and including falsely claiming pure conduct one does not actually possess, let alone genuinely aspiring toward the noble eightfold path or any renunciant fruit of realization at all. The eighteenth and final affliction is seeking ordination merely to survive rather than for the sake of nirvana: entering renunciant life because compelled by a king, pursued by an enemy or bandit, pressed urgently by a creditor, driven by fear, or simply because chronic poverty leaves one afraid of not surviving otherwise, none of which reflects any genuine wish to tame one's own afflictions, still the sufferings of birth and death, or realize nirvana without residue. With this, all eighteen secondary afflictions obstructing concentration have now been named and explained in full.

Chinese Source

云何於諸學處不甚恭敬？謂遭厄難，寧捨學處，不棄身命。志求身樂及與壽命，不能隨護所有學處。云何不顧沙門？謂棄捨學處，好為退轉。或犯尸羅，行諸惡法，於內腐爛，廣說乃至實非梵行自稱梵行，況當希求沙門果證八支聖道。云何唯希活命不為涅槃而求出家？謂或為王之所逼迫而求出家，或為怨賊之所逼迫、或為債主之所逼切、或為恐怖之所逼切、或為財寶常所匱乏恐畏不活而求出家。不為自調、自靜、自般涅槃而求出家。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is insufficient reverence toward the training rules? When meeting hardship, choosing to abandon the training rules rather than give up body or life, seeking bodily ease and longevity, unable to guard the training rules one has received. What is disregard for renunciant life? Abandoning the training rules, readily falling back, transgressing precepts, committing unwholesome deeds while rotten within, up to falsely claiming pure conduct one does not have, let alone hoping to realize the fruits of renunciant life through the noble eightfold path. What is seeking ordination merely to survive rather than for nirvana? Seeking ordination because compelled by a king, pursued by an enemy or bandit, pressed by a creditor, driven by fear, or afraid of not surviving due to constant poverty, not for the sake of taming oneself, stilling oneself, and realizing one's own nirvana.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eighteenth affliction's own list of motives, compulsion by a king, pursuit by an enemy, pressure from a creditor, fear, or plain poverty, as the treatise's own unusually concrete acknowledgment that not every historical renunciant entered monastic life for spiritual reasons at all, some fleeing entirely worldly troubles instead, a frank realism he finds striking in so idealized a text. He notes that this final affliction closes the eighteen-item list precisely where it opened, with an examination of motive, echoing the earlier tenth gateway on why a person takes precepts in the first place, from fascicle 61's own Three Kinds of Persons framework, so that the entire eighteen-fold survey turns out to circle back to the same underlying question of sincerity that has run through this whole extended discussion.

6. Section: Sorting Eighteen Afflictions by Their Root

Having named and explained all eighteen secondary afflictions in full, the text turns to distinguish the basis each one depends upon, grouped according to the three higher trainings. The first four, flattery, deceit, pretense, and deception, arise specifically from living together with fellow practitioners of pure conduct and having something to hide from them. Shamelessness arises from transgressing the higher training in precepts. Faithlessness, laziness, forgetfulness, lack of concentration, and wrong wisdom all arise, broadly speaking, from transgressing the higher training in mind or the higher training in wisdom, with faithlessness and laziness depending on violations of both together, forgetfulness and distraction depending specifically on violations of the higher training in mind, and wrong wisdom depending specifically on violations of the higher training in wisdom. Through the enhancing power of faithlessness and laziness together, dullness, coarseness, pressing eagerly forward, and casting off the yoke of solitude in turn arise. And through the enhancing power of forgetfulness, a scattered mind, and wrong wisdom together, insufficient reverence toward the training rules, disregard for renunciant life, and seeking ordination merely to survive in turn arise, completing the entire causal map behind all eighteen obstructions to concentration. A new topic then opens at once, turning from the obstacles themselves to the persons who are obstructed by them.

Chinese Source

當知此中，依同梵行而共止住有所違犯，發起最初四隨煩惱。依增上戒有所違犯，當知發起無慚無愧。依增上心及增上慧，當知發起始從不信，乃至惡慧諸隨煩惱。此中不信及與懈怠，依俱違犯；忘念、散亂，依增上心違犯而起；惡慧，依犯增上慧起。不信、懈怠增上力故，當知發起慢緩、猥雜、趣向前行、捨遠離軛。忘念、亂心及與惡慧增上力故，當知發起於諸學處不甚恭敬、不顧沙門、唯希活命不為涅槃而求出家。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, depending on transgressions committed while dwelling together with fellow practitioners of pure conduct, the first four secondary afflictions arise. Depending on transgression of the higher training in precepts, shamelessness arises. Depending on the higher training in mind and the higher training in wisdom, the afflictions from faithlessness through wrong wisdom arise. Among these, faithlessness and laziness depend on transgressing both; forgetfulness and distraction arise depending on transgressing the higher training in mind; wrong wisdom arises depending on transgressing the higher training in wisdom. Through the enhancing power of faithlessness and laziness, dullness, coarseness, pressing eagerly forward, and casting off the yoke of solitude arise. Through the enhancing power of forgetfulness, a scattered mind, and wrong wisdom, insufficient reverence toward the training rules, disregard for renunciant life, and seeking ordination merely to survive arise.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing causal map as the entire eighteen-affliction survey's own real payoff, since simply listing eighteen obstacles would leave a practitioner unsure where to intervene, while tracing each one back to a specific breach of precepts, mind, or wisdom training points directly at the actual remedy, guard the precepts and the first five afflictions never arise, stabilize the mind and forgetfulness and distraction lose their ground, sharpen wisdom and wrong views cannot take hold. He notes the elegant cascading structure the text reveals, faithlessness and laziness alone giving rise to a further four afflictions, and forgetfulness, distraction, and wrong wisdom together giving rise to yet another three, meaning a great many of the eighteen ultimately trace back to remarkably few genuine roots.

7. Section: Three Persons, Three Roots — Heedlessness, Indulgence, and Wrong Practice

The discussion now turns from the eighteen afflictions themselves to the persons in whom they arise, distinguishing three kinds of person, each drawing these afflictions from one of three distinct grounds. The first is one who has gone forth within the true Dharma itself, a genuine Buddhist renunciant, whose afflictions arise from a ground of heedlessness, itself of two kinds: falsely displaying one's own inner virtue, hinting at attainments one does not possess in order to secure robes, requisites, and honor, thereby giving rise to deceit; or, out of jealousy toward the wise and one's fellow practitioners of pure conduct, oppressing, harming, distressing, and humiliating them through bodily and verbal action in order to secure that same honor for oneself instead. The second is one who remains a householder enjoying the objects of desire, whose afflictions arise from a ground of indulgence in desire, committing the three kinds of unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental conduct simply through pursuing and enjoying the five sense pleasures without restraint. The third is one who has gone forth outside the true Dharma altogether, a non-Buddhist renunciant, whose afflictions arise from a ground of wrong practice, itself unfolding in three further kinds: pursuing solitude wrongly, pursuing rowdy company wrongly, and pursuing the training rules wrongly.

Chinese Source

復有三種補特伽羅，依三處引諸隨煩惱。云何三種補特伽羅？一、依正法而出家者，二、在居家受用欲者，三、正法外而出家者。所謂最初補特伽羅，依放逸處引隨煩惱。謂或依內妄顯己德，為衣服等利養恭敬，自說己德勝過人法。或復依他諸有智者同梵行等，以身語業逼迫、加害、損惱、毀辱。當知第二補特伽羅，依耽欲處引隨煩惱。彼由耽著諸欲因緣，受用諸欲，依身語意行三惡行。當知第三補特伽羅，依邪行處引隨煩惱。略有三種，謂隨逐遠離、隨逐憒鬧、隨逐學處起隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also three kinds of person who, depending on three grounds, draw forth the secondary afflictions. What are the three kinds of person? One who has gone forth within the true Dharma; one who remains a householder enjoying desires; one who has gone forth outside the true Dharma. The first kind of person draws forth secondary afflictions depending on a ground of heedlessness: either falsely displaying one's own inner virtue for the sake of robes and other requisites, honor, and respect; or, depending on others, oppressing, harming, distressing, and humiliating the wise and one's fellow practitioners through bodily and verbal action. The second kind of person draws forth secondary afflictions depending on a ground of indulgence in desire: through the causes and conditions of indulging in desires, enjoying desires, one commits the three unwholesome conducts of body, speech, and mind. The third kind of person draws forth secondary afflictions depending on a ground of wrong practice, briefly of three kinds: secondary afflictions arising from pursuing solitude, pursuing rowdy company, and pursuing the training rules.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this threefold classification of persons quietly reorganizes the eighteen afflictions already given, showing that the same eighteen obstructions do not arise identically in every kind of person but trace back to one of three distinct roots depending on who is obstructed, a genuine renunciant's own heedlessness, a householder's own indulgence, or a non-Buddhist renunciant's own wrong practice. He highlights the sober honesty of naming jealousy-driven harassment of fellow practitioners as a genuine form of heedlessness among Buddhist renunciants themselves, a plain acknowledgment that monastic life offers no automatic immunity to this same affliction the text has spent so much space describing.

8. Section: The Non-Buddhist Ascetic Who Pursues Solitude in Vain

The first of the non-Buddhist renunciant's three wrong practices, pursuing solitude wrongly, is explained through three further aspects. First, such a practitioner may withdraw to remote dwellings and austere bedding, yet remain covered within by the five hindrances, desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt, so that outward solitude accomplishes nothing against an inwardly still-defiled mind. Second, such a practitioner may instead dwell in sheer physical suffering, undergoing bodily and mental torment through self-mortifying austerities, smearing the body with ashes, tearing out the hair, standing balanced on one leg, leaping from cliffs into the sea, exposing the body to the noonday sun, or going about unclothed, all pursued in the mistaken belief that enduring pain in itself burns away past suffering, a view the treatise traces to those who hold that everything experienced now is fixed entirely by past karma alone, so that only extreme suffering endured in this present life can cleanse it, a position that mistakes a single, isolated cause for the true multiplicity of conditions any real phenomenon actually depends upon. Third, such a practitioner may simply lack the true antidote to affliction altogether, having abandoned the five faculties of faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, working only on the surface of the body rather than at affliction's own real root, and so enduring suffering to no genuine purpose. Because such a practitioner dwells in defilement, dwells in suffering, and possesses no antidote capable of removing either, this entire pattern is called the secondary afflictions arising from pursuing solitude.

Chinese Source

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on the catalogue of austerities named here, ash-smearing, hair-plucking, one-legged standing, cliff-leaping, sun-exposure, and nakedness, as historically grounded rather than rhetorical exaggeration, real ascetic practices the treatise had directly observed among non-Buddhist contemporaries. He treats the diagnosis of their underlying error as this passage's real teaching, mistaking a single fixed cause, past karma alone, for the true dependent multiplicity of causes any suffering actually arises from, since believing that raw pain alone can burn away accumulated karma bypasses the genuine antidote entirely, the five faculties of faith, effort, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom that alone can uproot affliction at its source.

9. Section: Wrong Practice Named in Full, and Wrong Training's Own Threefold Shape

The second aspect of wrong practice, pursuing rowdy company, closes here, the text noting that the Compiled Topics elsewhere names ten distinct secondary afflictions of this kind, anger, resentment, concealment, spite, jealousy, stinginess, deceit, and flattery among them, of which only the first eight are drawn upon in the present passage. A third aspect, pursuing the training rules wrongly, then unfolds across the three higher trainings in turn: in the training of precepts, observing one's own or another's actual transgressions without any shame or sense of disgrace, breaking either the fundamental precepts outright or merely fraying the lesser ones; in the training of mind, relying on worldly meditative attainment and, comparing oneself either to those of lesser attainment or to those of merely equal attainment, giving rise to an inflated sense of one's own superiority; and in the training of wisdom, having heard little of the Dharma and so being unable to discern wholesome states from unwholesome ones at all. All three non-Buddhist patterns together, pursuing solitude, rowdy company, and the training rules, each wrongly, are then given two summary names: non-dharma conduct, since none of it is genuinely joined to a wholesome state of mind, and unequal conduct, since the undesired results such conduct actually yields never match whatever was originally hoped for, closing the entire discussion of obstacles to concentration begun at this fascicle's own opening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the third training's own diagnosis, that mere lack of learning is itself already a form of wrong practice, as a quiet but important corrective against romanticizing solitary asceticism, since even a non-Buddhist practitioner earnestly withdrawn from all society and rowdy company can still fail simply by never having heard enough of the true Dharma to know wholesome from unwholesome in the first place. He treats the two closing names, non-dharma conduct naming a wrong cause and unequal conduct naming its correspondingly disappointing fruit, as this entire obstacles-to-concentration discussion's own final summary, cause and effect both named at once, before the treatise turns from what obstructs concentration to what liberation through concentration actually accomplishes.

10. Section: Ten Kasinas and the Miraculous Powers They Unlock

A wholly new topic opens, turning from the obstacles that prevent concentration to the liberation concentration itself makes possible, beginning with the ten kasinas, the meditative devices of earth, water, fire, wind, blue, yellow, red, white, space, and consciousness pervading without limit, cross-referenced to their own fuller treatment in the Grounds of Meditative Absorption. A practitioner who cultivates these ten kasinas accomplishes five distinct benefits. The first: cultivating the first eight kasinas, earth through white in turn, once thoroughly purified, draws forth the noble miraculous power of creating what did not exist before and transforming what already exists, able to conjure objects such as gold or silver fit for actual use, a power shared by no ordinary being or non-Buddhist practitioner alike. The second: cultivating the kasina of boundless space, in which every material form is seen as without obstruction, draws forth the noble miraculous power of coming and going utterly without hindrance, passing through stone, through walls, over mountains and across seas exactly as one wishes, a power the treatise notes still depends upon the fourth absorption, though the removal of form's own obstruction through the space kasina must come first.

Chinese Source

復次，若觀行者修十遍處，能為五事。何等為五？謂由修習地遍處等乃至白遍處故，便能引發化事、變事諸聖神通。又由修習空無邊處一切處故，便能引發往還無礙諸聖神通。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, if a practitioner of the contemplative path cultivates the ten kasinas, this can accomplish five things. What are the five? By cultivating the earth kasina and so on through the white kasina, one draws forth the noble miraculous powers of creation and transformation. By cultivating the kasina of boundless space in all its pervasiveness, one draws forth the noble miraculous power of coming and going without hindrance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi marks this as a genuine change of register within the fascicle, moving from the moral psychology of what obstructs a practitioner to the highly technical Abhidharma vocabulary of the ten kasinas, devices for stabilizing the mind on an all-pervading object such as pure earth, water, or color, cross-referencing their fuller treatment in the Grounds of Meditative Absorption for readers wanting the complete method. He notes Kuījī's own explanation, cited in the commentary, of why mastery over the first eight kasinas specifically yields transformative power over material objects, having achieved genuine mastery over form itself through sustained contemplation of it, the mind gains real freedom to reshape what form presents.

11. Section: From Consciousness Without Bound to Cessation Itself

The third benefit: cultivating the kasina of consciousness pervading without bound, seeing that every phenomenon is inseparable from consciousness itself, draws forth the excellent qualities of the absorption free of contention, the absorption of aspirational wisdom, and the four unhindered modes of eloquent explanation. The fourth: this same completed mastery of the consciousness kasina, once perfected, in turn accomplishes the liberations of the base of nothingness and the base of neither perception nor non-perception, carrying the practitioner beyond the kasinas themselves into these higher formless attainments. The fifth: this same completion in turn makes possible entry into the liberation of the cessation of feeling and perception, counted among the most supreme of all abidings, the very cessation attainment elsewhere described as one of the noble abidings alongside emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness. The kasinas are said to extend no further than consciousness itself, since the consciousness kasina already pervades without any boundary or measure whatsoever across the five aggregates and the entire receptacle world in its functioning, leaving no further object beyond it for any additional kasina to be established upon, which is also why no separate kasina is set up for the base of nothingness or the base of neither perception nor non-perception, since neither departs from consciousness either.

Because the base of boundless consciousness functions without boundary or measure, pervading all, no kasina is established beyond this point.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the bodhisattva grounds' own naming of cessation attainment as one of the noble abidings, alongside emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, calling this fifth and final benefit of the ten kasinas the entire practice's own summit, a state in which even feeling and perception themselves temporarily cease. He treats the explanation of the kasinas' own limit, that consciousness already pervades without any boundary and so leaves nothing further to be pervaded, as an elegant piece of internal reasoning explaining why the treatise's own enumeration of ten kasinas stops precisely where it does rather than being arbitrarily cut off, a boundary set by the very nature of consciousness rather than by convention.

12. Section: How the Eight Liberations Are Purified — Clay, Kiln, and Wheel

A further topic opens, revealing the various liberations in fuller detail, beginning with the relationship between the eight liberations, the eight mastery-bases, and the ten kasinas already discussed: the mastery-bases and the kasinas together serve as the very path that purifies the liberations themselves, the liberations providing the initial cultivation, the mastery-bases providing the skillful means that follow, and the kasinas bringing the entire process to its full completion, until, once completion is reached, the liberations themselves become genuinely complete in turn. The text preserves an evocative simile for this three-stage progression: as a potter, blacksmith, or goldsmith first kneads and works raw clay or metal while it remains unrefined and unresponsive, corresponding to the stage of the liberations themselves; then works it further until it becomes fully pliable and well-tempered, corresponding to the stage of the mastery-bases, in which the practitioner gains real freedom over the meditative object, transforming pure into impure, small into great, without any trace of pride at this new power; and finally shapes the well-tempered material into whatever form is wished, corresponding to the stage of the kasinas, in which that same object can now be extended, at will, to pervade everywhere without limit. Different schools of Abhidharma are noted to disagree on exactly which of the three should be called cause and which fruit, a disagreement the treatise sets aside in favor of its own stated position, that the liberations are the fruit toward which the mastery-bases and kasinas both serve as cause.

Chinese Source

勝處、遍處是諸解脫能清淨道。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mastery-bases and the kasinas are the path able to purify the liberations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on the potter, blacksmith, and goldsmith simile as one of this fascicle's most vivid images, an entirely ordinary craftsman's progression from raw material through skilled working to finished, freely shaped form standing in for a meditator's own progression from first liberating the mind from an object's grip, through gaining real mastery over it, to finally extending it, transformed, without any limit at all. He notes the treatise's own quiet departure from other Abhidharma schools on this exact point, some treating the kasinas as the true cause and the liberations as the fruit, others treating the mastery-bases as spanning both roles, and reads the treatise's own insistence that liberation itself is the fruit toward which everything else serves as cause as consistent with its broader emphasis throughout this entire fascicle, that freedom from affliction, not miraculous power for its own sake, remains the actual point of every technique described.

13. Section: Liberation from the Cognitive Obstruction, and Three Ways Craving Clings to Absorption

The eight liberations are further shown to manifest a genuine freedom from the cognitive obstruction, specifically the portion of that obstruction bound up with obstruction to concentration itself, and it is by this same liberation that śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas alike attain freedom of mind from the cognitive obstruction, though only from this one portion of it, since the cognitive obstruction's full scope is vast, and only a bodhisattva's own path uproots every last trace of it on the way to complete awakening. A wholly new topic then opens, distinguishing the very craving that clings to meditative attainment, unfolding across three characteristics. The first is recollective craving: having attained absorption and then emerged from it, clinging to the memory of how pure, delightful, lovely, and pleasing that state was, and savoring it through recollection alone. The second is craving that pursues what lies ahead, whether an absorption not yet attained or one already attained but not yet fully satisfying, seeking through the sheer force of this forward-looking craving to pursue and delight in it still further. The third is present, manifest craving: holding whatever absorption has actually been attained to be pure and delightful, and letting an active craving for it arise directly in the present moment, which draws the mind into a genuinely tainted state of concentration lacking the very qualities of a true absorption factor. Craving of every kind, the text clarifies, can only arise upon emerging from a state of pure concentration; while one actually remains within it, no craving for it arises at all.

Having attained absorption and emerged, holding it to be pure, delightful, lovely, and pleasing, and craving it through recollection. Whether not yet attained or already attained, through the enhancing power of craving for the future, pursuing and delighting in it. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the careful distinction between śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas attaining freedom from only a portion of the cognitive obstruction, versus a bodhisattva's own complete uprooting of it, as this passage's own quiet reminder that even the eight liberations, however advanced an attainment they represent, are not themselves the final word, cross-referencing the Buddha-Nature Treatise's own threefold classification of obstruction, affliction, meditation, and omniscience, each removed by a different degree of realization. He calls the closing rule, that craving for concentration can only arise once one has actually left it, this section's most practically useful point, since it means the danger to a meditator lies not in the depths of absorption itself but squarely in the mind's own activity immediately upon emerging from it.

14. Section: Why a Higher Realm's Attainments Do Not Follow One Downward Uninvited

The discussion turns to how this craving differs across the various realms. States belonging to a lower realm, generally speaking, do not manifest once a being is reborn into a higher realm, a desire-realm being's own craving simply having no further occasion to arise once reborn into the form realm, and a form-realm being's own craving having none once reborn into the formless realm. States belonging to a higher realm, conversely, may manifest for a being reborn into a lower realm, but only for one who has already genuinely become free of desire with respect to that higher state, since a being still bound by the lower realm's own affliction, not yet having attained the higher realm's own root absorption, has no occasion for the higher realm's own affliction to actually appear, in exactly the way a person not yet enthroned does not yet act as a sovereign acts; only once actually enthroned does royal conduct become possible. Craving that does arise in a lower realm toward a higher realm's own attainment, for one who has not yet become free of desire and so still possesses an unsettled mind, is said to remain bound to the desire realm throughout, and this same craving is further shown to be of two kinds, tainted and untainted, depending on whether it is actually accompanied by affliction or not.

Ru-Yi Translation

States belonging to a higher realm, if one is reborn into a lower realm, may manifest for one who has become free of desire. If one is reborn into a lower realm and craving arises toward a higher realm, for one who has not become free of desire and remains of unsettled mind, this craving should be said to be bound to the desire realm. One should know that this craving is either tainted or untainted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi singles out the enthronement analogy, that royal conduct only becomes possible once a person is actually enthroned, as an unusually accessible way of explaining why an unmastered practitioner's mind simply has no access to a higher realm's own affliction, mastery itself being the very condition that first makes any affliction proper to that level possible at all. He notes that the wider commentarial tradition, drawing on both the Abhidharmakośa and the Cheng Weishi Lun, debates this rule's own exceptions at considerable length, some noting that a being who slanders liberation while dying out of the formless realm can still generate lower-realm afflictions in the intermediate state, a reminder that this treatise's own stated rule holds for the general case rather than admitting no exception whatsoever.

15. Section: Tainted and Untainted Craving, Three Ways Affliction Actually Ceases

Craving toward a higher realm's own attainment is tainted when it takes either of two shapes: wishing simply to attain that vast, joy-accompanied absorption for its own sake, savoring in advance how one will enjoy it once attained; or wishing to be reborn permanently into that higher realm, remaining there always without change, mistaking rebirth in a heavenly realm for actual liberation from the three realms altogether, both still bound to the desire realm and still unwholesome by nature. Craving is untainted, by contrast, when a mind arises single-mindedly seeking freedom from desire itself, delighting in the very stillness of the higher realm purely in order to leave the lower realm's own unwholesome states behind, cultivating absorption to cut off the affliction of desire rather than to savor pleasure or to be reborn into some heavenly abode. A wholly new topic then opens, distinguishing three ways defiled states actually cease: cessation through antidote, manifested when the very grounds that give rise to affliction no longer arise, as when renunciation and precepts leave no further occasion for desire's own objects to appear; cessation through non-manifestation, manifested moment by moment as formations continue to arise and pass away yet affliction itself no longer manifests among them, as when meditative absorption suppresses affliction's own activity even though its underlying seed remains; and cessation through abandonment, manifested when a being is reborn into a higher realm or enters nirvana without residue, so that the lower realm's own formations, or in the final case all formations across all three realms whatsoever, are abandoned outright, whether temporarily, for an ordinary being who may still return to the lower realm, or permanently, for a non-returner or arhat who never will.

Chinese Source

If a mind arises that single-mindedly seeks freedom from desire, delighting in entering the higher realm's own stillness, one should know this craving is untainted. There are also three kinds of cessation of defiled states: cessation through antidote, cessation through non-manifestation, and cessation through abandonment. What is manifested through the non-arising of afflictive grounds is the first cessation. What is manifested through the arising and passing of formations is the second cessation. What is manifested through the abandonment of lower-realm formations, or of all formations, upon rebirth in a higher realm or entry into nirvana without residue, is the third cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the tainted-craving example of mistaking heavenly rebirth for liberation as a caution still relevant to practitioners today, since a genuinely blissful meditative state can easily be mistaken for the goal itself rather than recognized as still bound within saṃsāra, however exalted it may feel. He maps the three kinds of cessation onto three progressively deeper levels of practice, precepts removing affliction's own occasion, concentration suppressing its active manifestation while its seed remains, and only undefiled wisdom's own complete abandonment finally removing every trace, cross-referencing the Compiled Topics' own parallel threefold account of complete cessation for readers wanting the fuller technical picture.

16. Section: Three Ways a Practitioner Falls Back

A further topic opens, distinguishing three kinds of falling back from meditative progress. The first is falling back from what has not yet been attained: applying oneself to some absorption or noble attainment not yet reached, yet failing to bring it about because one's application of mind toward it was never sufficiently sustained, cultivated, or repeated with real thoroughness. The second is falling back from what has already been attained: an affliction actually arising and causing an attainment already secured to be lost, exactly as a practitioner of dull faculties, weak in aspiration despite real effort, may enter the first absorption or another absorption yet prove unable to bear its own joy, pleasure, or excellent qualities, and so fall back out of it. The third is falling back in habitual practice: various excellent qualities a noble one has already attained proving unable to manifest together or co-arise at any single moment, a subtler kind of falling back not driven by any affliction newly arising at all, but simply by the limits of what can be held in the mind at once. The Buddha alone among these is said to experience only the third kind; pratyekabuddhas and arhats of unshakeable faculties experience the first and third but never the second, since their realization is too firm to be lost through arising affliction, while arhats of the five duller faculties, including those liable to fall, to reflective recovery, to protective vigilance, to settled abiding, and to further breakthrough, remain subject to all three kinds in turn.

Chinese Source

復有三退。一、未得法退，二、已得法退，三、習行法退。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also three kinds of falling back: falling back from what has not yet been attained, falling back from what has already been attained, and falling back in habitual practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi finds the third kind of falling back, in which no new affliction arises at all yet excellent qualities still cannot manifest together, the most conceptually subtle of the three, since it locates a real limitation within realization itself rather than in any lapse of practice, a reminder that even a highly accomplished mind cannot hold every attainment present simultaneously. He notes the classical account, drawn from the Abhidharma commentarial tradition, that the Buddha alone is entirely free of the second kind of falling back, already-attained regression, while even arhats of the five duller faculties remain subject to it, a hierarchy that quietly ranks the security of realization itself as a further, finer measure of spiritual attainment beyond the initial breakthrough alone.

17. Section: Three Paths Within the Threshold of Absorption

A further topic opens, distinguishing access concentration, the threshold state bordering each of the four form-realm absorptions, according to what its own object of attention actually is. Depending on worldly access concentration, whether cultivated as the preparatory path, the uninterrupted path, or the liberation path, and whether undertaken in order to cut off affliction or in order to actually attain the absorption above, a practitioner's object of contemplation differs accordingly: contemplating the lower realm's own faults, or contemplating the higher realm's own stillness, in the classic pattern of seeing the lower as coarse, painful, and obstructed while seeing the higher as pure, wondrous, and free. The preparatory path, corresponding to the earliest stages of mental application before actually entering the root absorption, and the uninterrupted path, corresponding to the direct cutting off of the five hindrances and other afflictions that obstruct concentration, are both undertaken specifically to cut off the lower realm's own faults; the liberation path, corresponding to the actual attainment of the root absorption once affliction has been left behind, is undertaken specifically to attain the higher realm's own excellent, peaceful qualities. A further, more advanced path of continued advancement then opens for consideration, extending this same threefold structure one stage further still.

Ru-Yi Translation

Depending further on the various worldly threshold concentrations, whether the preparatory path, the uninterrupted path, or the liberation path, and whether cultivated in order to cut off affliction or in order to actually attain the higher absorption, the practitioner's object is either contemplating faults or contemplating stillness, contemplating the lower realm's own faults or the higher realm's own stillness accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the preparatory, uninterrupted, and liberation paths directly onto the seven kinds of mental application named elsewhere in the Grounds of Meditative Absorption, the preparatory path corresponding to the earliest application of understanding-the-signs and resolute-understanding, the uninterrupted path to the middle stages of withdrawal, gathering-joy, and perfecting-effort, and the liberation path to the final stage where effort itself reaches its fruit, giving the abstract threefold division a concrete, sequential shape. He treats the classic formula of seeing the lower realm as coarse, painful, and obstructed against the higher realm as pure, wondrous, and free as this entire fascicle's own most compact expression yet of the basic engine that actually drives a practitioner up through the levels of absorption, one stage's own visible limitation becoming the very motive to seek the stage above it.

18. Section: Six Mental Applications Complete the Threshold of Absorption

The path of continued advancement takes as its object either what is called the pervasive object, spanning insight and calm-abiding alike, spanning every phenomenon, spanning true reality itself, and spanning the very relation between cause and effect, or else an undefiled object, contemplating the five aggregates as disease, as a festering tumor, as an arrow lodged deep in the flesh, as impermanent, painful, empty, and without self, or directly contemplating the four truths as they truly are, or contemplating suchness, the nature of phenomena, and reality's own limit directly. Each of the threshold concentrations bordering the four absorptions is further shown to contain six kinds of mental application in full: understanding the signs, resolute understanding, withdrawal, gathering joy, observation, and the application that perfects effort. Understanding the signs and resolute understanding belong to the preparatory path; withdrawal and the application perfecting effort belong to the uninterrupted path; gathering joy spans all four paths at once, since its own joy first motivates preparation, then helps cut off middling affliction, then marks the very attainment of that cutting-off, and finally carries the practitioner onward toward further advancement; and observation itself belongs specifically to the path of continued advancement, carrying the practitioner ever upward. With this, the discussion of access concentration and its distinctions reaches its close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the undefiled object's own list, disease, tumor, and arrow, as a deliberately visceral trio meant to break the mind's natural attachment to the five aggregates, each image naming a wound already present rather than a danger merely threatened, precisely calibrated to provoke the disenchantment genuine renunciation actually requires. He calls gathering joy's own span across all four paths the most structurally interesting of the six mental applications, since the very same joy functions differently at each stage, motivating at the start, aiding the cutting-off of affliction in the middle, marking the attainment itself, and finally propelling the practitioner forward once again.

19. Section: Why Concentration Cannot Be Grasped by Thought Alone, and Two Magicians Whose Powers Collide

A new topic opens, distinguishing the very characteristics of concentration, beginning with a question: why is it said that the state of a meditator's own absorption cannot be grasped by thought at all? Because a practitioner who has thoroughly cultivated and polished the mind through sustained practice possesses a majesty and power such that whatever is wished for can actually be accomplished, never falling short of intention, this being simply the very nature of concentration's own power, and it is for this reason that such a state is said to lie beyond the reach of ordinary reasoning, knowable only through direct realization, exactly as one who drinks water alone knows for themselves whether it is warm or cool. The text then offers a vivid illustration: two meditators of equal power, both having gained mastery and miraculous ability over the same object, attempt to transform that same single object in different ways at once, their two miraculous powers proving mutually obstructive precisely because what each intends to accomplish differs from the other, though neither power is inherently greater than the other's own. One of the two, noticing the obstruction, begins to doubt whether his own concentration has actually degenerated, and this very doubt generates a stray thought apart from concentration itself, causing him to lose his absorption and fail outright; the other, applying his mind again and again without any such doubt, remains free of obstruction and succeeds in transforming the object exactly as wished. Where the two powers are unequal, the text concludes, the more powerful one simply prevails; where the two are genuinely equal, whichever meditator applied intention first is the one who succeeds.

Chinese Source

問：何緣故說修靜慮者靜慮境界不可思議？答：修靜慮者已善修治磨瑩其心，有如是相威德勢力，隨所欲為皆能成辦，非不如意，法性故爾。是故說彼由尋思道不可思議。若二神通威德不等，隨其勝者所作成辦；若二神通威德相似，先作意者成辦非餘。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: Why is it said that the absorption-state of a meditator is inconceivable? Answer: A meditator who has thoroughly cultivated and polished the mind possesses such majesty and power that whatever is wished can be accomplished, never falling short, this being simply the nature of the dharma. Therefore it is said that this cannot be grasped through the path of reasoning. If the power of two miraculous abilities is unequal, the more powerful one prevails; if the two are equal, whichever one applied intention first prevails, not the other.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls the two magicians' collision this fascicle's own most memorable illustration yet, a genuinely narrative moment inside an otherwise highly technical discussion, and notes its real teaching lies not in the miraculous powers themselves but in the fatal role of doubt, since it is the anxious second-guessing of one's own concentration, not any objective weakness in it, that actually breaks the absorption and causes failure. He treats the closing rule, that unequal power resolves in favor of the stronger while equal power resolves in favor of whoever applied intention first, as a small but telling detail suggesting that even at the level of miraculous accomplishment, the ordinary virtues of confidence and promptness remain decisive.

20. Section: Four Kinds of Meditator, and the Four Ways Craving, View, Pride, and Doubt Taint Absorption

A further topic distinguishes four kinds of person who cultivate absorption: one who cultivates by way of threshold concentration; one who cultivates by way of a tainted absorption; one who cultivates by way of a worldly absorption; and one who cultivates by way of a transcendent absorption. The tainted kind unfolds into four further patterns in turn, each illustrated with real human motivation. Craving-tainted absorption belongs to one who has heard only of the virtues of the various absorptions without ever hearing the means of actually renouncing them, and who, seeing nothing but their excellent qualities, enters the first absorption or another with vigorous energy, only to become attached to it afterward. View-tainted absorption belongs to one who has heard from a teacher that the world is eternal and the like, and who, entering absorption in pursuit of this view, is later able to recall many past aeons upon emerging, mistaking this genuine but limited memory for confirmation that the self and the world are truly eternal, clinging to this view ever more firmly through repeated reflection. Pride-tainted absorption belongs to one who, hearing that certain elders have entered the first absorption or higher, thinks competitively, if they can enter such absorption, why should I not be able to as well, and enters absorption driven by this very pride, afterward believing that only they themselves, and no one else, can truly attain such a state. Doubt-tainted absorption belongs to one of naturally dull faculties who has long delighted in calm-abiding practice and so enters absorption readily enough, yet upon striving further to realize the four noble truths directly, finds their own dullness prevents swift realization, and so falls into doubt about whatever remains still unattained.

Chinese Source

復有四種修靜慮者。一、依近分定修靜慮者，二、依染汙定修靜慮者，三、依世間定修靜慮者，四、依出世間定修靜慮者。愛上靜慮，廣說乃至疑上靜慮，當知第二。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also four kinds of person who cultivate absorption: one who cultivates by way of threshold concentration, one who cultivates by way of tainted concentration, one who cultivates by way of worldly concentration, and one who cultivates by way of transcendent concentration. Craving-tainted absorption, extensively explained through doubt-tainted absorption, is the second kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads all four tainted absorptions as sharing a single underlying pattern, real meditative attainment genuinely reached yet immediately misdirected by whatever the practitioner brought into it beforehand, craving turning attainment into attachment, wrong view turning a limited memory of past lives into false metaphysical certainty, pride turning attainment into competition, and doubt turning one's own natural limitations into a barrier against further progress. He singles out view-tainted absorption's own mechanism as especially instructive, since the ability to recall past lives is entirely genuine, a real fruit of concentration, yet mistaking that limited, personal memory for proof of the world's own eternal nature shows how even a true meditative attainment can still ground a false philosophical conclusion when approached with the wrong view already in place.

21. Section: Worldly Absorption, Transcendent Absorption, and the Absorption Without Perception

The third kind of meditator, cultivating by way of a worldly absorption, dwells in a wholesome worldly concentration free of the four defilements of craving, view, pride, and doubt just described, though without having severed affliction's own underlying seed, and this pure worldly absorption is able to draw forth both an immediate, present-life abiding in bliss and the four immeasurable states of mind as its own worldly excellent qualities, as well as playful miraculous powers of transformation. The fourth kind, cultivating by way of a transcendent absorption, is an absorption embraced by the very liberation gates of emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness belonging to decisive discernment itself, still technically bound by outflow until the path of seeing is actually reached, yet already oriented directly toward it, completing the fourfold classification of every meditator. A further topic then opens, turning to the two absorptions without any active mind at all. The first, the attainment without perception, is said to be exclusively an attainment with outflow, since although the mental factors of perception and feeling belonging to the sixth consciousness are extinguished, the seventh consciousness's own root grasping at self remains entirely intact, so that a being who emerges from this attainment still returns to circle through the three realms exactly as before.

Chinese Source

若住餘善世間靜慮，當知第三。為能引發現法樂住無量作意世間功德，又能引發遊戲神通。若順決擇分所攝受空、無願、無相解脫門所顯靜慮，當知第四。復次，無想等至，當言唯一有漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one dwells in a wholesome worldly absorption otherwise free of taint, this is the third kind, able to draw forth present-life abiding in bliss and the immeasurable states of mind as worldly excellent qualities, as well as playful miraculous powers. If the absorption is embraced by the liberation gates of emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness belonging to decisive discernment, this is the fourth kind. The attainment without perception, furthermore, should be said to be exclusively with outflow.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four-kind classification of meditators as this fascicle's own comprehensive map of every possible relationship a practitioner can have to absorption, purely preparatory, tainted by one of four specific distortions, wholesomely worldly, or genuinely oriented toward liberation, a map that lets a practitioner locate their own present practice honestly rather than assuming any depth of concentration automatically signals spiritual progress. He underscores the attainment without perception's own sobering verdict, that even a state in which ordinary mental activity has entirely ceased can remain merely a refined form of bondage so long as the seventh consciousness's own root self-grasping is left untouched beneath it, a reminder that stillness of mind and freedom from self-view are not automatically the same achievement.

22. Section: The Cessation of Feeling and Perception — Undefiled, Transcendent, Reserved Even by Bodhisattvas

The second mindless attainment, the cessation of feeling and perception, is shown to be genuinely undefiled, for four reasons: it is unassociated with affliction, since the practitioner has already become free of craving for the base of nothingness or the base of neither perception nor non-perception; it is unassociated in a deeper sense still, since every mental factor belonging to the six active forms of consciousness, and the seventh consciousness's own self-view along with them, has ceased entirely; it possesses no manifest object of its own; and it resembles nirvana itself, abiding in a genuine stillness and bliss that affliction plays no part in producing. This attainment is further said to be transcendent, since no ordinary unawakened being is able to enter it at all, with one striking exception: a bodhisattva who has already entered the seventh ground, the ground of proceeding afar, is fully capable of manifesting this transcendent state directly, yet through skillful means born of great compassion deliberately declines to abandon affliction outright, retaining a subtle trace of it on purpose in order to nourish future birth and so return again and again to the three realms to liberate other beings, rather than passing entirely beyond them as an arhat would. This same attainment, precisely because it lies beyond what any being still formally in training or already finished with training actually practices, and because it so closely resembles the unconditioned itself, is classified as belonging to neither the trainee's own path nor the adept's.

Chinese Source

滅盡等至當言無漏，由與煩惱不相應故、非相應故、無所緣故、非諸煩惱之所生故。是出世間，一切異生不能行故。唯除已入遠地菩薩。菩薩雖能起出世法，令現在前，然由方便善巧力故，不捨煩惱。又此等至，當言非學非無學攝，非所行故，似涅槃故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The attainment of cessation should be said to be undefiled, being unassociated with affliction, being unassociated, being without object, and not being produced by affliction. It is transcendent, since no ordinary being is able to practice it. The sole exception is a bodhisattva who has already entered the far-going ground. Though a bodhisattva can manifest this transcendent state, through the power of skillful means affliction is not abandoned. This attainment should be said to belong to neither the trainee's path nor the adept's, since it is not what either practices, and it resembles nirvana.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls the seventh-ground bodhisattva's own deliberate retention of a trace of affliction one of Mahāyāna doctrine's most striking reversals of ordinary spiritual logic, an advanced being fully capable of a transcendent cessation choosing not to complete it, precisely so that compassion for other beings can keep drawing them back into the three realms rather than departing from it entirely, a choice the treatise frames as skillful means rather than any incompleteness in realization. He notes that classifying this attainment as belonging to neither the trainee's own path nor the adept's places it in a genuinely unique category within the entire path structure, resembling the unconditioned so closely that ordinary categories built for conditioned practice simply no longer apply to it.

23. Section: Four Sutras Cited in Turn, Beginning with Mindfulness Carried with the Body

A major new topic opens, citing and explaining four sutras bearing directly on meditation practice, the first being the Sutra of Mindfulness Carried with the Body, unfolding across five distinct points. The first point names four secondary afflictions troubling a monk who practices purifying the mind: transgressing the precepts, scattered deliberation, clinging to the inner body, and clinging to outer objects. Transgressing the precepts obstructs concentration specifically through the gateway of worry and regret, the mind troubled by remorse over a past transgression rather than settled upon its object. Scattered deliberation arises through three further gateways in turn: recollecting past objects, in which the mind grows restless and agitated simply by dwelling on what was once experienced, whether through love or aversion toward it; a mixture of restlessness, distraction, wrong view, and a preference for merely hearing and thinking about the Dharma over actually practicing concentration, all of which leave the mind without sufficient self-restraint to settle; and the sheer force of habituated distraction itself, carried forward simply because scattering the mind has already become a deeply worn groove through long repetition. A third affliction, clinging to the inner body, is then introduced through the specific gateway of finding no real weariness with birth and death at all.

Chinese Source

復次，當釋隨身念經。謂心清淨行苾芻，有四種隨煩惱。一、毀犯禁戒，二、散亂尋思，三、保著內身，四、保著外境。毀禁戒者，由憂悔門，於三摩地能為障礙。亂尋思者，由三種門。一、由於過去境隨念散亂門；二、由依掉舉、流散、惡見、惡聞、惡語、惡行，唯樂聞思散亂門；三、由先串習勢力所持散亂門。保著內身者，由於生死無厭患門。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sutra of Mindfulness Carried with the Body shall now be explained. A monk practicing purity of mind has four secondary afflictions: transgressing the precepts, scattered deliberation, clinging to the inner body, and clinging to outer objects. Transgressing the precepts obstructs concentration through the gateway of worry and regret. Scattered deliberation arises through three gateways: the gateway of scattering through recollecting past objects; the gateway of scattering through restlessness, distraction, wrong view, and delighting only in hearing and thinking; and the gateway of scattering sustained by the force of prior habituation. Clinging to the inner body arises through the gateway of finding no weariness with birth and death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the shift in register this new topic represents, turning from abstract Abhidharma classification toward the direct exegesis of a named sutra addressed specifically to monks striving for purity of mind, grounding the entire preceding technical apparatus back in ordinary daily practice. He highlights the second affliction's own third gateway, scattering sustained purely by habituation's own accumulated force, as a genuinely practical warning, since it names a cause of distraction that has nothing to do with any external provocation at all, arising instead simply because the mind has worn a deep enough groove toward wandering that it continues wandering out of sheer momentum.

24. Section: Clinging to Body and World, and Six Applications That Cure Them

Clinging to the inner body, the third affliction, arises through the specific gateway of finding no genuine weariness with birth and death at all, cherishing and protecting the body so single-mindedly that one cannot bear the hardship real practice requires. Clinging to outer objects, the fourth, arises through delighting in and longing for future objects of wealth, form, reputation, food, and rest, the mind constantly reaching outward rather than gathering inward upon a pure object of concentration. Six mental applications are then given as the direct antidote to all four afflictions in turn. The first, the application of right knowing, cures transgressing the precepts by maintaining a constant, correct awareness of one's own bodily, verbal, and mental conduct. The second, third, and fourth together cure scattered deliberation's own three gateways: matching the antidote to the object itself, contemplating impurity for one prone to greed, loving-kindness for one prone to hatred, the breath for one prone to distraction, dependent origination for one prone to delusion, and the elements' own distinct nature for one prone to pride; discerning the very signs and causes behind restlessness and wrong view so as to counter them directly; and training the mind that underlies all of this through the nine stages of mental abiding, gradually settling a mind long weakened by habituated distraction. The fifth, the application of analyzing what has merely aggregated, cures clinging to the inner body by seeing it as nothing more than earth, water, fire, and wind, and mind as nothing more than feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, gathered together without any real self to be found within. The sixth, the application of contemplating impurity, cures clinging to outer objects directly.

Chinese Source

保著內身者，由於生死無厭患門。保著外境者，於未來境由欣樂門。當知此中，為欲對治初隨煩惱故，修一作意，謂正知作意。為欲對治第二隨煩惱故，修三作意。謂稱順彼所緣作意；了別彼相，能對治彼尋思作意；彼所依心調練作意。為欲對治第三隨煩惱故，應修分析積聚作意。為欲對治第四隨煩惱故，應當修習不淨作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Clinging to the inner body arises through the gateway of finding no weariness with birth and death. Clinging to outer objects arises through the gateway of delighting in future objects. To counter the first secondary affliction, one cultivates one mental application, the application of right knowing. To counter the second, one cultivates three applications: the application matching the object, the application discerning its signs to counter deliberation, and the application training the underlying mind. To counter the third, one should cultivate the application of analyzing aggregation. To counter the fourth, one should cultivate the application of impurity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of matching-antidote-to-disposition, impurity for greed, loving-kindness for hatred, breath-counting for distraction, dependent origination for delusion, and elemental analysis for pride, as this sutra's own compact restatement of the five classic contemplative antidotes, here applied specifically to the problem of a wandering mind rather than presented in the abstract. He calls the analysis of the body into mere earth, water, fire, and wind, with no self anywhere to be found within the aggregation, this passage's own most direct challenge to ordinary self-clinging, precisely because it asks the practitioner to examine the very body being so carefully protected and find, on close inspection, nothing there actually worth protecting in the way imagined.

25. Section: What Each Application Actually Cures, Down to the Four Kinds of Sexual Desire

Each of the six applications is now matched explicitly to what it cures through sustained cultivation. The first cures forgetting right mindfulness outright. The second cures both kinds of craving, for the inner body and for outer objects alike. The third cures every form of distraction rooted in restlessness, wrong view, wrong hearing, wrong speech, and wrong conduct. The fourth cures distraction sustained purely by the force of long habituation. The fifth cures the deep-seated perception of one's own body as something pure and as a genuine sentient self. The sixth, the application of impurity, is shown to cure four distinct kinds of desire bound up with sexual craving in particular: attachment to complexion, delighting in fair or rosy skin, countered by contemplating a corpse turned bluish and bloated; attachment to form, delighting in a shapely or imposing figure, countered by contemplating a corpse further decayed and reddened; attachment to touch, delighting in soft and smooth contact, countered by contemplating bare bone and the bare chain of joined bones; and attachment to being attended upon, delighting in another's eager service, countered by contemplating a corpse scattered and fallen entirely apart, since no one still whole enough to serve another remains in such a state at all.

Chinese Source

又初作意修習、多修習故，能斷忘念。第二作意修習、多修習故，能斷保著內身、外境二種貪欲。第三作意修習、多修習故，能斷惡見等所有散亂。第四作意修習、多修習故，能斷先所串習勢力任持所有散亂。第五作意修習、多修習故，能斷於身淨有情想。第六作意修習、多修習故，能斷四貪。所謂色貪、觸貪、形貪，及承事貪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Cultivating and repeatedly cultivating the first application cuts off forgotten mindfulness. Cultivating the second cuts off both cravings, for the inner body and outer objects. Cultivating the third cuts off all distraction rooted in wrong view and the like. Cultivating the fourth cuts off distraction sustained by prior habituation. Cultivating the fifth cuts off the perception of the body as pure and as a sentient being. Cultivating the sixth cuts off four cravings: craving for complexion, for touch, for form, and for being attended upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the progression of corpse-contemplations named for the four kinds of sexual desire, bluish and bloated for complexion, reddened decay for form, bare bone for touch, and scattered dissolution for the desire to be served, as an unusually specific and graphic teaching tool, each stage of decomposition matched precisely to the particular quality of attractiveness it is meant to dismantle. He notes that this same sixfold antidote framework, drawn from the Grounds of Śrāvakas' own fuller treatment, shows the treatise's consistent method throughout, naming an affliction with real precision and then supplying an equally precise, practicable remedy rather than leaving the practitioner with only an abstract diagnosis.

26. Section: Touching Genuine Concentration, and Knowing Impermanence Rightly

A further section turns to the purity of the very ground of concentration itself. Relying on the cultivation of these six mental applications to cut off the four secondary afflictions already described, the mind is able to touch genuine concentration, attaining both the threshold and the root absorptions of the four form-realm stages, and thereby realizing the excellent qualities proper to each: the first and second absorptions bring bliss accompanied by joy, the third brings bliss with joy left behind, and the fourth brings a bliss that is purely still and clear. Relying on this same genuinely pure and settled mind, the practitioner is then able to know, exactly as it truly is, the impermanent nature of every being reborn into the higher realms, and the impermanent nature of every conditioned formation whatsoever, whether belonging to the aggregates or to the surrounding world itself, a right knowing that now opens for its own further explanation.

Chinese Source

如是作意修習為依，斷隨煩惱，心觸正定，證得近分、根本勝定，又能證得有喜、離喜、清淨安樂。又依如是正定心故，如實了知上地有情無常性，及諸行無常性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Relying on the cultivation of these mental applications, the secondary afflictions are cut off, the mind touches genuine concentration, and both threshold and root excellent absorptions are attained, along with bliss accompanied by joy, bliss with joy left behind, and pure, still bliss. Relying further on this genuinely settled mind, one truly knows the impermanent nature of beings in the higher realms, and the impermanent nature of all conditioned formations.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section as the sutra's own natural payoff for everything explained so far, four afflictions named, six antidotes prescribed, and now the genuine fruit those antidotes actually produce, concentration itself finally attained in its threshold and root forms, complete with the distinct qualities of joy, freedom from joy, and pure stillness proper to each of the four absorptions in turn. He highlights the closing move toward right knowing, using this same purified concentration to see clearly that even beings reborn into the most exalted higher realms remain impermanent, as this entire practice's own ultimate direction, concentration cultivated not as an end in itself but precisely in order to see reality, including the highest heavens themselves, exactly as it actually is.

27. Section: Seeing Impermanence with the Divine Eye, and the Threefold Basis of Practice

Right knowing of a being's own impermanent nature is explained first: a practitioner attains the divine eye, a clarity of vision surpassing the ordinary, and thereby directly sees beings dying and being reborn across countless lives, their forms fair or foul, their circumstances fortunate or wretched, a being once an animal now born human, now born into a heavenly realm of form or formlessness, exactly as the sutras describe at length. Right knowing of every formation's own impermanent nature follows: a mind able to move freely through past, future, and present alike gives rise to the wisdom of dependent origination itself, directly understanding that every conditioned thing throughout the three times arises entirely through causes and conditions, an understanding that directly counters ignorance of the past, ignorance of the future, and ignorance of the present all at once, since clinging to any of the five aggregates in any of the three times as a genuine self or as belonging to a self is simply dispelled by seeing plainly that all such things are dependently arisen. A further section then opens, turning to the very basis this practice of mindfulness carried with the body depends upon, itself threefold: the cultivating ground of requisites supporting both the higher training in mind and the higher training in wisdom; the specific basis of the higher training in mind, calm-abiding itself; and the specific basis of the higher training in wisdom, insight itself, all three together serving as what any remaining subtler afflictions must be cut off through, and as the very foundation every factor of awakening ultimately depends upon.

Chinese Source

云何了知有情無常性？謂得天眼，清淨過人，見諸有情，乃至廣說。云何了知諸行無常性？謂能隨入未來、過去、現在作意緣生智故。此隨身念依於三處。謂依增上心、增上慧學修治業地，依增上心學處，依增上慧學處。為欲斷除所餘諸結，修念住等所有一切菩提分法之所依止。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is right knowing of a being's impermanent nature? Attaining the divine eye, a purity surpassing the ordinary, one sees beings, as extensively explained. What is right knowing of formations' impermanent nature? Being able to move through future, past, and present, giving rise to the wisdom of dependent origination. This mindfulness carried with the body depends on three bases: the cultivating ground of the higher training in mind and higher training in wisdom, the basis of the higher training in mind, and the basis of the higher training in wisdom. These serve to cut off remaining fetters, and are what the four foundations of mindfulness and every factor of awakening depend upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the divine eye's own vision of beings cycling endlessly between animal, human, and heavenly rebirth as this passage's own vivid, experiential confirmation of impermanence, no longer a doctrine merely accepted on faith but something a sufficiently purified mind can actually witness directly. He cross-references the Grounds of Śrāvakas' own list of fourteen requisites, aspiration for wholesome states, precepts, guarding the sense faculties, moderation in food, wakefulness, right knowing, good companionship, and unobstructed hearing and reflection among them, as the concrete content behind this section's own more abstract phrase, the cultivating ground of requisites, the actual daily disciplines that make the higher trainings in mind and wisdom possible in the first place.

28. Section: What Sets Buddhist Practice Apart, and Four Fruits of Complete Requisites

A further section explains what distinguishes this practice from every non-Buddhist path, through three specific causes: the requisites supporting this cultivating ground are genuinely complete, where a non-Buddhist practitioner's own requisites need not be; there is no craving whatsoever for the very calm-abiding attained, where a non-Buddhist practitioner commonly clings to whatever absorption is reached; and the mind pursues nirvana itself with genuine, sustained aspiration, a state that is not the birthplace of affliction's own fire the way even the highest heavens still are, whereas a non-Buddhist practitioner commonly mistakes rebirth in the form or formless heavens for liberation itself, remaining bound within saṃsāra regardless. Complete requisites, as the first cause, are then shown to yield four distinct fruits of purity in turn: remaining unmoved by whatever affliction the eight worldly winds of gain, loss, praise, blame, honor, ridicule, pleasure, and pain stir up while dwelling in a village or town; remaining unmoved by the fear that storms, wild animals, bandits, or non-human threats can provoke while dwelling alone in the wilderness; remaining unmoved regardless of which of these two settings, town or wilderness, one happens to be in; and remaining unmoved even by simple physical hardship, a leaking roof or the bite of insects, in either setting alike.

Chinese Source

由三因緣，不與一切外道沙門、婆羅門共。謂於修治業地資糧圓滿故；於奢摩他聚無有愛味故；於般涅槃愛樂隨逐心，非煩惱火所生處故。又此修念資糧圓滿為依止故，四果清淨。謂處聚落，世法所生煩惱不染；處阿練若，空閑所生煩惱不染；處阿練若，聚落所生煩惱不染；於二處所，不能堪忍淋漏等苦煩惱不染。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through three causes, this is not shared with any non-Buddhist renunciant or brahmin: the requisites of the cultivating ground are complete; there is no craving whatsoever for the calm-abiding attained; and the mind aspires toward nirvana, which is not the birthplace of affliction's own fire. Relying on the completeness of these requisites for cultivating mindfulness, four fruits of purity result: remaining unstained by the affliction that worldly conditions produce while in a village; remaining unstained by the affliction that solitude produces in the wilderness; remaining unstained regardless of either setting; and remaining unstained by the affliction of being unable to bear hardships such as a leaking roof in either setting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third distinguishing cause, aspiring toward nirvana as something categorically different from the highest heavens, as the deepest of the three, since it correctly diagnoses the exact error non-Buddhist meditators most commonly fall into, treating an exalted but still conditioned state as though it were the unconditioned goal itself. He notes the concrete, almost mundane detail of the fourth fruit, tolerance of a leaking roof and insect bites, as a deliberately unglamorous inclusion, a reminder that genuine equanimity is finally tested not only by grand worldly winds or wilderness terrors but by the small, physical discomforts of daily life that most practitioners overlook entirely.

29. Section: Five Fruits Free of Craving, Nine Fruits That Follow the Wish for Nirvana

Freedom from craving toward whatever calm-abiding has been attained, the second distinguishing cause, is shown to yield five further fruits of purity: the four genuinely pure absorptions themselves, and the peaceful liberation of the four formless attainments taken together as a single further fruit, since none of these remains tainted by the very attachment that so often accompanies them in non-Buddhist practice. The mind's aspiration toward nirvana, the third and final distinguishing cause, in turn yields nine fruits of purity: the first three noble fruits of stream-entry, once-returning, and non-returning, together with the six higher forms of direct knowledge, the divine eye, the divine ear, miraculous power, knowledge of others' minds, recollection of past lives, and the knowledge of the extinction of outflows, this last belonging to the arhat alone and thereby implying the fourth and final noble fruit as well, even though it is not separately named. This mindfulness carried with the body, the text now concludes in summary, should be understood as comprised, in its entirety, of five kinds of purity, a summary of everything explained so far now beginning to unfold.

Chinese Source

奢摩他聚無有愛味為依止故，五果清淨。謂四種清淨靜慮，及寂靜解脫。於般涅槃愛樂隨逐，心非煩惱火所生處為依止故，九果清淨。謂初三果及六神通。此隨身念，當知五種清淨所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Relying on freedom from craving toward the calm-abiding attained, five fruits of purity result: the four pure absorptions, and the peaceful liberation of the formless attainments. Relying on the mind's sustained aspiration toward nirvana, which is not the birthplace of affliction's fire, nine fruits of purity result: the first three noble fruits, and the six higher forms of direct knowledge. This mindfulness carried with the body should be known as comprised of five kinds of purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the ninth fruit, knowledge of the extinction of outflows, is quietly said to belong to the arhat alone, meaning the passage's own count of nine fruits actually implies all four noble fruits together even though only the first three are named outright, a subtle way of completing the full path without spelling out every step. He treats this section's own closing move toward a fivefold summary as characteristic of the entire sutra's own method throughout this exposition, building up an intricate structure of causes and fruits piece by piece before finally gathering everything into one compact, memorable framework a practitioner could actually hold in mind during daily practice.

30. Section: Five Kinds of Purity Gathered Together, and a Second Sutra Opens

The Sutra of Mindfulness Carried with the Body now gathers its own teaching into the five kinds of purity announced earlier: purity of the ground even without full concentration, achieved through the six mental applications curing the four secondary afflictions; purity of the ground of concentration itself, achieved through attaining the four absorptions' own joy, freedom from joy, and pure bliss, together with rightly knowing the impermanence of beings and formations; purity of what is gathered, achieved through the threefold basis of requisites, the higher training in mind, and the higher training in wisdom; purity not shared with non-Buddhist paths, achieved through the three distinguishing causes of complete requisites, freedom from craving toward calm-abiding, and genuine aspiration toward nirvana; and purity of the fruits not shared with non-Buddhist paths, the fourfold, fivefold, and ninefold fruits already described. The sutra is said to take its very name, mindfulness carried with the body, precisely because this entire fivefold purity follows and is embraced by the practitioner's own body and mind as they actually turn moment to moment through practice. A second sutra then opens for citation and explanation, the Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra, corresponding to the Madhyama Āgama's own Great Koṭṭhita Sutra, a dialogue exploring two kinds of liberation in careful and extensive detail: liberation through wisdom, and liberation of mind, each of which unfolds in turn into a mundane and a transcendent form, each with its own distinct scope, essence, and fruit.

Chinese Source

謂不定地清淨故、定地清淨故、攝清淨故、不共清淨故、不共果清淨故。又隨逐身轉故，彼所攝受故，名隨身念。復次，當釋摩訶俱瑟祉羅經。有二解脫。一、慧解脫，二、心解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely purity of the ground even without concentration, purity of the ground of concentration, purity of what is gathered, purity not shared with others, and purity of fruits not shared with others. Because it follows the body's own turning and is embraced by it, it is named mindfulness carried with the body. The Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra shall now be explained. There are two kinds of liberation: liberation through wisdom, and liberation of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sutra's own explanation of its name as a fitting close to this entire lengthy exposition, mindfulness carried with the body meaning simply that genuine practice is not an abstract doctrine held apart from daily life but something that follows and embraces the practitioner's own body and mind exactly as they move through the world. He notes that the Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra now opening corresponds to an actual dialogue preserved in the Madhyama Āgama, and looks forward to its wisdom-liberation and mind-liberation framework as offering this fascicle's own most systematic account yet of how mundane and transcendent practice genuinely differ, not merely in degree but in kind.

31. Section: Mundane Wisdom, Mundane Mind, and the Fruit They Share

Mundane wisdom-liberation is explained first: the worldly right view of one still possessing outflow, drawing on the wisdom that abides in the nature of things, correctly establishing what unwholesome conduct is and what wholesome conduct is, together with the causes and conditions, greed, hatred, and delusion, or their wholesome opposites, that give rise to each, this being both the very scope such wisdom operates within and its own essential nature, a genuine but still worldly right view capable of subduing manifest affliction from the desire realm up through, though not beyond, the base of neither perception nor non-perception. Mundane mind-liberation is explained next: through this same worldly wisdom, knowing that the mind itself is the very basis upon which either defilement or purity depends, since mind alone actually takes in and discerns every object of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch encountered, a practitioner is able to bring correct attention to these objects without affliction arising in response, mind held apart from defiled mental factors even while still bound within the world. Together, these two kinds of mundane liberation yield a shared mundane fruit: freedom from the manifest activity of affliction from the desire realm's own defilement up through the base of neither perception nor non-perception, precepts kept genuinely pure, and the actual attainment of the form and formless realms' own worldly absorptions, though without cutting affliction's underlying seed or ever truly leaving the three realms behind.

Chinese Source

此中依慧解脫，謂世間慧之所行者，當知即是建立惡行及與善行并彼因緣。彼體性者，當知即是惡行、善行并彼因緣，善不善等體性差別如實正智。又依心解脫，謂心染淨之所依者，當知即是色等境界能取了別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, regarding wisdom-liberation, the scope of worldly wisdom is establishing unwholesome and wholesome conduct together with their causes. Its essence is the wisdom that truly knows, as they actually are, the distinct natures of what is wholesome and unwholesome, together with unwholesome and wholesome conduct and their causes. Regarding mind-liberation, that mind is the very basis of defilement and purity means that it is mind which takes in and discerns objects of form and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Madhyama Āgama's own preserved dialogue directly, quoting Śāriputra's question to Mahākoṭṭhita about what unwholesomeness and its root actually are, and Mahākoṭṭhita's own reply naming the three unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental deeds as unwholesomeness itself, and greed, hatred, and delusion as its root, Śāriputra's own delighted approval following immediately after, a genuinely warm exchange between two senior disciples that this technical passage draws its entire framework from. He notes that mundane mind-liberation's own bar, correct attention without arising affliction, describes real but limited progress, since the underlying tendency toward affliction remains fully intact beneath this outward composure.

32. Section: Transcendent Wisdom, Transcendent Mind, and Freedom from Every Bond

Transcendent wisdom-liberation is explained in turn: undefiled wisdom directly realizing the very nature of phenomena, attaining the fruit of the noble path and the wisdom of nirvana itself, freeing the mind from both the bondage of affliction and the bondage of characteristics, this realization arising precisely through the enhancing power of the mundane wisdom and mind-liberation already described, its essence being non-discriminating wisdom itself, and its scope being suchness directly, without any intervening concept standing between the mind and reality. Transcendent mind-liberation follows: through the enhancing power of a genuinely purified mind, the five sense faculties no longer generate any defilement whatsoever in response to their five respective objects, and all six sense faculties alike become pure, this same purification culminating, at its highest, in the very attainment of the cessation of feeling and perception. Together, these two kinds of transcendent liberation yield a shared transcendent fruit far surpassing the mundane: complete freedom from every bond of affliction and every bond of characteristics across the entirety of the three realms, without exception, a liberation that genuinely leaves saṃsāra behind rather than merely holding its manifest activity temporarily in check.

Chinese Source

出世間慧解脫，指由無漏慧證法性，證得聖道果，成就涅槃智，令心解脫煩惱縛及相縛。出世間心解脫，由清淨心增上力故，令五根於五塵境不生雜染，及令六根清淨；最高能入滅盡定。出世間慧、心二種解脫所得果，能令行者解脫三界一切煩惱縛及相縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Transcendent wisdom-liberation is undefiled wisdom realizing the nature of phenomena, attaining the fruit of the noble path, accomplishing the wisdom of nirvana, and freeing the mind from the bondage of affliction and of characteristics. Transcendent mind-liberation is, through the enhancing power of a purified mind, causing the five faculties to give rise to no defilement toward their five objects, and purifying all six faculties, culminating in entry into the cessation of feeling and perception. The fruit of these two transcendent liberations together is freeing the practitioner from every bond of affliction and of characteristics across the three realms.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the phrase bondage of characteristics alongside bondage of affliction as this passage's own reminder that liberation must free the mind not only from raw affliction itself but from the subtler bondage of conceptual signs and categories the mind habitually imposes on experience, which is why transcendent wisdom's own scope is suchness directly rather than any refined concept about suchness. He treats the cessation of feeling and perception's own appearance here, as the very culmination of transcendent mind-liberation, as confirming its status as this entire meditative path's own highest possible attainment, already discussed earlier in this fascicle as reserved even by an advanced bodhisattva out of compassion rather than incapacity.

33. Section: What Wisdom Truly Knows — the Four Noble Truths Themselves

The shared fruit of mundane wisdom and mind-liberation is now stated more precisely: freedom specifically from the desire realm's own lowest class of defilement, both the affliction directly associated with mind and the further affliction that intermingles with it through repeated contact with sense objects, a genuine but still limited freedom that leaves the deeper bonds of the higher realms untouched. Transcendent wisdom's own scope is then given directly, in the sutra's own voice: asked what wisdom itself actually is, Mahākoṭṭhita answers that wisdom is simply knowing, and what it knows is this, knowing suffering exactly as it truly is, knowing the origin of suffering, the cessation of suffering, and the path to the cessation of suffering exactly as they truly are, the four noble truths themselves forming wisdom's own complete and entire object. Asked further what value, what excellence, and what merit wisdom actually holds, Mahākoṭṭhita answers that wisdom carries the meaning of weariness with the world, the meaning of freedom from desire, and the meaning of seeing reality exactly as it is, to which Śāriputra responds only with delight, excellent, excellent. The essence of right view itself is then unified: whether arising as mundane or as transcendent wisdom, both are, in their simplest and most general description, called nothing more nor less than right view itself, the mundane form drawing on the wisdom that abides in the nature of things, the transcendent form drawing on the wisdom of nirvana directly.

Chinese Source

彼世間慧心解脫果，當知即是於彼相應、相雜下類雜染出故。即慧解脫增上力故，出世間慧之所行者，當知即是由世間慧及心解脫增上力故，如實了知一切境界。此中若世間慧、若出世慧，總略為一，說名正見。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fruit of mundane wisdom and mind-liberation is being freed from the desire realm's own lowest class of defilement, both what is associated and what is intermingled with it. Through the enhancing power of wisdom-liberation, the scope of transcendent wisdom is truly knowing every object as it is, through the enhancing power of mundane wisdom and mind-liberation. Among these, whether mundane wisdom or transcendent wisdom, both are, in brief, together called right view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi quotes the Madhyama Āgama's own dialogue directly at this point, Śāriputra asking Mahākoṭṭhita what wisdom is, and Mahākoṭṭhita answering simply that wisdom is knowing, knowing the four truths of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path exactly as they are, a definition he calls strikingly economical for so vast a concept. He treats wisdom's own threefold value, weariness with the world, freedom from desire, and seeing reality truly, as this dialogue's own compact summary of the entire spiritual path in three words, disenchantment leading naturally to renunciation, renunciation opening naturally into clear seeing.

34. Section: Two Causes of Right View, and Why Cessation Has No Counterpart

The cause of right view, mundane or transcendent alike, is given directly: a buddha's own appearance in the world, hearing the true Dharma once a buddha has appeared, and reflecting on it without any distortion, since without a buddha's own appearance no being could ever come to know wholesome from unwholesome, or the four truths, on their own. The sutra's own dialogue confirms this exactly: asked how many causes and conditions give rise to right view, Mahākoṭṭhita answers there are two, hearing from another and correct reflection within oneself, nothing more. The fruit of transcendent right view is then examined with unusual precision: nirvana itself, the very fruit realized, is said to be without any counterpart at all, remaining constant before and after without change, precisely because it is unconditioned, neither arising nor ceasing, untouched by whether the affliction it corresponds to is present or absent, or whether the realizing wisdom that discloses it has yet arisen or not, exactly as Mahākoṭṭhita confirms when asked directly whether cessation itself has any counterpart at all, and answers simply that cessation has none. The wisdom that actually realizes nirvana, by contrast, does have a counterpart, since as a conditioned state of mind it genuinely arises and ceases in direct correspondence with whether the affliction it counters is still present, continuing without interruption for one still in training, and arising anew the moment one passes from training into its completion.

Chinese Source

正見因緣，當知即是有佛出世、聽聞正法、無倒思惟。又出世間正見果相，當知即是前後常故，無所對故。若法，所治若有，生已無滅；所治若無，滅已有生，彼法名有所對。若法，所治若有若無、若生不生，常時是有，彼法名無所對。

Ru-Yi Translation

The cause of right view is a buddha's appearance in the world, hearing the true Dharma, and correct reflection without distortion. The fruit-characteristic of transcendent right view is remaining constant before and after, without any counterpart. A phenomenon that, once the affliction it corresponds to still exists, arises and continues without ceasing, or once that affliction is exhausted, having ceased then arises anew, has a counterpart. A phenomenon that remains constantly existent regardless of whether the affliction it corresponds to exists or not, or whether the corresponding wisdom has arisen or not, has no counterpart.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Mahākoṭṭhita's own laconic answer, cessation has no counterpart, as one of the sutra's most philosophically loaded single lines, quietly distinguishing nirvana itself, wholly unconditioned and therefore unchanging regardless of anything else, from even the noble wisdom that realizes it, which remains a conditioned mental event still subject to arising and ceasing. He notes that this same distinction quietly resolves a question a careless reader might otherwise raise, why would a fully liberated being's own wisdom still seem to arise and continue, when the goal itself, nirvana, plainly does not, since goal and the mind that finally sees it are not, on this analysis, the same kind of thing at all.

35. Section: Mind as the Newspaper's Editor, and Why a Lamp's Flame Depends on Its Own Light

The purity of this transcendent fruit is explained through the very relationship between mind and the five senses: through the enhancing power of a genuinely liberated mind, the physical sense faculties no longer generate defilement toward their own respective objects, and every faculty becomes purified in turn, because mind itself, the sutra explains directly, is what ultimately receives everything the five senses each separately gather. Asked how five faculties, each operating differently and each perceiving its own distinct field, nonetheless have their combined output received and depended upon by something, Mahākoṭṭhita answers plainly that mind receives it all, and mind is what they depend upon, exactly as a modern reader might picture five field reporters, sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch, each filing separate dispatches from their own separate beat, while mind alone sits as the editor who receives every report and assembles them into one coherent story. The very continuation of life within the deepest of all meditative states, the cessation of feeling and perception, is explained through an equally vivid image: life force and bodily warmth are shown to depend upon each other in turn, sustaining one another the entire time such absorption lasts, exactly as a lamp's oil and wick together produce a flame, and that flame in turn produces light, so that because of life there is warmth, and because of warmth there is life, precisely as flame depends on light and light depends on flame, neither able to exist for a moment without the other.

Chinese Source

又即此果，由心解脫增上力故，於自所行及彼所依得清淨者，當知即是由清淨心增上力故，於諸色根所行境界不生雜染，及令諸根順清淨故。又解脫心住者，當知即是壽之與煖展轉相依相屬而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

This same fruit, through the enhancing power of mind-liberation, achieves purity in its own field of activity and in what it depends upon, which is, through the enhancing power of a purified mind, that the physical sense faculties no longer give rise to defilement toward their objects, and every faculty is brought into accord with purity. The abiding of the liberated mind should be known as life force and warmth sustaining one another in turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the modern Buddhist scholar Yìnshùn's own gloss on this passage directly, mind as the newspaper's editorial desk gathering dispatches filed separately by five field reporters, sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch, each restricted to its own single beat yet all of it eventually landing on the same desk to be assembled into one coherent account of experience, a simile he finds remarkably apt for explaining how six distinct faculties nonetheless produce one unified stream of awareness. He calls the oil-lamp image for life force and warmth this entire sutra's own most beautiful passage, since it explains, through a wholly ordinary household object, how the body can remain genuinely alive even while every ordinary mental activity has entirely ceased within the deepest meditative attainment, life and warmth simply sustaining each other the way flame and light always do.

36. Section: Ignorance That Breeds Rebirth, Wisdom That Ends It

The difference between the mundane and the transcendent fruit of liberation is now drawn directly, through the very feelings a being experiences and the causes and conditions that give rise to them: wherever delusion and craving remain present within contact and the feeling it produces, further rebirth into future existence is bound to follow, but wherever delusion and craving are genuinely absent, no further rebirth follows at all. The sutra's own dialogue makes this precise: asked what actually gives rise to future existence, Mahākoṭṭhita answers that an ignorant, unlearned ordinary being, covered over by ignorance and bound by the fetter of craving, never encountering a genuine spiritual friend and never knowing or following the noble Dharma, is precisely what gives rise to future existence; asked in turn what prevents future existence from arising at all, he answers that once ignorance has been exhausted and clear knowledge has arisen, suffering itself is thereby brought to its complete end, and this alone is what prevents any further existence from arising.

Chinese Source

又此世間及出世間二種解脫果差別者，當知即是於諸受中及因緣中，有癡愛者後有當生，無癡愛者後有不生。

Ru-Yi Translation

The difference between the fruit of the mundane and the transcendent kinds of liberation is this: among the feelings and their causes and conditions, one in whom delusion and craving remain will give rise to future existence; one in whom delusion and craving are absent will not give rise to future existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing exchange as the entire Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra's own final, plainest statement of what actually separates the mundane from the transcendent fruit, not any difference in outward conduct or even in meditative depth, but simply whether ignorance and craving still remain present at the very moment feeling arises from contact. He notes the elegant symmetry of Mahākoṭṭhita's two answers, ignorance and craving together generating future existence, and their exhaustion alone preventing it, as confirming that this entire sutra's elaborate framework of mundane and transcendent wisdom and mind-liberation ultimately reduces to this single, decisive point.

37. Section: A Second Meaning of the Fruit's Difference, and Six Kinds of Stillness Begin

A second meaning is given for the very same difference between mundane and transcendent liberation: the mundane fruit remains genuinely intermixed with and associated with affliction, since delusion and craving still color the feeling that arises through contact, while the fruit of transcendent liberation, resting upon a mind whose supporting mental factors are already purified, is no longer stained by any present object at all, receiving even contact born of clarity without any trace of delusion or craving arising in response. A further topic then opens, distinguishing the very way these two kinds of liberation abide, unfolding across six kinds of stillness found only in the deepest of all attainments, the cessation of feeling and perception, and absent from the highest attainment reachable through mundane concentration alone, the base of neither perception nor non-perception. The first stillness concerns consciousness's own settling: in cessation attainment, the six forms of consciousness together with their mental factors genuinely cease and settle into equanimity, not through any dullness or sinking but through a true and even stillness, whereas in the base of neither perception nor non-perception consciousness itself remains subtly active throughout, which is precisely why the sutra's own dialogue distinguishes the two so sharply: a monk who enters cessation attainment has both perception and feeling actually cease, while a monk who enters the base of neither perception nor non-perception has neither perception nor feeling cease at all.

Chinese Source

又彼相雜而相應故，彼能依止心法清淨，當知即是非現在緣之所染汙領受彼故。即二解脫住差別者，當知即是六寂靜故。由清淨識沒平等故，非由彼所依平等故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The mundane fruit remains intermixed and associated with affliction. The dependent mental factors of the transcendent fruit are purified, meaning it is not stained by present objects while receiving them. The difference in how these two liberations abide should be known through six kinds of stillness. Through the settling of purified consciousness into equanimity, not through the settling of the faculties it depends upon.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi quotes the sutra's own precise distinction directly, cessation attainment ending both perception and feeling while the base of neither perception nor non-perception ends neither, as this entire section's own clearest possible line between the very highest mundane attainment and the lowest genuinely transcendent one, a boundary easy to blur from the outside since both appear, superficially, to be states of profound stillness. He notes the commentary's own careful clarification that the first stillness belongs to the six forms of consciousness themselves rather than to the underlying sense faculties, the body remaining physically intact and alive throughout cessation attainment even while every ordinary act of consciousness has genuinely settled.

38. Section: Entering and Leaving Without Effort, Stillness Even in the Body's Own Activity

The second stillness concerns entering cessation attainment itself: unlike ordinary absorption, no deliberate intention such as I shall now enter concentration is needed at all, the practitioner settling into this profound stillness spontaneously, without any effortful application of mind, exactly as the sutra's own dialogue confirms when Mahākoṭṭhita explains that a monk entering cessation attainment does not think, I am now entering cessation attainment, at all, this simply being how the mind was trained beforehand to proceed. The third stillness concerns leaving it: emerging likewise requires no deliberate effort, occurring spontaneously and in a fixed sequence, touching in turn three specific kinds of contact as one emerges, the touch of being unmoved by any trace of pride, the touch of possessing no attachment to any object whatsoever, and the touch of signlessness, remaining free of conceptual elaboration even in the very moment of returning to ordinary awareness. The fourth stillness concerns the state while actually within cessation attainment: the three kinds of activity, bodily, verbal, and mental, all cease to function entirely, in-breath and out-breath ceasing as bodily activity ceases, deliberation and investigation ceasing as verbal activity ceases, and feeling and perception ceasing as mental activity ceases, this genuine and total cessation of activity itself constituting the very stillness of being within the attainment, precisely as Mahākoṭṭhita confirms when he explains that a monk entering cessation attainment first stills bodily activity, then verbal activity, and only then mental activity, in careful and deliberate sequence.

Chinese Source

於入定時，不由加行入寂靜故。於出定時，不由加行出寂靜故，次第出故。於在定時，三行不行得寂靜故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Upon entering, stillness is achieved without effortful application. Upon emerging, stillness is achieved without effortful application, emerging in sequence. While remaining within the attainment, stillness is achieved through the three kinds of activity ceasing to function.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the effortless entry and exit named in the second and third stillness as this attainment's own most counterintuitive feature, since ordinary meditative progress is so often described in terms of sustained, deliberate effort, whereas cessation attainment is instead reached and left the way a well-trained habit simply unfolds on its own, the deliberate work having already been done long before this final moment arrives. He highlights the three touches experienced upon emerging, unmoved by pride, free of attachment, and free of conceptual elaboration, as together describing a mind that returns to ordinary awareness carrying nothing extra with it at all, neither self-satisfaction at what was just accomplished nor any grasping toward what comes next.

39. Section: A Mind Kept Still Whether Among Companions or Crowds

The fifth stillness concerns the mind's own state upon leaving solitude behind, unfolding across three occasions in turn: dwelling in the forest wilderness together with fellow practitioners of pure conduct, the mind remains constantly still, entirely free of the scattering entanglements of ordinary seeing and hearing that worldly company so often stirs up; dwelling in a village or town together with householders, the mind likewise remains constantly still, entirely free of the scattering entanglements that ordinary worldly enjoyment so often produces; and moving between both settings at once, mixing with fellow practitioners and householders alike, the mind remains constantly still in exactly the same way, free of every such entanglement regardless of company. This same constancy across all three occasions is what marks the fifth stillness as belonging uniquely to one who has genuinely attained cessation, since even solitude itself is no longer strictly required to preserve the mind's own settled clarity once this depth has actually been reached.

Chinese Source

於遠離出心寂靜故，謂於三時。一、於阿練若，與同梵行共相雜住；二、於聚落中，與諸在家共相雜住；三、於二處行相現行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Stillness of mind upon leaving solitude is found across three occasions. First, dwelling in the wilderness together with fellow practitioners of pure conduct. Second, dwelling in a village together with householders. Third, the activity of both settings occurring together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifth stillness as a genuinely important corrective to any assumption that physical solitude is itself the mark of spiritual depth, since it describes a mind that stays equally settled whether alone with fellow renunciants in the forest or immersed in an ordinary village's own daily bustle, the stillness belonging to the mind itself rather than to any particular external circumstance surrounding it. He notes the commentary's own careful clarification that what is actually meant by leaving solitude here is a kind of settled mind, not merely a physically isolated body, since physical solitude alone, without this same inner stillness, would offer no real protection against the entanglements the passage names.

40. Section: Three Touches Felt on Returning from the Deepest Stillness

The sixth and final stillness concerns the mind's own state upon approaching the very moment of leaving cessation attainment, unfolding across three occasions in turn, each marked by its own distinct touch. Emerging into a supreme worldly absorption still possessing form, the fourth absorption at its highest, no trace of pride disturbs the mind, no thought such as this self exists or this self shall continue arising at all, and this is called the touch of being unmoved. Emerging into a supreme worldly absorption beyond form altogether, any of the four formless attainments, greed, hatred, and delusion alike fail to arise toward any object whatsoever, and this is called the touch of possessing nothing. And emerging into the transcendent absorption that still possesses active mind, resting in the signless liberation gate itself, no conceptual sign of any kind is entertained at all, and this is called the touch of signlessness. Because a being who has genuinely mastered cessation attainment can move between any of these three concentrations upon emerging, form-realm, formless-realm, or transcendent and signless, the mind remains constantly still across all three occasions of approaching this return, which together complete the sixth and final kind of stillness belonging uniquely to this deepest of all attainments.

Chinese Source

於親近出心寂靜故，謂於三時。一、於有色世間勝定出故；二、於無色世間勝定出故；三、於出世間有心定出故。又此諸定非唯滅定出已方得，亦先已得，今起現前。

Ru-Yi Translation

Stillness of mind upon approaching the exit is found across three occasions: emerging into a supreme worldly concentration still possessing form; emerging into a supreme worldly concentration without form; and emerging into a transcendent concentration still possessing mind. These concentrations are not necessarily newly attained only upon emerging from cessation; they may already have been attained before, and now simply arise again in the present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi acknowledges the extraordinary depth of scholarly debate this single passage on the three touches has generated across the commentarial tradition, Vasumitra and several other Abhidharma masters each proposing slightly different accounts of exactly which concentration corresponds to which touch, and treats this very proliferation of interpretation as itself a sign of how seriously later generations took the precise phenomenology of returning from the deepest possible meditative state. He notes the clarifying point that these three concentrations need not be freshly attained at the moment of emerging at all, since a sufficiently advanced practitioner may simply be returning to an absorption already long familiar, the genuine achievement lying in cessation attainment itself rather than in whatever state one happens to emerge into afterward.

41. Section: How a Concentration with Signs Differs from One Without

A further clarification distinguishes how these various concentrations are actually entered, dwelt within, and left. The first two of the three, form-realm and formless-realm absorption alike, are reached progressively, one stage building upon the last through the nine successive levels of concentration, and their own purity is likewise achieved progressively in just this way. The third, the transcendent absorption still possessing mind, is reached by an entirely different route, not through this same gradual progression at all, but through directly penetrating the signless realm itself, its own purity achieved precisely through this direct penetration rather than through any step-by-step advance. A concentration that possesses conceptual signs, the text further clarifies, is entered, dwelt within, and left entirely through mental application that itself still bears such signs, a consistent mode of engagement from beginning to end. A signless concentration, however, is entered and dwelt within through mental application genuinely free of any sign at all, yet is left, one should know, through mental application that has once again taken up a sign, since ordinary awareness itself cannot resume without some conceptual content returning to it.

Chinese Source

此中前二，由次第定故，展轉獲得勝定清淨。後一，不由次第定故，然由通達無相界故，展轉獲得勝定清淨。又有相定，由有相作意，入、住、出定。若無相定，由無相作意入定、住定；由有相作意，當知出定。

Ru-Yi Translation

Among these, the first two are attained progressively through successive concentration, gradually achieving the purity of supreme concentration. The last is not attained progressively, but through penetrating the signless realm, gradually achieving the purity of supreme concentration in that way. A concentration possessing signs is entered, dwelt within, and left through mental application possessing signs. A signless concentration is entered and dwelt within through signless mental application; one should know it is left through mental application possessing signs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the asymmetry named here, a signless concentration entered and dwelt within without signs yet necessarily left through mental application that has resumed them, as a logically necessary feature rather than any imperfection in the attainment itself, since ordinary conceptual awareness simply cannot resume functioning at all without some sign or content returning to it, however briefly. He calls this entire six-stillness exposition, now essentially complete, this fascicle's own most sustained and technical passage yet, and notes that its underlying purpose throughout has never been mere classification for its own sake, but establishing exactly how the deepest transcendent attainment differs from even the most exalted mundane one, a distinction the Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra has now examined from every conceivable angle.

42. Section: A Third Sutra Opens with Eight Aspects for Asking the Dharma Rightly

The Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra's own exposition closes here, its final passages acknowledging just how much scholarly debate the precise nature of signlessness, and its relationship to cessation attainment, has generated across the Abhidharma tradition, the term signless itself carrying at least five distinct senses across different contexts, the concentration on emptiness, the path of seeing, the unshakeable liberation of mind, the base of neither perception nor non-perception, and signless concentration proper, a genuine complexity this fascicle leaves duly noted rather than fully resolved. A third sutra then opens for citation and explanation, the Sutra of the Causes and Conditions of the Dharma, addressed to any skillful monk or devoted lay follower who wishes to inquire properly into the Abhidharma and the Abhivinaya, the higher teaching and the higher discipline, and who should, the sutra instructs, frame such an inquiry through eight distinct aspects: understanding the actual matter at hand, understanding what is to be overcome, understanding the result of overcoming it, understanding the very essential nature of the path itself, understanding the distinctions among different results, understanding the basis such results depend upon, understanding the virtues that follow, and understanding what genuine realization actually consists of.

Chinese Source

復次，當釋法因緣經。謂於阿毗達摩、阿毗毗奈耶中，善巧苾芻、或鄔波索迦，欲依解了而請問者，當依八相而興請問。何等八相？謂解了事、解了所治、解了果、解了自性、解了果差別、解了所依、解了功德、解了證得。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sutra of the Causes and Conditions of the Dharma shall now be explained. A skillful monk or lay devotee wishing to inquire, with real understanding, into the higher teaching and the higher discipline, should frame the inquiry through eight aspects. What are the eight? Understanding the matter, understanding what is to be overcome, understanding the result, understanding the essential nature, understanding the distinction of results, understanding the basis, understanding the virtues, and understanding the realization.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Mahā-Koṭṭhita Sutra's own closing acknowledgment of unresolved scholarly complexity as an unusually honest note for a treatise otherwise so systematic throughout, a reminder that even within a single unified tradition, terms as central as signlessness could carry genuinely different senses depending on context, a complexity worth naming rather than artificially smoothing over. He calls this third sutra's own eightfold framework a genuinely practical gift to any sincere student, since it transforms the vague impulse to ask a teacher about the Dharma into eight concrete, answerable questions, what exactly is the problem, what must be overcome, what results from overcoming it, and so on in turn.

43. Section: The Five Aggregates, Two Defilements, and the Noble Eightfold Path

The first aspect, understanding the actual matter at hand, is explained as understanding the five aggregates of clinging themselves, form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, the very ground upon which every affliction, every suffering, and every unwholesome deed ultimately depends, exactly as the sutras themselves describe an ignorant, unlearned ordinary being clinging to these five aggregates as self, while a wise and learned noble disciple does not. The second aspect, understanding what is to be overcome, names two kinds of defilement in turn: craving's own defilement, which clings to this present body and to the body one will receive in a future life alike, delighting in whatever pleasant circumstance is encountered or hoped for, and precisely because of this clinging, taking no genuine joy in the very cessation of future existence itself; and view's own defilement, which, even in one who does wish for liberation and genuinely aspires toward it, distorts the understanding of the path through wrong view, whether eternalism or nihilism, so that liberation is desired yet never actually realized. The third aspect, understanding the result, is simply the permanent severing of both these defilements together, craving and view alike, bringing the complete and genuine fruit of the noble path. The fourth aspect, understanding the very essential nature of the path itself, is the noble eightfold path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration, the very road a noble one travels all the way to nirvana.

Chinese Source

解了事者，謂能解了五取蘊故。解了所治者，謂愛雜染及見雜染。由愛雜染故，於後有滅不生欣樂。由見雜染故，雖生欣樂而不能證。解了果者，謂此二種雜染永斷。解了自性者，謂八聖支道。

Ru-Yi Translation

Understanding the matter is understanding the five aggregates of clinging. Understanding what is to be overcome is the defilement of craving and the defilement of view. Through the defilement of craving, one does not delight in the cessation of future existence. Through the defilement of view, even while delighting in it, one cannot realize it. Understanding the result is the permanent severing of these two defilements. Understanding the essential nature is the noble eightfold path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the precise division of labor between craving's defilement and view's defilement as this sutra's own sharpest diagnostic tool, craving explaining why a being fails even to wish for liberation in the first place, clinging too tightly to present and future existence alike, while wrong view explains the different and in some ways more troubling case of a being who genuinely wishes for liberation yet cannot reach it, because the very view guiding the search has gone astray. He notes that naming the eightfold path as the essential nature of the entire path shows this sutra's own confidence that, whatever its surrounding technical apparatus, the actual practice it points toward remains exactly what the Buddha's very first teaching already named.

44. Section: Three Aggregates Behind Eight Path-Factors, and the Fruits of Severing Defilement

The eightfold path, explained many ways elsewhere throughout this treatise, is here explained through its own founding purpose and internal structure: established specifically to counter every mistaken path taught outside the Buddha's own teaching, and unfolding into three further aggregates precisely in order to counter the three kinds of defilement already explored at such length across this fascicle and those before it, affliction, karma, and rebirth in turn. Right speech, right action, and right livelihood together form the aggregate of precepts, countering unwholesome conduct itself; right mindfulness and right concentration together form the aggregate of concentration, countering the desire realm's own defiling objects; and right view, right intention, and right effort together form the aggregate of wisdom, countering wrong view directly, this entire eightfold path belonging to the transcendent conditioned teachings. The fifth aspect, understanding the distinctions among different results, is simply the various kinds of severing already described earlier in this fascicle, cessation through antidote, cessation through non-manifestation, and cessation through abandonment, together with the further threefold analysis of complete severing by non-manifestation, by realm, and by matter, this entire class of result belonging instead to the transcendent unconditioned, the very cessation truth itself.

Chinese Source

此復種種差別宣說。對治外道諸邪道故，名八聖支道。對治三種雜染故，建立三蘊。謂對治惡行雜染故、對治諸欲雜染故、對治諸見雜染故。是出世間有為所攝。解了果差別者，謂諸斷滅。是出世間無為所攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is explained through various distinctions. It is called the noble eightfold path because it counters every wrong path of non-Buddhist teachings. Three aggregates are established to counter three kinds of defilement: countering the defilement of unwholesome conduct, countering the defilement of desire, and countering the defilement of views. This belongs to the transcendent conditioned. Understanding the distinction of results is the various kinds of severing. This belongs to the transcendent unconditioned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the mapping of precepts, concentration, and wisdom directly onto affliction, karma, and rebirth defilement as this entire treatise's own quiet summary statement, tying its most abstract eightfold path teaching back to the very three-part structure that has organized everything discussed since fascicle 58's own opening pages. He highlights the distinction between the path itself, classified as conditioned, and the result of severing defilement, classified as unconditioned, as a precise reminder that practice and its fruit belong to two genuinely different categories of existence, the path something one actively does, the result something that simply is, once defilement has truly been left behind.

45. Section: Concentration's Own Lineage, Signs, Objects, and Requisites

The sixth aspect, understanding the basis all these results depend upon, names concentration itself, unfolding into four further considerations. First, understanding by way of lineage: every concentration whatsoever belongs to the very lineage of meditative absorption, so that whatever factors a given absorption is said to possess, the first absorption's investigation, reflection, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness, the second absorption's inner clarity, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness, and so through the third and fourth, can all be genuinely understood once this shared lineage itself is understood. Second, understanding by way of characteristic: the coarser and subtler activities involved in entering absorption, the signs that arise just before entry, and the distinct feelings experienced once absorption is actually reached. Third, understanding by way of the object that gives rise to concentration: the pervasive object, the five purifying objects matched to the five different temperaments prone to greed, hatred, delusion, pride, or distraction, skillful objects, and the four noble truths themselves as the object that purifies affliction directly. Fourth, understanding by way of the causes and conditions that actually accomplish concentration: the requisites of the concentration ground already described earlier in this fascicle, aspiration for wholesome states, precepts, guarding the senses, moderation in food, wakefulness, right knowing, good companionship, and unobstructed hearing and reflection among them.

Chinese Source

解了所依者，謂三摩地。此復四種應知。一、由種姓故，謂所有定一切皆由靜慮種姓，隨所宣說諸靜慮支皆能解了。二、由相故。三、由生所緣相故。四、由成辦因緣相故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Understanding the basis is understanding concentration itself, which should be known through four further aspects. First, by way of lineage, all concentration belongs to the lineage of meditative absorption, through which every stated factor of absorption can be understood. Second, by way of characteristic. Third, by way of the object giving rise to it. Fourth, by way of the causes and conditions that accomplish it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this fourfold breakdown of concentration itself, lineage, characteristic, object, and accomplishing cause, gathers together threads scattered across this entire fascicle into one compact checklist, the very factors distinguishing each absorption, the felt qualities of entering and dwelling within it, the specific objects suited to different temperaments, and the daily requisites that make any of it possible in the first place. He treats the sutra's own insistence on naming a distinct purifying object for each of the five different temperaments prone to greed, hatred, delusion, pride, or distraction as a practical reminder that no single meditation object suits every practitioner equally, real progress depending on matching the antidote correctly to one's own particular tendency.

46. Section: What the Buddha Himself First Contemplated About Feeling

The seventh aspect, understanding the virtues that follow, points back to the cessation of feeling and perception already explained at length, the very first and foremost among every abiding available to one liberated through wisdom alone or liberated in both ways together, its own virtues unfolding across the five aspects already described earlier in this fascicle, the settling of consciousness, its resemblance to nirvana's own image, the causes for entering it, the causes for leaving it, and the virtues that follow upon leaving. The eighth and final aspect, understanding what genuine realization actually consists of, is explained through eight ways of observing feeling directly across the five aggregates of clinging: observing feeling's own nature, observing the cause of its present arising, observing the cause of its present cessation, observing the cause of its future arising, observing the cause of its future cessation, observing the cause behind that very arising-cause, observing the cause behind that very cessation-cause, and observing cessation itself, an eightfold contemplation the sutras themselves attribute to the Buddha Vipaśyin's own reflection before his awakening, sitting alone in solitude and asking exactly these same questions: what is feeling, what is its arising, what is its cessation, what is the path leading to its arising, what is the path leading to its cessation, what is feeling's flavor, what is its danger, and what is release from it, and through this very reflection coming to know that there are three feelings, pleasant, painful, and neither, that contact's own arising is feeling's arising and contact's own cessation is feeling's cessation.

Chinese Source

解了功德者，謂滅盡定最第一住，說名解了功德。解了證得者，謂於五取蘊以八種相觀察諸受。謂觀察自性、觀察現法轉因緣、觀察現法轉滅因緣、觀察當來轉因緣、觀察彼轉滅因緣、觀察轉因緣因緣、觀察還因緣因緣，及觀察還。

Ru-Yi Translation

Understanding the virtues is understanding that the cessation attainment is the very first among abidings. Understanding the realization is observing feeling across the five aggregates of clinging through eight aspects: observing its nature, the cause of its present arising, the cause of its present cessation, the cause of its future arising, the cause of its future cessation, the cause of that arising-cause, the cause of that cessation-cause, and observing cessation itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the direct attribution of this eightfold contemplation to the Buddha Vipaśyin's own reflection before awakening as one of this entire fascicle's most moving moments, an ancient buddha, still unenlightened, sitting alone and simply asking the most basic possible questions about feeling itself, what it is, where it comes from, and how it ends, the same questions this whole elaborate treatise ultimately circles back to answering in full. He notes that mapping these eight observations directly onto the four noble truths, feeling's nature as suffering, contact as its origin, contact's cessation as its own cessation, and the path as what accomplishes this, shows that even the Buddha's own path to awakening began with exactly the same fourfold structure taught to every later disciple.

47. Section: Four Ways Things Pair Off, Closing the Third Sutra

A further clarification closes this eighth aspect, distinguishing the very sense in which paired terms are said to correspond to one another, through four distinct kinds of pairing. The first, pairing by differing kind within the same class, is how pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling stand opposed to one another, each a feeling yet each genuinely different in kind. The second, pairing by shared companionship, is how neutral feeling, itself the subtle suffering of formations, pairs with ignorance, since neutral feeling accompanies every feeling exactly as ignorance accompanies every affliction, each serving as the other's constant companion. The third, pairing of antidote to what it cures, is how clear knowing pairs with ignorance, the transcendent wisdom that actually overcomes delusion standing opposed to the very delusion it overcomes. And the fourth, pairing by cause and effect, is how clear knowing pairs with nirvana itself, the wisdom of the path serving as cause and the cessation of nirvana following as its own fruit. With this fourfold analysis of pairing complete, the Sutra of the Causes and Conditions of the Dharma, and its eight aspects for properly inquiring into the Dharma, reaches its own close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing fourfold analysis of pairing as a fittingly systematic conclusion to a sutra that has, from its very first aspect, insisted on precise and careful understanding rather than vague impression, even the relationship between paired concepts themselves receiving this same careful sorting. He notes that the third sutra now closes having covered, across its eight aspects, essentially the entire path in miniature, the aggregates that are clung to, the defilements to be overcome, the result of overcoming them, the path itself, its distinct fruits, its meditative basis, its highest virtue, and finally how a practitioner comes to realize all of it directly through observing feeling exactly as the Buddha himself once did.

48. Section: Ten Ways to Chase Off Sleep, and Five Ways to Keep What Concentration Is Won

A fourth sutra opens for citation, the Sleep Sutra, corresponding to the Madhyama Āgama's own Elder's Sleep Sutra, named either because it removes the very hindrance of drowsiness that concentration requires overcoming, or, as Kuījī explains, because sleep itself becomes the very gateway through which absorption is entered. Monks earnestly cultivating the inner stillness of calm-abiding, the sutra explains, should know five distinct antidotes for cutting off the secondary afflictions already described throughout this fascicle: removing the very sign of darkness through the contemplation of light, whether light that counters literal darkness, starlight and moonlight for the night, sunlight for an overcast day, firelight or lamplight for an enclosed room, the light of the Dharma itself that dispels ignorance and doubt about the aggregates and the Three Jewels alike, or the light naturally arising from a heavenly being's own karmic fruit, perfecting and purifying the first absorption; skillfully rousing a sinking mind through joyful, uplifting reflection, recollecting the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, giving, precepts, or the heavens themselves at exactly the moment drowsiness threatens to take hold; damaging wrong views directly, cutting off clinging to mistaken doctrine; damaging the very function such views produce, cutting off attachment to false austerities like an ox's or dog's own vow; and damaging the very basis such views rest upon, cutting off the self-view that underlies every mistaken conviction in the first place. The Madhyama Āgama itself lists ten concrete methods for chasing off drowsiness in turn: no longer dwelling on the drowsy image itself; reciting and reviewing what has already been learned; teaching that same learning to another in detail; turning it over silently in one's own mind; pulling both ears firmly with the hands; washing the face and eyes with cold water and sprinkling the body; rising to look at the stars and their own light after washing the eyes; walking in the open air with the senses carefully guarded; sitting cross-legged at the walking path's own end; and finally, returning indoors to lie on the right side in a lion's own posture, one foot resting upon the other, contemplating light, holding mindfulness and clear awareness, and forming a deliberate intention to rise again before sleep is allowed to overtake the mind at all.

Chinese Source

謂勤修習內心寂靜奢摩他行諸苾芻等，為欲斷除諸隨煩惱，應知五種對治之相。謂遠離闇相；於能隨順舉歡喜法，發生正舉加行道理；損害諸見；諸見功用；諸見所依功用。

Ru-Yi Translation

Monks earnestly cultivating the inner stillness of calm-abiding should know five antidotes for cutting off the secondary afflictions: removing the sign of darkness; rousing the mind through joyful reflection, giving rise to the principle of properly uplifting effort; damaging wrong views; damaging their function; and damaging their basis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls the ten concrete methods for overcoming drowsiness this entire fascicle's own most immediately usable piece of practical instruction, moving from purely mental techniques, reciting, teaching, reflecting, through simple physical ones, pulling the ears, washing with cold water, to finally a specific and considered posture for sleep itself, should sleep genuinely become unavoidable, each step escalating only as far as actually needed. He notes that even lying down to sleep is not exempted from mindful intention here, the lion's posture, deliberate foot placement, contemplation of light, and a formed intention to rise again all turning even rest itself into an extension of practice rather than a simple abandonment of it.

49. Section: Five Ways to Hold On to Concentration Once It Is Won

Once these five antidotes have fully cut off the secondary afflictions, the Sleep Sutra continues, five further methods sustain the concentration thereby attained. The first, solitude in its every form, bodily solitude, mental solitude, freedom from mixing improperly with others, freedom from rowdy company, and freedom from affliction itself, gathers together everything already said about withdrawal throughout this fascicle. The second, dwelling in a truly remote place while hearing teachings that accord with concentration, means actually living in surroundings suited to meditative practice while continuing to hear the Dharma expounded on exactly this subject. The third, requisites of life that give rise to no defiled mind, means keeping food, clothing, bedding, and medicine reduced to the barest sufficient minimum, so that little time is spent pursuing more than what genuine practice actually requires. The fourth, receiving instruction and admonition in beautiful language from a wise fellow practitioner of pure conduct, points to the real importance of a genuine spiritual friend, one already experienced in exactly this same path, whose guidance in concentration and wisdom, and whose correction of any fault in body, speech, or mind, helps keep the precepts, concentration, and wisdom already gained genuinely pure. The fifth, freeing the mind of craving toward every kind of worldly stabilizing and culminating absorption while bringing correct attention to undefiled practice, means never lingering contentedly within a merely mundane level of concentration, but continuing forward to cultivate the four noble truths and other undefiled contemplations directly, so that whatever concentration has already been reached does not decline for want of further progress. With this, the Sleep Sutra, and the fourth and final of the sutras cited in this entire fascicle, comes to its own close.

彼隨煩惱既斷滅已，復有五種任持定法。何等為五？謂諸遠離；遠離處所、順定言說；順無染心資生眾具；從有智者同梵行所，獲得隨順教授教誡美妙言說；於諸世間等持、等至遠離愛味，及無漏行如理作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Once these secondary afflictions have been cut off, there are five further methods for sustaining concentration. What are the five? Solitude in every form; dwelling in a remote place while hearing teachings that accord with concentration; requisites of life giving rise to no defiled mind; receiving instruction and admonition in beautiful language from a wise fellow practitioner of pure conduct; and freeing the mind of craving toward worldly stabilizing and culminating absorption while bringing correct attention to undefiled practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth method, a wise fellow practitioner's own instruction, as this closing list's own quiet acknowledgment that no amount of solitude, simplicity, or personal effort fully substitutes for genuine spiritual companionship, since even the most disciplined practitioner still needs correction from someone who has already walked the same path. He notes that the fifth method's own warning against lingering in mundane concentration closes all four sutras cited across this fascicle on precisely the note they all share, that concentration itself, however genuinely attained, remains only a means, its entire value found in the transcendent wisdom it makes possible rather than in any pleasure or accomplishment belonging to concentration alone.
