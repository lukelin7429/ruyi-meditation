Fascicle 63 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十三 · The Ground of Meditative Equipoise Continues

Five Weighty Faults That Keep the Mind from Settling

Having spent fascicle 62 on everything that accords with genuine concentration, the treatise now turns to its opposite: the specific faults, coarse and subtle alike, that actively prevent a sincere practitioner from ever reaching it, and the concrete forms of solitude that counter them one by one.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 63 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Five Ways Concentration Is Actively Opposed, and Three Kinds of Solitude That Cure Them

Maitreya marks the structure directly: decisive discernment of the Ground of Meditative Equipoise unfolds across three sub-topics, dharmas that accord with or oppose concentration, the four absorptions themselves, and various further distinctions of kind, and having completed the first of these, according with concentration, across the whole of fascicle 62, the treatise now turns to its second half, what actually opposes concentration. Five dharmas are named as directly opposed to attaining absorption: transgressing the precepts, since impure conduct closes off the very possibility of genuine stillness; lacking uninterrupted effort, practicing only sporadically rather than day after day without a break; lacking earnest, thoroughgoing effort, never bringing genuine seriousness and care to the practice actually undertaken; the presence of sinking, a subtle dulling in which the mind, even while remaining outwardly calm, loses its own clarity toward the object of meditation, distinct from ordinary dullness and drowsiness in that it may still feel tranquil while quietly losing its own sharpness of focus; and disturbance from external sources, noise and distraction by day, insects and discomfort by night, fear of danger, scarcity of basic necessities, or the company of unwholesome friends rather than good ones. Three kinds of solitude are then given as the direct antidote to all five: solitude of place, dwelling somewhere genuinely free of external disturbance; solitude of view, holding correct views so precepts remain naturally pure; and solitude of hearing, listening to genuine instruction on concentration itself, which alone corrects the lack of uninterrupted effort, the lack of earnest effort, and the subtle presence of sinking.

Chinese Source

復有五種定相違法。一、毀犯禁戒，二、無無間加行，三、無殷重加行，四、有沈沒，五、他所擾惱。復有三種遠離。一、住處遠離，二、見遠離，三、聞遠離。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also five dharmas opposed to concentration: transgressing the precepts, lacking uninterrupted effort, lacking earnest effort, the presence of sinking, and disturbance by others. There are also three kinds of solitude: solitude of place, solitude of view, and solitude of hearing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the classical distinction between sinking and ordinary dullness at some length, dullness leaving the mind with neither clarity nor stillness at all, while sinking may still feel calm and even lucid on its surface while quietly losing its grip on the object of meditation, a distinction he considers essential for any practitioner trying to diagnose their own meditation honestly rather than mistaking a subtly unproductive calm for genuine progress. He treats the threefold solitude offered here as directly matched to the five faults just named, place countering outside disturbance, view countering impurity of precepts, and hearing genuine instruction countering the remaining three faults of inconsistency, half-heartedness, and sinking all at once.

2. Section: Five Weighty Faults That Burden the Purifying Mind

A further topic opens, turning specifically to a monk earnestly practicing purification of mind, for whom five further faults are named, each carrying a particularly heavy and persistent weight against genuine stability of concentration: anger, pride, sensual craving, the view of a real self, and an inability to endure. These five are called weighty specifically because each corresponds to and directly obstructs one of five equally weighty qualities that stable concentration itself would otherwise possess, the very obstruction itself, on the one side, being every bit as heavy and persistent as the deeply rooted excellent quality it prevents from arising, on the other.

Chinese Source

復次，心清淨行苾芻，略有五種等持相違厚重過失，能為定障。一、忿，二、慢，三、欲貪，四、薩迦耶見，五、不能堪忍。有五厚重三摩地相，由彼於此障礙而住，是故說名厚重過失。云何五相？

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, a monk practicing purity of mind has, briefly, five weighty faults opposed to concentration, able to obstruct absorption: anger, pride, craving for sensual desire, the view of a real self, and inability to endure. There are five weighty characteristics of concentration which these faults obstruct by dwelling in opposition to them; this is why they are called weighty faults. What are the five characteristics?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this second list of five faults narrows the focus considerably from the first, moving from fairly external and circumstantial obstacles, precepts, effort, and disturbance, to faults rooted deep in the character of the practitioner themselves, anger, pride, craving, self-view, and simple lack of endurance, each one a persistent disposition rather than a passing circumstance. He calls the term weighty itself an apt description of exactly this difference, a merely occasional distraction being comparatively light and easily addressed, while these five, left unaddressed, settle in and quietly hold a practitioner back for a very long time indeed.

3. Section: Five Weighty Qualities, and How Anger Alone Undoes Them

The five weighty qualities that stable concentration itself possesses are now named in turn: contentment with whatever requisites happen to be obtained, so that one of few desires easily finds the very stillness concentration requires; genuine delight in seeking every wholesome quality, a delight that itself drives sustained practice forward; no attachment whatsoever to body or wealth, leaving ample time free for practice rather than for their upkeep or pursuit; seeing great fault in birth and death and supreme excellence in nirvana, a correct view without which no genuine stability can be reached at all; and patient endurance of whatever hardship sustained effort itself demands, whether from posture, from exhaustion, or from meager requisites, remaining unmoved and undiscouraged regardless. Detailed exposition then turns to the first weighty fault directly opposing all this, anger: a monk who goes on alms round yet receives nothing, or too little, or something coarse, or receives it slowly, or is treated without respect, and in response knits the brow and burns with resentment, thinking how could this donor treat me so, gives rise from this very anger to thoughts of malice and harm toward others, dwelling on them again and again, and because of this, whatever excellent concentration was not yet attained fails to arise at all, and whatever concentration had already been attained falls away once more.

Chinese Source

一者、獲得隨宜資具便生喜足；二者、好樂求諸善法；三、於身財無所顧戀；四、於生死及與涅槃，見大過失、最勝功德；五、於加行堪忍勤苦。此中忿者，謂懷忿故，若往他家不得利養，或得而少，或弊或遲，或不恭敬，由此便生顰蹙憤慼。從此因緣，發恚尋思及害尋思，多隨尋伺。彼由此故，先所未生勝三摩地不能得生，設彼已生還復退失。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, contentment upon receiving whatever requisites are obtained. Second, delight in seeking every wholesome dharma. Third, no attachment toward body or wealth. Fourth, seeing great fault in birth and death and supreme virtue in nirvana. Fifth, patient endurance of hardship in effort. Among these, anger means that, holding anger, one who goes to another's house and receives no support, or little, or coarse, or slow, or disrespectful support, gives rise to a scowling resentment. From this cause, thoughts of malice and harm arise, dwelt upon repeatedly. Because of this, excellent concentration not yet arisen fails to arise, and if already arisen, falls away again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the alms-round scenario as a deliberately ordinary, almost mundane trigger for anger, precisely the point, since it shows how easily even a minor social slight, being served last or treated brusquely, can quietly undo concentration that took real effort to build, without any dramatic provocation being necessary at all. He notes that the same formula, an affliction arising, dwelt upon through repeated deliberation, then either preventing new concentration or reversing concentration already gained, structures every one of these five faults in turn, giving the whole passage a single, memorable causal shape worth committing to memory.

4. Section: Pride That Refuses to Ask, and Craving That Contemplating the Body Can Cure

The second weighty fault, pride, is explained next: a mind ruled by its own sense of superiority shows no reverence toward the Dharma at all, and cannot bring itself to humbly and respectfully approach a teacher or elder worthy of reverence to ask, simply and sincerely, what is wholesome and what is not, nor does it earnestly seek out any of the other requisites concentration depends upon, from which contemptuous deliberation toward others soon follows, dwelt upon repeatedly, undoing concentration exactly as anger does. The third weighty fault, craving for sensual desire, arises from a mind deeply attached to its own body and possessions, scattering itself among the five external objects of desire and giving rise to deliberation on desire itself, on beloved family members impossible to let go of, on the pleasures of distant lands, and on cultivating powerful patrons for the sake of one's own reputation and support, each equally undoing concentration in turn. Its antidote is contemplating impurity, unfolding across three aspects: the mark of filth itself, since whatever is eaten is soon transformed into waste, the body itself no cleaner, in truth, than a walking latrine that no ocean could ever wash pure; the mark of filth's own outflow, since the very flesh and blood nourished by that same food is nothing more than an ongoing product of this same impure process; and the mark distinguishing what is supported from what supports it, since the body itself arose from the impure union of parent and parent, an impure support built upon an impure origin from the very start, contemplation that directly dismantles the craving that clings to the body's imagined purity.

Chinese Source

所言慢者，謂懷慢故，慢所制伏為性，於法不生恭敬，於諸師範尊重福田，不能時時身心卑屈敬問諮請：云何為善？云何不善？亦不勤求所有善法。言欲貪者，謂懷貪故，多諸愛染，於身財等深生顧戀，由此於外五妙欲中多生散亂。彼復不淨能為對治。應知不淨略有三相。謂糞穢相、彼等流相、能依所依差別相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pride means that, ruled by pride, one shows no reverence toward the Dharma, and cannot humbly and respectfully inquire of a teacher worthy of reverence what is wholesome and what is not, nor earnestly seek every wholesome dharma. Craving for sensual desire means that, holding much attachment, one deeply cherishes body and possessions, thereby scattering greatly among the five external objects of desire. Contemplating impurity serves as its antidote, briefly of three aspects: the mark of filth, the mark of filth's outflow, and the mark distinguishing the supported from its support.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats pride's own refusal to simply ask a teacher a plain question as this fault's own most self-defeating feature, since the very humility pride prevents is exactly what would let a practitioner discover, and correct, their own mistakes before those mistakes ever became entrenched habits. He calls the threefold impurity contemplation, filth, filth's own ongoing product, and an impure support built on an impure origin, this section's own most vivid teaching, deliberately unflattering in a way meant not to demean the body but to loosen a craving that mistakes it for something worth the intensity of attachment so often placed upon it.

5. Section: How the View of a Real Self Makes the Eight Worldly Winds Impossible to Release

The fourth weighty fault, the view of a real self, opens into an extended analysis of its own, beginning with how this view makes ordinary worldly conditions so difficult to release. A mind still ruled by self-view, encountering any of the eight worldly conditions, gain or loss, praise or blame, honor or ridicule, pleasure or pain, is inevitably elated by what is favorable and dejected by what is unfavorable, and even when genuinely wishing to let go of this very reactivity, remains held back by the self-view standing directly opposed to any such release, since without truly seeing that no fixed self can actually be found anywhere within these fluctuating experiences, the attachment to one's own person simply cannot be set down. This same self-view further ensures that affection itself becomes almost impossible to release, a mind endlessly swept along, since beginningless time, by the very bonds of attachment, drowning again and again in love for family, friends, and the pleasures the world offers, so that even a sincere wish to release this affection runs directly into the same self-view blocking its way.

Chinese Source

薩迦耶見者，謂由身見制伏因緣，會遇世法，便為高欣、下慼塗染。設欲棄捨，便為身見相違而住。又即於彼世法眾相，親愛恆流之所漂溺。設欲棄捨，便為身見相違而住。

Ru-Yi Translation

The view of a real self means that, controlled by the cause of self-view, one who encounters worldly conditions is stained by elation and dejection. Wishing to release this, one remains held back, opposed by self-view. And amid the many aspects of these same worldly conditions, one is drowned by the constant stream of affection. Wishing to release this, one remains held back, opposed by self-view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the image of affection as a constant, driving stream one has been drowning in since beginningless time as an unusually stark description of ordinary attachment, framing even the most tender human bonds as a form of continuous submersion a practitioner genuinely seeking liberation must eventually learn to swim free of. He notes the treatise's own careful logic here, that self-view is not merely one affliction among many but the specific obstacle that prevents even a sincere wish to release worldly reactivity or affection from actually succeeding, since without first seeing through the assumption of a fixed self, the very thing doing the wishing to let go remains exactly what keeps holding on.

6. Section: Four More Ways Self-View Holds On, and Why Enduring Hardship Matters

Self-view's obstruction unfolds across four further ways in which release proves impossible. Every unwholesome deliberation stirred up by worldly conditions remains just as hard to abandon, self-view standing in the way of any genuine release. The very distraction and agitation that pursuing worldly conditions produces likewise remains impossible to set down. Grief and sorrow themselves, arising because clinging constantly seeks permanence in what is actually impermanent, whether craving, wrong view, or misguided precept and vow, cannot be released either, again blocked by this same self-view. And even the subtler defilement belonging to a higher realm proves just as hard to release as the defilement of a lower realm, one still bound by self-view finding no genuine joy in cultivating freedom from any level of desire whatsoever, lower or higher alike. Self-view's deepest consequence is then named directly: even when a mind sincerely intends to see great fault in birth and death and supreme virtue in nirvana, self-view causes this very intention to become inverted, pulling the mind back toward saṃsāra despite its own best wishes, so that whatever concentration had been reached before, the transcendent concentration of direct realization of the noble truths specifically fails to arise at all. The fifth and final weighty fault, inability to endure, is explained last: even one who has already reached this direct realization of the noble truths may still be unable to bear the bite of mosquitoes and gnats or other bodily discomfort, and abandons sustained practice out of sheer laziness as a result, so that the root absorption not yet attained fails to arise, and if already attained, falls away once more.

Chinese Source

由此因故，雖作是心：我當於彼生死、涅槃，觀大過失、觀勝功德，便復顛倒。由此因緣，先雖獲得諸三摩地，然於未生聖諦現觀勝三摩地，不能得生。不堪忍者，謂懷不忍故，雖已獲得聖諦現觀勝三摩地，不能堪忍蚊蝱等苦，捨離加行，多生懈怠。由此因緣，於所未生入根本定不能生起，設復已生還即退失。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of this cause, even while intending to see great fault in birth and death and supreme virtue in nirvana, this intention becomes inverted. Because of this, though various concentrations were previously attained, the transcendent concentration of direct realization of the noble truths, not yet arisen, fails to arise. Inability to endure means that, holding this intolerance, even one who has attained the transcendent concentration of direct realization of the noble truths cannot bear the suffering of mosquitoes and gnats, abandons sustained effort, and grows lazy. Because of this, root absorption not yet arisen fails to arise, and if already arisen, falls away again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls self-view's capacity to invert even a sincerely held wish to see saṃsāra's fault and nirvana's virtue this entire passage's own most sobering point, showing that correct intention alone, however genuine, cannot overcome a view of self left fundamentally unexamined, since the very perspective doing the wishing remains quietly compromised by what it has not yet seen through. He notes that inability to endure closing this list with something as physically ordinary as insect bites brings the whole discussion back down to earth, real spiritual attainment still vulnerable to being abandoned over discomforts most people would consider entirely trivial.

7. Section: Which Fault Blocks Which Stage, and Eight Reasons a Teacher Gives Up a Student

These five faults are now mapped precisely onto the specific stage of concentration each one obstructs: anger, pride, and craving for sensual desire together obstruct only the very earliest, threshold concentration; self-view alone obstructs the transcendent concentration of direct realization of the noble truths; and inability to endure alone obstructs actually entering the root absorptions themselves. A further, quite different application of inability to endure is then given, concerning eight reasons a teacher might abandon a student residing under their guidance, arising specifically from a teacher still affected by this same intolerance, something a fully liberated arhat, ever abiding in mindfulness, would never actually do: harboring ill will toward the student while wishing to receive all reverence oneself; likewise wishing to receive all material support oneself; the student committing repeated transgressions and behaving improperly; benefiting the student only inconsistently, helping when something is needed from them and withholding help otherwise; being unable to bear the effort of instructing and correcting the student in precepts; being unable to bear the effort of instructing and correcting them in concentration; being unable to bear the effort of instructing and correcting them in wisdom; and being unable to bear the general burden of managing communal affairs on the student's behalf. With this, the analysis of every cause preventing the attainment of concentration, and the antidotes to each, brings the entire topic of what accords with and what opposes concentration to its own close.

Chinese Source

前三過失，能為最初三摩地障。次一過失，為諦現觀三摩地障。後一過失，為入根本三摩地障。復有差別。謂有八種棄捨近住弟子因緣，於彼雜染之所染汙。由染汙故，彼便棄捨近住弟子，非無煩惱諸阿羅漢常善住念有如斯事。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first three faults obstruct only the earliest concentration. The next single fault obstructs the concentration of direct realization of the noble truths. The last fault obstructs entering root concentration. There is a further distinction: eight causes for abandoning a resident student, arising from being stained by this same defilement, something free of which no arhat, ever well abiding in mindfulness, would ever do.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the precise mapping of each fault to a specific stage, the first three blocking only entry-level concentration while self-view and inability to endure guard the far harder thresholds of noble realization and root absorption respectively, as confirmation that spiritual obstacles themselves grow subtler exactly as attainment deepens. He reads the eight reasons a teacher might abandon a student as a remarkably candid acknowledgment that teachers themselves remain fallible, wanting exclusive honor or support, growing impatient with a difficult student, or simply exhausted by the labor of instruction and communal management, a passage he finds genuinely useful for any teacher honestly examining their own motives rather than assuming fault always lies with the student alone.

8. Section: A New Topic Opens — Why Exactly Four Absorptions

A second major sub-topic now opens, turning from what accords with or opposes concentration to the four absorptions themselves, gathered into a single summary verse covering nine points in turn: their number, what each one overcomes, their own distinct factors, the reasons behind how those factors are established, the meaning of seclusion belonging to the first absorption, the sense in which even the first absorption remains touched by suffering, why the first three absorptions in particular are called subject to agitation, why the factors established in each higher absorption differ from those below, and finally the distinctions among all four taken together. The discussion opens with a direct question: why are there exactly four absorptions, neither more nor fewer? The answer given is that only four are needed to bring a practitioner completely beyond both suffering and pleasure, since it is precisely from the first absorption through the fourth that suffering and pleasure are progressively transcended stage by stage until nothing of either remains, the first absorption overcoming grief, the second overcoming pain, the third overcoming joy, and the fourth overcoming pleasure itself, this exact progression exhausting every kind of feeling there is to transcend, leaving no need for a fifth stage and no room to manage with only three.

Chinese Source

復次，嗢柁南曰：數及所對治　支分廣建立　遠離苦散動　上支分差別。問：如先所說四種靜慮，何因緣故唯四靜慮，不增不減？答：由能究竟超苦樂故。所以者何？從初靜慮乃至第四，漸超苦樂方究竟故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, the summary verse says: number, and what is overcome; factors, and their extensive establishment; seclusion, suffering, agitation; and the differences among the upper factors. Question: as previously stated, there are four absorptions — for what reason exactly four, neither more nor fewer? Answer: because this alone brings one completely beyond suffering and pleasure. Why is this so? Because from the first absorption through the fourth, suffering and pleasure are progressively transcended until this is complete.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā's own fourfold definition of what genuinely qualifies as an absorption at all, requiring both the actual cutting of fetters and genuine insight together, a definition that excludes desire-realm concentration for lacking the power to cut fetters and excludes the formless attainments for lacking genuine discerning insight, leaving exactly the four form-realm absorptions as true absorption in the fullest sense. He treats the elegant one-to-one correspondence, each successive absorption overcoming exactly one further kind of feeling, grief, then pain, then joy, then pleasure itself, as this entire ninefold verse's own most satisfying answer to why the number four is not arbitrary at all, but precisely calibrated to the four kinds of feeling a practitioner must move entirely beyond.

9. Section: What Each of the Four Absorptions Actually Overcomes

The first absorption is shown to overcome five things: craving for sensual desire itself; the three unwholesome deliberations of desire, malice, and harm; the grief and suffering that desire produces; any transgression of the precepts, since a mind settled in genuine absorption naturally holds the very precepts that concentration itself confers; and distraction, since the desire realm's own restlessness must first be left behind entirely. The second absorption overcomes five further things: attachment to the first absorption itself, since a practitioner must grow weary of it before advancing; the coarse activity of investigation and reflection that the first absorption still depends upon; the very suffering that even the first absorption remains touched by; restlessness; and the first absorption's own comparatively inferior quality of concentration. The third absorption overcomes four things: attachment to the second absorption; the second absorption's own joy, itself a subtly unsettling excitement despite its pleasant character; the exuberant agitation that this joy produces; and the second absorption's own inferior quality of concentration. The fourth absorption overcomes five things: the very breath itself, since beyond this point in-breath and out-breath cease entirely, life sustained instead through the underlying consciousness alone; attachment to the third absorption; the third absorption's own bliss, the most exalted pleasure found anywhere in the three realms and for that very reason difficult for many practitioners to relinquish; the very intention that seeks after this same bliss; and the third absorption's own inferior quality of concentration.

Chinese Source

云何名初靜慮所治？謂有五種。一者、欲貪，二、欲、恚、害三種尋思，三者、憂苦，四者、犯戒，五者、散亂。云何第二靜慮所治？亦有五種。一、初靜慮貪，二、尋伺，三、苦，四、掉，五、定下劣性。云何第三靜慮所治？謂有四種。一、第二靜慮貪，二、喜，三、踊躍，四、定下劣性。云何第四靜慮所治？謂有五種。一、入息、出息，二、第三靜慮貪，三、樂，四、於樂發悟，五、定下劣性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What does the first absorption overcome? Five things: craving for sensual desire; the three deliberations of desire, malice, and harm; grief and suffering; transgressing precepts; and distraction. What does the second overcome? Also five: attachment to the first absorption; investigation and reflection; suffering; restlessness; and inferior quality of concentration. What does the third overcome? Four things: attachment to the second absorption; joy; exuberant agitation; and inferior quality of concentration. What does the fourth overcome? Five things: in-breath and out-breath; attachment to the third absorption; bliss; the intention seeking bliss; and inferior quality of concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the consistent pattern running through all four lists, each higher absorption overcoming attachment to the one just below it and its own inferior quality of concentration, so that advancement through the four absorptions is never simply addition, gaining ever more refined pleasure, but is equally subtraction, letting go of the very state just attained in order to reach something still more complete. He highlights the third absorption's own bliss, named here as the single most exalted pleasure anywhere across the three realms, as the passage's own frank acknowledgment of just how difficult true renunciation becomes precisely at its most rewarding moments, since it is exactly the sweetest attainment that proves hardest of all to release.

10. Section: Five Factors of the First Absorption, Four of the Second

The first absorption is shown to possess five factors: investigation, a coarser mode of examining the object; reflection, a subtler mode of the same examination; joy, the mind's own delight, vigor, and ease, settled and untroubled by any other concern; bliss, a pervasive tranquil pleasure filling the transformed body itself once the underlying basis has shifted through genuine absorption; and one-pointedness, the mind resting again and again, undistracted, on this same single object. The second absorption possesses four factors in place of five: inner clarity replaces investigation and reflection directly, the coarse and subtle examination that once stirred the mind like dust now settled entirely, its own essential nature being nothing other than mindfulness, clear knowing, and equanimity together, joined still by joy, bliss, and one-pointedness carried forward from the stage below. Asked directly what inner clarity's own nature actually consists of, the treatise answers plainly: mindfulness holding the mind steadily on its object, clear knowing catching and correcting any lapse the moment investigation or reflection threatens to stir again, and equanimity itself, a mind no longer agitated or dull, upright and unmoving, resting without further effort in its own natural evenness.

Chinese Source

問：初靜慮有幾支？答：有五支。何等為五？一、尋，二、伺，三、喜，四、樂，五、心一境性。問：第二靜慮有幾支？答：有四支。何等為四？一、內等淨，二、喜，三、樂，四、心一境性。問：內等淨何法為自性？答：念、正知、捨為自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: how many factors does the first absorption have? Answer: five. What are the five? Investigation, reflection, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness of mind. Question: how many factors does the second absorption have? Answer: four. What are the four? Inner clarity, joy, bliss, and one-pointedness of mind. Question: what is inner clarity's own nature? Answer: mindfulness, clear knowing, and equanimity are its nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi likens investigation and reflection's own settling in the second absorption to dust finally allowed to settle out of once-turbid water, the mind's own natural clarity, mindfulness, clear knowing, and equanimity, having been present all along but only now becoming visible once the coarser activity obscuring it has genuinely quieted. He treats this exact three-part definition of inner clarity as this entire section's own key to everything that follows, since these same three qualities, mindfulness, clear knowing, and equanimity, will reappear under different names at every remaining stage of absorption, differently emphasized each time depending on exactly what still needs to be restrained.

11. Section: The Third Absorption's Deep Immersion, and the Fourth's Utter Stillness

The third absorption possesses five factors: mindfulness, maintaining clear awareness that joy itself remains a subtly agitated state, unfit to be allowed free rein, and thereby keeping it from arising again; clear knowing, catching the moment joy's own agitation threatens to reassert itself and correcting it immediately; equanimity, a genuine aversion to joy's own restlessness, the mind now abiding evenly, upright, and unmoved, free of any lingering attachment to it; bliss, the deepest and most complete pleasure found anywhere in the three realms, pervading body and mind together entirely, unlike joy's own partial delight, described as the difference between a swimmer whose head still breaks the surface and one who rests fully submerged in the water's own embrace; and one-pointedness, as before. The fourth and final absorption possesses four factors: purified equanimity, now surpassing every trace of agitation belonging to the first three absorptions entirely, upright, still, and unmoved; purified mindfulness, remaining unshaken by any of the eight disturbances that troubled the stages below, investigation, reflection, joy, bliss, grief, suffering, and the very in-breath and out-breath now finally at rest; a feeling neither painful nor pleasant, the body's transformed state leaving neither the discomfort of the lower stages nor the pleasure of the one just below, resting instead in genuine equanimity; and one-pointedness once more, completing the full structure of all four absorptions together.

Question: how many factors does the fourth absorption have? Answer: four. What are the four? Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the swimmer's own image, joy as a swimmer whose head still breaks the surface and bliss as one resting fully submerged, as this entire fourfold structure's own most memorable illustration, capturing in a single picture the difference between a pleasure still tinged with excitement and one that has settled into complete, whole-body ease. He calls the fourth absorption's own freedom from all eight disturbances, investigation, reflection, joy, bliss, grief, suffering, and breath itself, this entire progression's own genuine terminus, a stillness so complete that even the most refined pleasure found in the stage just below it is finally set aside in favor of an equanimity beyond either suffering or delight.

12. Section: Why Mindfulness and Wisdom Are Named Only Where They Are Most Needed

The treatise explains why mindfulness, clear knowing, and equanimity, though genuinely present at every stage, receive distinct names only where each is most urgently required: mindfulness is named specifically in the third and fourth absorptions because it is precisely there that a practitioner risks being swept away, first by the third absorption's own exceptional joy and then by its exceptional bliss, exactly as the Buddha himself is said to have taught, remain established in mindfulness, lest you be carried off by the joy or bliss of the stage below and fall back from your own; clear seeing wisdom is named specifically in the third absorption alone, since its own pleasant feeling there is the single most supreme found anywhere in the lower stages, tempting a practitioner to cling rather than advance further; and pliancy is named in the first two absorptions while equanimity is named in the last two, since each pair mutually obscures the other, pliancy countering the coarse heaviness that the first two absorptions still carry over from the desire realm's own sense consciousness, while equanimity, once that heaviness has genuinely fallen away, counters instead the third absorption's own tainted joy and the fourth's own residual attachment to bliss.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: for what reason are five or four factors established in the four absorptions as they are? Answer: through abiding in what is depended upon, abiding in what benefits, and abiding in one's own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this entire discussion as confirming what the earlier factor-by-factor breakdown already implied, that mindfulness, clear knowing, and equanimity run through all four absorptions continuously, receiving a distinct name only at the exact stage where a practitioner is most likely to be swept away, joy and bliss in the third and fourth absorptions proving far more seductive dangers than the comparatively coarse investigation and reflection of the first stage. He introduces the treatise's own new organizing question, why exactly five or four factors are established in each absorption, answered first through a threefold lens: abiding in what is depended upon, the factor that actually makes each absorption possible; abiding in what benefits, the pleasant fruit that absorption yields; and abiding in one's own nature, the very essence of concentration itself, one-pointedness of mind, a threefold pattern that will now be examined from several further angles in turn.

13. Section: Six Ways of Seeing the Same Three-Part Pattern

This same threefold pattern, dependence, benefit, and own-nature, is now examined from five further angles, each casting fresh light on why the absorptions are structured just as they are. Considered by way of their object, dependence corresponds to contemplating the object directly, benefit corresponds to actually enjoying it, and own-nature corresponds to remaining undistracted from it. Considered by way of what they serve as a basis for, dependence serves as the basis of benefit itself, benefit in turn nourishes the higher training in mind, and own-nature serves as the basis for the higher training in wisdom, since insight itself can only be cultivated once the mind has genuinely settled. Considered by way of the three kinds of defiled abiding each factor overcomes, dependence overcomes an abiding still touched by defilement, benefit overcomes an abiding touched by suffering, and own-nature overcomes an abiding touched by outright delusion, concentration itself being the very basis from which insight can finally dispel ignorance. Considered through the analogy of one who enjoys the objects of desire, dependence corresponds to first seeking out wealth, benefit corresponds to actually enjoying that wealth once obtained, and own-nature corresponds to the freedom to use it exactly as one wishes, exactly as a practitioner first seeks the very factor concentration depends upon, then enjoys the pleasant feeling concentration yields, and finally gains the freedom that one-pointed mastery itself makes possible. And considered by way of countering one's own suffering directly, three kinds of antidote correspond to the same three factors: the antidote to deficiency itself, since the desire realm lacks any wholesome investigation or reflection at all to counter its own three unwholesome deliberations; the antidote to bodily and mental distress; and the antidote to a mind still scattered and unsettled toward external objects, met at last by one-pointedness of mind alone.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these six successive lenses, abiding, object, basis, defiled-abiding countered, the desire-enjoyer's own analogy, and self-suffering countered, as the same underlying threefold structure examined from every conceivable angle rather than six genuinely different theories, a deliberately thorough method meant to make the pattern impossible to forget rather than to multiply distinct doctrines. He singles out the desire-enjoyer analogy as this passage's own most approachable image, a layperson's ordinary progression from seeking wealth, to enjoying it, to finally using it freely, mapped directly onto a meditator's own progression from establishing the factor concentration depends on, to enjoying its pleasant fruit, to finally exercising the freedom one-pointed mastery itself confers.
