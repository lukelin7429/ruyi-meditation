Fascicle 54 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十四 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Continues

Where Consciousness Dwells

This fascicle completes consciousness's own fivefold analysis — where it dwells across the three realms, how it becomes defiled, what it depends on, the four stations where it takes root, and its countless changing aspects — then closes the six general aspects shared by all five aggregates before carrying out a complete, ten-angle examination of form itself, from the eye's subtlest constituents to why two realms' matter can share one body like sand settled in water.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 54 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Twenty Angles on Five Aggregates — And Consciousness's Own Fivefold Analysis Begins With Where It Dwells

Fascicle 54 opens by situating itself within the treatise's own vast architecture for examining the aggregates: skillful understanding of the aggregates unfolds across three major sections spanning twenty distinct angles in total. The first section, already substantially covered in fascicle 53, generally distinguishes six aspects of each aggregate — essential nature, meaning, distinctions, sequence, subsumption, and support — with only consciousness's own distinctions, together with sequence, subsumption, and support for all five aggregates, remaining to be covered here. The second section, occupying the remainder of this fascicle, further clarifies form and mind through a more detailed tenfold analysis: their kind, their subtlest constituents, their arising, their establishment, their continuity, their function, their momentary cessation, their solitary or accompanied nature, what they operate upon, and their intermixing. The third section, reserved for fascicles 55 and 56, decides four further matters: how the aggregates relate to the noble truths, their various stages, their further distinctions, and their overall establishment. The treatise explains that this entire twenty-angle examination serves a single purpose: dismantling the ordinary being's persistent clinging to this body and mind as a self or as belonging to a self, thereby revealing the truth of selflessness ever more thoroughly. Having already completed essential nature, meaning, and the distinctions of form, feeling, perception, and formations in fascicle 53, the treatise now turns to consciousness's own distinctions, asking what they are, then explaining they are, in brief, five: consciousness's dwelling place, its defilement, its support, the stations where it takes root, and its changing aspects. It opens with the first: where consciousness actually dwells, distinguishing three kinds of beings by realm. Those habituated to desire, in the desire realm, have consciousness grasping external sense-objects, dwelling in form. The pure gods of the form realm have consciousness grasping their own internal name-and-form together, dwelling in both at once — their identity residing in their own luminous inner body together with the mental factors accompanying deep meditative absorption. And the formless realm's own consciousness grasps only internal name, dwelling in name alone, having transcended physical embodiment entirely to abide purely within its own inner mental states.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens this fascicle by mapping out its own place within a genuinely vast undertaking: twenty total angles on the five aggregates, six already substantially covered, ten more to come across the rest of this fascicle, and four final angles reserved for two fascicles still ahead — all in service of a single goal, dismantling the illusion of a solid self clinging to this very body and mind. He treats the threefold classification of where consciousness dwells as mapping precisely onto the three realms' own defining character: desire-realm beings restlessly seeking pleasure through external objects, form-realm gods turning inward toward their own luminous embodiment and meditative absorption, and formless-realm beings abiding purely within mental states alone, having left physical form behind entirely. Having established where consciousness dwells across the three realms, he notes, the treatise turns to its second angle: how consciousness actually becomes defiled, distinguishing affliction that arises through ordinary sensory engagement in this very life from the deeper defilement that drives rebirth into a future life.

2. Section: Two Doorways of Defilement — And the Fire That Burns the Same Whether Fed by Straw or Dung

The treatise turns to consciousness's second distinction: defilement. It asks how consciousness becomes defiled, then explains that ordinary, unlearned beings are defiled through two distinct doorways. The first doorway operates within the present life, through the very engagement with sensory objects: craving arises when consciousness moves toward pleasant feeling, aversion arises when it moves toward painful feeling, and delusion arises when it settles into neutral feeling — this ordinary sensory engagement itself becoming the doorway through which affliction first defiles consciousness. The second doorway operates toward a future life, through the very process of aging and the rest: karma accumulated through this same sensory engagement ripens into a future rebirth, and that rebirth's own aging, sickness, and death constitute a further, deeper defilement in their own right, since the ripened embodiment itself is already an afflicted condition, perpetuating the entire cycle onward without end unless the Buddhist path intervenes. The treatise then turns to its third distinction: what consciousness depends upon. It explains that the six active consciousnesses each depend on their own corresponding sense-faculty in order to actually manifest — eye-consciousness depending on the eye faculty in order to engage visible form, and so on through mind-consciousness depending on the mental faculty — illustrated through the analogy of ordinary fire: fire fed by rice husks, by cattle dung, by firewood, or by wood chips is called husk-fire, dung-fire, wood-fire, or chip-fire respectively, yet the underlying nature of fire itself remains exactly the same regardless of its fuel; in just the same way, consciousness's own basic nature of discernment remains constant throughout, while its particular name — eye-consciousness, ear-consciousness, and so on through mind-consciousness — simply follows whichever sense-faculty happens to support it at the time.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two doorways of defilement as capturing an entire lifetime's worth of affliction in miniature: ordinary craving, aversion, and delusion generated through everyday sensory engagement create the karma that ripens into a future rebirth, whose own aging, sickness, and death then constitute a still deeper layer of defilement — the whole cycle repeating indefinitely without the intervention of genuine practice. He treats the fire-and-fuel analogy as this treatise's own most elegant illustration of a point developed at great length elsewhere: consciousness's own underlying nature of discernment never changes, only the label attached to it shifts according to whichever sense-faculty happens to support it at a given moment, exactly as fire remains fire regardless of what fuel happens to feed it. Having completed consciousness's second and third distinctions, he notes, the treatise turns to its fourth and most doctrinally significant distinction: the four stations where consciousness takes root, a teaching whose scholarly interpretation the lecture traces through centuries of debate between the Sarvāstivādin and Mahāyāna traditions.

3. Section: The Four Stations Where Consciousness Takes Root — A Teaching Aimed Directly at the Heretic's Own Sense of Self

The treatise turns to consciousness's fourth distinction: the stations where it takes root. The lecture supplements this teaching with an extended account of its own historical purpose and interpretation. Regarding why the Buddha taught this doctrine at all: certain non-Buddhist schools held consciousness itself to be the true self, with the four remaining aggregates — form, feeling, perception, and formations — merely serving as this self's own dwelling place; the Buddha, refuting this view directly, taught instead that form, feeling, perception, and formations are simply where consciousness itself takes root, never that consciousness constitutes any self dwelling within them. Regarding what actually constitutes a "station" in this sense: only the contaminated, defiled aggregates function this way, since only they can give rise to craving and thereby provide consciousness a place to settle and take root; the uncontaminated aggregates, incapable of generating this same craving, provide no such foothold, and consciousness itself is never counted as its own station, since the entire teaching exists precisely to correct the mistaken notion that consciousness could serve as its own independent, self-sufficient basis. The lecture then traces a substantial scholarly disagreement between traditions: the Sarvāstivādin school held that only one's own, present-life aggregates properly constitute a station for one's own consciousness, strictly confined to the very same realm, level, and body, since consciousness and its station must share a single moment together; the Mahāyāna tradition held a considerably broader view, that both dependence and objective reference each constitute a station, so that consciousness taking any object whatsoever as its focus — whether belonging to oneself or another, whether form or formless, and reaching across all three time-periods and all three realms alike — already counts as a station, the sole exception being the uncontaminated truths of cessation and the path, which alone generate no craving capable of anchoring consciousness in the first place.

Having laid out this rich historical background, he notes, the treatise turns to identifying the four stations themselves by name — form, feeling, perception, and formations — before explaining each individually.

4. Section: The Four Stations Named — Form, Feeling, Perception, and Formations — Through Three Modes of Attachment Each

The treatise names the four stations directly: consciousness follows form and dwells there, taking form as its object — and the scripture explains this at length, extending in exactly the same way through feeling, perception, and formations. The lecture cites the Compendium of Manifest Teaching's own threefold analysis of how consciousness relates to each of these four stations: consciousness adheres to form, since form serves as what consciousness clings to and appropriates as its own basis; consciousness attends to form, since consciousness takes form as the very object of its own cognition; and consciousness relies on form, since the coarse, burdensome weight of affliction still binds consciousness to form as a necessary support — and precisely the same threefold relationship of adherence, attention, and reliance applies without exception to feeling, perception, and formations in turn. The lecture also cites Venerable Master Yìn Shùn's own summary: the entire teaching of the five aggregates derives from this teaching of the four stations, since sentient beings cling to consciousness's own attachment across bodily craving, emotional grasping, cognitive fixation, and volitional pursuit alike, and it is precisely this fourfold clinging that binds them to saṃsāra's own endless cycle. The treatise then quotes the Buddha's own celebrated declaration: I never say this consciousness goes to the east, or to any of the eight cardinal or intercardinal directions — rather, I say only that within this very life, it becomes forever free of desire and of feeling's own shadow, reaching quiescence, tranquility, coolness, and purity — since an arhat's consciousness, upon entering final nirvāṇa, no longer clings to form, feeling, perception, or formations at all, and asking which direction it has gone is as meaningless as asking which direction an extinguished fire has traveled.

5. Section: Five Technical Terms Unpacked — From the Bases of Grasping to the Four Bonds That Fetter Every Being

The treatise unpacks five terms drawn from this same scriptural passage on the four stations. The bases of grasping name the craving found within the aggregates themselves, together with whatever aggregates that same craving has come to permeate — craving being what refuses to relinquish either the future rebirth it seeks or the present existence it clings to, and it is precisely this craving, together with the aggregates it infuses, that constitutes consciousness's own basis and object of grasping. The object names this same pair viewed from a slightly different angle: since the aggregates permeated by craving genuinely serve as something consciousness takes as its focus, and since craving itself, as a mental factor, genuinely operates in concert with consciousness, both qualify as consciousness's own object in their own distinct ways. The object of craving's own pursuit names how craving itself, as the decisive condition, drives the mind to actively pursue and cling to whatever it becomes entangled with — the treatise identifying four specific kinds of such grasping: craving for sensory pleasure, clinging to wrong views, clinging to misguided moral or ritual observances, and clinging to the false notion of self embedded in ordinary language itself. The object bound by affliction's own fetters names how four corresponding bonds — the bond of craving, of aversion, of clinging to misguided observances, and of clinging to a falsely absolutized view — actually generate karma, householders typically driven by the bonds of craving and aversion in pursuit of pleasant objects and in hostility toward other beings, while renunciants remain more susceptible to the bonds of misguided observance and false absolutization, mistakenly seeking heavenly rebirth through improper practice or mistakenly denigrating nirvāṇa itself through wrong view. And the establishing factor names how the latent seeds of both craving and these four bonds continue to follow a being's own life-stream, ready to manifest again whenever the right conditions converge, thereby establishing the entire ongoing cycle of the five aggregates across life after life.

6. Section: Two Outer Limits of the Station — Coming and Going Versus Simply Dying and Being Reborn

The treatise closes its account of the station's sustaining condition by confirming that everything already explained — the bases of grasping, the object, the object of craving's pursuit, the object bound by the four bonds, and the establishing factor — together constitutes this same sustaining condition in full. It then turns to two further outer limits: the limit of the station itself, and the limit of its sustaining condition. Regarding the first: consciousness belonging to a being with form — whether in the desire realm or the form realm — genuinely comes and goes, since such beings pass through an intermediate state connecting one life to the next, and are not reborn in any fixed, predetermined location; whereas consciousness belonging to a formless-realm being simply dies and is reborn directly, without any intervening state at all, since such existence is purely mental, arising again in the very place it left. Both these two kinds of station continue, without exception, until the very exhaustion of the being's own lifespan — and it is precisely this same craving and these same four bonds that cause both kinds of station to continually grow, strengthen, and expand throughout that entire span, right up until liberation is finally achieved. The treatise concludes: this, precisely, constitutes both the outer limit of the station itself and the outer limit of its sustaining condition — an ordinary being's own consciousness cycling without genuine end throughout the three realms, exactly as the scriptures describe the ocean of suffering as boundless, until the noble path finally brings it to its true limit.

7. Section: Refuting the Real Self — Three Fatal Flaws in the Heretic's Doctrine, and the Store Clerk Who Was Never Truly Good or Bad

The treatise addresses an alternative account some non-Buddhist teachers propose: since the Buddha's own teaching describes only consciousness cycling through the three realms, never any self doing the cycling, those who instead posit an eternal, unchanging self merely traveling from one body to the next like moving between rented rooms are, the treatise insists, only ever offering an alternative in name, never in genuine substance — since no such self can actually be found anywhere at all, positing one changes nothing about reality itself, however different the words used to describe it. The treatise identifies two resulting faults in this rival doctrine: first, when a genuine challenger presses the question directly, such teachers simply have no coherent answer to give, precisely because the self they posit was never actually there to find in the first place; and second, even a sufficiently sharp-witted adherent of this same doctrine, upon later reflecting honestly and searching for this self themselves, becomes quietly troubled, privately recognizing their own view as foolish, even if pride keeps them from openly admitting defeat. The lecture illustrates the search for a fixed, unchanging self with the vivid example of a store clerk: one customer, served with a warm smile and an easy refund, declares him a genuinely good person; but the very next customer, arriving just after his employer scolded him, meets only a sour, unaccommodating mood and declares him genuinely bad — yet there was never a single, fixed "good clerk" or "bad clerk" to be found at all, only ever a shifting response arising from whatever conditions happened to converge at that particular moment, exactly as the treatise insists: search through form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness alike, and no permanent, self-ruling self is ever actually found anywhere, only this same ceaseless, conditioned change.

8. Section: Bringing the Sustaining Condition to Rest — Different Afflictions for Householder and Renunciant Alike

The treatise turns to how the station's own sustaining condition may be brought to cessation. It explains that genuinely wise practitioners — śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas alike — are capable of permanently severing both the craving attachment toward form, feeling, perception, and formations, and the four karma-generating bonds that belong to affliction's own causal side; severing both these entanglements together is what actually brings the station's sustaining condition to rest. The treatise then explains why these particular afflictions arise so differently for householders and renunciants. Householders generate karma primarily through the bonds of craving and aversion: craving drives the pursuit of sensory pleasure, often through improper or even harmful means, while aversion drives harm toward other beings whenever personal interest is threatened. Renunciants, including non-Buddhist ascetics, remain more susceptible instead to the bonds of misguided observance and false absolutization: misguided observance parallels craving in seeking a desirable result, except the desired result here is heavenly rebirth pursued through improper practices such as imitating animal behavior in the mistaken belief this secures a favorable rebirth; and false absolutization parallels aversion in generating harm, except the harm here is directed at truth itself — mistaking deep meditative absorption for genuine liberation, or seizing upon speculative views about self and world as eternal, non-eternal, bounded, or unbounded, and thereby actively slandering nirvāṇa's own genuine reality through this same confusion. The lecture stresses that a renunciant's own imbalanced cultivation — clinging to precepts or concentration without genuinely completing the path through wisdom — is precisely what leaves this second, subtler pair of afflictions unaddressed, preventing the station's sustaining condition from ever actually coming to rest.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this careful pairing of householder and renunciant afflictions as a genuinely useful diagnostic for practitioners of any background: craving and aversion are the more obvious afflictions of ordinary worldly life, while misguided observance and false absolutization are the subtler, more insidious afflictions specific to a life already devoted to spiritual practice, where mistaking depth of concentration for depth of wisdom, or mistaking a cherished view for settled truth, can quietly derail even sincere renunciation. He notes the treatise's own implicit warning here — that imbalanced cultivation of precepts and concentration without genuine wisdom leaves exactly this second, more dangerous pair of afflictions fully intact — echoes a caution found throughout this entire treatise's own account of the path: discipline and concentration alone, however sincerely practiced, can never substitute for the wisdom that alone actually severs affliction at its root. Having explained the underlying cause of the station's own sustaining condition for both householder and renunciant, he notes, the treatise turns next to the actual mechanism by which affliction's own root is severed, distinguished according to its own manifest, latent, and habitual dimensions.

9. Section: The Four Bonds Are Purely Mental — And the Sustaining Condition Finally Reaches Its Own Complete Rest

The treatise clarifies that all four karma-generating bonds belong exclusively to the mental faculty, since they arise entirely through mental consciousness's own discriminating activity — the lecture explaining, via the Compendium of Determinations, that these four bonds obstruct a practitioner's own capacity for concentration precisely by scattering the mind: craving for wealth and possessions scatters the mind, improper conduct amid dispute and conflict scatters the mind, painful austerities pursued through misguided observances scatter the mind, and speculation about reality unguided by proper reasoning scatters the mind — all four operating through mental consciousness alone. The treatise then explains that sustained cultivation, employing an ever more superior antidote, eventually permanently severs both remaining latent seeds — craving's own seed and the four bonds' own seed together — beyond merely suppressing their present manifestation. Once both are genuinely, permanently severed, the objects these afflictions once clung to — form, feeling, perception, and formations — no longer continue to arise as objects of attachment at all, this being ultimate freedom from affliction's own bondage; and because these objects no longer continue, the latent, seed-level affliction that once followed consciousness reaches its own final cessation, so that consciousness itself no longer takes up residence within any of the four stations whatsoever, having become permanently purified through this same antidote. With this, the treatise concludes, the cessation of the station's own sustaining condition stands fully explained.

10. Section: The Station Itself Comes to Rest — Twelve Angles Beginning With the Three Gates of Liberation

The treatise turns to the station's own cessation, explaining first why it earns this name: because the very cause capable of generating a future rebirth has itself ceased, consciousness no longer takes hold of, nor brings to completion, any future embodiment at all, and saṃsāra's own continuity is therefore decisively ended — this is called the cessation of the station. The treatise then distinguishes twelve further angles describing this same cessation. The first, non-abiding, names the purified consciousness achieved through this very antidote. The second, cessation of growth, names how this same causal severance prevents consciousness from ever again growing entangled with its former objects. The third, purposelessness, names how skillful cultivation of the gate of emptiness — realizing the selflessness of both person and phenomena — brings purified consciousness to a state where it no longer generates any further karmic activity at all; the lecture notes that a śrāvaka's own contemplation of emptiness focuses on personal selflessness alone, seeking liberation from one's own affliction, while a bodhisattva's contemplation extends to the selflessness of phenomena themselves, since a bodhisattva seeks neither to remain bound to saṃsāra nor to abide in nirvāṇa exclusively, but to abide in reality's own freedom from all verbal designation for the sake of others. The fourth, contentment, names how skillful cultivation of the gate of wishlessness brings purified consciousness to no longer crave any further result within the three realms whatsoever — a śrāvaka contemplating the impermanence and suffering of the aggregates to relinquish all further seeking, a bodhisattva instead relinquishing entanglement with verbal designation itself. The fifth, abiding, names how skillful cultivation of the gate of signlessness brings purified consciousness to rest entirely free of any grasped mark, a śrāvaka contemplating the quiescent, markless nature of the aggregates directly, a bodhisattva extending this same freedom to every mark generated by conceptual proliferation. And the sixth, ultimate liberation, names the culmination of all the preceding five together — the complete, settled result of freedom from growth, purposeless action, contentment, and markless abiding all achieved at once.

Having named six of the twelve angles describing the station's own cessation, he notes, the treatise turns next to elaborating "ultimate liberation" in far greater depth, illustrated through some of this literature's most striking accounts of dying without fear.

11. Section: Dying Without Fear — The Arhat Bitten by a Poisonous Snake, and Xuánzàng's Own Final, Lucid Days

The treatise elaborates ultimate liberation's own further characteristic: because such a practitioner clings to no self and nothing belonging to self within any conditioned phenomenon whatsoever, the eventual dissolution of form and the rest at the natural end of this present life brings no fear at all — this very fearlessness itself revealing that one's own being has already achieved the genuine purity belonging to nirvāṇa with residue remaining. The lecture illustrates this fearlessness with a celebrated account: an arhat, bitten by a poisonous snake, remained entirely composed, his complexion undisturbed, calmly instructing those nearby to carry him out to open ground before his body finally came apart; when Śāriputra, visiting him, asked how he could remain so utterly at ease facing death, the arhat explained simply that he had never regarded this body as himself or as belonging to himself, and so felt nothing to fear in its dissolution — precisely the fearlessness ordinary beings, still clinging to self and to what is theirs, could never achieve without the same depth of concentration or wisdom. The lecture extends this same theme to Master Xuánzàng's own final days: injured and aware his own life was nearing its end, he lay down and stopped eating, privately troubled by whether some error in his own translation work had caused his affliction — until a deity revealed that this present suffering was simply karmic debt from a past life as a king who had harmed others, now ripening all at once precisely because his own accumulated merit had otherwise reached completion, after which he would be reborn in Tuṣita heaven; reassured, he calmly stopped eating and remained lying on his right side, his students anxious yet finding his breath still present when they checked, Xuánzàng himself explaining that he abstained from food specifically to keep his own mind lucid as death approached, so that he might depart with full awareness and self-command.

12. Section: Six Final Angles — Freedom From Desire, From Feeling's Shadow, and All the Way to Utter Purity

The treatise completes the twelve-part analysis of the station's own cessation with six further angles. The seventh, permanent freedom from desire, names how such a practitioner no longer seeks either continued life or death itself, both equally beyond wishing for. The eighth, permanent freedom from shadow, names how the arhat, having transcended all feeling altogether, no longer casts what the lecture calls consciousness's own tree-shadow — pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling alike being likened to the shadow a tree casts once sunlight strikes it, ordinary beings driven to create karma by exactly this play of feeling, while the arhat, no longer subject to any of the three feelings, dwells at all times in the most complete equanimity toward whatever the six sense faculties encounter. The ninth, quiescence, names how every contaminated, afflicted consciousness has been utterly exhausted within present form, feeling, perception, and formations. The tenth, tranquility, names the uncontaminated consciousness belonging in sequence to the learners still progressing from the first to the third fruit, who have severed only some portion of the three realms' afflictions. The eleventh, coolness, names the accomplished liberation belonging to the arhat beyond further learning, who has already secured freedom from the three realms' craving and views and dwells within nirvāṇa with residue remaining. And the twelfth, purity, names the arhat's entry into nirvāṇa without residue remaining, at which point form, feeling, perception, and formations are permanently extinguished once and for all — the treatise's own extensive summary drawing every one of these twelve angles together into a single description of what it truly means for the station to come to rest.

Master Cháng Bǎi draws special attention to the eighth angle's own tree-shadow image: feeling itself, he notes, is not the problem — it is the compulsive craving and aversion feeling provokes in an unliberated mind that generates karma, so that the arhat's freedom lies not in becoming numb to feeling but in no longer being moved by it either way, dwelling instead in what the text calls the most complete equanimity. He also notes the treatise's own careful distinction, in its closing summary, between coolness (associated with nirvāṇa with residue remaining, still embodied) and purity (associated with nirvāṇa without residue remaining, the final passing), a distinction that becomes important immediately afterward when the treatise turns to explaining why, despite everything just said about the four stations, consciousness itself was never counted as a fifth station alongside form, feeling, perception, and formations — since, the treatise explains, consciousness's own essential nature is never itself defiled the way these four continually are, and no one, the lecture notes, ever falls in love with their own bare awareness the way they fall in love with a beautiful appearance, a pleasant feeling, or a cherished idea, precisely because consciousness is simply too subtle to become an object of attachment in its own right — a point Master Cháng Bǎi likens to the classical commentarial simile of a king's own throne: just as one speaks of "the king's road" or "the king's throne" without the king himself being the road or the throne, so too consciousness is what dwells within the four stations without ever being a station unto itself. With this, the account of the fourth of consciousness's five distinctions — by station — reaches its full and complete conclusion, and the treatise turns at last to the fifth and final distinction: consciousness's own changing aspects.

13. Section: Consciousness's Fifth and Final Distinction — Twenty Kinds of Mind Through Craving, Aversion, and Their Absence

The treatise opens the fifth and final of consciousness's five distinctions, its changing aspects, explaining that consciousness's own character shifts constantly according to what mental factors currently accompany it: a mind presently accompanied by craving is a craving mind, a mind from which craving has been overcome is a mind free of craving, a mind presently accompanied by aversion is an aversive mind, a mind from which aversion has been overcome is a mind free of aversion, and so on, the scriptures extending this same pairing all the way up to the unliberated mind and the utterly liberated mind — this single approach alone yielding what the treatise calls one entire way of distinguishing consciousness's changing aspects. The lecture notes that an earlier discussion, within the Stage of the Hearer's Vehicle, had already drawn out ten such paired afflictions and their ten corresponding antidotes from this same scheme, yielding twenty specific kinds of mind in total, each a snapshot of consciousness colored by whichever affliction or antidote currently accompanies it.

14. Section: Mind Classified by Realm — Four Kinds in the Desire Realm, Three Each Above, and Two Beyond All Three

The treatise turns to a second approach, classifying consciousness's changing aspects by realm. Beings in the desire realm possess four kinds of mind: a wholesome mind, arising when the first six consciousnesses correspond to non-craving, non-aversion, non-delusion, and the other wholesome mental factors, itself divided into innate wholesomeness and wholesomeness cultivated through effort; an unwholesome mind, arising when the first six consciousnesses correspond to craving, aversion, delusion, and the rest; an obscured-but-neutral mind, belonging solely to the mental faculty — the seventh consciousness's innate self-view and extreme-view, or the sixth consciousness's own innate versions of the same, counting as obscured because such views obstruct the noble path even though they are neither strongly wholesome nor unwholesome in themselves; and an unobscured-neutral mind, itself of four kinds — a mind that simply ripens from past karma, a mind occupied with ordinary deportment such as walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, a mind occupied with skilled craft such as painting or music, and a mind capable of miraculous transformation, this last belonging only to innately gifted beings such as devas, nāgas, and yakṣas, never to a meditatively cultivated result within the desire realm. The form realm possesses three of these four minds, lacking only the unwholesome, since beings there have already left behind aversion and sensual craving; the formless realm likewise possesses three, also lacking the unwholesome. And beyond all three realms, the uncontaminated mind itself is of two kinds — that of the learner, who has severed only part of the three realms' craving and views, and that of the one beyond learning, whose severance is already complete.

The treatise then details these same categories further: the desire realm's own wholesome mind divides into innate wholesomeness (natural goodness present from birth) and effortful wholesomeness (goodness cultivated through education or practice); its unobscured-neutral mind's four kinds are explained one by one — the karma-ripened mind belonging to the store-consciousness and to the first six consciousnesses spontaneously engaging their twelve sense-fields, the deportment mind occupied with everyday postures, the craft mind occupied with bodily or verbal skill, and the transformation mind, which in the desire realm is always an innate capacity belonging to devas, nāgas, and yakṣas rather than a result of cultivated meditative attainment — the lecture illustrating this with a modern account of a forensic examiner in Taiwan reportedly able to perceive the hell, hungry-ghost, and animal realms directly, itself an innate rather than meditatively cultivated capacity. 15. Section: Sixteen Minds Classified by What They Sever — The Four Truths Across Three Realms, Plus the Uncontaminated

17. Section: The Second Grade's Eight Minds — Scattering, Sinking, Rousing, Over-Rousing, and Finally Settling Into Quiet

The treatise turns to the second grade of practitioner, who has set out toward meditative concentration but has not yet attained it, describing eight distinct mental states such a practitioner passes through. The first, the contracted mind, names the moment attention settles inward and briefly quiets upon its object. The second, the scattered mind, names how attention, losing its mindfulness, runs outward toward the five sensory pleasures — suddenly recalling lunch, or a pleasant sound, or an attractive sight from the day before — the mind wandering off in exactly the way any beginning meditator recognizes within moments of sitting down. The third, the sunken mind, names how, when attention is drawn in too tightly and too quietly, dullness and drowsiness instead creep over the mind, a state easily mistaken for deep stillness but in fact simply torpor. The fourth, the rousing mind, names the practitioner's own remedy for this dullness: turning attention toward the six radiant objects of recollection — the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, moral discipline, generosity, and the heavens — deliberately lifting and brightening a mind that has begun to sink. The fifth, the agitated mind, names what happens when this same rousing is applied carelessly, without properly settling back onto the meditation object, so that attention instead spins off into thoughts of one's homeland, one's family, or fear of death. The sixth, the unagitated mind, names the correction of this same excess, attention properly returned to its object once agitation has been recognized. The seventh, the unsettled mind, names the state that persists so long as the two hindrances of dullness and agitation remain unsevered, neither calming nor insight yet able to rest quietly. 19. Section: Why Form Comes First — Five Reasons the Aggregates Are Always Listed in the Same Order

The treatise turns to the third of the six general aspects, explaining why the aggregates are consistently listed as form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, through five distinct approaches. By arising, the sequence follows how consciousness itself comes to be — eye and visible form, as conditions, giving rise to eye-consciousness, and so on through the mental faculty and mental phenomena giving rise to mental consciousness — form and consciousness thus named first and second since they are what feeling, perception, and formations, as mental factors bound to consciousness, actually depend upon in order to arise at all, precisely as the scriptures say: the meeting of the three gives rise to contact, and contact conditions feeling and the rest. By antidote, the sequence instead follows the four foundations of mindfulness taught to correct the four inverted views — mistaking the impure body for pure, suffering for pleasure, the selfless for self, and the impermanent for permanent — yielding the order body, feeling, mind, phenomena, and so form, feeling, consciousness, then perception and formations together. By continuity, the sequence follows how affliction actually operates across a lifetime: the six faculties and their objects, both belonging to form, serve as the basis; feeling and perception generate defilement in the present, by relishing or dressing up whatever is encountered; formations alone perform the karma, wholesome or unwholesome, that ripens into future birth, aging, and death; and consciousness, being that which is defiled by all three others rather than an agent of defilement itself, is named last of all. By abiding, the sequence simply follows the four stations already discussed at such length — form, feeling, perception, and formations named first as what consciousness takes up residence within, consciousness itself named last as what abides. And by convention, the sequence follows the natural order in which two people meeting each other actually come to know one another: the body is seen first, hence form is named first; next, from complexion and bearing, one senses whether the other is flourishing or declining, and whether their circumstances are painful or pleasant, hence feeling is named second; next, through language, one learns a name, a lineage, a type, hence perception is named third; next, through conduct and speech, one judges whether a person is foolish or wise, hence formations is named fourth; and finally, since none of this — appearance, feeling, name, or judgment — exists apart from the single moment of discriminating awareness that grasps it all, consciousness is named last of all, established as the innermost self upon which everything else depends. A second version of this same convention-based approach explains the very same order instead through what one depends upon for one's own requisites and one's own being: the body and the objects one makes use of belong to form; the pleasure or pain felt in using them belongs to feeling; the language used to describe oneself or another belongs to perception; and the lawful or unlawful conduct by which requisites are amassed and pleasure or pain thereby incurred belongs to formations — these four together constituting what belongs to the self, while consciousness alone, upon which all four depend and apart from which none of them exist, constitutes the self as such.

20. Section: Subsumption — Mapping Each Aggregate Onto the Elements, Sense-Fields, Links, and Faculties

The treatise turns to the fifth general aspect, subsumption, asking how many aggregates, elements, sense-fields, links of dependent origination, valid-or-invalid grounds, and faculties are subsumed under form, and so on through each of the remaining four aggregates — sixteen kinds of subsumption in total, the first six organized around these six categories themselves. Form aggregate, the treatise explains, subsumes the whole of the form aggregate alone among the five; ten full elements and ten full sense-fields, namely the five sense faculties and their five objects; a mere portion of the phenomena-element and phenomena-sense-field, namely whichever form belongs there; a portion of six links of dependent origination — formations, name-and-form, the six sense-fields, existence, birth, and aging-and-death, wherever bodily or verbal activity, or the form-portion of each link, is involved; a portion of the valid-and-invalid ground, since this category pertains to all phenomena and form is only part of it; and the whole of seven faculties, the five sense faculties together with the male and female faculties. Feeling aggregate subsumes the whole of the feeling aggregate alone; a portion of the phenomena-element and phenomena-sense-field; the whole of the single link of dependent origination named feeling itself; a portion of three further links — name-and-form, birth, and aging-and-death, wherever feeling is present; a portion of the valid-and-invalid ground; the whole of the five feeling faculties — pleasure, pain, joy, distress, and equanimity; and a portion of the three uncontaminated faculties, since three of the nine faculties used to establish them — joy, pleasure, and equanimity — belong to feeling. Perception aggregate subsumes the whole of the perception aggregate alone; a portion of the phenomena-element and phenomena-sense-field; a portion of the same three links — name-and-form, birth, and aging-and-death; a portion of the valid-and-invalid ground; and none of the twenty-two faculties at all, since no separate "perception faculty" was ever established, perception itself being subsumed under wisdom within those schemes. Formations aggregate, by far the broadest of the five since it contains every mental factor besides feeling and perception, subsumes the whole of the formations aggregate alone; a portion of the phenomena-element and phenomena-sense-field; the whole of four links — ignorance, contact, craving, and grasping; a portion of five further links — formations, name-and-form, existence, birth, and aging-and-death; a portion of the valid-and-invalid ground; the whole of six faculties — the five wholesome faculties of faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom, together with the life faculty; and a portion of the three uncontaminated faculties, since the five wholesome faculties belong here. Just as these relations obtain for the aggregates, the treatise notes, so too do analogous relations of mutual subsumption obtain among the elements, sense-fields, links, valid-and-invalid grounds, and faculties themselves.

21. Section: Ten Further Kinds of Subsumption, From Seed and Characteristic to Ultimate Truth Itself

The treatise adds a further ten kinds of subsumption applicable across the aggregates, elements, sense-fields, links, valid-and-invalid grounds, and faculties alike. Subsumption by seed groups each phenomenon under its own generating cause. Subsumption by characteristic groups each phenomenon under its own particular nature and its shared, universal nature — form's particular nature being obstruction and change, feeling's being experience, perception's being the grasping of a sign, formations' being volitional construction, consciousness's being discernment, while impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness are shared by all. Subsumption by category groups each phenomenon under others of the identical kind, such as earth, water, fire, and air all belonging together as form on account of their shared changeability. Subsumption by condition-state groups each phenomenon according to whether it presently accompanies pleasant, painful, or neutral feeling. Subsumption by inseparability, illustrated at length, groups each phenomenon together with everything it depends upon and everything that depends upon it in turn — form aggregate, for instance, never existing apart from feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, since recognizing a form at all already requires perception to name it, feeling to register it, formations to act upon it, and consciousness to know it; the lecture illustrates this with the everyday observation that physical discomfort readily provokes irritation, while a mind settled in meditative ease resists provocation far more easily, precisely because body and mind, form and consciousness, are never actually separable in experience. Subsumption by time groups each phenomenon according to whether it belongs to the past, future, or present, the treatise's own commentary noting that a being's present character can only be understood by tracing its past causes, exactly as compassion for someone behaving badly today deepens once one considers what past conditions produced them, and exactly as continuing wholesome cultivation now plants the causes of a better future. Subsumption by place groups each phenomenon according to where it currently occurs or arises, as when eye-consciousness is said to occur "at" its visible object, or feeling and thought arise "at" a remembered landscape. Subsumption by completeness observes that the whole of any given category — all five aggregates, all eighteen elements, all twelve sense-fields, and so on — is entirely accounted for by exactly that number, with nothing left over and nothing further to add, a point about the exhaustiveness of the entire scheme rather than about any one item within it. Subsumption by portion groups each phenomenon under whichever fractional part of a larger category it occupies, as form's own share of the phenomena-element and phenomena-sense-field. And subsumption by ultimate truth groups every one of these phenomena, without exception, under suchness itself — since none of them possesses any real, findable essence apart from this same ultimate nature, itself also called emptiness, the realm of phenomena, or the true limit. Sixteen kinds of subsumption altogether, the treatise concludes — six by category and ten by these further approaches — hold true not only for the aggregates but, by the same reasoning, for the elements, sense-fields, links, valid-and-invalid grounds, and faculties as well. The treatise then adds a final, simpler threefold subsumption covering all conditioned and unconditioned phenomena without remainder: form aggregate subsumes every conditioned form-phenomenon, mental sense-field subsumes every conditioned mental phenomenon, and phenomena-element, being broader still, subsumes every unconditioned phenomenon as well — form aggregate and mental sense-field together exhausting the conditioned, phenomena-element alone exhausting the unconditioned.

22. Section: What Form Depends Upon, What Mind Depends Upon — And the Six General Aspects at Last Complete

The treatise turns to the sixth and final general aspect, support, asking first how many grounds form aggregate depends upon in order to occur, and answering with six: the ground of establishment, namely the primordial wind, water, and earth wheels upon which the physical universe itself is first built at the start of a cosmic age; the ground of shelter, namely dwellings that protect beings from wind, rain, and predators; the ground of requisites, namely the clothing, food, bedding, medicine, furnishings, and lighting of daily life; the ground of the faculties themselves, namely the five physical sense organs; the ground the faculties in turn depend upon, namely the visible, fleshly form of the eye, ear, and the rest that houses the subtle faculty proper; and the ground of the meditative states possessed by beings of great spiritual power, referring to the fundamental absorptions attained by eighth-ground bodhisattvas, arhats, and pratyekabuddhas capable of actually transmuting matter — an eighth-ground bodhisattva's transformation of ordinary soil into genuinely usable gold, the lecture notes, unlike an ordinary meditator's merely temporary transformation that reverts the moment concentration lapses, since such a bodhisattva's own mastery over matter endures far longer. The treatise maps these seven grounds onto four kinds of practitioner: one who enjoys sensory pleasure depends on the first four — desire, hope, the object, and investigation-and-analysis, describing a householder's condition; one who upholds moral discipline or practices with diligence depends on the single ground of correct awareness, describing a renunciant's condition, since maintaining precepts or sustained effort above all requires constant self-monitoring of one's own arising thoughts; one who has attained access concentration depends on the expedient means to purity alone, describing the threshold just prior to full absorption; and one who abides in a fundamental absorption depends on purity alone, having already left behind the desire realm's craving, aversion, and harmfulness. 23. Section: How Many "Things" Make an Eye? Three Ways of Counting, From One Up to Ten

The treatise opens the first of the ten angles, kind, with a pointed question: of the eleven kinds of matter belonging to form aggregate — the five sense faculties, their five objects, and the form subsumed under the mental sense-field — how many distinct "things" does the eye faculty itself actually contain? The answer depends entirely on which of three modes of subsumption one applies. By subsumption according to essential nature, the eye is only a single thing: the pure, subtle matter upon which eye-consciousness depends, its own defining essence and nothing more. By subsumption according to inseparability, the eye is seven things at once, since the eye faculty never actually occurs apart from the body it depends upon, the earth-element that gives it solidity, and the color, smell, taste, and touch that any piece of matter inevitably carries with it — eye, body, earth, color, smell, taste, and touch together forming a single inseparable cluster. And by subsumption according to elemental seed, the eye is ten things, since even though earth-element predominates in its makeup, the seeds of water, fire, and wind are latently present as well, ready to manifest their own function the moment conditions call for it — hence the same seven things already named, plus the water, fire, and wind elements besides. The same threefold analysis applies identically to the ear, nose, and tongue faculties, each also being one thing by essential nature, seven by inseparability, and ten by elemental seed — only the particular consciousness each depends upon differing between them. The body faculty alone requires a small but telling exception: since a body can and does exist without eyes, ears, nose, or tongue — as when a being is first conceived, nothing yet present but a single drop of parental fluid, long before any of the four other faculties have developed — inseparability for the body faculty must exclude those four, counting instead only body, earth, color, smell, taste, and touch, six things in total, while by essential nature the body faculty remains, as with the others, the single pure matter that bodily consciousness depends upon.

24. Section: Why Sound Is Different — Present Only Sometimes, Yet Latent Everywhere

The treatise turns to the external objects, explaining first that color, smell, taste, and touch may be analyzed by the same inseparability logic just used for the internal faculties, simply omitting the internal sense faculties themselves from the count, since not every faculty actually perceives every one of these four objects — the point being that color, smell, taste, and touch are mutually inseparable from one another, any one of the four always accompanied by the other three within a single cluster of matter. Sound, however, the treatise stresses, is not always present the way these four are, since it arises only when the right causes and conditions come together — the striking of one thing against another, wind moving through some obstruction, and so on — and so must be treated separately: wherever sound actually occurs, that location's inseparable cluster gains one further member beyond the usual set, while considered instead by elemental seed, sound's own seed is latent absolutely everywhere, present in potential within every cluster of matter whatsoever, ready to manifest the instant fire crackles, water splashes, or wind gusts, precisely as the Stage of the Hearer's Vehicle had already explained at length when it built up its account of color, sound, smell, taste, and touch one by one and then multiplied each into ten further varieties.

25. Section: Twenty Tactile Sensations, All Provisional Names for the Four Elements' Shifting States

The treatise turns to a detailed breakdown of the tactile object, explaining that everything spoken of under touch — smoothness, heaviness, and the rest — is nothing more than a provisional designation given to some particular state of the four elements, never a separate substance of its own. Smoothness is provisionally named for the elements' own purity, illustrated by the Buddha's own famously smooth skin, upon which even a mosquito cannot find purchase and simply slides off, unlike the coarser skin of an ordinary being on which insects can easily land. Heaviness is provisionally named for the elements' own solidity and firmness; roughness and lightness, conversely, for their impurity and lack of firmness. Softness is provisionally named for an impure, sluggish state of the elements, as when the whole body feels limp and without energy. Coldness is provisionally named for water and wind combining together, the very combination that also brings on rheumatic dampness in cold places. Hunger, thirst, and weakness are provisionally named for a deficiency and imbalance in what sustains the body, while their opposites, strength and satiation, are provisionally named for the very same elements being neither deficient nor imbalanced. Sickness is provisionally named for the four elements' own imbalance and disordered change — excess fire bringing feverish heat, excess wind bringing tremors or paralysis, excess earth bringing obesity, excess water bringing swelling. Aging is provisionally named for change and imbalance accumulating over time; death, for the vital faculty's own final and irreversible imbalance, the four elements finally coming apart. Itching is provisionally named for some disorder in the blood, as when a meditator sitting quietly suddenly feels as though ants were crawling somewhere on the skin — simply the qi and blood failing to circulate smoothly. Fainting is provisionally named for improper food throwing the elements into imbalance. Stickiness is provisionally named for earth and water combining, as in the clamminess of sweat. Fatigue is provisionally named for the imbalance brought on by constant coming and going, while its relief, rest, is provisionally named for that same imbalance settling back into equilibrium — the lecture noting that even a bodhisattva devoted entirely to benefiting others must still build genuine rest into their practice, since without it the body soon breaks down and the work cannot continue. And vigor is provisionally named for a body free of grime and lassitude, appearing energetic and full of spirit. The Compendium, the lecture notes, lists twenty-two such tactile qualities in total rather than this treatise's twenty, the two additional being slowness and urgency, otherwise agreeing in substance throughout. The treatise then draws all of these together under six broader states of the four elements — pure-or-impure, firm-or-not-firm, sluggish, combined, imbalanced, and balanced — and notes finally that these same six may equally be expanded into eight or contracted back to six, the two groupings being entirely equivalent, since neither adds or omits anything beyond the same twenty tactile qualities already explained.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this entire twenty-fold breakdown of touch as a striking demonstration of just how far the Abhidharma tradition is willing to push the principle that ordinary, seemingly solid experiences — hunger, fatigue, itching, even sickness and death — are never substances in their own right but only provisional labels for the four elements shifting between states of balance and imbalance, purity and impurity. Having completed this detailed physical inventory of matter down to its most granular tactile qualities, he notes, the treatise has now finished the first sub-portion of the kind analysis — form's basic constituent inventory across all eleven categories — and turns next to form's own relationship to consciousness, existence, and provisional designation.

26. Section: Two Consciousnesses Know Every Object, and Why the Form Realm Has No Smell or Taste

The treatise turns to several further clarifications about form aggregate. First, every one of the objects from color through touch is known by two consciousnesses working together — its own dedicated sense-consciousness and mental consciousness accompanying it — sometimes apprehending gradually, one uniform aspect of an object at a time, and sometimes suddenly, an entire scene of varied colors or textures grasped all at once; the five sense faculties themselves, by contrast, are known only by mental consciousness, since none of the five sense-consciousnesses can perceive its own supporting faculty directly. Second, beings in the form realm have no actually functioning smell or taste, and consequently no nose- or tongue-consciousness either, since they no longer rely on solid food to sustain their bodies, nourished instead by the joy of meditative absorption and freed from all the daily labor of preparing and eating meals — yet this absence, the treatise stresses, holds only for present function, not for underlying capacity, since the seeds of smell and taste remain latent even in beings who no longer need them, the nose and tongue themselves still present, merely ornamental and inactive.

27. Section: What Is Genuinely Real Within Form, and What Is Merely a Convenient Label

The treatise turns to distinguishing real from provisional matter within form aggregate, explaining that nine kinds of matter are genuinely real substances: the five sense faculties together with color, sound, smell, and taste. Even among these, the treatise notes, further distinctions of real and provisional could be drawn, but the point is made most clearly through touch, where the difference is starkest: blue, yellow, red, and white are real qualities of color, while length, shortness, roundness, squareness, and size are merely descriptive labels applied to color's shape, without any real substance of their own; sound caused by living beings and sound not so caused are both real, while pleasant or unpleasant sound is simply a judgment made about it; smell arising from a genuine combination of substances is real, while good or bad smell is likewise just a judgment of discrimination; and bitter, sour, salty, bland, sweet, and pungent taste are real, while palatable or unpalatable taste is again merely descriptive. Among the tactile qualities, only the four elements themselves — earth, water, fire, and wind, with their respective natures of solidity, moisture, warmth, and movement — are genuinely real substances capable of producing further matter; everything else discussed earlier under touch, smoothness, heaviness, and the rest, is entirely provisional, being nothing more than a particular state of these same four elements given a convenient name.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this real-versus-provisional distinction as the treatise's own quiet warning against over-literalizing ordinary descriptive language: calling a sound "pleasant" or a smell "good" describes a judgment consciousness makes about an object, not some additional substance layered onto it, a caution worth carrying into everyday experience where such judgments are so often mistaken for objective facts about the world rather than the mind's own overlay. Having sorted out real and provisional matter among form's eleven basic categories, he notes, the treatise turns to a further, more intricate case: the form subsumed under the mental sense-field itself, which turns out to include some of the most philosophically significant matter in the entire system.

28. Section: Five Kinds of Mind-Object Matter, From the Subtlest Particle to Form Born of Mastery in Meditation

The treatise turns to form subsumed under the mental sense-field — matter that mental consciousness alone perceives, rather than any of the five sense-consciousnesses — explaining that this too divides into the real and the provisional. The lecture supplements the root text with the Demonstration of Consciousness-Only's own five-fold classification of this same category: matter reduced through analysis, the imagined result of mentally breaking any object down to its smallest conceivable unit, itself merely mental consciousness's own construction rather than any genuinely findable smallest particle; matter reduced through abstraction, similarly analyzing empty space or subtle qualities like light and shadow down to an imagined final unit, likewise a mental construction; matter arising from feeling, referring to the subtle, unmanifest matter constituting a precept-vow, visualized as descending from the crown at ordination and becoming part of one's own being, itself provisionally established upon the seed of the volitional mental factor; matter arising from conceptual proliferation, meaning the ordinary images an unenlightened mind's second moment of discrimination constructs after the first bare moment of sensory contact has already passed — a table, a chair, a person, recognized and named through language, though these mental images are not themselves the real substance being named; and matter born of mastery in meditation, the actual physical result a sufficiently powerful concentration can produce, available in principle to both ordinary and noble beings alike, though only the meditative mastery of eighth-ground bodhisattvas and above is capable of producing genuinely real, functionally usable matter this way, ordinary meditators producing only imagined, provisional versions of the same. The treatise explains that whatever falls within the domain of a being possessed of great meditative power, appearing much like a supernatural transformation, together with the resulting object and the consciousness and mental factors that correspond to it, counts as genuinely real matter, while precept-vows both wholesome and unwholesome are entirely provisional, established only upon the strength of a vow and a fixed duration of intention, so that someone acting well or badly without any such deliberate commitment counts as having neither.

Master Cháng Bǎi draws a striking parallel between this ancient analysis of "the subtlest reducible particle" and modern physics: just as this treatise concludes that no genuinely findable smallest unit of matter actually exists, mere mental construction standing in for whatever analysis cannot ultimately locate, so too, he notes, contemporary particle physics has kept dividing matter further and further — down through nuclei, then quarks — only to find that no final, indivisible substance is ever actually reached, a convergence he treats as remarkably confirming this scripture's own ancient insight that "the smallest particle" was always a convenient fiction rather than a discovered fact, one whose real purpose is to loosen the ordinary, unreflective conviction that one's own body is something solid and truly one's own. Having laid out the five kinds of mind-object matter, he notes, the treatise turns to an extended scholarly discussion of the last and most remarkable of the five — meditatively mastered matter — asking exactly which beings are actually capable of producing it.

29. Section: Can Ordinary Meditators Really Transform Matter, or Only Buddhas and Advanced Bodhisattvas?

The treatise takes up an extended discussion of meditatively mastered matter's own scope, explaining first that such matter always belongs to whichever realm the meditation itself belongs to, its four elements matching that realm's own elements. It further explains that this matter belongs to the conventional, worldly level of truth rather than the ultimate, since it still arises through concentration built upon deliberate, conceptual intention, common to both contaminated and uncontaminated meditators alike — the lecture noting a genuine scholarly disagreement over exactly which beings can produce it, one line of interpretation holding that even bodhisattvas prior to the path of insight, and beings in realms with no physical bodies at all, can manifest such matter, citing scriptural accounts of formless-realm devas said to weep tears at the Buddha's side, while a second, more restrictive interpretation, following the Compendium of Determinations' insistence that only a mind of "utmost stillness and great power" can generate matter this way, restricts the capacity to eighth-ground bodhisattvas and above, whose transformations function exactly like real matter — one hundred million dollars a bodhisattva at that level manifests can genuinely be spent — unlike an ordinary meditator's transformation, which reverts the moment concentration lapses and was never functionally real to begin with; the lecture sides with this second, more restrictive reading as the more carefully reasoned one. The treatise further specifies that only a settled, effortless, luminous, and already-purified state of mind — free of any of the eleven classical obstacles to concentration, arrived at simply by prior intention rather than laborious present effort — is capable of producing this matter at all, a mind that must still strain and repeatedly deliberate to force an image into view never succeeding at genuine transformation; and it adds a final, remarkable qualification: certain fully accomplished buddhas and bodhisattvas can manifest such matter without even requiring conceptual, effortful intention as its cause at all, an entirely inconceivable capacity belonging to their subsequently attained wisdom, though it remains distinct from the wholly non-discriminating wisdom of the supramundane path itself.

30. Section: Six Marks of Form — Own-Nature, Shared Nature, Mutual Dependence, and the Four Elements' Own Distinct Functions

The treatise first contrasts the desire realm's matter with the form realm's: form-realm matter is pure and supreme, radiating its own light, extremely subtle beyond the reach of any lower realm's sense faculties, and free of all painful sensation, since form-realm beings' bodies never fall ill, while desire-realm matter is neither radiant nor subtle and carries a great deal of painful sensation, exactly the coarse, opaque, frequently afflicted bodies familiar from ordinary experience. The treatise then draws the entire kind analysis to its close with a six-mark summary of form aggregate as a whole. Own-nature names each element's distinctive character — earth's solidity, water's moisture, fire's warmth, wind's movement — and each sense faculty's own particular pure matter. Shared nature names what every form has in common: constant change and the occupying of space. Mutual dependence names how the four elements serve as what all further derived matter depends upon, the elements themselves the support, everything built from them the supported. Function of use names how form arises to serve the needs of the internal sense faculties — sometimes a cluster of matter arising as pure solidity, sometimes as pure moisture, warmth, or movement, sometimes as some combination of these, precisely according to what the sense faculties require, a point the treatise offers as vivid, if implicit, testimony that the outer world itself arises in dependence on beings' own need to make use of it, never separate from mind. Function itself names each element's own active operations — earth able to strike, touch, transform, and break; to establish structures such as houses or the very framework of the body; to serve as the ground all earthbound beings and their world depend upon; sometimes to cause harm, as in an earthquake; and to gather other matter to itself, as a body gathers water, fire, and wind, or a mountain gathers plants and trees. Water is able to flow and moisten; to bind loose matter together, as it binds grains of sand; to irrigate crops; sometimes to cause harm, as in a flood; and to sustain living things, as fish depend on water to live. Fire is able to illuminate; to ripen, as it cooks raw rice into food; to burn away refuse, grass, and trees; sometimes to cause harm, as when it burns down a house; and to sustain life, as bodily warmth keeps a being alive and as external fire generates power. And wind is able to set things into motion, as it moves leaves and turns windmills; to move along with things; to dry out moisture, as coastal wind dries and chaps the skin, or dries orange peel for medicine; sometimes to cause harm, as in a typhoon or mudslide; and to sustain living beings, since a single missing breath ends a life entirely.

31. Section: Interpenetrating Bodies of Light, a Boy Who Ran Through Glass Unharmed, and Kumārajīva's Bell — Kind's Closing Mark

The treatise completes form's kind analysis with the sixth mark, subtlety, itself of three kinds. Subtlety through reduction names the imagined smallest unit reached by analyzing any piece of matter down as far as it will go — the particle discussed earlier. Subtlety of category names things that are simply subtle by their very nature, such as wind, invisible to the eye, and the matter of an intermediate-state being between death and rebirth, visible only to a purified divine eye and never to an ordinary one. And subtlety through mental mastery names the matter belonging to beings of the form and formless realms, whose minds, no longer bound by desire, can move freely and manifest matter however deeply concentrated intention wishes, this matter too being extremely subtle. The treatise illustrates this third kind with a striking scriptural account: certain devas of "equal mind" — the lecture identifies them as the five Pure Abodes at the peak of the fourth absorption — stand, when visiting the Buddha to hear the teaching, bodies sixteen thousand yojanas in height, upon a space no larger than the tip of a single hair, their bodies able to pass freely through one another without any obstruction whatsoever, precisely like separate beams of lamplight overlapping in the same room without ever colliding. The lecture explains that these devas attained this capacity by cultivating, while still human, a mind of genuine equanimity free of all discrimination between large and small, self and other — the very habit of distinguishing size and identity being, the lecture stresses, exactly what causes ordinary beings to obstruct one another in the first place. Three illustrations drive the point home: the account of the Buddha once setting his sitting-mat upon the surface of water without it sinking, something unthinkable to anyone still convinced that solid objects must obstruct and be obstructed; a boy at a youth summer camp, rushing without any awareness that he was about to hit anything, who ran straight through an unframed pane of glass at Wànfó Temple and shattered it in the shape of his own body, yet suffered nothing worse than a minor cut, precisely because his mind held no expectation of obstruction at the moment of impact; and the childhood story of Kumārajīva, who as an eight-year-old lifted a temple's great bronze bell onto his own head without difficulty, only to find it suddenly unbearably heavy the instant the thought "how am I lifting something so heavy" arose in his mind — proof, the lecture concludes, that what obstructs and what does not is ultimately determined by the mind's own conceptual overlay rather than by matter's own fixed, independent nature. With this sixth mark, the treatise's own six-part summary of form aggregate — own-nature, shared nature, mutual dependence, function-of-use, function, and subtlety — stands complete, closing the entire first of the ten angles, kind, and the treatise turns next to the second angle: the subtlest constituent of matter, the so-called particle, opening with the very question modern physics itself continues to ask — by how many marks is such a particle actually established?

32. Section: Five Ways Particles Are Established, and Why Clustering Matter Still Counts as Obstructive

The treatise explains that the subtlest particle is established through five distinct approaches. Through analysis, it is simply the imagined final unit reached by analytical wisdom breaking any piece of matter down as far as possible — not something that genuinely exists in its own right, and therefore never actually arising or ceasing, nor is any cluster of matter truly built up out of really existing particles; matter, the treatise stresses, arises all at once as consciousness's own manifestation rather than accumulating gradually from smaller pieces, particles being posited only as a convenient device for weakening ordinary beings' attachment to matter as something solid and real. Through category, there are fifteen kinds altogether: one for each of the five sense faculties, one for each of their five objects, four for the elements earth, water, fire, and wind, and one for the single kind of genuinely real matter subsumed under the mental sense-field, namely matter born of meditative mastery. Through independence, each of these fifteen categories establishes its own distinct nature through its own particle, an eye-particle distinct from an ear-particle and so on. Through companionship, particles of different categories cluster inseparably together at a single location — the earth-particle's own location, for instance, always sharing space with water-, fire-, and wind-particles as well, none crowding the others out. This last point provoked a natural question: if matter possessing physical resistance can still share the very same location without displacing anything else there, why isn't it simply classified as non-resistant instead? The treatise answers that these co-located particles arise in mutual accord without ever obstructing one another, precisely because a shared karmic cause brings them into being together in the first place, so that a single cluster of matter can be seen, smelled, tasted, and touched all at once by the different sense faculties without contradiction — proof, the treatise argues, that such matter remains genuinely resistant, sharing space through harmonious co-arising rather than through any absence of resistance. The treatise illustrates the same principle with the intermediate-state being between death and rebirth, whose subtle body passes freely through mountains and oceans exactly as though possessing supernatural power, yet becomes obstructed the instant it reaches the site of its own future conception — proof that even this remarkably unobstructed form of matter remains, in the end, genuinely resistant rather than devoid of resistance altogether, obstruction and non-obstruction alike being simply a function of where a given karmic result is due to manifest. Finally, through indivisibility, the particle is established as that which, unlike any ordinary cluster of matter, has no further parts capable of being analyzed out — the true floor beneath which no finer distinction remains possible.

33. Section: Which Eyes Can See a Particle, and Five Mistaken Ways of Imagining One

The treatise asks which of the five kinds of eye — the physical eye, the divine eye, the wise eye of the noble ones, the dharma eye, and the buddha eye — actually takes the particle as an object, answering that only the latter three do, since the physical eye can perceive only coarse, obstructed matter, and even the divine eye, capable of seeing through physical obstruction across vast distances, can perceive only a cluster's surface, its top, bottom, front, back, and two sides, its light or darkness — never the particle itself, which exists only as wisdom's own analytical construction rather than as any genuinely perceivable location. Asked next why the particle is said to neither arise nor cease, the treatise answers with a simile: a cluster of matter, whenever it first arises, arises completely all at once, and whenever it finally ceases, ceases completely as well, without lingering down to some final, particle-sized remainder in between — exactly like a drop of water evaporating in sunlight, which disappears all at once rather than shrinking down to some final, indivisible speck of moisture before vanishing. The treatise then names five specific ways of conceiving the particle that it rejects as fundamentally unreasonable: first, the view, held by certain non-Mahāyāna schools and by non-Buddhist Vaiśeṣika philosophers, that particles genuinely exist in their own right within a cluster of matter, clusters being built up from them the way a building is built from bricks; second, the view that particles themselves arise and cease, some proponents even positing eternal "parent particles" from which lesser particles are generated; third, the view, again from the Vaiśeṣika school, that particles combine and disperse — scattering into space when a cosmic age ends in conflagration, then recombining from these same scattered parent particles when a new age begins; fourth, the view, associated with certain other non-Mahāyāna schools, that derived matter accumulates and mutually interpenetrates within a fixed quantity of elemental particles, a position the treatise considers just as mistaken as positing particles that simply accumulate into a cluster without qualification; and fifth, the Vaiśeṣika view that particles are literally generative, "parent" particles combining to beget "child" particles the way parents beget children, one and the same substance simply reproducing itself indefinitely. Genuine practice, the treatise concludes, requires deliberately overturning these five mistaken conceptions through proper reflection, recognizing instead that the particle, being without any real essence, is without arising or ceasing, without combining or dispersing, and therefore never actually the building block from which any cluster of matter is composed.

34. Section: Five Benefits of Seeing Through Matter, and the King's Chariot That Could Not Be Found

The treatise names five benefits that come from properly reflecting on the particle. The first is the capacity to purify and expand one's meditation object — analyzing a solid cluster of matter down into its finest components, as in the ten kasina exercises where earth, water, fire, wind, and the colors blue, yellow, red, and white are each contemplated, particle by particle, as pervading all of space, this capacity to see through matter's apparent solidity eventually yielding genuine supernatural attainment. The second is the gradual severing of the view of a real, permanent self, since analyzing the body down to its smallest constituent parts reveals that no findable "self" or "what belongs to self" was ever really there to begin with. The third, following directly from the second, is the gradual severing of pride, since the arrogance that so often accompanies a false sense of self naturally diminishes once that self can no longer be found under analysis. The fourth is the gradual subduing of afflictions generally, self-view and pride being merely the most prominent among many entanglements this same analytical practice loosens. And the fifth is the swift removal of attachment to any fixed characteristic whatsoever, extending even to attachment toward phenomena themselves, not merely toward a personal self. All of this, the treatise concludes, is how the particle should be reasonably contemplated. The lecture closes this second angle with a celebrated illustration from the Milinda-questions literature: the sage Senaka, asked by the king whether "Senaka" was truly present, replied that no such person existed at all — is the hair Senaka? the head? the eyes, the flesh, the organs? — the king conceding, item by item, that none of these alone was Senaka; Senaka then asked the king how he had arrived, and when the king answered "by chariot," pressed him the same way — is the canopy the chariot? the wheel, the axle, the seat, the yoke? — until the king, unable to locate any single part that was truly "the chariot," was forced to admit that he had, in a sense, spoken falsely: he had certainly ridden in something, yet no findable thing called "chariot" was ever really there, "chariot" being merely a convenient name given to a particular assembly of parts, precisely as "a person" is merely a convenient name given to a particular assembly of aggregates. The king, delighted, recognized Senaka as a genuinely wise teacher and continued their conversations daily at court, applying this same method of relentless analysis to death, rebirth, and the perennial puzzle of whether the consciousness that is reborn is the very same consciousness that died, or an entirely different one — the same technique, the lecture notes, that any practitioner can apply directly to their own experience: searching from head to toe for something that is truly "me," and finding, at every point, only parts that could just as easily belong to someone else.

35. Section: Five Ways Matter Arises — Beginning With What Derived Matter Depends Upon

The treatise turns to the third angle, explaining that matter arises in briefly five ways: through support, through seed, through generative momentum, through sustaining, and through diminishment. Arising through support, the first of these, describes how derived matter always arises at the very location occupied by the four elements it depends upon — wherever earth, water, fire, and wind gather in the womb to form what will become the eye faculty, for instance, the derived matter of the eye and its surrounding fleshly support arises there together with them, the four elements and the matter they produce always sharing a single location, the elements themselves able to gather and sustain what they produce precisely because of this shared location, and for this reason properly called the "producer." The treatise further explains how this inseparability should actually be verified in any given cluster: wherever a particular element's own characteristic can genuinely be detected within a cluster, that element should be understood to be present there; wherever it cannot be detected, it should be understood to be absent — a rule directed, the lecture notes, against certain non-Mahāyāna schools which insisted that all four elements must always be present in every cluster of matter even when three of them cannot be detected at all, the treatise instead holding that presence or absence should be judged strictly by whether each element's own actual characteristic can be found, a cluster already arisen showing a detectable characteristic, one not yet arisen or already ceased showing none — this rule applying, the lecture stresses, only to matter's actual, momentary presence, not to the underlying elemental seeds, which remain latent everywhere regardless.

36. Section: Momentum, Sustaining, and Diminishment — And Why Matter Is Still "Made" by the Elements Even Though It Arises From Its Own Seed

The treatise completes its account of the five modes of arising. Arising through generative momentum names how the internal sense faculties' own power gives rise, in succession, to an entire external world capable of being perceived, and how past karma's own power gives rise to the particular quality of one's internal faculties — some beings born with especially sharp eyes, ears, or other senses, entirely as a result of prior karmic momentum; the treatise notes further that desire-realm heavens either enjoy their pleasures directly and immediately, as in the four heavens up through Tuṣita, or conjure pleasures at will, as in the heavens of manifest and controlled enjoyment, while the northern continent of Uttarakuru enjoys food and music that simply appears from its own trees the moment it is wished for — human beings alone, by contrast, inherit only the natural world itself, with no such pleasures given in advance, everything else having to be earned through labor. Arising through sustaining names how a favorable condition, like water nourishing a seed into a sprout, causes some cluster of matter to grow progressively stronger and more excellent; arising through diminishment names simply the reverse, some unfavorable condition instead causing a cluster of matter to weaken and decline. The treatise then turns to how such clusters actually form when put to use, observing that matter of one kind blends completely into an undifferentiated whole, like grain ground to powder and mixed with water, precisely because its constituent elements must function together as a single, useful unit for the sense faculties, unlike matter such as sesame, wheat, or beans, whose individual grains remain visibly distinct even heaped together. This raises a further question: since everything ultimately arises from its own seed, why does the treatise still speak of the four elements as the "makers" of derived matter? The answer lies in three further causes through which the elements themselves change, in turn changing whatever they produce: human effort, meaning either an individual's own or another's deliberate action — a blow from a stone or stick bruising the body, or the elements assembling themselves into shelters, furniture, and every other requisite of daily life, the lecture noting the vivid case of raindrops that become pearls when they fall into an oyster's mouth, blades when they fall into hell, and fire when they fall among hungry ghosts, each element responding differently depending on where it lands; karmic action, meaning that past karma's own accumulated force first gathers the elements at the very location a new form of matter is about to arise, then shapes the derived matter that follows; and superior meditative power, meaning that deep concentration itself first draws forth a different realm's own elements at the location of an ordinary faculty — a desire-realm meditator first generating form-realm elements around the eye before the form-realm's own divine eye can actually manifest there. The treatise adds a final list of five conditions capable of further transforming already-formed matter: the elements' own force, as when wind or frost spoils a ripening crop; human effort, as when careful cultivation prevents blight; the power of spells, as when a malicious incantation withers a tree or stunts a child's growth; supernatural power, capable of turning good matter bad or bad matter good; and karmic force, since a change of heart changes the karma that follows, and karma in turn changes the matter that karma produces.

37. Section: Not an Echo, Not a Reflection, Not Mere Grasping — Why the Intermediate Being Must Truly Exist

The treatise closes its account of arising with a discussion of the intermediate-state being that continues after death, asking by what cause and condition it arises at all, and answering that it arises from its own seed as cause and from the karma that generates rebirth as condition — the underlying continuity ultimately driven by beginningless self-love and beginningless habitual attachment to conceptual proliferation, together with the ripening seeds of past wholesome and unwholesome karma. Asked how one can even know an intermediate being exists, the treatise answers first that without some physical basis to depend upon, mind and its mental factors would have no coherent way of transferring from one place to another at all. The treatise then refutes three objections proposing that no such physical basis is actually needed. Against the claim that mind could transfer the way an echo travels through a valley without anyone carrying it there, the treatise replies that an echo is merely a confused, illusory sound, not something genuinely existing the way mind and its factors genuinely exist, and so cannot serve as a valid comparison. Against the claim that mind could transfer the way a reflection travels from mirror to mirror, the treatise replies that a reflection only ever appears while its original object still exists before the mirror — once the original person is gone, no reflection remains to travel anywhere, whereas after death the original body is indeed gone, so nothing remains that a "reflection" could possibly be based on. And against the claim that mind could simply reach out and grasp a future rebirth-site as an object of awareness, the way any object of attention can be reached without traveling there physically, the treatise replies that the intermediate being is not merely mental attention reaching toward a place — the whole point of an intermediate state is that something actually travels, a subtle body borne along by consciousness, not consciousness alone drifting bodilessly through space. Since none of these three tempting analogies withstands scrutiny, the treatise concludes, one should definitely accept that an intermediate-state being truly exists, this discussion completing the third of the ten angles, arising.

38. Section: Which Element Claims the Space First, and Why Sound Behaves Like a Sudden Flash of Light

The treatise opens the fourth angle, establishment, asking which element occupies a given location first, before any other matter can arise there, and answering that the four elements always take hold of the space first, with all further derived matter then occurring within the very same space they have claimed and sustained — the derived matter's own extent precisely matching whatever space the elements themselves occupy, sustained entirely by their power. The treatise further explains that the four elements manifest in an order from coarse to subtle: earth, being coarsest, together with its own resulting matter, serves as the foundational support for everything else; water, fire, and wind then flow, burn, and move only in dependence upon this same foundation, each subtler than the last, wind being the most subtle and least perceptible of all. The treatise then turns to several specific distinctions worth noting. Sound, unlike the elements themselves, does not persist continuously within a cluster of matter, vanishing again almost the instant it is produced — yet when it does arise, it arises suddenly and all at once, filling every location where it can be heard simultaneously rather than spreading gradually outward from its source, precisely like a flame's light, which does not creep gradually across a room but fills the space with brightness the instant it is kindled — the lecture noting that sound can be heard both at the very location of its source and at some distance from it, exactly as an echo, though clearly originating from a person's own voice, seems to come from the valley itself.

The treatise turns to wind, dividing it into constantly continuous and not constantly continuous forms. 40. Section: Space Itself Is Made of Light and Dark, and Shape Is Nothing But a Convenient Comparison

The treatise turns to space, explaining that it is actually known only through the derived matter of light and darkness, and that it too divides into constantly and not constantly continuous forms: wherever beings dwell in permanent darkness or permanent light — the heavens' own bodies of constant radiance, or hell's own unbroken gloom — space there counts as constantly continuous, while everywhere else, alternating between the two, counts as not constantly continuous; space filled with light further counts as pure, space confined to a crack or hollow as impure, this too always occurring in dependence on some cluster of matter, exactly as the empty interior of a car exists only because the car's own body defines it. The treatise then asks whether shape — long, short, round, square, and the rest — is genuinely real or merely provisional, answering firmly that it is provisional, for three reasons. First, because shape names nothing more than an accumulated arrangement of already-existing matter — there is no separately findable "longness" apart from the particular things called long, only ordinary matter gathered into some configuration and then labeled. Second, because shape is inherently relative — the very same table is "long" beside a shorter one and "short" beside a longer one, so that if length possessed some fixed, independent essence, calling one and the same thing both long and short would be an outright contradiction; the fact that no contradiction actually arises proves shape was never more than a comparison to begin with. 41. Section: Does Meditatively Produced Matter Include Smell and Taste? A Second Round of Scholarly Debate

The treatise states that matter subsumed under the mental sense-field and produced through meditative mastery possesses only the characteristics of visible color and the four elements' own tangible qualities, lacking smell and taste altogether, since the causes that would generate them are absent and no use for them exists — a being who has attained such mastery, dwelling beyond any need for solid food, having no occasion to manifest fragrance or flavor even though the nose and tongue remain present, merely ornamental rather than functional. The lecture notes a real disagreement among later commentators on this very point: some hold this limitation applies specifically to ordinary practitioners' weaker meditative power, or to the uncontaminated meditation of the two lesser vehicles, or to matter modeled on the form realm's own elements which never included smell and taste to begin with, while fully accomplished bodhisattvas and buddhas, they argue, manifest matter complete with all five qualities — citing the Flower Ornament Sūtra's account of a bodhisattva's nose perceiving the fragrance of formless-realm palaces, and the Lotus Sūtra's account of practitioners smelling the birth and passing of beings throughout the heavens, evidence, on this reading, that meditatively produced matter can indeed include genuine smell and taste when the practitioner's own attainment is great enough. The treatise then turns to two further illustrative cases: wind moving through open space carries no inherent smell of its own, only a fragrance temporarily borrowed from flowers or other objects it happens to pass over and carry along; and light shining apart from its source — sunlight or moonlight considered apart from the sun or moon itself — possesses only the fire-element together with its own color and tactile warmth, none of the other three elements, and no smell or taste, being found within it at all. The treatise closes this discussion by clarifying that meditatively produced matter depends specifically on meditative power itself, not directly on the four elements, even though it is still loosely called "produced by" whichever elements the practitioner's own visualized image resembles, since the meditating mind takes on the appearance of some familiar kind of matter without literally being generated from that matter's own physical substance — exactly as a vividly visualized lotus, however convincing its appearance, was never actually grown from mud and water the way a real lotus is.

42. Section: Visible-and-Obstructive, Invisible-and-Obstructive, Invisible-and-Unobstructive — Establishment's Close

The treatise closes the fourth angle, establishment, by asking how many kinds of matter within form aggregate exist as visible and obstructive, how many as invisible yet obstructive, and how many as invisible and entirely unobstructive. It answers that only what the eye itself perceives — visible color — is both visible and obstructive; everything else perceived by the remaining four sense faculties, sound, smell, taste, and touch, is obstructive yet invisible; and matter subsumed under the mental sense-field alone, imperceptible to any of the five senses and offering no resistance whatsoever, is both invisible and unobstructive. The lecture closes this angle with two illustrative stories about how thoroughly the notion of a findable, substantial self must be abandoned even when discussing something as significant as the Buddha's own passing. Asked by a non-Buddhist teacher where the Buddha would go after his final nirvāṇa, a disciple could not answer and brought the question to the Buddha directly; rather than answering outright, the Buddha asked in return whether the disciple could locate "the Buddha" anywhere within form, feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness, or indeed anywhere apart from these five aggregates altogether — the disciple, in each case, admitting he could not — at which point the Buddha observed that if no findable "Buddha" could be located even now, while plainly alive and speaking, the question of where such a "Buddha" might go after death simply dissolves, since there was never a fixed, substantial entity there to begin with. The second story concerns a dying disciple, gravely ill in a potter's household, who told the visiting Buddha he had no regrets except one — that he could no longer come and be near him — to which the Buddha replied that studying and living out the teaching is itself no different from being close to him, since, as the Flower Ornament Sūtra says, countless buddhas arise within the mind of any being who awakens even for a single moment: to see the teaching truly is already to see the Buddha. The lecture closes with a reminder of what "emptiness" actually means in this system — not that nothing at all remains, the way pouring water from a cup still leaves the cup's own air behind, but that the self and the phenomena so persistently imagined to fill out ordinary experience are simply not found there upon careful examination, precisely as this entire fourth angle's careful sorting of matter into real, provisional, visible, and invisible categories has been demonstrating from the very beginning.

43. Section: Continuity — Three Kinds of Flow, and Four Ways Ripened Matter Keeps Changing

The treatise opens the fifth angle, continuity, asking by how many kinds of flow form aggregate continues moment to moment — the very image recalling Confucius's own remark, standing beside a river, that what flows past never pauses day or night, since the water one sees an instant later is never truly the same water seen before, exactly as an unenlightened being mistakes their own continuously changing body and mind for one single, unchanging person. The treatise answers that there are three such flows: the flow of natural continuation, the flow of karmic ripening, and the flow of nourishment. The first of these, natural continuation, itself divides into four further kinds. Ripening-based continuation names how form aggregate persists for whatever span of time past karma has already determined — a life karmically destined for fifty years, or eighty, simply continuing under that karma's own sustaining power for exactly that long. Nourishment-based continuation names how good food, adequate rest, and freedom from affliction, or the cultivation of meditative absorption, actively strengthens body and mind, extending and improving the very continuity that karma has set in motion. Transformation-based continuation names how the four elements, changing moment to moment, still yield something recognizably continuous even as sickness, aging, or simply growing from childhood to adulthood visibly alters the body along the way, each new state resembling the one before closely enough that ordinary perception reads it as one and the same person — even though, the lecture notes, every cell is in fact being replaced from instant to instant, far too quickly and finely for any but the most refined perception to actually track. And nature-based continuation names simply those stretches where change is so gradual it goes entirely unnoticed, yesterday's appearance and today's seeming, for all practical purposes, identical.

The flow of nourishment divides into growth in extent and growth in vigor: growth in extent names the sheer increase in a body's own size and volume, nourished by food, sleep, celibate restraint, and meditative attainment; growth in vigor names, instead, an increase in strength, radiance, and capability without any corresponding growth in size, applying to mind and its mental factors as much as to the body — the lecture noting a survey reporting that reciting a buddha's name or a mantra continuously for roughly twenty thousand repetitions, sustained across two or three hours, measurably calms the practitioner's own brainwaves, meditation and recitation alike serving as genuine nourishment for body and mind in a way an ordinary vacation, full of traffic, noise, and other people's preferences, often fails to provide. With this, the treatise concludes, the fifth angle, continuity, has been fully explained.

45. Section: Five Functions Each for Earth, Water, Fire, and Wind — Then Five More for Whatever They Produce

The treatise opens the sixth angle, function, with a question already touched upon earlier in this same fascicle: how many distinct kinds of work do earth, water, fire, and wind each perform? The answer, briefly restated, is five apiece — earth able to strike and break, to build, to serve as support, to cause harm, and to nurture growth; water able to moisten, to bind, to irrigate, to cause harm, and to sustain life; fire able to illuminate, to ripen, to burn, to cause harm, and to sustain warmth; and wind able to set things moving, to accompany motion, to dry things out, to cause harm, and to sustain the breath and the body's own movement — each element, in other words, alternately life-giving and potentially destructive depending on the circumstances. The treatise then adds a further, distinct fivefold function belonging specifically to the elements' relationship with whatever derived matter they produce: a generative function, the elements always leading whenever their derived matter undergoes some change; a supportive function, the elements never actually parting from their derived matter's location, remaining its constant foundation even as it changes; an establishing function, the elements sharing every fortune and misfortune together with what they produce, since derived matter's own well-being rises and falls together with theirs; a sustaining function, the elements maintaining derived matter's own proper measure so that it does not diminish or waste away; and a growth function, the elements enabling derived matter to accumulate, develop, and expand over time — a body growing heavier, for instance, precisely as its own earth-element grows more pronounced within it.

46. Section: Why Only Sight and Sound Carry Moral Weight, and Whether Movement Is Anything Beyond Matter Itself

The treatise asks why color and sound, alone among the five sensory objects, can be wholesome or unwholesome, while smell, taste, and touch cannot, answering that this depends on three grades of the volitional mental factor: preparatory intention, which deliberates whether to act at all; determining intention, which settles on acting; and executing intention, which actually carries the action into effect through bodily or verbal expression — only the strongest, executing grade of this intention being capable of generating wholesome or unwholesome bodily and verbal expression, and such expression manifesting only through what the eye and ear perceive, namely visible gesture and audible speech, never through smell, taste, or touch, which is why moral quality attaches only to color and sound. The treatise then takes up a further question: is the movement one perceives in a cluster of matter identical with that cluster, or something distinct from it? It answers that movement is not distinct from the matter that moves, since no separately findable "movement" could ever be located apart from the matter itself undergoing change from one moment to the next — the appearance of continuous motion being simply how a rapid, unbroken succession of momentarily arising and ceasing clusters of matter presents itself to an observer, rather than evidence of any additional substance called "motion" layered on top of matter's own successive moments.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the restriction of moral quality to sight and sound as a practical, almost legal observation about how karma is actually recorded and communicated between beings — intention becomes visible and verifiable to others specifically through gesture and speech, which is exactly why ordination, testimony, and moral judgment in general rely on what can be seen and heard rather than smelled, tasted, or touched. He treats the conclusion that movement is nothing over and above matter's own rapid succession of momentary states as this treatise's own quiet extension of impermanence into an unexpected corner of ordinary experience — even something as seemingly continuous and self-evident as a moving hand turns out, on analysis, to be nothing more than an unbroken chain of arising and perishing instants. Having opened this question of movement's relationship to matter, he notes, the treatise turns next to why exactly this comparison was raised — a question of what genuine consequences would follow if movement and matter were, in fact, held to be different things.

47. Section: Momentary Cessation — Why Nothing That Arises Needs a Separate Cause to Perish

The treatise closes function, the sixth angle, by observing that whether the movement one perceives in a cluster of matter is said to arise, cease, both, or neither together with that matter, every option leads to the same conclusion: there is no separately findable "movement" apart from matter's own successive arising, only a rapid, unbroken succession of momentarily produced clusters that gives the false impression of a single thing continuously traveling from place to place. This directly opens the seventh angle, momentary cessation, with the treatise's own firm declaration that every form of matter, without exception, possesses the nature of ceasing moment by moment, argued through three considerations. First, this is simply what direct observation confirms: whatever arises perishes again almost the same instant, with nothing coming between its arising and its perishing, exactly as a single thought is gone again the moment it appears. Second, the very cause that generates something can never also be the cause of its ceasing, since arising and ceasing are opposite characteristics — one cannot coherently be produced by the very same power that brings about its own end, any more than lighting a fire could itself be the reason the fire eventually goes out. And third, once something has arisen, no separate cause can ever be found that keeps it in existence even for a moment longer, which is exactly why nothing lasts: existence was never sustained by anything to begin with, so cessation requires no further cause either — it happens entirely on its own, spontaneously, the instant arising itself is complete.

48. Section: Fire Doesn't Destroy the Firewood — Refuting Every Theory of What Causes Cessation

The treatise systematically refutes four further, increasingly refined attempts to locate some genuine cause of cessation. First, against the view that fire, water, and wind themselves cause things to perish, the treatise observes that fire and the firewood it burns arise and cease together — the very moment the wood is entirely gone, the flame goes out as well — so that fire cannot be firewood's cause of cessation, since a cause must exist prior to and independent of its effect; fire's true role is only to serve as the condition for the wood's transformation into ash, not the cause of the wood's disappearance into nothingness, since the ash itself is still a real, existing thing, not evidence that some destructive power reduced the wood to nothing. Second, against the view that the "mark of cessation" itself is the true cause — a subtle, non-physical, non-mental factor lying latent within a thing from the moment of its birth, waiting to trigger its eventual end — the treatise replies that this mark, however conceived, would either have to coexist with the thing's own arising, in which case the thing could never actually arise at all before this same mark cancelled it out, or exist separately from the thing, in which case it plainly cannot be found anywhere, contradicting ordinary direct perception, which sees only fire burning wood, never any accompanying "mark of cessation" burning anything. Third, against the view that cessation requires some auxiliary helper — fire, water, or wind merely assisting a thing's own inherent tendency to perish — the treatise points out that a flash of lightning, an oil lamp's flame, and an arising thought all cease perfectly well without any such helper whatsoever, disproving the claim that cessation universally requires external assistance. And fourth, against the view that arising and ceasing each possess their own distinct causal functions, differing case by case depending on what kind of thing is involved, the treatise notes that no such distinguishing function can actually ever be identified — one instance of cessation is exactly like any other, lightning ceasing no differently than a lamp's flame or a fleeting thought — proving that cessation has no distinct functional cause to speak of at all. Having eliminated every proposed cause of cessation individually and in combination, the treatise concludes that all conditioned phenomena cease entirely spontaneously, requiring no cause whatsoever — and adds one final, unifying reflection: since every conditioned phenomenon is ultimately mind's own product, and mind itself is universally acknowledged to perish moment by moment, matter too, being mind's product, must share this same momentary nature. With this, the treatise concludes, the seventh angle, momentary cessation, has been fully explained.

Master Cháng Bǎi singles out the fire-and-firewood argument as the most intuitively satisfying of the entire sequence: watching a fire burn down to ash, it feels obvious that the fire "destroyed" the wood, yet the treatise points out that fire and wood are never actually present at two separate moments where one could be said to act upon and eliminate the other — they exist together and vanish together, fire's real contribution being only to transform, never to annihilate, the ash left behind proving that something always remains rather than nothing at all. 49. Section: Solitary or Accompanied — Derived Matter Always Differs From the Elements That Produce It

The treatise takes up the eighth angle, asking whether derived matter — everything the senses actually perceive — possesses a different character from the elements that produce it, or the very same character. It answers firmly that derived matter differs, offering three proofs. First, different faculties perceive them: only the body faculty can directly sense the four elements' own tangible qualities of solidity, moisture, warmth, and movement, while the eye, ear, nose, and tongue perceive only derived matter, never the bare elements themselves. Second, derived matter can move from place to place while the elements cannot — fragrance genuinely travels when carried from flowers into nearby sesame plants, yet no one would say solidity or moisture itself has traveled anywhere. And third, derived matter visibly changes while the elements underlying it do not — clarified butter turns golden and its flavor changes entirely when cooked, yet the earth, water, fire, and wind composing it have not themselves altered in the least; it is always the derived product that transforms, never the elemental substance producing it. The treatise closes this angle by confirming that derived matter and the elements are indeed distinct, adding that anyone who insisted otherwise would be forced into the absurd position that the four elements themselves — earth, water, fire, and wind, plainly different from one another — must also be a single undifferentiated thing, which no one accepts.

50. Section: When Is an Object Truly "Present"? Distance for the Eye, Contact for the Tongue, and an Owl's Night Vision

The treatise opens the ninth angle, what is operated upon, first distinguishing which of the eleven categories of matter count as faculties and which as objects: the five pure sense faculties alone possess faculty-nature, while color, sound, smell, taste, touch, and matter subsumed under the mental sense-field — six categories in all — possess object-nature, the domain within which faculties and consciousness actually operate. The treatise then asks precisely what conditions must be met before such an object counts as genuinely "present" to its corresponding faculty. For the eye, color must neither touch the eye directly nor be shrouded in darkness, must be neither too minute nor too distant to register, and must be free of obstruction — only then is it truly present, present meaning visible, illuminated, unobstructed, and within the eye's own effective range; the treatise notes one exception, however, observing that a certain class of physical eyes can perceive color even in darkness, exactly as an owl's eyes are naturally equipped to see clearly at night. For the ear, sound likewise must not touch the ear directly, and must be neither too faint nor too distant, but unlike color it may be obstructed by some barrier and still be heard, and neither darkness nor light makes any difference to it at all — sound counting as present wherever it falls within hearing range, obstructed or not, lit or dark. For the nose, tongue, and body, by contrast, smell, taste, and touch must make direct contact with their respective faculty in order to register at all, presence for these three meaning nothing other than actual contact within reach. The treatise then contrasts these ordinary conditions with two more exalted forms of perception: the divine eye perceives visible matter regardless of obstruction, darkness, or distance, everything within its own effective range counting as present to it; and the wise eye of the noble ones takes every kind of matter whatsoever — visible or invisible, obstructive or unobstructive — as its own domain, since its function is to discern and analyze every category of phenomena rather than merely to perceive within some fixed physical range.

51. Section: What Damages a Sense Faculty — And a Modern Meditator Fleeing a Retreat on the Fourth Day

The treatise explains that the five sense faculties become damaged through two conditions — partial impairment or total destruction — and remain undamaged in the absence of both, then names four further causes through which the faculties specifically vary: external causes, meaning either helpful or harmful physical objects encountered in daily use, or the beneficial or harmful influence of other beings, a skilled or careless physician alike capable of strengthening or weakening the eyes; internal causes, meaning either the afflictions of craving and the rest arising from improper reflection, which damage the faculties, or the meditative attainments arising from proper reflection, which strengthen them; karmic causes, meaning that past karma's own ripening determines whether a being is born with attractive, well-functioning faculties or with impaired, unattractive ones, the lecture citing the legendary monk who, after ten lifetimes of monastic practice, allowed a single moment of pride to arise and consequently manifested a disfiguring, karmically caused growth on his leg; and causes arising from a faculty's own inherent nature, meaning simply the natural course of aging, as faculties that were vigorous and clear in youth inevitably decline toward old age like fruit finally ripe enough to fall at the first breath of wind. The treatise then turns to the mental faculty specifically, naming four analogous causes of its own impairment: obstruction by one of the five hindrances — desire, aversion, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, or doubt — clouding the mind; disturbance by a scattered, distracted mind, including, the lecture notes, disturbance by malevolent spirits, illustrated by the case of a nun who, after thirty years living alone in the mountains, became so tormented by a clinging spirit that she eventually placed a public notice begging for help, and by the venerable teacher Miàojìng's own three suggested remedies for such disturbances — naming the spirit aloud by its true nature to break its disguise, as one might address a fox-spirit directly and watch it flee once exposed; keeping a mirror in one's room, since such beings are said to fear being shown their own true reflection; and reciting the precepts, since a mind anchored in moral discipline resists such intrusion — the lecture adding a further, more personal account of a lay meditator in Vancouver who, during a seven-day retreat, found himself spontaneously reciting the Great Compassion Mantra and receiving visions of bodhisattvas from the very first sitting, only to have these same visions turn terrifying by the fourth day, appearing instead as menacing demons, until, unable to simply stop thinking about them as his teacher advised, he fled the retreat entirely rather than complete it; forcing the mind toward an unattained meditative attainment before it is ready; and forcing oneself through unfamiliar scholarly or practical work before genuine proficiency has developed — the lecture stressing throughout that body and mind are never actually separable, so that harm to one domain inevitably ripples into the other.

52. Section: Mind Alone Creates the Scene — Six Ways an Object Becomes "Present," and the Attention That Completes It

The treatise completes its survey of how sense objects differ, running through color, sound, smell, taste, and touch one by one according to three shared categories: their own particular character, their function, and their momentary quality. Color's own character includes blue, yellow, red, white, light, shadow, brightness, darkness, cloud, smoke, dust, and mist, together with every shape — long, short, square, round, coarse, fine, upright, crooked, raised, and sunken; its function includes expressive and non-expressive matter, and the precept-substance of wholesome vows, unwholesome vows, and neither; its momentary quality is simply being pleasing, displeasing, or neutral. Sound's own character divides into sound caused by a sentient being's own body, sound caused by insentient matter alone, and sound caused by the two combined — a bird's call, wind through leaves, and a struck bell or chanted verse respectively; its function is verbal expression; its momentary quality follows color's own threefold pattern. Smell's own character follows a plant's own parts — root, stem, bark, fruit, flower, and leaf — while smell, taste, and touch share the same lack of any moral function, being incapable of registering as wholesome or unwholesome the way color and sound can. The treatise then adds four further ways objects present themselves: by direction, the same object differing according to the eight compass points from which it is regarded; by time, past, future, and present each shading what is perceived; by whether what is grasped is a genuine, present object or merely a hazy memory of something no longer there, mental consciousness alone, without any of the five sense-consciousnesses accompanying it, registering only a dim, unclear recollection by comparison with a vivid, present encounter; and by whether a faculty takes in only a portion of some cluster or its entirety at once — attention itself determining how much of what lies before the eyes actually registers, so that two people can look at the very same scene and one sees everything while the other notices almost nothing, exactly as one may sit beside the same classmate for years without ever truly registering their face. The lecture draws out the deeper teaching hiding in this last point: since perceived objects never occur apart from consciousness and are themselves nothing but consciousness's own projection, what one actually experiences is always shaped by one's own mind rather than simply given from outside — which is why the great teacher Miàojìng, in his own final years, urged his students above all to keep meditating no matter how much scripture they had mastered, since knowing every teaching by heart offers no defense at all against the ordinary winds of gain and loss, praise and blame, pleasure and pain the moment they actually blow, just as he equally scolded students who meditated without ever studying, insisting that both wings — study and practice — must develop together, in balance, rather than one at the expense of the other. Finally, the treatise identifies attention itself as the last necessary condition completing perception: given an undamaged faculty and a genuinely present object, it is the arising of attention — the mental factor that actively rouses consciousness to engage its object — that finally allows the whole act of perception to take place. With this, the ninth angle, what is operated upon, stands fully explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Miàojìng's own deathbed reminder — that mastering every scripture is worthless without meditation, since the ordinary winds of gain and loss will overwhelm pure scholarship the instant they blow — as this entire ninth angle's own real payoff: all this careful analysis of how objects become "present" ultimately serves the discovery that experience itself is always the mind's own construction, never simply given from outside, a discovery that only becomes useful through sustained practice rather than mere intellectual assent. He treats the closing identification of attention as perception's final missing ingredient — beyond an undamaged faculty and a genuinely present object — as a fitting close to the ninth angle: even everything necessary being in place, nothing is actually perceived until the mind itself rouses to engage it. Having completed the ninth angle in full, he notes, the treatise turns to the tenth and final angle of this entire analysis of form — intermixing, asking how the coarse matter of the desire realm and the radiant matter of the form realm can possibly coexist within a single body once meditative attainment brings the two together.

53. Section: Like Sand Settled in Water — How Two Realms' Matter Share One Body, and Why Every Act Leaves Its Mark

The treatise closes its entire ten-angle analysis of form aggregate with the tenth and final angle, intermixing, posing a natural question: when a being in the desire realm attains meditative absorption and form-realm elements consequently arise within that same desire-realm body, how do the two coexist — do they occupy separate locations, or the very same one? The treatise answers with a simple, memorable image: they should be understood exactly as sand settled within water, not as occupying separate locations at all, since form-realm matter is so subtle that it presents no obstruction to desire-realm matter whatsoever, the two thoroughly intermingling within a single body precisely as a meditator who develops the divine eye finds this same subtle faculty coexisting seamlessly with the ordinary physical eye it accompanies. The lecture extends this same principle to the storehouse consciousness itself, citing the earlier teaching that wherever a being is born, in whichever of the three realms, that same storehouse consciousness always carries the latent seeds of all three realms together — a desire-realm being's own storehouse consciousness containing form- and formless-realm seeds as well, and so for every realm in turn — which is why the tradition says it is better to be reborn in hell as Devadatta, having already planted the seed of eventual buddhahood through genuine contact with the teaching, than to spend eighty thousand great eons enjoying the utterly rarefied peace of the formless realm's own summit, since even that summit's bliss inevitably ends in a fall, while a single seed of genuine aspiration, once planted, is never truly lost, continuing to ripen across however many future lives it takes. The lecture closes this fascicle's entire discussion of the aggregates' skillful understanding with a sober reminder drawn directly from this same teaching: every act of body, speech, and mind, the instant it occurs, is already stored as a seed within the storehouse consciousness, a single glance or a single word leaving its own permanent trace the moment it happens — seed generating manifestation, manifestation in turn generating further seed, the two processes operating simultaneously and continuously, so that everything a being now experiences is the ripening of traces left long ago, and everything a being now does is itself becoming a trace that later experience will one day ripen from in turn, a sobering thought the lecture offers as this entire fascicle's own closing exhortation to careful, mindful conduct in body, speech, and mind alike.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sand-in-water image as an unusually elegant close to this entire, extraordinarily long analysis of matter: two things can share a single space without displacing one another whenever one is subtle enough, a principle this fascicle has now illustrated at every scale, from interpenetrating devas' bodies down to a single meditator's own two eyes.
