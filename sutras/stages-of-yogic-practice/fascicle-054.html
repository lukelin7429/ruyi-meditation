Fascicle 54 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十四 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Continues

Where Consciousness Dwells

This fascicle completes consciousness's own fivefold analysis — where it dwells across the three realms, how it becomes defiled, what it depends on, the four stations where it takes root, and its countless changing aspects — bringing the treatise's first great pass through the five aggregates to its close, before opening a further, even more detailed tenfold examination of form and mind together.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 54 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Twenty Angles on Five Aggregates — And Consciousness's Own Fivefold Analysis Begins With Where It Dwells

Fascicle 54 opens by situating itself within the treatise's own vast architecture for examining the aggregates: skillful understanding of the aggregates unfolds across three major sections spanning twenty distinct angles in total. The first section, already substantially covered in fascicle 53, generally distinguishes six aspects of each aggregate — essential nature, meaning, distinctions, sequence, subsumption, and support — with only consciousness's own distinctions, together with sequence, subsumption, and support for all five aggregates, remaining to be covered here. The second section, occupying the remainder of this fascicle, further clarifies form and mind through a more detailed tenfold analysis: their kind, their subtlest constituents, their arising, their establishment, their continuity, their function, their momentary cessation, their solitary or accompanied nature, what they operate upon, and their intermixing. The third section, reserved for fascicles 55 and 56, decides four further matters: how the aggregates relate to the noble truths, their various stages, their further distinctions, and their overall establishment. The treatise explains that this entire twenty-angle examination serves a single purpose: dismantling the ordinary being's persistent clinging to this body and mind as a self or as belonging to a self, thereby revealing the truth of selflessness ever more thoroughly. Having already completed essential nature, meaning, and the distinctions of form, feeling, perception, and formations in fascicle 53, the treatise now turns to consciousness's own distinctions, asking what they are, then explaining they are, in brief, five: consciousness's dwelling place, its defilement, its support, the stations where it takes root, and its changing aspects. It opens with the first: where consciousness actually dwells, distinguishing three kinds of beings by realm. Those habituated to desire, in the desire realm, have consciousness grasping external sense-objects, dwelling in form. The pure gods of the form realm have consciousness grasping their own internal name-and-form together, dwelling in both at once — their identity residing in their own luminous inner body together with the mental factors accompanying deep meditative absorption. And the formless realm's own consciousness grasps only internal name, dwelling in name alone, having transcended physical embodiment entirely to abide purely within its own inner mental states.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens this fascicle by mapping out its own place within a genuinely vast undertaking: twenty total angles on the five aggregates, six already substantially covered, ten more to come across the rest of this fascicle, and four final angles reserved for two fascicles still ahead — all in service of a single goal, dismantling the illusion of a solid self clinging to this very body and mind. He treats the threefold classification of where consciousness dwells as mapping precisely onto the three realms' own defining character: desire-realm beings restlessly seeking pleasure through external objects, form-realm gods turning inward toward their own luminous embodiment and meditative absorption, and formless-realm beings abiding purely within mental states alone, having left physical form behind entirely. Having established where consciousness dwells across the three realms, he notes, the treatise turns to its second angle: how consciousness actually becomes defiled, distinguishing affliction that arises through ordinary sensory engagement in this very life from the deeper defilement that drives rebirth into a future life.

2. Section: Two Doorways of Defilement — And the Fire That Burns the Same Whether Fed by Straw or Dung

The treatise turns to consciousness's second distinction: defilement. It asks how consciousness becomes defiled, then explains that ordinary, unlearned beings are defiled through two distinct doorways. The first doorway operates within the present life, through the very engagement with sensory objects: craving arises when consciousness moves toward pleasant feeling, aversion arises when it moves toward painful feeling, and delusion arises when it settles into neutral feeling — this ordinary sensory engagement itself becoming the doorway through which affliction first defiles consciousness. The second doorway operates toward a future life, through the very process of aging and the rest: karma accumulated through this same sensory engagement ripens into a future rebirth, and that rebirth's own aging, sickness, and death constitute a further, deeper defilement in their own right, since the ripened embodiment itself is already an afflicted condition, perpetuating the entire cycle onward without end unless the Buddhist path intervenes. The treatise then turns to its third distinction: what consciousness depends upon. It explains that the six active consciousnesses each depend on their own corresponding sense-faculty in order to actually manifest — eye-consciousness depending on the eye faculty in order to engage visible form, and so on through mind-consciousness depending on the mental faculty — illustrated through the analogy of ordinary fire: fire fed by rice husks, by cattle dung, by firewood, or by wood chips is called husk-fire, dung-fire, wood-fire, or chip-fire respectively, yet the underlying nature of fire itself remains exactly the same regardless of its fuel; in just the same way, consciousness's own basic nature of discernment remains constant throughout, while its particular name — eye-consciousness, ear-consciousness, and so on through mind-consciousness — simply follows whichever sense-faculty happens to support it at the time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two doorways of defilement as capturing an entire lifetime's worth of affliction in miniature: ordinary craving, aversion, and delusion generated through everyday sensory engagement create the karma that ripens into a future rebirth, whose own aging, sickness, and death then constitute a still deeper layer of defilement — the whole cycle repeating indefinitely without the intervention of genuine practice. He treats the fire-and-fuel analogy as this treatise's own most elegant illustration of a point developed at great length elsewhere: consciousness's own underlying nature of discernment never changes, only the label attached to it shifts according to whichever sense-faculty happens to support it at a given moment, exactly as fire remains fire regardless of what fuel happens to feed it. Having completed consciousness's second and third distinctions, he notes, the treatise turns to its fourth and most doctrinally significant distinction: the four stations where consciousness takes root, a teaching whose scholarly interpretation the lecture traces through centuries of debate between the Sarvāstivādin and Mahāyāna traditions.

3. Section: The Four Stations Where Consciousness Takes Root — A Teaching Aimed Directly at the Heretic's Own Sense of Self

The treatise turns to consciousness's fourth distinction: the stations where it takes root. The lecture supplements this teaching with an extended account of its own historical purpose and interpretation. Regarding why the Buddha taught this doctrine at all: certain non-Buddhist schools held consciousness itself to be the true self, with the four remaining aggregates — form, feeling, perception, and formations — merely serving as this self's own dwelling place; the Buddha, refuting this view directly, taught instead that form, feeling, perception, and formations are simply where consciousness itself takes root, never that consciousness constitutes any self dwelling within them. Regarding what actually constitutes a "station" in this sense: only the contaminated, defiled aggregates function this way, since only they can give rise to craving and thereby provide consciousness a place to settle and take root; the uncontaminated aggregates, incapable of generating this same craving, provide no such foothold, and consciousness itself is never counted as its own station, since the entire teaching exists precisely to correct the mistaken notion that consciousness could serve as its own independent, self-sufficient basis. The lecture then traces a substantial scholarly disagreement between traditions: the Sarvāstivādin school held that only one's own, present-life aggregates properly constitute a station for one's own consciousness, strictly confined to the very same realm, level, and body, since consciousness and its station must share a single moment together; the Mahāyāna tradition held a considerably broader view, that both dependence and objective reference each constitute a station, so that consciousness taking any object whatsoever as its focus — whether belonging to oneself or another, whether form or formless, and reaching across all three time-periods and all three realms alike — already counts as a station, the sole exception being the uncontaminated truths of cessation and the path, which alone generate no craving capable of anchoring consciousness in the first place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own original teaching purpose here as directly continuing this entire treatise's own central project: just as certain non-Buddhist schools mistook consciousness itself for a real, abiding self with the other four aggregates as its mere residence, the Buddha's teaching of the four stations reframes the relationship entirely — consciousness is not a self inhabiting these aggregates, but simply what takes root within them under the right conditions, itself equally without any substantial, independent existence. He treats the Sarvāstivādin-Mahāyāna dispute over how broadly "station" should be defined as reflecting a deeper disagreement about consciousness's own scope: the stricter view confines it tightly to one's own present-life aggregates alone, while the more expansive Mahāyāna view recognizes that consciousness can take root in reference to virtually any object across all three times and realms, so long as that object is capable of generating craving — with only the truths of cessation and the path standing permanently outside consciousness's reach in this respect, since wisdom directed toward liberation itself generates no craving for consciousness to cling to. Having laid out this rich historical background, he notes, the treatise turns to identifying the four stations themselves by name — form, feeling, perception, and formations — before explaining each individually.

4. Section: The Four Stations Named — Form, Feeling, Perception, and Formations — Through Three Modes of Attachment Each

The treatise names the four stations directly: consciousness follows form and dwells there, taking form as its object — and the scripture explains this at length, extending in exactly the same way through feeling, perception, and formations. The lecture cites the Compendium of Manifest Teaching's own threefold analysis of how consciousness relates to each of these four stations: consciousness adheres to form, since form serves as what consciousness clings to and appropriates as its own basis; consciousness attends to form, since consciousness takes form as the very object of its own cognition; and consciousness relies on form, since the coarse, burdensome weight of affliction still binds consciousness to form as a necessary support — and precisely the same threefold relationship of adherence, attention, and reliance applies without exception to feeling, perception, and formations in turn. The lecture also cites Venerable Master Yìn Shùn's own summary: the entire teaching of the five aggregates derives from this teaching of the four stations, since sentient beings cling to consciousness's own attachment across bodily craving, emotional grasping, cognitive fixation, and volitional pursuit alike, and it is precisely this fourfold clinging that binds them to saṃsāra's own endless cycle. The treatise then quotes the Buddha's own celebrated declaration: I never say this consciousness goes to the east, or to any of the eight cardinal or intercardinal directions — rather, I say only that within this very life, it becomes forever free of desire and of feeling's own shadow, reaching quiescence, tranquility, coolness, and purity — since an arhat's consciousness, upon entering final nirvāṇa, no longer clings to form, feeling, perception, or formations at all, and asking which direction it has gone is as meaningless as asking which direction an extinguished fire has traveled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the threefold relationship of adherence, attention, and reliance as giving real texture to what might otherwise seem an abstract teaching: consciousness doesn't merely "have" form, feeling, perception, and formations as objects — it clings to them as its own basis, actively attends to them as what it cognizes, and remains bound to them by the sheer weight of unresolved affliction, three genuinely distinct ways the same underlying attachment expresses itself. He treats the Buddha's own "does the fire go anywhere?" teaching as this entire passage's most vivid image: an arhat's consciousness at parinirvāṇa doesn't travel to some far-off location, it simply ceases to cling to anything at all, exactly as an extinguished flame has nowhere to "go" once its fuel is exhausted. Having established the four stations by name and illustrated their final cessation, the treatise turns to unpacking five further technical terms found in this same scriptural passage — the bases of grasping, the object, the object of craving's own pursuit, the object bound by affliction's own fetters, and the establishing factor — each clarifying a different facet of exactly how consciousness becomes entangled with these four stations in the first place.

5. Section: Five Technical Terms Unpacked — From the Bases of Grasping to the Four Bonds That Fetter Every Being

The treatise unpacks five terms drawn from this same scriptural passage on the four stations. The bases of grasping name the craving found within the aggregates themselves, together with whatever aggregates that same craving has come to permeate — craving being what refuses to relinquish either the future rebirth it seeks or the present existence it clings to, and it is precisely this craving, together with the aggregates it infuses, that constitutes consciousness's own basis and object of grasping. The object names this same pair viewed from a slightly different angle: since the aggregates permeated by craving genuinely serve as something consciousness takes as its focus, and since craving itself, as a mental factor, genuinely operates in concert with consciousness, both qualify as consciousness's own object in their own distinct ways. The object of craving's own pursuit names how craving itself, as the decisive condition, drives the mind to actively pursue and cling to whatever it becomes entangled with — the treatise identifying four specific kinds of such grasping: craving for sensory pleasure, clinging to wrong views, clinging to misguided moral or ritual observances, and clinging to the false notion of self embedded in ordinary language itself. The object bound by affliction's own fetters names how four corresponding bonds — the bond of craving, of aversion, of clinging to misguided observances, and of clinging to a falsely absolutized view — actually generate karma, householders typically driven by the bonds of craving and aversion in pursuit of pleasant objects and in hostility toward other beings, while renunciants remain more susceptible to the bonds of misguided observance and false absolutization, mistakenly seeking heavenly rebirth through improper practice or mistakenly denigrating nirvāṇa itself through wrong view. And the establishing factor names how the latent seeds of both craving and these four bonds continue to follow a being's own life-stream, ready to manifest again whenever the right conditions converge, thereby establishing the entire ongoing cycle of the five aggregates across life after life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five terms as tracing an entire causal chain in miniature: craving takes hold of the aggregates as its basis, that same craving-permeated pair becomes consciousness's own object, craving itself then drives active pursuit through four specific channels, that pursuit hardens into four binding fetters that actually generate karma, and finally the latent seeds of it all persist across lifetimes, re-establishing this same cycle again and again. He treats the fourfold grasping — sensory craving, wrong views, misguided observance, and false self-view — alongside the fourfold bonds of craving, aversion, misguided observance, and false absolutization as together offering a genuinely comprehensive diagnostic of exactly how attachment operates differently in householders versus renunciants, each susceptible to a different pairing of these same four fundamental afflictions. Having unpacked these five technical terms in the scripture's own words, he notes, the treatise turns to explaining the deeper meaning behind two of its central terms — the station itself and its sustaining condition — beginning with what it actually means for an ordinary being's consciousness to "dwell" in saṃsāra at all.

6. Section: Two Outer Limits of the Station — Coming and Going Versus Simply Dying and Being Reborn

The treatise closes its account of the station's sustaining condition by confirming that everything already explained — the bases of grasping, the object, the object of craving's pursuit, the object bound by the four bonds, and the establishing factor — together constitutes this same sustaining condition in full. It then turns to two further outer limits: the limit of the station itself, and the limit of its sustaining condition. Regarding the first: consciousness belonging to a being with form — whether in the desire realm or the form realm — genuinely comes and goes, since such beings pass through an intermediate state connecting one life to the next, and are not reborn in any fixed, predetermined location; whereas consciousness belonging to a formless-realm being simply dies and is reborn directly, without any intervening state at all, since such existence is purely mental, arising again in the very place it left. Both these two kinds of station continue, without exception, until the very exhaustion of the being's own lifespan — and it is precisely this same craving and these same four bonds that cause both kinds of station to continually grow, strengthen, and expand throughout that entire span, right up until liberation is finally achieved. The treatise concludes: this, precisely, constitutes both the outer limit of the station itself and the outer limit of its sustaining condition — an ordinary being's own consciousness cycling without genuine end throughout the three realms, exactly as the scriptures describe the ocean of suffering as boundless, until the noble path finally brings it to its true limit.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between "coming and going" and "simply dying and being reborn" as tracking a genuinely important structural difference between embodied and purely mental existence: only a being with form requires an intermediate transitional state to bridge one life and the next, since a formless being, lacking any body to leave behind or travel toward, simply ceases in one place and immediately arises again wherever its own karma determines. He notes the treatise's own closing image — an ordinary being's consciousness cycling through the three realms without genuine end until liberation, precisely mirroring the scriptural teaching of a boundless ocean of suffering — sets up its next task: refuting the mistaken non-Buddhist doctrines that arise from misunderstanding this same cycling process as evidence of a real, abiding self traveling from life to life.

7. Section: Refuting the Real Self — Three Fatal Flaws in the Heretic's Doctrine, and the Store Clerk Who Was Never Truly Good or Bad

The treatise addresses an alternative account some non-Buddhist teachers propose: since the Buddha's own teaching describes only consciousness cycling through the three realms, never any self doing the cycling, those who instead posit an eternal, unchanging self merely traveling from one body to the next like moving between rented rooms are, the treatise insists, only ever offering an alternative in name, never in genuine substance — since no such self can actually be found anywhere at all, positing one changes nothing about reality itself, however different the words used to describe it. The treatise identifies two resulting faults in this rival doctrine: first, when a genuine challenger presses the question directly, such teachers simply have no coherent answer to give, precisely because the self they posit was never actually there to find in the first place; and second, even a sufficiently sharp-witted adherent of this same doctrine, upon later reflecting honestly and searching for this self themselves, becomes quietly troubled, privately recognizing their own view as foolish, even if pride keeps them from openly admitting defeat. The lecture illustrates the search for a fixed, unchanging self with the vivid example of a store clerk: one customer, served with a warm smile and an easy refund, declares him a genuinely good person; but the very next customer, arriving just after his employer scolded him, meets only a sour, unaccommodating mood and declares him genuinely bad — yet there was never a single, fixed "good clerk" or "bad clerk" to be found at all, only ever a shifting response arising from whatever conditions happened to converge at that particular moment, exactly as the treatise insists: search through form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness alike, and no permanent, self-ruling self is ever actually found anywhere, only this same ceaseless, conditioned change.

He notes the treatise's own two-part diagnosis of the self-doctrine's failure — unable to answer a direct challenge, and quietly self-doubting even in its own adherents' private reflection — describes a view that fails not through external refutation alone, but through its own inherent hollowness becoming apparent to anyone, including its own proponents, who actually goes looking for what it claims to describe. Having thoroughly refuted the alternative doctrine of a real self, he notes, the treatise turns to its final task regarding the four stations: how their own underlying sustaining condition can actually be brought to complete and final cessation.

8. Section: Bringing the Sustaining Condition to Rest — Different Afflictions for Householder and Renunciant Alike

The treatise turns to how the station's own sustaining condition may be brought to cessation. It explains that genuinely wise practitioners — śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas alike — are capable of permanently severing both the craving attachment toward form, feeling, perception, and formations, and the four karma-generating bonds that belong to affliction's own causal side; severing both these entanglements together is what actually brings the station's sustaining condition to rest. The treatise then explains why these particular afflictions arise so differently for householders and renunciants. Householders generate karma primarily through the bonds of craving and aversion: craving drives the pursuit of sensory pleasure, often through improper or even harmful means, while aversion drives harm toward other beings whenever personal interest is threatened. Renunciants, including non-Buddhist ascetics, remain more susceptible instead to the bonds of misguided observance and false absolutization: misguided observance parallels craving in seeking a desirable result, except the desired result here is heavenly rebirth pursued through improper practices such as imitating animal behavior in the mistaken belief this secures a favorable rebirth; and false absolutization parallels aversion in generating harm, except the harm here is directed at truth itself — mistaking deep meditative absorption for genuine liberation, or seizing upon speculative views about self and world as eternal, non-eternal, bounded, or unbounded, and thereby actively slandering nirvāṇa's own genuine reality through this same confusion. The lecture stresses that a renunciant's own imbalanced cultivation — clinging to precepts or concentration without genuinely completing the path through wisdom — is precisely what leaves this second, subtler pair of afflictions unaddressed, preventing the station's sustaining condition from ever actually coming to rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this careful pairing of householder and renunciant afflictions as a genuinely useful diagnostic for practitioners of any background: craving and aversion are the more obvious afflictions of ordinary worldly life, while misguided observance and false absolutization are the subtler, more insidious afflictions specific to a life already devoted to spiritual practice, where mistaking depth of concentration for depth of wisdom, or mistaking a cherished view for settled truth, can quietly derail even sincere renunciation. He notes the treatise's own implicit warning here — that imbalanced cultivation of precepts and concentration without genuine wisdom leaves exactly this second, more dangerous pair of afflictions fully intact — echoes a caution found throughout this entire treatise's own account of the path: discipline and concentration alone, however sincerely practiced, can never substitute for the wisdom that alone actually severs affliction at its root. Having explained the underlying cause of the station's own sustaining condition for both householder and renunciant, he notes, the treatise turns next to the actual mechanism by which affliction's own root is severed, distinguished according to its own manifest, latent, and habitual dimensions.

9. Section: The Four Bonds Are Purely Mental — And the Sustaining Condition Finally Reaches Its Own Complete Rest

The treatise clarifies that all four karma-generating bonds belong exclusively to the mental faculty, since they arise entirely through mental consciousness's own discriminating activity — the lecture explaining, via the Compendium of Determinations, that these four bonds obstruct a practitioner's own capacity for concentration precisely by scattering the mind: craving for wealth and possessions scatters the mind, improper conduct amid dispute and conflict scatters the mind, painful austerities pursued through misguided observances scatter the mind, and speculation about reality unguided by proper reasoning scatters the mind — all four operating through mental consciousness alone. The treatise then explains that sustained cultivation, employing an ever more superior antidote, eventually permanently severs both remaining latent seeds — craving's own seed and the four bonds' own seed together — beyond merely suppressing their present manifestation. Once both are genuinely, permanently severed, the objects these afflictions once clung to — form, feeling, perception, and formations — no longer continue to arise as objects of attachment at all, this being ultimate freedom from affliction's own bondage; and because these objects no longer continue, the latent, seed-level affliction that once followed consciousness reaches its own final cessation, so that consciousness itself no longer takes up residence within any of the four stations whatsoever, having become permanently purified through this same antidote. With this, the treatise concludes, the cessation of the station's own sustaining condition stands fully explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the identification of all four bonds as purely mental as this passage's own quietly important reminder that the sixth consciousness alone is where genuine transformation must occur — however the affliction first appeared to arise through bodily craving or ritual observance, its actual root always traces back to mental discrimination, meaning practice must ultimately target the mind itself rather than merely its outward expressions. Having thoroughly explained how the sustaining condition itself reaches complete cessation, he notes, the treatise turns to the station's own cessation directly — an extensive, twelve-angle analysis of what it actually means for consciousness to finally stop taking up residence in form, feeling, perception, and formations at all.

10. Section: The Station Itself Comes to Rest — Twelve Angles Beginning With the Three Gates of Liberation

The treatise turns to the station's own cessation, explaining first why it earns this name: because the very cause capable of generating a future rebirth has itself ceased, consciousness no longer takes hold of, nor brings to completion, any future embodiment at all, and saṃsāra's own continuity is therefore decisively ended — this is called the cessation of the station. The treatise then distinguishes twelve further angles describing this same cessation. The first, non-abiding, names the purified consciousness achieved through this very antidote. The second, cessation of growth, names how this same causal severance prevents consciousness from ever again growing entangled with its former objects. The third, purposelessness, names how skillful cultivation of the gate of emptiness — realizing the selflessness of both person and phenomena — brings purified consciousness to a state where it no longer generates any further karmic activity at all; the lecture notes that a śrāvaka's own contemplation of emptiness focuses on personal selflessness alone, seeking liberation from one's own affliction, while a bodhisattva's contemplation extends to the selflessness of phenomena themselves, since a bodhisattva seeks neither to remain bound to saṃsāra nor to abide in nirvāṇa exclusively, but to abide in reality's own freedom from all verbal designation for the sake of others. The fourth, contentment, names how skillful cultivation of the gate of wishlessness brings purified consciousness to no longer crave any further result within the three realms whatsoever — a śrāvaka contemplating the impermanence and suffering of the aggregates to relinquish all further seeking, a bodhisattva instead relinquishing entanglement with verbal designation itself. The fifth, abiding, names how skillful cultivation of the gate of signlessness brings purified consciousness to rest entirely free of any grasped mark, a śrāvaka contemplating the quiescent, markless nature of the aggregates directly, a bodhisattva extending this same freedom to every mark generated by conceptual proliferation. And the sixth, ultimate liberation, names the culmination of all the preceding five together — the complete, settled result of freedom from growth, purposeless action, contentment, and markless abiding all achieved at once.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between a śrāvaka's and a bodhisattva's own approach to each of the three gates of liberation as this passage's own quiet but consistent theme: both traditions cultivate emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness, yet the śrāvaka's own scope remains centered on personal liberation from affliction, while the bodhisattva's scope extends outward to the selflessness of all phenomena and freedom from conceptual proliferation itself, since a bodhisattva's own goal is never simply personal escape from saṃsāra but the capacity to remain freely available to all beings without being bound by either saṃsāra or nirvāṇa exclusively. Having named six of the twelve angles describing the station's own cessation, he notes, the treatise turns next to elaborating "ultimate liberation" in far greater depth, illustrated through some of this literature's most striking accounts of dying without fear.

11. Section: Dying Without Fear — The Arhat Bitten by a Poisonous Snake, and Xuánzàng's Own Final, Lucid Days

The treatise elaborates ultimate liberation's own further characteristic: because such a practitioner clings to no self and nothing belonging to self within any conditioned phenomenon whatsoever, the eventual dissolution of form and the rest at the natural end of this present life brings no fear at all — this very fearlessness itself revealing that one's own being has already achieved the genuine purity belonging to nirvāṇa with residue remaining. The lecture illustrates this fearlessness with a celebrated account: an arhat, bitten by a poisonous snake, remained entirely composed, his complexion undisturbed, calmly instructing those nearby to carry him out to open ground before his body finally came apart; when Śāriputra, visiting him, asked how he could remain so utterly at ease facing death, the arhat explained simply that he had never regarded this body as himself or as belonging to himself, and so felt nothing to fear in its dissolution — precisely the fearlessness ordinary beings, still clinging to self and to what is theirs, could never achieve without the same depth of concentration or wisdom. The lecture extends this same theme to Master Xuánzàng's own final days: injured and aware his own life was nearing its end, he lay down and stopped eating, privately troubled by whether some error in his own translation work had caused his affliction — until a deity revealed that this present suffering was simply karmic debt from a past life as a king who had harmed others, now ripening all at once precisely because his own accumulated merit had otherwise reached completion, after which he would be reborn in Tuṣita heaven; reassured, he calmly stopped eating and remained lying on his right side, his students anxious yet finding his breath still present when they checked, Xuánzàng himself explaining that he abstained from food specifically to keep his own mind lucid as death approached, so that he might depart with full awareness and self-command.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the snakebitten arhat's own simple explanation — "I never regarded this body as myself" — as the single most concentrated expression of everything this entire section on the four stations has been building toward: fearlessness before death is not achieved through denial or suppression, but through the genuine, lived realization that there was never a self here to lose in the first place. He treats Xuánzàng's own final days as a moving, more relatable counterpart to the arhat's story — even one of history's greatest translators still wondered, in his own final illness, whether he had somehow erred, and it was precisely the deity's own reassurance about karmic ripening, not any denial of suffering itself, that allowed him to face his own death with the same lucid self-command, his final choice to stop eating a deliberate act of maintaining clarity rather than mere passive decline. Having illustrated ultimate liberation through both an ancient arhat's composure and a beloved historical figure's own more human struggle, he notes, the treatise turns to a further, quieter illustration: how the same fearlessness can be cultivated even by an ordinary person facing an entirely different kind of death.

12. Section: Six Final Angles — Freedom From Desire, From Feeling's Shadow, and All the Way to Utter Purity

The treatise completes the twelve-part analysis of the station's own cessation with six further angles. The seventh, permanent freedom from desire, names how such a practitioner no longer seeks either continued life or death itself, both equally beyond wishing for. The eighth, permanent freedom from shadow, names how the arhat, having transcended all feeling altogether, no longer casts what the lecture calls consciousness's own tree-shadow — pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling alike being likened to the shadow a tree casts once sunlight strikes it, ordinary beings driven to create karma by exactly this play of feeling, while the arhat, no longer subject to any of the three feelings, dwells at all times in the most complete equanimity toward whatever the six sense faculties encounter. The ninth, quiescence, names how every contaminated, afflicted consciousness has been utterly exhausted within present form, feeling, perception, and formations. The tenth, tranquility, names the uncontaminated consciousness belonging in sequence to the learners still progressing from the first to the third fruit, who have severed only some portion of the three realms' afflictions. The eleventh, coolness, names the accomplished liberation belonging to the arhat beyond further learning, who has already secured freedom from the three realms' craving and views and dwells within nirvāṇa with residue remaining. And the twelfth, purity, names the arhat's entry into nirvāṇa without residue remaining, at which point form, feeling, perception, and formations are permanently extinguished once and for all — the treatise's own extensive summary drawing every one of these twelve angles together into a single description of what it truly means for the station to come to rest.

Because the uncontaminated consciousness belonging, in sequence, to the learner's own liberation still remains, this is called tranquility. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws special attention to the eighth angle's own tree-shadow image: feeling itself, he notes, is not the problem — it is the compulsive craving and aversion feeling provokes in an unliberated mind that generates karma, so that the arhat's freedom lies not in becoming numb to feeling but in no longer being moved by it either way, dwelling instead in what the text calls the most complete equanimity. He also notes the treatise's own careful distinction, in its closing summary, between coolness (associated with nirvāṇa with residue remaining, still embodied) and purity (associated with nirvāṇa without residue remaining, the final passing), a distinction that becomes important immediately afterward when the treatise turns to explaining why, despite everything just said about the four stations, consciousness itself was never counted as a fifth station alongside form, feeling, perception, and formations — since, the treatise explains, consciousness's own essential nature is never itself defiled the way these four continually are, and no one, the lecture notes, ever falls in love with their own bare awareness the way they fall in love with a beautiful appearance, a pleasant feeling, or a cherished idea, precisely because consciousness is simply too subtle to become an object of attachment in its own right — a point Master Cháng Bǎi likens to the classical commentarial simile of a king's own throne: just as one speaks of "the king's road" or "the king's throne" without the king himself being the road or the throne, so too consciousness is what dwells within the four stations without ever being a station unto itself. With this, the account of the fourth of consciousness's five distinctions — by station — reaches its full and complete conclusion, and the treatise turns at last to the fifth and final distinction: consciousness's own changing aspects.

13. Section: Consciousness's Fifth and Final Distinction — Twenty Kinds of Mind Through Craving, Aversion, and Their Absence

The treatise opens the fifth and final of consciousness's five distinctions, its changing aspects, explaining that consciousness's own character shifts constantly according to what mental factors currently accompany it: a mind presently accompanied by craving is a craving mind, a mind from which craving has been overcome is a mind free of craving, a mind presently accompanied by aversion is an aversive mind, a mind from which aversion has been overcome is a mind free of aversion, and so on, the scriptures extending this same pairing all the way up to the unliberated mind and the utterly liberated mind — this single approach alone yielding what the treatise calls one entire way of distinguishing consciousness's changing aspects. The lecture notes that an earlier discussion, within the Stage of the Hearer's Vehicle, had already drawn out ten such paired afflictions and their ten corresponding antidotes from this same scheme, yielding twenty specific kinds of mind in total, each a snapshot of consciousness colored by whichever affliction or antidote currently accompanies it.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this first, single-pair approach to consciousness's changing aspects as deliberately the simplest of what turns out to be three separate schemes for classifying the same underlying phenomenon — mind classified here purely by what mental factor currently accompanies it, without yet any reference to which of the three realms a being belongs to or which level of the path they have reached; those two further refinements, he notes, follow immediately next.

14. Section: Mind Classified by Realm — Four Kinds in the Desire Realm, Three Each Above, and Two Beyond All Three

The treatise turns to a second approach, classifying consciousness's changing aspects by realm. Beings in the desire realm possess four kinds of mind: a wholesome mind, arising when the first six consciousnesses correspond to non-craving, non-aversion, non-delusion, and the other wholesome mental factors, itself divided into innate wholesomeness and wholesomeness cultivated through effort; an unwholesome mind, arising when the first six consciousnesses correspond to craving, aversion, delusion, and the rest; an obscured-but-neutral mind, belonging solely to the mental faculty — the seventh consciousness's innate self-view and extreme-view, or the sixth consciousness's own innate versions of the same, counting as obscured because such views obstruct the noble path even though they are neither strongly wholesome nor unwholesome in themselves; and an unobscured-neutral mind, itself of four kinds — a mind that simply ripens from past karma, a mind occupied with ordinary deportment such as walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, a mind occupied with skilled craft such as painting or music, and a mind capable of miraculous transformation, this last belonging only to innately gifted beings such as devas, nāgas, and yakṣas, never to a meditatively cultivated result within the desire realm. The form realm possesses three of these four minds, lacking only the unwholesome, since beings there have already left behind aversion and sensual craving; the formless realm likewise possesses three, also lacking the unwholesome. And beyond all three realms, the uncontaminated mind itself is of two kinds — that of the learner, who has severed only part of the three realms' craving and views, and that of the one beyond learning, whose severance is already complete.

The treatise then details these same categories further: the desire realm's own wholesome mind divides into innate wholesomeness (natural goodness present from birth) and effortful wholesomeness (goodness cultivated through education or practice); its unobscured-neutral mind's four kinds are explained one by one — the karma-ripened mind belonging to the store-consciousness and to the first six consciousnesses spontaneously engaging their twelve sense-fields, the deportment mind occupied with everyday postures, the craft mind occupied with bodily or verbal skill, and the transformation mind, which in the desire realm is always an innate capacity belonging to devas, nāgas, and yakṣas rather than a result of cultivated meditative attainment — the lecture illustrating this with a modern account of a forensic examiner in Taiwan reportedly able to perceive the hell, hungry-ghost, and animal realms directly, itself an innate rather than meditatively cultivated capacity. The form and formless realms further lack any craft mind at all, since embodied beings absorbed in meditative concentration have no need of manual skills, the formless realm having no body at all to speak of; and it should be understood, the treatise adds, that the wholesome mind — like the form and formless realms' possession of both innate and effortfully cultivated wholesomeness — extends everywhere across all three realms without exception.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this realm-by-realm inventory as a practical map of what actually becomes possible as a practitioner ascends through the three realms: the loss of the unwholesome mind above the desire realm reflects the simple fact that beings absorbed in meditative concentration have already left behind both aversion and sensual craving, while the loss of the craft mind reflects that embodied skill has no further use once the practitioner's attention has turned wholly inward. Having completed this classification by realm, he notes, the treatise turns to a third and final scheme — classifying mind according to which stage of severing affliction it belongs to.

15. Section: Sixteen Minds Classified by What They Sever — The Four Truths Across Three Realms, Plus the Uncontaminated

The treatise turns to a third classification, this time by what kind of affliction a given mind is in the process of severing. The desire realm has five such minds: the mind severed through seeing suffering, the mind severed through seeing its origin, the mind severed through seeing its cessation, the mind severed through seeing the path, and the mind severed through the path of cultivation — the first four naming the direct realization of the four noble truths that severs views arising from intellectual error the moment a practitioner truly understands suffering as suffering, its origin as origin, its cessation as cessation, and the path as path, thereby severing self-view, extreme-view, view-attachment, wrong view, and attachment to rules and rituals, together with the conceptually generated forms of craving, aversion, conceit, ignorance, and doubt; the fifth names the further severing, after stream-entry, of the same afflictions in their innate, habitually arising form through sustained cultivation on the path. Just as the desire realm has these five, the treatise explains, so too do the form and formless realms each have their own five, and adding the single further category of the uncontaminated mind belonging to the noble ones, the total across all three realms and beyond comes to sixteen distinct kinds of mind altogether.

16. Section: Three Grades of Practitioner, and the Very Beginner's Simple Alternation Between Affliction and Its Absence

The treatise now explains the twenty kinds of mind described earlier in terms of three grades of being. The first grade has not yet set out toward meditative concentration at all; the second has set out toward it but has not yet attained it; the third has already attained it. Each of these three, the treatise further explains, may be either impure or utterly pure — impure describing both the first grade and the second grade prior to attainment, utterly pure describing, from the standpoint of the mundane path, attainment of a fundamental meditative absorption, or, from the standpoint of the supramundane path, the complete severance of the three realms' craving and views; someone who has attained only the preparatory stage just short of the first absorption, the treatise notes, still counts as impure, since their afflictions remain far from fully severed. Within the first grade — the ordinary person who has not yet turned toward meditative cultivation at all — the mind is characterized simply by an ordinary alternation: at times a defiled mind arises, bound up with craving, aversion, and the other afflictions as the six sense faculties meet their six corresponding objects in the ordinary course of daily life; at other times, when this same person happens to awaken briefly to their own excess, a wholesome or neutral mind arises instead, craving and its companions momentarily set aside though not yet uprooted at the root.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first grade's own simple alternation — defiled mind, then briefly wholesome or neutral mind, then defiled again — as an accurate and rather humbling portrait of ordinary life before any real practice has begun: moments of genuine goodness do arise even for someone who has never formally taken up meditation, but they arise unsupported by any sustained method, and so pass as quickly as they came, the underlying affliction never actually severed, merely set momentarily aside. Having described this first grade, he notes, the treatise turns to the second grade — the practitioner who has begun to strive toward concentration but has not yet attained it — whose own inner experience turns out to be considerably more varied and, for anyone who has ever tried to meditate, immediately recognizable.

17. Section: The Second Grade's Eight Minds — Scattering, Sinking, Rousing, Over-Rousing, and Finally Settling Into Quiet

The treatise turns to the second grade of practitioner, who has set out toward meditative concentration but has not yet attained it, describing eight distinct mental states such a practitioner passes through. The first, the contracted mind, names the moment attention settles inward and briefly quiets upon its object. The second, the scattered mind, names how attention, losing its mindfulness, runs outward toward the five sensory pleasures — suddenly recalling lunch, or a pleasant sound, or an attractive sight from the day before — the mind wandering off in exactly the way any beginning meditator recognizes within moments of sitting down. The third, the sunken mind, names how, when attention is drawn in too tightly and too quietly, dullness and drowsiness instead creep over the mind, a state easily mistaken for deep stillness but in fact simply torpor. The fourth, the rousing mind, names the practitioner's own remedy for this dullness: turning attention toward the six radiant objects of recollection — the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, moral discipline, generosity, and the heavens — deliberately lifting and brightening a mind that has begun to sink. The fifth, the agitated mind, names what happens when this same rousing is applied carelessly, without properly settling back onto the meditation object, so that attention instead spins off into thoughts of one's homeland, one's family, or fear of death. The sixth, the unagitated mind, names the correction of this same excess, attention properly returned to its object once agitation has been recognized. The seventh, the unsettled mind, names the state that persists so long as the two hindrances of dullness and agitation remain unsevered, neither calming nor insight yet able to rest quietly. And the eighth, the settled mind, names the state achieved once both hindrances are finally overcome, the mind becoming even, upright, and requiring no further effort to sustain itself, luminous and still — the access concentration bordering the first absorption, though not yet the absorption itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the second grade, at times the mind is made to settle quietly inward. At times, losing mindfulness, it runs scattered toward the five sensory pleasures. At times, through excessive quieting, dullness and drowsiness come to envelop the mind. At times, in order to sever this, the mind is placed upon a radiant object. At times, improperly placed there, the mind becomes agitated. When properly placed, it does not become agitated. Because the two hindrances of dullness and agitation remain unsevered, in both respects the mind is not yet settled. Once they are severed, the mind attains settledness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these eight minds as perhaps the single most immediately useful passage in this entire section for anyone actually attempting to meditate, since every one of the eight states described — the wandering, the dozing, the deliberate rousing, the overcorrection into restlessness, and the eventual settling — describes not some distant, abstract doctrinal category but precisely what happens during an ordinary meditation session, moment by moment, long before any real concentration has been attained. He notes especially the honest admission that dullness is easily mistaken for stillness, a caution any beginning meditator can recognize in their own experience of "enjoying" a session that was, in fact, mostly drowsiness. Having completed the second grade's eight-fold description, the treatise turns finally to the third grade — the practitioner who has already attained concentration — whose own remaining distinctions turn out to be considerably fewer, since the hardest work described above lies behind them.

18. Section: The Third Grade's Mind, and the Complete Closing of Consciousness's Five Distinctions

The treatise completes its account of the twenty kinds of mind with the third grade, the practitioner who has already attained concentration. Such a one, having attained a fundamental meditative absorption through proper reflection, possesses what is called a concentrated mind, while one who has not yet done so remains in an unconcentrated mind. Further, having brought the path of calming and insight to full mastery — practiced over a long time until it can be entered at will, without difficulty or obstruction — such a one possesses what is called a well-cultivated mind, while having brought the severance of afflictive seeds to its own ultimate completion through the supramundane path, such a one possesses what is called the mind of utmost liberation; the opposite of each — inability to enter calming and insight freely, and incomplete severance of affliction's seeds — are called, respectively, the poorly cultivated mind and the unliberated mind. Everything from the concentrated mind onward, the treatise concludes, belongs to this third grade, and with this the entire account of consciousness aggregate's distinction by changing aspects reaches its close — the treatise's own summary reviewing all three grades together: the first grade's ordinary alternation between affliction and its momentary absence; the second grade's eight-fold experience of scattering, sinking, rousing, over-rousing, and eventual settling, still short of the first absorption; and the third grade's own settled distinctions of concentrated versus unconcentrated, well-cultivated versus poorly cultivated, and utterly liberated versus unliberated. With the fifth and final distinction thus concluded, consciousness aggregate's own five distinctions — by dwelling place, by defilement, by support, by station, and by changing aspects — stand together fully explained, bringing to completion the entire discussion of consciousness's distinctions that occupies the second of the six general aspects belonging to skillful understanding of the aggregates.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having, through proper reflection, already attained a fundamental meditative absorption, this is called a concentrated mind; not yet having attained it, this is called an unconcentrated mind. The path having reached its own completion, this is called a well-cultivated mind; the severance having reached its own completion, this is called the mind of utmost liberation. The opposite of these are called the poorly cultivated mind and the unliberated mind. From the concentrated mind onward, one should know this belongs to the third grade. This is called consciousness aggregate's distinction by changing aspects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the completion of these twenty minds across three grades as a satisfying close to the entire, very long discussion of consciousness aggregate's five distinctions that began several sections earlier with the simple question of where consciousness dwells in each of the three realms — noting that the whole arc, from dwelling place through defilement, support, the elaborate twelve-part station-and-its-cessation, and now these twenty changing aspects, has consistently moved from the most abstract doctrinal categories toward increasingly concrete, practice-relevant description, ending here with states any meditator can recognize directly in their own experience. With consciousness's own distinctions now fully closed, he notes, the treatise turns next to the aggregates' own sequence — the third of the six general aspects belonging to skillful understanding of the aggregates, explaining why form is spoken of first, feeling second, and so on, through five distinct explanatory approaches: by arising, by antidote, by continuity, by abiding, and by establishment.
