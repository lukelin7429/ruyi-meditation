Fascicle 18 · 瑜伽師地論卷第十八 · The Essential-Body Verses Continue

The Superior Kind of Person Who Leads Others to Freedom

Fascicle 18 continues directly from fascicle 17's stopping point, taking up the sixth of fourteen topics in the essential-body verses: who is genuinely qualified to guide others toward liberation, what the path of release actually consists in, and where a practitioner should stand and what they should learn to never fear death again.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 18 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Topic Six Opens: Who Can Truly Guide Others to Freedom?

Continuing directly from fascicle 17, the sixth of fourteen topics opens with a deity's question about genuine spiritual leadership: who is actually qualified to guide the different classes of society toward liberation, and what does the path itself consist in? The Buddha's answer compresses the entire path — from his own long preparation through the stages of realization to final, fearless freedom — into two dense verses.

Chinese Source

誰獎勝類生，及開出離道？於何住何學，不懼後世死？

Ru-Yi Translation

Who guides the superior kinds of beings, and opens the path of release? In what should one abide, and what should one train in, so as not to fear death in a future life?

Chinese Source

戒慧自熏修，具定念正直，斷諸愁熾然，正念心解脫；能獎勝類生，及開出離道，住此於此學，不懼後世死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precepts and wisdom, self-cultivated; possessed of concentration, mindfulness, and uprightness; having cut off sorrow's blaze; the mind liberated through right mindfulness — such a one truly guides the superior kinds of beings, and opens the path of release. Abiding in this, training in this, one no longer fears death in a future life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the Buddha's answer as tracing his own path from the very beginning: as a bodhisattva, he renounced the finest sensual pleasures and household life, kept pure bodily and verbal discipline, and reflected deeply on beings' universal suffering — cultivating the six perfections across three incalculable eons until, with no teacher needed, the mind of renunciation arose naturally and insight into the five aggregates' true nature followed. From there, resting on the four absorptions and eight attainments, he cultivated the four foundations of mindfulness and the full thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, generating the noble eightfold path that runs counter to saṃsāra's current and severing every affliction eliminated by the path of seeing — the attainment of stream-entry. Continued cultivation of the same factors then secured non-returner status, permanently free of sorrow's grief and craving's blaze; and continued cultivation once more secured arhatship, or, for one following the bodhisattva path, full buddhahood, mind liberated from both the afflictive and cognitive obstructions.

He treats this compressed account as establishing exactly why only the Buddha is qualified to answer the deity's opening question: having personally traversed every stage of the path from renunciation through complete liberation, he alone can guide beings of every social class toward the same release, and open the same path for others that he walked himself. Whoever, of any class, takes up this same threefold training — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — in genuine practice will, the verse promises, reach the same freedom from fear of future death. With the compressed answer given, the treatise turns to explaining who exactly these "superior kinds of beings" are, and what makes the Buddha uniquely their true guide.

2. The Four Classes, and Why Only the Buddha Is the True Guide

The treatise names the four classes of ancient Indian society the verse addresses, then explains precisely what genuine guidance toward liberation requires — and argues that only the Buddha, having realized every stage of the path himself, actually qualifies as both the true guide and the true teacher of the path.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，言勝類者，即是四種勝上姓類。一、婆羅門，二、剎帝利，三、吠舍，四、戍達羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, "the superior kinds" refers to the four superior classes: first, brahmins; second, kṣatriyas; third, vaiśyas; and fourth, śūdras.

Chinese Source

以法、以正、以制、以導，教勝類生，故名為獎。此中顯示唯佛世尊能以法、以正、以制、以導，教勝類生。由此因緣，世尊自顯唯我獨為真獎導者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Teaching the superior kinds of beings through the Dharma, through right refuge, through established precepts, and through guidance — this is called "guiding." This shows that only the Blessed One is able to teach the superior kinds of beings through the Dharma, through right refuge, through established precepts, and through guidance. For this reason, the Blessed One reveals that he alone is the true guide.

Chinese Source

又唯世尊能為四種勝上類生，宣說出離一切眾苦聖八支道。此中世尊亦自顯示是真說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

And only the Blessed One is able to teach the four superior classes of beings the noble eightfold path that releases every kind of suffering. Here, the Blessed One likewise reveals himself as the true teacher.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the four classical Indian classes briefly: brahmins, the priestly and scholarly caste responsible for ritual and learning, traditionally held to wield influence exceeding even royalty; kṣatriyas, kings and warriors charged with governance and defense; vaiśyas, commoners engaged in farming, herding, and trade; and śūdras, the lowest and most impoverished class, historically bound to menial and degrading labor in service of the other three. He notes the treatise's point is not to endorse the caste hierarchy itself but simply to observe that all four classes, whatever their worldly station, are equally "superior" in one specific sense — capable, unlike beings in the lower realms, of actually learning and practicing the Dharma.

He unpacks "guiding" into four increasingly complete functions drawn from the Compendium of Establishments: expelling what should not be done, encouraging what should be done, clearly disclosing both, and — the fullest sense — dispelling every doubt a student might have, along with all remorse over past error and every affliction, gross or subtle. Only someone who has personally exhausted every doubt and every affliction in themselves can offer this fullest guidance to another, which is exactly why the treatise insists this designation belongs to the Buddha uniquely, not even to his most advanced disciples. He draws the same distinction for "teacher": the Compendium of Establishments names three roles — one who genuinely teaches from direct realization, one who transmits that teaching faithfully (Śāriputra, whom the Buddha often asked to teach in his place, is the classic example), and one who simply practices in accordance with what was taught — and only the first, belonging to the Buddha alone, counts as the "true teacher" this verse names. With guide and teacher both defined and reserved for the Buddha specifically, the treatise turns to explaining, one by one, the nine specific qualities that actually make him so.

3. Precepts, Wisdom, and Self-Cultivation: The First Three of Nine Qualities

The treatise begins its nine-part breakdown of the compressed verse with the first three qualities: the precepts kept as a bodhisattva, the wisdom that recognized suffering's universal reach, and the self-cultivation sustained across three incalculable eons that let renunciation and insight finally arise without any teacher's prompting.

Chinese Source

云何具戒？謂佛世尊昔菩薩時，棄上妙欲，捨離居家，受持身語所有律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of precepts"? It means that the Blessed Buddha, when formerly a bodhisattva, gave up the finest sensual pleasures, left household life behind, and upheld all bodily and verbal discipline.

Chinese Source

云何具慧？謂即於彼受持身語律儀住者，起如是相，內正思惟，深心籌量，審諦觀察：今此世間多遭艱苦，所謂若生、若老，如經廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of wisdom"? It means that, abiding in this bodily and verbal discipline, such a mark arose: reflecting rightly within, weighing it deeply, examining it truly — recognizing that this world constantly meets with hardship, whether through birth, through aging, and so on, as the sutras explain at length.

Chinese Source

云何自熏修？謂於往昔無量餘生，經三大劫阿僧企耶，於六波羅蜜多修習、善修習；由彼因緣，今無師自然心趣出離，又於眾緣所生諸行，以微妙智能隨悟入。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "self-cultivated"? It means that, across countless former lives spanning three incalculable eons, the six perfections were cultivated, and cultivated well. By this cause, in this life, without any teacher, the mind naturally inclined toward renunciation — and, toward all conditioned things arising from many causes, subtle wisdom was able to penetrate and awaken accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first quality, precepts, as beginning with the bodhisattva's own great renunciation — leaving the finest palace pleasures and royal household behind for pure bodily and verbal conduct — the same concrete act of renunciation the treatise has held up as the model throughout this entire ground. The second quality, wisdom, he reads as the specific realization that followed directly from that same disciplined life: not abstract philosophy, but a settled recognition, arrived at through genuinely deep reflection, that birth, aging, and every stage of life bring hardship universally, to every being without exception — the same starting insight later systematized as the first noble truth.

The third quality, self-cultivation, he treats as the crucial explanation for why the Buddha needed no teacher where every other being does: three incalculable eons of sustained practice across the six perfections (generosity, precepts, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom) had already, by the time of his final life, brought the causal conditions to full ripeness — renunciation and insight into dependent origination's true nature (impermanent, suffering, empty, without self) arose naturally, the way fruit ripens and falls of its own accord once conditions are complete, needing no external prompting. Cháng Bǎi stresses this quality specifically distinguishes the Buddha's realization from a disciple's: a śrāvaka needs to hear the teaching from someone else, while the Buddha's own vast prior cultivation let the same insight arise entirely from within. With precepts, wisdom, and self-cultivation established as the foundation, the treatise turns to the next three qualities: the concentration, mindfulness, and stream-entry that follow from this foundation.
