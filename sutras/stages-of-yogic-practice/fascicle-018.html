Fascicle 18 · 瑜伽師地論卷第十八 · The Essential-Body Verses, Topics Six Through Fourteen

The Superior Kind of Person Who Leads Others to Freedom

Fascicle 18 continues directly from fascicle 17's stopping point and carries thirteen of the fourteen essential-body-verse topics to completion — from genuine spiritual guidance and the spring of craving, through the true meaning of "brahmin" and the Buddha's own toil, to the start of the fourteenth and largest topic, "discourse," whose first five of twenty-seven sub-parts (impermanence, heedfulness, craving's bondage, pure precepts, and the fruit of karma) are translated here in full.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 18 complete (topic 14 of 14 partly translated, continues in fascicle 19) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 18 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Topic Six Opens: Who Can Truly Guide Others to Freedom?

Continuing directly from fascicle 17, the sixth of fourteen topics opens with a deity's question about genuine spiritual leadership: who is actually qualified to guide the different classes of society toward liberation, and what does the path itself consist in? The Buddha's answer compresses the entire path — from his own long preparation through the stages of realization to final, fearless freedom — into two dense verses.

Chinese Source

誰獎勝類生，及開出離道？於何住何學，不懼後世死？

Ru-Yi Translation

Who guides the superior kinds of beings, and opens the path of release? In what should one abide, and what should one train in, so as not to fear death in a future life?

Chinese Source

戒慧自熏修，具定念正直，斷諸愁熾然，正念心解脫；能獎勝類生，及開出離道，住此於此學，不懼後世死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precepts and wisdom, self-cultivated; possessed of concentration, mindfulness, and uprightness; having cut off sorrow's blaze; the mind liberated through right mindfulness — such a one truly guides the superior kinds of beings, and opens the path of release. Abiding in this, training in this, one no longer fears death in a future life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the Buddha's answer as tracing his own path from the very beginning: as a bodhisattva, he renounced the finest sensual pleasures and household life, kept pure bodily and verbal discipline, and reflected deeply on beings' universal suffering — cultivating the six perfections across three incalculable eons until, with no teacher needed, the mind of renunciation arose naturally and insight into the five aggregates' true nature followed. From there, resting on the four absorptions and eight attainments, he cultivated the four foundations of mindfulness and the full thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, generating the noble eightfold path that runs counter to saṃsāra's current and severing every affliction eliminated by the path of seeing — the attainment of stream-entry. Continued cultivation of the same factors then secured non-returner status, permanently free of sorrow's grief and craving's blaze; and continued cultivation once more secured arhatship, or, for one following the bodhisattva path, full buddhahood, mind liberated from both the afflictive and cognitive obstructions.

He treats this compressed account as establishing exactly why only the Buddha is qualified to answer the deity's opening question: having personally traversed every stage of the path from renunciation through complete liberation, he alone can guide beings of every social class toward the same release, and open the same path for others that he walked himself. Whoever, of any class, takes up this same threefold training — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — in genuine practice will, the verse promises, reach the same freedom from fear of future death. With the compressed answer given, the treatise turns to explaining who exactly these "superior kinds of beings" are, and what makes the Buddha uniquely their true guide.

2. The Four Classes, and Why Only the Buddha Is the True Guide

The treatise names the four classes of ancient Indian society the verse addresses, then explains precisely what genuine guidance toward liberation requires — and argues that only the Buddha, having realized every stage of the path himself, actually qualifies as both the true guide and the true teacher of the path.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，言勝類者，即是四種勝上姓類。一、婆羅門，二、剎帝利，三、吠舍，四、戍達羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, "the superior kinds" refers to the four superior classes: first, brahmins; second, kṣatriyas; third, vaiśyas; and fourth, śūdras.

Chinese Source

以法、以正、以制、以導，教勝類生，故名為獎。此中顯示唯佛世尊能以法、以正、以制、以導，教勝類生。由此因緣，世尊自顯唯我獨為真獎導者。……

Ru-Yi Translation

Teaching the superior kinds of beings through the Dharma, through right refuge, through established precepts, and through guidance — this is called "guiding." This shows that only the Blessed One is able to teach the superior kinds of beings through the Dharma, through right refuge, through established precepts, and through guidance. For this reason, the Blessed One reveals that he alone is the true guide...

Chinese Source

故為彼天作如是說，具戒、具慧以自薰修。

Ru-Yi Translation

So he spoke thus to that god: complete in ethics and complete in wisdom, one perfumes and cultivates oneself.

Chinese Source

又唯世尊能為四種勝上類生，宣說出離一切眾苦聖八支道。此中世尊亦自顯示是真說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

And only the Blessed One is able to teach the four superior classes of beings the noble eightfold path that releases every kind of suffering. Here, the Blessed One likewise reveals himself as the true teacher.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the four classical Indian classes briefly: brahmins, the priestly and scholarly caste responsible for ritual and learning, traditionally held to wield influence exceeding even royalty; kṣatriyas, kings and warriors charged with governance and defense; vaiśyas, commoners engaged in farming, herding, and trade; and śūdras, the lowest and most impoverished class, historically bound to menial and degrading labor in service of the other three. He notes the treatise's point is not to endorse the caste hierarchy itself but simply to observe that all four classes, whatever their worldly station, are equally "superior" in one specific sense — capable, unlike beings in the lower realms, of actually learning and practicing the Dharma.

He unpacks "guiding" into four increasingly complete functions drawn, via the commentarial gloss (披尋記), from the Compendium of Synonyms: expelling what should not be done, encouraging what should be done, clearly disclosing both, and — the fullest sense — dispelling every doubt a student might have, along with all remorse over past error and every affliction, gross or subtle. Only someone who has personally exhausted every doubt and every affliction in themselves can offer this fullest guidance to another, which is exactly why the treatise insists this designation belongs to the Buddha uniquely, not even to his most advanced disciples. He draws the same distinction for "teacher": the Compendium of Synonyms, as the commentarial gloss cites it, names three roles — one who genuinely teaches from direct realization, one who transmits that teaching faithfully (Śāriputra, whom the Buddha often asked to teach in his place, is the classic example), and one who simply practices in accordance with what was taught — and only the first, belonging to the Buddha alone, counts as the "true teacher" this verse names. With guide and teacher both defined and reserved for the Buddha specifically, the treatise turns to explaining, one by one, the nine specific qualities that actually make him so.

3. Precepts, Wisdom, and Self-Cultivation: The First Three of Nine Qualities

The treatise begins its nine-part breakdown of the compressed verse with the first three qualities: the precepts kept as a bodhisattva, the wisdom that recognized suffering's universal reach, and the self-cultivation sustained across three incalculable eons that let renunciation and insight finally arise without any teacher's prompting.

Chinese Source

云何具戒？謂佛世尊昔菩薩時，棄上妙欲，捨離居家，受持身語所有律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of precepts"? It means that the Blessed Buddha, when formerly a bodhisattva, gave up the finest sensual pleasures, left household life behind, and upheld all bodily and verbal discipline.

Chinese Source

云何具慧？謂即於彼受持身語律儀住者，起如是相，內正思惟，深心籌量，審諦觀察：今此世間多遭艱苦，所謂若生、若老，如經廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of wisdom"? It means that one who, upholding this bodily and verbal discipline, abides [in the discipline of meditative absorption] raises such an image [as the object of meditation]: reflecting rightly within, weighing it deeply, examining it truly — recognizing that this world constantly meets with hardship, whether through birth, through aging, and so on, as the sutras explain at length.

Chinese Source

云何自熏修？謂於往昔無量餘生，經三大劫阿僧企耶，於六波羅蜜多修習、善修習；由彼因緣，今無師自然心趣出離，又於眾緣所生諸行，以微妙智能隨悟入。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "self-cultivated"? It means that, across countless former lives spanning three incalculable eons, the six perfections were cultivated, and cultivated well. By this cause, in this life, without any teacher, the mind naturally inclined toward renunciation — and, toward all conditioned things arising from many causes, subtle wisdom was able to penetrate and awaken accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first quality, precepts, as beginning with the bodhisattva's own great renunciation — leaving the finest palace pleasures and royal household behind for pure bodily and verbal conduct — the same concrete act of renunciation the treatise has held up as the model throughout this entire ground. The second quality, wisdom, he reads as the specific realization that followed directly from that same disciplined life: not abstract philosophy, but a settled recognition, arrived at through genuinely deep reflection, that birth, aging, and every stage of life bring hardship universally, to every being without exception — the same starting insight later systematized as the first noble truth.

The third quality, self-cultivation, he treats as the crucial explanation for why the Buddha needed no teacher where every other being does: three incalculable eons of sustained practice across the six perfections (generosity, precepts, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom) had already, by the time of his final life, brought the causal conditions to full ripeness — renunciation and insight into dependent origination's true nature (impermanent, suffering, empty, without self) arose naturally, the way fruit ripens and falls of its own accord once conditions are complete, needing no external prompting. Cháng Bǎi stresses this quality specifically distinguishes the Buddha's realization from a disciple's: a śrāvaka needs to hear the teaching from someone else, while the Buddha's own vast prior cultivation let the same insight arise entirely from within. With precepts, wisdom, and self-cultivation established as the foundation, the treatise turns to the next three qualities: the concentration, mindfulness, and stream-entry that follow from this foundation.

4. Concentration, Mindfulness, and Uprightness: Reaching the Stream

The fourth through sixth qualities trace the path from meditative mastery through the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment to the noble eightfold path itself — arising in direct opposition to saṃsāra's current — culminating in stream-entry: the first genuine foothold in the stream of noble ones.

Chinese Source

云何具定？謂能乃至離無所有處欲，證得非想非非想處。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of concentration"? It means being able to become free of desire even for the sphere of nothingness, and on to realizing the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

Chinese Source

云何具念？謂依如是所得勝定，為斷見斷諸煩惱故，修四念住。即以如是所修念住為其導首，乃至修習三十七種菩提分法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of mindfulness"? It means that, resting on this superior concentration attained, in order to sever the afflictions eliminated by the path of seeing, the four foundations of mindfulness are cultivated. Taking this cultivated mindfulness as the leading factor, the full thirty-seven factors of enlightenment are cultivated in turn.

Chinese Source

云何正直？謂彼生起逆流正直聖八支道，能斷見斷所有煩惱，於逆流道得預隨流。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "uprightness"? It means that the noble eightfold path arises, upright and running counter to the current of saṃsāra, able to sever every affliction eliminated by the path of seeing — and, on this path running counter to the current, one becomes established in the stream of the noble ones.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "possessed of concentration" as naming the full range of the eight meditative attainments, climbing from the first absorption through the four formless spheres to their very peak, neither-perception-nor-non-perception — the complete mastery of worldly meditation the Buddha had already achieved before his final breakthrough. "Possessed of mindfulness" then shows how that same concentration becomes the platform for actual liberating insight: the four foundations of mindfulness (observing body, feeling, mind, and mental objects) serve as the entry point for the full sequence of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment already introduced in earlier fascicles — the four right efforts, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment, and the eightfold path itself, each building on the last until non-conceptual wisdom directly severs every affliction the path of seeing eliminates (the same 112 conceptually-produced afflictions across the three realms named in earlier fascicles).

"Uprightness," the sixth quality, he reads as the moment this cultivation actually bears fruit: the noble eightfold path arising in direct, active opposition to saṃsāra's own current — since ordinary craving and ignorance carry beings downstream into further rebirth, while the eightfold path alone runs the other way — and severing every seeing-path affliction in a single stroke secures a practitioner's first genuine, irreversible foothold in the stream of the noble ones: stream-entry itself. With concentration, mindfulness, and uprightness accounting for the path up through this first noble attainment, the treatise turns to the three qualities that carry a practitioner from stream-entry through to full and final liberation.

5. Topic Six Concludes: From Sorrow's End to Full Awakening

The final three qualities carry the path from stream-entry through non-returner status to complete liberation from both obstructions — culminating, for the bodhisattva specifically, in full buddhahood — before the treatise closes topic six with a summary confirming the Buddha alone as both true guide and true teacher.

Chinese Source

云何永斷一切愁憂熾然？謂從諦現觀俱得成不還者。又能永斷五下分結，瞋恚似順愁憂，貪欲似順熾然，於如是等皆已永斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "permanently cutting off all sorrow's blaze"? It means that, from direct realization of the truths, one becomes a non-returner, thereby permanently severing the five lower fetters. Aversion resembles and accords with sorrow; craving resembles and accords with blazing fire — both of these are now permanently severed.

Chinese Source

云何正念？謂為永斷上分諸結，復更修習四種念住，乃至修習三十七種菩提分法。云何心解脫？謂已永斷上分結故，於二種障心善解脫，謂煩惱障及所知障。其心如是善解脫故，得成如來應正等覺……

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "right mindfulness"? It means that, in order to permanently sever the higher fetters as well, the four foundations of mindfulness are cultivated once more, and the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment cultivated in turn. What is "mind liberated"? It means that, the higher fetters now permanently severed, the mind becomes genuinely liberated from both obstructions — the afflictive obstruction and the cognitive obstruction. Because the mind is thus genuinely liberated, one attains the state of the Tathāgata, worthy of offerings, perfectly and fully awakened...

Chinese Source

廣說如經。由此故能獎勝類生，開出離道。

Ru-Yi Translation

As explained at length in the sūtra. Because of this, he can encourage those of superior kind and open the path of release.

Chinese Source

諸有四種勝類，隨一於此聖教愛樂正行，為欲證得聖八支道，於三學中勤修學者，彼定能證聖八支道及涅槃果。由證彼故，不懼當來生老病死。……又復示現佛是天人無上大師。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whichever of the four superior classes delights in genuine practice within this teaching, and diligently trains in the three trainings in order to realize the noble eightfold path, will surely realize both the noble eightfold path and the fruit of nirvāṇa. Through this realization, one no longer fears future birth, aging, sickness, and death... This also reveals the Buddha as the unsurpassed teacher of gods and humans alike.

Chinese Source

當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "sorrow's blaze" with the two afflictions among the five lower fetters that remain after stream-entry's first three are cut — aversion, whose felt quality resembles grief and sorrow, and craving, whose felt quality resembles a fire building in intensity — both fully extinguished only at non-returner status. He then distinguishes the five higher fetters targeted next: craving for the form realm, craving for the formless realm, the restlessness that arises from over-valuing meditative attainment, the pride of comparing one's own concentration favorably against others', and the subtle ignorance of still not recognizing that even the most refined absorption remains impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — subtler afflictions than the lower five precisely because they arise from spiritual attainment itself rather than ordinary sense-craving.

Full severance of these five higher fetters, he notes, yields arhatship for a śrāvaka — but the treatise specifically frames this ninth and final quality around the bodhisattva path, where the same cultivation, carried further to include severance of the cognitive obstruction as well as the afflictive one, yields complete buddhahood; he cites the classical contrast between an arhat's limited claim to knowledge ("I will answer what I know") and the Buddha's unlimited one ("ask whatever you wish") as illustrating exactly this difference in scope. With all nine qualities named — precepts, wisdom, self-cultivation, concentration, mindfulness, uprightness, sorrow's end, right mindfulness, and mind liberated — the treatise closes topic six by confirming, once more, that only someone who has completed this entire nine-part path is qualified to be called the true guide of the four classes and the true teacher of the path that frees them, and turns to its seventh topic: what actually brings a person genuine renown, wealth, praise, and friendship.

6. Topic Seven Opens: What Actually Brings Renown, Wealth, Praise, and Friends

The seventh topic turns to entirely practical concerns — the very things most people spend their lives pursuing — and the Buddha's answer is disarmingly direct: precepts bring renown, generosity brings wealth, honesty brings praise, and giving brings genuine friendship. The treatise then illustrates the first with one of the tradition's most memorable stories about Mahākāśyapa.

Chinese Source

云何擅名譽？云何具珍財？云何獲美稱？云何攝親友？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one come to command renown? How does one come to possess treasured wealth? How does one come to earn genuine praise? How does one come to gather true friends?

Chinese Source

持戒擅名譽，布施具珍財，諦實獲美稱，惠捨攝親友。

Ru-Yi Translation

Keeping precepts, one commands renown; giving generously, one possesses treasured wealth; speaking truthfully, one earns genuine praise; giving freely, one gathers true friends.

Chinese Source

云何持戒能擅名譽？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does keeping the precepts win renown?

Chinese Source

謂如有一，或男或女，具足尸羅及賢善法，乃至命終斷除殺罪、遠離殺生，如經廣說……乃至十方所有沙門、婆羅門等，常所稱歎。由是因緣，為諸國王、群臣、長者，乃至城邑聚落人民，恭敬供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one, man or woman, who fully possesses moral discipline and the qualities of a genuinely good person — abandoning the fault of killing, keeping far from taking life, until the very end of their days, as the sutras explain at length... down to being constantly praised by ascetics and brahmins in every direction. For this reason, they receive the reverent support of kings, ministers, elders, and the people of every town and village alike.

Chinese Source

云何布施能具珍財？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does giving bring wealth and treasure?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the qualities of a "genuinely good person" as more than mere rule-following: born to either a noble or prosperous family and gone forth with genuine faith, such a person carries themselves with a naturally composed and dignified bearing, has genuinely studied the teaching, speaks skillfully and completely, and — however small the wisdom, insight, or peace so far attained — neither grows arrogant about it nor looks down on others still further behind, recognizing that only actually practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma is genuinely worthwhile. This settled humility, sustained across the full ten wholesome courses of action rather than the precept against killing alone, is what the treatise means by "possessing precepts" in its fullest sense.

He illustrates renown's actual mechanism with the well-known story of Mahākāśyapa, famed above all disciples for the purity of his conduct: children who saw the Buddha and his monks pass by would offer only a passing greeting, still clutching their sweets — but the moment they spotted Mahākāśyapa among the group, they ran to offer him their treats directly. When Mahākāśyapa redirected them to make their offering to the Buddha and the wider community instead, the other monks asked the Buddha why the children had singled him out. The Buddha's answer was simple: Mahākāśyapa's precepts are so purely kept that anyone who merely sees him, monastic or lay, spontaneously feels joy. Cháng Bǎi treats the story as proof that renown built on genuine virtue requires no self-promotion at all — it simply radiates outward on its own, exactly as the tradition's saying has it, that precepts themselves carry their own fragrance. With renown's source explained, the treatise turns to the second question: what actually produces lasting wealth.

7. Topic Seven Concludes: Wealth, Praise, and Friendship's Real Sources

The remaining three questions close out with matching directness: wealth traces back to generosity practiced in past lives, praise to honest livelihood untainted by deception, and genuine friendship to present generosity that makes others glad to help in return. A closing summary sorts all four causes into three underlying forces.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，昔餘生中作及增長施福業事；由此因緣，今生巨富大財寶家，乃至眾多府庫盈積。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who, in some former life, performed and repeatedly cultivated meritorious acts of giving; by this cause, in this life, one is born into a family of vast wealth, its storehouses filled to overflowing.

Chinese Source

云何諦實能獲美稱？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does truthfulness earn praise?

Chinese Source

謂如有一，不以假偽斗稱函等，諂誑陵懱妄言等事，而致財寶；但以如法作業技能，依法不暴，而致財寶。彼既如是，眾咸唱言：賢哉儒士！乃能如法作業技能引致財寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who does not resort to false measures and scales, deceptive containers or letters, flattery, contempt, or falsehood to acquire wealth, but earns it only through lawful work and genuine skill, honestly and without violence. Such a person, everyone declares: what a worthy soul! — able to acquire wealth entirely through lawful labor and skill.

Chinese Source

云何惠捨能攝親友？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does generosity gather friends?

Chinese Source

謂如有一，現前多有種種家產，遠離慳垢，不吝資具，以正安樂而自歡娛，乃至友朋、親戚、耆長。彼諸人等便相佐助，引致財寶，守護滋息。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who, possessing considerable family wealth, remains free of the stain of stinginess, is never grudging with their resources, and takes wholesome joy in sharing it — with friends, relatives, and elders alike. Such people, in turn, gladly help them, bringing further wealth their way, protecting it, and making it grow.

Chinese Source

復次，今當略辨上所說義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out.

Chinese Source

謂略顯示恭敬、利養二種因緣。持戒擅名譽者，顯恭敬因緣；所餘諸句，顯利養因緣。謂因力故，士用力故，助伴力故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This briefly reveals two kinds of cause: the cause of respect, and the cause of material gain. "Keeping precepts, one commands renown" reveals the cause of respect; the remaining lines reveal the causes of material gain — the power of karmic cause, the power of human effort, and the power of supportive companions.

Chinese Source

當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads wealth's cause as purely a matter of seeds already planted: generosity practiced and, crucially, sustained with genuine gladness (rather than followed by regret, which the treatise elsewhere notes actively diminishes the resulting merit) in a past life ripens as wealth in this one — a straightforward case of the "power of karmic cause." Praise's cause, by contrast, operates entirely within this present life: honest livelihood, free of false measures or deceptive dealing, earns spontaneous public respect through nothing more than consistent integrity, illustrating the "power of human effort." Friendship's cause he reads as a virtuous cycle: present-life generosity, freely shared with genuine joy rather than grudging obligation, naturally draws capable people to a person's side, and their combined support then compounds the very wealth that started the cycle — the "power of supportive companions."

He treats the three-force summary as the topic's real teaching: renown rests on respect (which precepts alone secure), while wealth, praise, and friendship all trace back to material gain through one of three distinct mechanisms — past karmic seed, present honest effort, or present generous cultivation of allies — a tidy causal map any practitioner can use to diagnose exactly which of these four goods they may be neglecting. With this topic complete, the treatise turns to its eighth: a spring and its water, a vivid extended metaphor for the six senses and the craving they continuously generate.

8. Topic Eight Opens: The Spring That Never Stops Flowing

The eighth topic poses three linked riddles through a single sustained image: when does the spring stop flowing, when does the path close, and where is all worldly suffering and pleasure extinguished without remainder? The Buddha's answer names a single place — the complete cessation of the six senses and the five aggregates — before the treatise unpacks exactly what the spring itself represents.

Chinese Source

齊何泉止息？於何徑不通？世間諸苦樂，何處無餘滅？

Ru-Yi Translation

At what point does the spring cease flowing? At what point does the path no longer run through? Where is the world's suffering and pleasure alike extinguished, without remainder?

Chinese Source

若於是處所，眼耳及與鼻，舌身意名色，永滅盡無餘；齊此泉止息，於斯徑不通，世間諸苦樂，是處無餘滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wherever eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind — together with mental and physical existence itself — are permanently and completely extinguished without remainder: precisely there does the spring cease flowing, precisely there does the path no longer run through, and precisely there is the world's suffering and pleasure alike extinguished without remainder.

Chinese Source

云何為泉？謂六觸處。何以故？譬如泉池能生諸水，水所繫屬，堪任觸用；又能存養男女大小，下及禽獸，乃至一切未盡枯竭。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the spring? It refers to the six fields of contact. Why is this so? Just as a spring or pool constantly generates water, water belonging to and drawn from it, fit for use and contact — sustaining men and women, young and old, and even animals — so long as it has not yet run dry.

Chinese Source

六內觸處亦復如是。一切愚夫六境界觸之所觸用；又能存養，乃至是中諸貪愛水未盡枯竭。

Ru-Yi Translation

The six internal fields of contact are just the same: every foolish, unlearned being makes use of contact with the six kinds of objects, and is sustained by it, so long as the water of craving within has not yet run dry.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the deity's three questions as three angles on a single destination: the spring's flowing, the path's passage, and the presence of worldly suffering and pleasure are simply three names for saṃsāra's continued operation, and the Buddha's answer names exactly one place where all three stop at once — the complete, remainderless cessation of the six senses and the aggregates of body and mind they depend on, which is to say, nirvāṇa itself.

He unpacks the spring metaphor with real precision: just as an actual spring continuously generates water that sustains every kind of creature for as long as it flows, the six sense-fields (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind) continuously generate contact with their objects, and that contact — for an ordinary, unenlightened being — continuously generates craving, the very "water" that sustains the ongoing life of affliction and further rebirth. The metaphor's power, he notes, lies in its passivity: a spring does not choose to flow, and an untrained sense faculty does not choose to generate craving upon contact — it simply does, automatically, moment after moment, for as long as ignorance keeps the source unblocked. Only when contact itself, and the aggregates that make contact possible, cease entirely does the spring finally run dry — precisely the state the deity's questions were pointing toward from the start. With the central image established, the treatise turns to unpacking the deity's second question: what exactly the "path" refers to.

9. Topic Eight Concludes: A Graduated Map From Ordinary Being to Final Nirvāṇa

Having explained the spring, the treatise defines the "path" as affliction and karma's own two-part road toward suffering, then closes topic eight with an unusually precise graduated map: exactly how much of the spring, the path, and suffering-and-pleasure remains present in an ordinary being, a trainee, an arhat still living, and an arhat who has passed into final nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

云何為徑？徑有二種。一、煩惱徑，二者、業徑。此中徑者，意明因義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the path? The path has two kinds: first, the path of affliction; and second, the path of karma. Here, "path" means precisely "cause."

Chinese Source

云何苦樂？謂或於現法六種觸處為緣所生，或安受，受所攝；或不安受，受所攝；或於後法煩惱攝持妙行、惡行為緣所生，或安受，受所攝；或不安受，受所攝。於何處所，如是六處及名色等無餘滅盡？謂無餘依涅槃界中。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are pain and pleasure? In this present life, those born from the six bases of contact as condition, either included in comfortable feeling or included in uncomfortable feeling; or, in a later life, those born from good conduct or bad conduct, held by the afflictions, as condition, either included in comfortable feeling or included in uncomfortable feeling. Where do these six bases, name-and-form, and the rest cease without remainder? In the realm of nirvana without remainder.

Chinese Source

若諸異生，泉徑苦樂一切無闕，亦未有捨。若諸有學，闕而未捨。若諸無學，徑及當來所有苦樂亦闕亦捨，不復現行；泉及現法所有苦樂亦闕亦捨，有餘依故，猶復現行。是故無餘涅槃界中，說彼一切無餘盡滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

In ordinary beings, the spring, the path, and suffering-and-pleasure are all entirely present, without deficiency, and not yet abandoned. In trainees, they are deficient but not yet abandoned. In those beyond training, the path and all future suffering-and-pleasure are both deficient and abandoned, no longer manifesting; but the spring and the suffering-and-pleasure of the present life are both deficient and abandoned, yet still manifest, since a remainder of past conditions still remains. Therefore, it is said that within the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder, all of this is extinguished without exception, leaving nothing behind.

Chinese Source

復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂略顯示於現法中因及苦樂，於後法中因及苦樂，於無餘依涅槃界中皆悉永滅。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. It briefly shows that the causes, pain, and pleasure of this present life, and the causes, pain, and pleasure of later lives, all cease for ever in the realm of nirvana without remainder. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the "path" as simply naming causation itself in its two forms — affliction, the mental cause, and karma, the resulting action — together forming the road that carries a being from present affliction to future suffering. He then treats the fourfold graduated map as this topic's real payoff, tracking precisely how much of the spring (sense-contact generating craving), the path (affliction and karma), and suffering-and-pleasure remains at each stage: an ordinary being has all three fully intact, bound by unbroken affliction with nothing yet reduced; a trainee (any of the six graded stages from stream-enterer through the approach to arhatship) has begun reducing affliction's grip but has not yet abandoned it entirely, since the subtler afflictions eliminated only by the path of cultivation still occasionally manifest.

The most striking distinction, he notes, comes at the level of the arhat: one who has attained arhatship while still alive has genuinely severed the *path* — no further karma is being generated, and no future suffering-and-pleasure will ever arise again — yet the *spring* itself, ordinary sense-contact with a body still shaped by past karma, continues operating, since a residue of prior conditions (the physical aggregates themselves) remains until death. Crucially, he stresses, this continued sense-contact produces only bodily feeling, never mental affliction — an arhat eating, walking, or encountering pleasant and unpleasant circumstances experiences physical sensation exactly as anyone would, but without the reflexive craving, aversion, or confusion that would turn that sensation into fresh suffering for an unawakened mind. Only at the arhat's death, entering nirvāṇa without any remainder at all, does even the spring itself finally run dry — the single point where spring, path, and suffering-and-pleasure alike are extinguished without exception. With this precise map complete, topic eight closes, and the treatise turns to its ninth topic, addressing floods once more from a different angle.

10. Topic Nine Opens: Who Crosses the Flood?

The ninth topic returns to the image of the flood — the deity's four-part question of who can cross the flood, cross the great sea, relinquish all suffering, and attain purity, answered by the Buddha with a matching four-part reply naming faith, heedfulness, diligence, and wisdom as the respective means.

Chinese Source

誰能越暴流　誰能超大海　誰能捨眾苦　誰能得清淨

Ru-Yi Translation

Who can cross the flood? Who can cross the great sea? Who can relinquish all suffering? Who can attain purity?

Chinese Source

正信越暴流　無逸超大海　精進捨眾苦　智慧得清淨

Ru-Yi Translation

Right faith crosses the flood. Heedfulness crosses the great sea. Vigor relinquishes all suffering. Wisdom attains purity.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，云何正信能越暴流？謂如有一，為欲了知諸欲過患，聽佛所說若弟子說所有正法。聞是法已，獲得正信，便生欲樂，為斷事欲及煩惱欲，遂能棄捨居家事欲，正信出家，往趣非家。既出家已，為欲斷除煩惱諸欲，遠離而住。彼由熾燃勤精進故，乃至修習正思惟故，斷煩惱諸欲，得離欲定地。如是正信為依為導，便能越度諸欲暴流。云何無逸能超大海？謂於彼定終不愛味，乃至亦無堅著安住；唯除為證諸漏盡智，專注其心。由此定心清淨、鮮白、正直、調柔，於四聖諦能入現觀，乃至證得諸漏永盡。如是由不放逸為依、為導，能斷色、無色繫二有暴流，及斷一切無明與見二種暴流，是故名為超渡大海。云何精進能捨眾苦？謂如有一，有學見迹，作是思惟：我應當證三界離欲，諸結永盡。便臻遠離，於彼勇猛精勤而住，不多安止貪欲纏心。又能如實了知現在諸欲貪纏所有出離，於貪欲蓋淨修其心，遂能斷滅諸貪欲纏，及貪欲纏為緣所生心諸憂苦。如貪欲蓋，乃至疑蓋，當知亦爾。如是精進為依、為導，能捨眾苦。云何智慧能得清淨？謂彼除滅能染污心，乃至能障究竟涅槃五種蓋已，即依未至安住未至。如先所得苦集滅道諸無漏智，於諸苦中思惟真苦，乃至於道思惟真道，便得無餘三界離欲，諸漏永盡。如是由先所得智慧為依、為導，能證清淨。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵於此頌中，略顯異生先已離欲，後於聖諦現觀清淨；及顯有學於諸聖諦現觀為先，離欲清淨。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, how does right faith cross the flood? Someone, wishing to understand the dangers of sense pleasures, listens to the true Dharma taught by the Buddha or by his disciples. Having heard this Dharma, they gain right faith and give rise to the wish to cut off desire for objects and desire as affliction. So they are able to give up the sense objects of household life and, with right faith, go forth from home into homelessness. Having gone forth, in order to cut off the desires that are afflictions, they live in seclusion. Through their blazing, diligent effort, up to their cultivation of right reflection, they cut off the desires that are afflictions and attain the stage of concentration free from desire. In this way, with right faith as support and guide, they can cross the flood of sense desire. How does heedfulness cross the great sea? They never relish that concentration, nor cling to it or rest in it; they focus their mind solely on realizing the knowledge of the exhaustion of the outflows. Through this, the concentrated mind is pure, bright, upright, and pliant, and can enter direct realization of the four noble truths, up to realizing the exhaustion of the outflows for ever. In this way, with heedfulness as support and guide, they can cut off the two floods of existence bound to the form and formless realms, and cut off all the two floods of ignorance and views; so it is called crossing the great sea. How does vigor relinquish all suffering? Someone in training who has seen the path thinks: 'I should realize freedom from desire in the three realms and the exhaustion of all fetters for ever.' So they go into seclusion and abide there with courage and diligent effort, not letting the mind rest much in the entanglement of sensual desire. They also know as it really is the escape from the entanglements of sensual desire present now, purify their mind of the hindrance of sensual desire, and so can cut off the entanglements of sensual desire and the mental sorrow and pain born from them. As with the hindrance of sensual desire, so likewise through the hindrance of doubt. In this way, with vigor as support and guide, they can relinquish all suffering. How does wisdom attain purity? Having removed the five hindrances, which defile the mind and can obstruct ultimate nirvana, they rely on the preliminary stage and abide in it. With the uncontaminated knowledges of suffering, origination, cessation, and path attained before, they reflect on true suffering within suffering, and so on up to reflecting on the true path within the path, and so attain freedom from desire in all three realms without remainder and the exhaustion of the outflows for ever. In this way, with the wisdom attained before as support and guide, they can realize purity. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows ordinary beings who first become free from desire and afterward are purified by direct realization of the noble truths; and shows those in training who first directly realize the noble truths and are then purified by freedom from desire. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the four answers as a stage of practice. "Right faith crosses the flood": someone who, through hearing the Dharma, comes to see the danger in worldly desire develops genuine confidence in the Three Jewels, goes forth from household life to sever both circumstantial entanglements (family, property, obligation) and afflictive craving itself — this severing of desire for the six sense objects is what "crossing the flood" means. "Heedlessness-free diligence crosses the great sea": having gone forth, the practitioner does not rest content with the calm of concentration, but stays vigilant, using that stability to directly realize the Four Noble Truths and exhaust the corruptions rooted in form and formless-realm attachment together with ignorance and views — this is crossing the "great sea" of saṃsāra itself. "Vigor relinquishes all suffering": a trainee who has already attained stream-entry does not stop there, but presses on to purify the mind of the five hindrances (desire, ill will, dullness, restlessness, and doubt) that still veil the innate afflictions, thereby relinquishing the sorrow those hindrances generate. "Wisdom attains purity": relying on the same unbroken concentration and the direct realization already gained, the practitioner brings the Four Truths fully to bear until every corruption is exhausted without remainder — the fruit of full liberation.

He notes the Buddha closes this first half of the topic by summarizing it as two pairings: faith and heedfulness describe how an ordinary being first leaves desire behind and then purifies through direct realization of the truths; vigor and wisdom describe how a trainee, already grounded in that direct realization, presses the same insight further until every subtler affliction is gone and arhatship is reached.

11. Topic Nine Continues: Crossing the Flood Without Drowsiness

A second set of three verses returns to the same theme with sharper focus: who crosses all four floods without a trace of dullness day or night, and who attains the deep, unimpaired state of being free of both mental grasping (攀) and mental lodging (住) — a state the treatise identifies specifically with cessation-attainment (滅盡定), the highest meditative attainment available to a living being.

Chinese Source

誰超越暴流　晝夜無惛昧　於無攀無住　甚深無減劣

Ru-Yi Translation

Who crosses beyond the flood, free of dullness day and night, abiding in that grasping-free, lodging-free state — deep, complete, and undiminished?

Chinese Source

圓滿眾尸羅　具慧善安定　內思惟繫念　能度極難度

Ru-Yi Translation

One who perfects the precepts entire, is endowed with wisdom and settled well in concentration, reflects inwardly with mindfulness fixed — such a one crosses what is hardest of all to cross.

Chinese Source

諸欲想離染　亦超色界結　彼無攀無住　甚深無減劣

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of the stain of desire's every notion, surpassing also the fetters of the form realm — such a one abides grasping-free and lodging-free, deep, complete, and undiminished.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，云何暴流？所謂四流。欲流、有流、見流、無明流。云何無攀無住？所謂諸愛永盡、離欲、寂滅、涅槃、及滅盡定。所以者何？所言攀者，諸煩惱纏；所言住者，煩惱隨眠。於彼處所二種俱無，是故說言無攀無住。此謂涅槃無攀無住。又想名攀，受名為住。若於是處二種俱無，即說彼處無攀無住。如是顯示滅想受定無攀無住。今此義中，意取滅定。云何圓滿眾尸羅？謂善安住身語律儀，修治淨命。云何具慧？謂於苦聖諦如實了知，乃至於道聖諦亦復如是。云何善安定？謂遠離諸欲，乃至具足安住第四靜慮，或第一有三摩鉢底。云何內思惟？謂於二十二處數數觀察。言我今者，容飾改常，去俗形好，廣說如經。云何繫念？謂於二十二處數觀察時，依沙門想恒作恒轉，而現在前；由此因緣，為斷餘結，修四念住。云何能度極難度？謂一切結無餘斷故，能度最極難度有頂。彼非一切愚夫異生可能度故。云何於諸欲想而得離染？謂於下分諸結已斷已知。云何超於色界諸結？謂於色繫上分諸結已斷已知。云何於無攀無住甚深中無有減劣？謂於無色界或已離欲、或未離欲，已得非想非非想處。堪能有力入滅盡定。學與無學俱容有此，故不定言超無色結。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵於此頌中，略顯能得最究竟道，及顯能證第一住道。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, what are the floods? The four floods: the flood of sense desire, the flood of existence, the flood of views, and the flood of ignorance. What is being without hold and without lodging? It is the exhaustion of all craving, freedom from desire, cessation, nirvana, and the attainment of cessation. Why? 'Hold' means the entanglements of the afflictions; 'lodging' means the latent tendencies of the afflictions. Where both are absent, it is said to be without hold and without lodging. This means nirvana is without hold and without lodging. Again, perception is called hold and feeling is called lodging. Where both are absent, that place is said to be without hold and without lodging. This shows that the attainment of the cessation of perception and feeling is without hold and without lodging. In the present meaning, the attainment of cessation is intended. What is being complete in all ethics? It is abiding well in the restraint of body and speech and cultivating pure livelihood. What is being complete in wisdom? It is knowing the noble truth of suffering as it really is, and likewise up to the noble truth of the path. What is being well concentrated? It is being free from sense desires and so on up to fully abiding in the fourth dhyāna, or in the first attainment of existence. What is inner reflection? It is examining the twenty-two points again and again: 'I have now changed my appearance and left behind lay attire and adornment', and so on, as explained at length in the sūtra. What is fixing mindfulness? It is that, when examining the twenty-two points repeatedly, the perception of being an ascetic is constantly practiced, constantly active, and present; and for this reason, to cut off the remaining fetters, one cultivates the four foundations of mindfulness. How can one cross what is extremely hard to cross? Because all fetters are cut off without remainder, one can cross the peak of existence, which is the hardest of all to cross; it cannot be crossed by foolish ordinary beings. How does one become free from defilement in perceptions of sense desire? By having cut off and fully known the lower fetters. How does one surpass the fetters of the form realm? By having cut off and fully known the higher fetters bound to form. How is one undiminished in the depth of being without hold and without lodging? It means that, whether or not one is already free from desire for the formless realm, one has attained the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception and has the capacity and strength to enter the attainment of cessation. Both those in training and those beyond training may have this, so it does not say definitely that one surpasses the formless fetters. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. In this verse the Blessed One briefly shows the path by which the most ultimate is attained, and shows the path by which the foremost abiding is realized. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "four floods" named here as desire, existence, views, and ignorance — the desire-realm's pull, the deep undertow of craving for form and formless-realm rebirth and for concentration itself, the five wrong views, and the pervasive misconception that the five aggregates and all things are truly "I" and "mine." Being "grasping-free and lodging-free" he glosses two ways: afflictions actively manifesting are "grasping," their dormant seeds are "lodging" — full nirvāṇa is free of both; or, more precisely for this passage, "grasping" is the initial mental image-taking that starts any afflictive chain and "lodging" is the feeling that follows contact and settles into craving — and the one state where neither the taking of an image nor the settling into feeling occurs at all, for a being who is still alive, is cessation-attainment (滅盡定), where the six operating consciousnesses' feeling and perception, along with the afflicted seventh consciousness, temporarily cease altogether, as if entering nirvāṇa itself.

He then works through the second verse's own list of qualities as a single ascending path: perfecting the precepts (disciplined conduct and a purified livelihood) as the first step; being endowed with wisdom, meaning genuine realization of the Four Noble Truths as they truly are; settling well in concentration, meaning mastery running from the preparatory stage up through the fourth absorption and the formless attainments; reflecting inwardly by constantly examining, through the twenty-two aspects the *Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation* sets out, one's going forth, precepts, and wholesome practice, and whether, not yet having attained the path, one is still followed by the four kinds of suffering; and fixing mindfulness by letting the very identity of "one who seeks the path" stay constantly present in the mind, which alone furnishes the strength to press on rather than settling for stream-entry's first taste of relief. Only this sustained self-examination, he stresses, is what "crosses what is hardest of all to cross" — severing the innate self-grasping that clings even to the most rarefied, barely-there state of the peak of existence (非想非非想處), a grasping so subtle that no ordinary meditator, however accomplished in worldly concentration, could ever hope to sever it unaided.

The third verse's remaining phrases mark two further milestones on the same path, he explains: freedom from desire's every notion corresponds to the non-returner who has severed the five lower fetters (self-view, precept-attachment, doubt, desire-realm craving and ill will) entire; surpassing the form realm's fetters corresponds to that same practitioner's mastery extending upward through form-realm attachment as well. Because both non-returners and arhats alike are capable of entering cessation-attainment — though only the arhat has fully left formless-realm craving behind — the verse deliberately stops short of claiming that this stage surpasses the formless realm's own fetters, leaving that distinction for arhatship alone. With this, the treatise closes its account of "who crosses the flood," summarizing this set's second and third verses as describing, respectively, the path to the ultimate goal of nirvāṇa and the path to cessation-attainment, the highest abiding available within a still-living body — and turns next to a new topic: the causes and conditions behind craving and aversion themselves.

12. Topic Ten Opens: Craving and Aversion, and the Six Feelings That Grow From Them

The tenth topic addresses a deity's question about six ordinary mental disturbances — craving, aversion, delight, sorrow, fear that raises the hair on the body, and restless mental rumination — comparing them to a child clinging to its wet-nurse, and the Buddha's answer, which traces all six back to the moisture of craving and to their own individual seeds, spreading as readily as a banyan tree and binding a being as tightly as jungle vines strangle the trees they climb.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the cause of craving and aversion? What gives rise to delight and sorrow, to hair standing on end, and to mental rumination — clinging as a child clings to its wet-nurse?

潤所生自生　如諾瞿陀樹　別縛於諸欲　猶摩迦處林
是貪恚因緣　由斯故欣慼　毛豎意尋思　如孩依乳母
知彼彼因緣　生已尋除滅　超昔未超海　暴流無後有

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of moisture, and born of themselves — like the banyan tree. Bound each to its own desire, as the vine binds the forest. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the six feelings across three settings a practitioner passes through. In household life, craving arises on meeting a pleasing person or circumstance, and aversion on meeting an unpleasing one — the ordinary condition of anyone still living amid family and possessions. Among those who have gone forth, two further feelings can appear depending on why they left home in the first place: someone who ordained out of genuine faith but without careful reflection may still feel a pang of loss recalling family and wealth once mixing again with worldly company — that pang is "sorrow" — while someone who ordained less from faith than from being unable to survive in lay life, and who finds monastic life comfortable and well-provided-for, may feel a private, complacent satisfaction at how easy the robes have made things — that is "delight," and he notes it is no less a form of attachment than sorrow is, just wearing a happier face.

For the practitioner who has gone forth from genuine faith and then withdrawn into solitary retreat to actually practice, two further feelings can still arise precisely because withdrawal removes every distraction that used to occupy the mind: at night, unfamiliar sounds — thunder amplified by open mountain air, wild animals, bandits, or non-human beings — can trigger real fear, hair standing on end, especially before a practitioner has attained enough stability to no longer be shaken by such things; and by day, the same undistracted mind can drift into vivid, absorbing rumination about attractive people or circumstances remembered or imagined, which the treatise treats as a genuine obstacle to practice — a "demonic" interference in the technical sense of anything that pulls the mind off its object, not necessarily anything supernatural. He offers, by way of illustration, the well-known story of a widower haunted nightly by what seemed to be his late wife's ghost commenting on his courtship of a new woman, until an adviser suggested he ask the apparition to count a handful of soybeans in his palm — a number he did not know himself, and so a question the "ghost" (his own projection) could never answer, at which point it stopped appearing entirely. The night's fears he ties to real outer occasions — thieves on the mountain, wild beasts, non-human beings — whereas the day's ruminations, as the story shows, are the mind's own projections, and for these the corrective is to return immediately to one's own practice — counting the breath, reciting the Buddha's name — rather than following the image wherever it leads.

13. Topic Ten Concludes: Common and Individual Causes, and How the Bond Is Finally Cut

Having named the six feelings, the treatise explains what actually produces them: a "common" cause shared by all six — the moisture of craving itself — and an "individual" cause specific to each — the particular seed each affliction carries in the mind — together with a threefold map of exactly which objects bind a person into craving, aversion, delight, sorrow, fear, and rumination. It closes by explaining how the bond is severed for good.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the "common cause" as craving's own moisture: whenever affection for oneself, for a loved object, or for future rebirth waters the mind, it can produce grasping and, through grasping, any of the six feelings — this is what all six share, regardless of who experiences them. The "individual cause" is each affliction's own seed, laid down by past instances of that very affliction manifesting and then fading back into a latent trace, ready to sprout again the moment the right condition appears — this is why one person's particular vulnerability (to fear, say, rather than delight) differs from another's, even though both share the same underlying moisture of craving.

He then works through the three kinds of binding object the treatise names, each splitting into two: circumstantial desire (事欲) binds a householder to craving or aversion depending on whether daily encounters through one's own effort are pleasant or not, and binds someone who ordained for survival to delight in present comforts, or someone who ordained on a passing impulse to sorrow over a remembered or imagined past and future; afflictive desire itself (煩惱欲) binds through simple attachment to one's own body and to the requisites that sustain it; and unexamined, still-unabandoned craving (妄分別貪) binds the solitary retreatant either to fear, when night brings unfamiliar sounds and shapes, or to rumination, when day brings attractive images the mind has not yet learned to release. All six feelings, he notes, actually reduce to just two underlying families — everything colored by delight, longing, or absorbed attachment belongs to the craving family; everything colored by aversion, sorrow, or fright belongs to the aversion family — a genuinely useful diagnostic for anyone examining their own mind in practice, in or out of retreat.

The verses' closing two lines explain how the bond ends: a practitioner — whether ordained or a learned lay brahmin — who has truly understood each of these causes, and who has additionally realized that everything arising from causes and conditions is impermanent, no longer grasps at whatever feeling arises; it is abandoned the instant it appears, its dormant seed is purged, and eventually nothing — neither manifest affliction nor latent seed — remains at all. Master Cháng Bǎi reads this threefold sequence (abandoning what has manifested, purging the seed, and finally exhausting both without remainder) as the standard pattern of the entire path of cultivation, applied here specifically to craving and aversion: by leaving behind attachment to the form and formless realms, one crosses the flood of existence itself; by leaving behind desire-realm craving, one crosses the flood of desire — and once genuinely crossed for the first time, this crossing, he stresses, never reverses. With this, the tenth topic, on craving and aversion, comes to a close, and the treatise turns to its eleventh topic: what a practitioner should actually strive to accomplish.

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, 'without hold and without lodging' means nirvana and the attainment of cessation, as explained before. The World-Honored One, referring to the time when he showed himself practicing the bodhisattva path, with all the extremely difficult austerities and the courageous effort not included in skillful means; and referring to his showing himself seated on the seat of awakening, where through full knowledge of abandonment there was no courageous effort that was not skillful means, spoke these words: 'God, know that the more I laboured in vain, the more I declined; the more I declined, the more I came to a halt; the more I came to a halt, the more I was swept away.' The bright side should be understood as the opposite of this. This shows that, when he practiced austerities, courageous effort not included in skillful means is called labor. Practicing wrong means and losing wholesome dharmas is called declining. Having realized that he had lost wholesome dharmas, ceasing the wrong means is called coming to a halt. Giving up the austerities and seeking other teachers, then following and examining the wrongly held positions of Udraka, Ārāḍa, and others, is called being swept away. Later, seated on the seat of awakening, he gave up all courageous effort not included in skillful means, and his wholesome dharmas then grew. The more his wholesome dharmas grew, the more he was never content with them and did not pause, but sought ever higher and subtler attainment in what was to be cultivated and abandoned. Because he was not content in this way, he did not seek any other non-Buddhist teacher. Without a teacher, of his own accord, he cultivated the thirty-seven factors of awakening, realized unsurpassed, perfect awakening, and is called the Greatly Awakened One. These four meanings should be understood as matching, in order, the four lines of the sūtra beginning with giving up labor. How, again, is the distinction shown with reference to nirvana as without support and without lodging? Being unable to cross the floods of the entanglements and latent tendencies of the afflictions is due, in brief, to four causes. What are the four? First, there are thoughts that depend on indulgence. With thoughts that depend on indulgence as support, there is laziness. With laziness as support, one abides in the state of an ordinary being. With abiding in the state of an ordinary being as support, the force of craving that follows the stream of birth and death sweeps one downstream in the river of birth and death of the five destinies. Through the four causes opposite to these, one can cross the floods, as should be understood accordingly. How, again, is the distinction shown with reference to the attainment of the cessation of perception and feeling? For this reason they fall away from the attainment of cessation. Having fallen away, they come to rest again in the causes of birth in a lower stage. Abiding in those causes, the mind is then set on the result to be obtained there. The four distinctions of the bright side should be understood as the opposite of this.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this exchange as the Buddha candidly naming his own pre-awakening mistake: six years living on a single grain of sesame and a single grain of wheat a day, reduced to skin and bone, following non-Buddhist teachers' methods of extreme self-mortification — genuinely strenuous effort, but effort aimed the wrong way, so it produced no result, then decline, then abandonment, then only further drifting when he sought out other non-Buddhist teachers instead. He illustrates the futility of misapplied austerity with the well-known story of the brahmin called Parrot (鸚鵡摩牢兜羅子), whose father was reborn as his own household dog after a lifetime of sincere but doctrinally confused fire-worship — the Buddha's remark to the growling dog that "you needn't bark, you barked just this way in your last life too" led, after the son tested and confirmed the dog's identity by having it locate his father's buried gold, to the son eventually understanding from the Buddha exactly why sincere effort in a mistaken framework still leads nowhere good.

Only when the Buddha gave up that "black" path — accepting the herdswoman's offering of milk, taking the middle way of neither indulgence nor self-torture — did his practice actually begin to progress, the "white" path where each stage genuinely built on the last until, sitting beneath the bodhi tree contemplating dependent origination, he attained unsurpassed awakening. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Dalai Lama has made exactly the same point about his own decades of disciplined practice still not yielding full realization — a corrective, he stresses, against the common and rather naive modern fantasy that a year or two of retreat should be enough to attain the path; genuine progress, whether the Buddha's own or anyone else's since, requires that the method itself be correct, not merely that the effort be sincere.

16. Topic Twelve Concludes: The Same Four Causes, Now Applied to Nirvāṇa and to Cessation-Attainment

The treatise then generalizes the black-and-white pattern just illustrated into two precise fourfold analyses — one explaining exactly what keeps an ordinary being from ever reaching nirvāṇa, the other explaining why a trainee who has already attained cessation-attainment can still lose it — with each "black" cause answered by its mirror-image "white" cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first fourfold analysis, on why beings fail to reach nirvāṇa at all: craving's own thought-pattern of savoring pleasant feeling is the root cause, which breeds laziness, which leaves a being simply parked in the ordinary unawakened condition, which finally surrenders the being entirely to saṃsāra's current, carried helplessly through the five realms of rebirth by the sheer momentum of craving. Each has its mirror: the thought-pattern of renunciation in place of savoring, diligence in place of laziness, genuine attainment of the noble path in place of staying parked as an ordinary being, and swimming against that same current via the eightfold noble path in place of being swept along by it — the same four-part shape, he notes, that structured the Buddha's own toil-decline-halt-adrift verse, now generalized to any being's relationship to liberation itself.

The second fourfold analysis addresses something more specific: why even a non-returner who has already tasted cessation-attainment (滅盡定) — the deepest meditative state available to a living being — can still lose it. The cause, he explains, is simple complacency: settling back into the ordinary joy-and-perception of the four absorptions rather than repeatedly returning to cessation itself, which lets the attainment decay, which leaves the practitioner once again dependent on rebirth-generating conditions belonging to the lower absorptions, which finally fixes their next rebirth in the form realm rather than carrying them onward to final nirvāṇa. Earlier, closing the first analysis, he tells the story of the "Black Ascetic" (黑氏梵志), a non-Buddhist teacher who had genuinely attained the four absorptions and five mundane superknowledges through non-Buddhist methods, was warned by Yama himself that a moment of hatred at his life's end would drop him straight into hell despite all that attainment, and who was saved only by seeking out the Buddha and being taught, through the repeated instruction to "put it down" — first the two branches he carried as an offering, then the six sense-faculties, six sense-objects, and six consciousnesses themselves — after which he went forth and, his concentration already strong, soon reached arhatship. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it is precise: mundane concentration alone, however deep, is never itself the safeguard; only genuine renunciation of self-clinging is. With the two fourfold analyses complete, the twelfth topic — on wasted toil versus toil rightly spent — closes, and the treatise turns to its thirteenth topic: the true benefit of solitary retreat.

17. Topic Thirteen: The True Benefit of Going Alone Into the Wilderness

The thirteenth topic answers a pointed question: why does a practitioner abandon wealth and companionship to live alone in the wild — is it genuine practice, or merely a calculated ploy for reputation and royal patronage? The Buddha's answer names the real benefit sought: the noble fruits of the path, and the noble powers that come only from having actually conquered the pull of beautiful form.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the benefit" as two things, the treatise specifying that its intent here is chiefly the second: attaining any of the four noble fruits (stream-entry through arhatship), which alone lets a being overthrow the "demon army" of craving for beautiful form — humanity's single hardest attachment to see through — and attaining genuine noble psychic power, built on the eight liberations and brought to full mastery through the ten kasina totalities (meditating on earth, water, fire, wind, and the four colors, then on the boundlessness of space and of consciousness, until the mind can freely transform its object at will). He is careful to distinguish this from the ordinary superknowledges any sufficiently advanced non-Buddhist meditator can also reach: noble psychic power is uncontaminated, free of any trace of pride, craving, or wrong view riding along with it, which is exactly what makes it safe in a way mundane concentration alone never is.

He then unpacks what makes the resulting peace of mind genuine rather than merely performed: a true forest-dweller has actually seen through where craving's causes lie (constant socializing and networking with the wealthy and well-placed, which is precisely what draws a person back into wanting), has seen its inevitable cost (everything gained this way is impermanent, painful, and bound to fall apart, however good it feels in the moment), knows what renouncing it means (gradually subduing, then severing, then finally uprooting even the seeds of that craving through the seven stages of mental application — from first recognizing the danger, through growing resolve, through the actual work of distancing and finding a stiller kind of joy in meditation itself, through checking one's own progress by testing whether desirable objects still stir anything at all, to the final effort that exhausts even the subtlest remaining trace), and knows the way there — the eightfold noble path itself. By contrast, he notes, both the householder still chasing wealth and the monk who prefers the comfortable company of like-minded but undisciplined companions lack every part of this insight; they cannot even recognize what draws them back into craving, let alone see its danger or find the way out — and, tellingly, such people often end up disparaging genuine solitary practitioners rather than understanding them, precisely because they have no frame of reference for what real inner peace even is. Only someone who has actually attained the eight liberations, he stresses, has the capacity to see, know, appraise, and fathom this peace directly rather than merely believing in it secondhand. With this, the thirteenth topic closes, and the treatise opens its fourteenth and final topic — itself the largest of the fourteen, spanning twenty-seven separate verses of doctrinal explanation, beginning with Buddhism's single most famous line on impermanence.

18. Topic Fourteen Opens: All Conditioned Things Arise and Cease — Their Quieting Is Bliss

The fourteenth and final topic, "discourse," gathers twenty-seven separate verses of doctrinal explanation — not one throughline but a sequence of discrete teachings. It opens with a variant of Buddhism's most celebrated line on impermanence, the same teaching later echoed in the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra's famous "all conditioned things are impermanent, this is the nature of arising and ceasing; when arising-and-ceasing itself is stilled, that stillness is bliss."

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

All conditioned things are impermanent — they are subject to arising and ceasing. Precisely because they arise and cease, their quieting is bliss.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "conditioned things" here as meaning specifically the five aggregates of clinging that make up an unawakened being's own life — traced across the three times: the past aggregates already wholly gone, the future aggregates not yet arisen but bound to arise given the fuel of craving still present, and the present aggregates already, moment by moment, in the process of dying, since nothing sustaining a life is ever more than borrowed and temporary. He stresses this holds at two scales at once — a lifetime's worth of birth and death, and equally the ceaseless moment-to-moment arising and perishing beneath the surface continuity that makes a person feel, wrongly, like one stable "self" persisting through time.

He then explains why the aggregates' final quieting counts as bliss, in two ways: first, because for an arhat every cause of suffering — the craving and views that generate it — has been exhausted down to the seed, so the present life itself is already genuinely untroubled, the mind meeting whatever arises without any of the anxious grasping an ordinary being feels toward their own body and circumstances; and second, because no further birth means no further old age, sickness, death, or any of the sufferings that inevitably attend having a body at all — so that even before death, an arhat's own account of facing mortality (as read earlier in this same fascicle) is one of genuine joy, likened to setting down a poisoned cup, precisely because nothing about the body or mind was ever truly "self" to lose in the first place. He contrasts this with an ordinary being's very different relationship to loss and impermanence — attached wherever something touches their own stake in it, indifferent wherever it doesn't — illustrating with the Dharmapada story of a young man paralyzed by grief over a fiancée's sudden death, whom the Buddha guided out of that grief simply by pointing out that clinging itself, not death, was the actual wound.

The treatise then re-examines this same four-line verse from nine further analytical angles in turn — as the basis and fruit of right view; as what is to be fully known and its cessation; as the object of full knowledge together with the knowing itself; as the defilement and purity running through past, present, and future; as dependent origination and its cessation; as the truths of suffering and cessation; as the basis for the emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness liberation-gates respectively; as what overcomes conceptual proliferation both about a self existing and about utter nonexistence being something to fear; and finally as the two forms of freedom from distortion — about what is known (impermanence, correctly seen, rather than mistaken for something permanent) and about what is realized (cessation as genuine peace, rather than mistaken for mere annihilation) that set the Buddhist teaching apart from every non-Buddhist system. Master Cháng Bǎi treats all nine restatements as converging on one point from different directions rather than adding new content, which is why, he notes, this verse on impermanence is placed first among the fourteenth topic's twenty-seven — it is the single most load-bearing teaching in the entire path.

Master Cháng Bǎi defines heedfulness through four concrete safeguards a monastic keeps regarding the four requisites (robes, food, bedding, medicine): guarding one's livelihood by acquiring these only through proper means, never through any of the four wrong forms of livelihood (farming or dispensing medicine for gain, reading omens and stars for gain, flattering the powerful for gain, or practicing divination for gain); guarding one's strength by eating neither too much nor too little, just enough to sustain the body for practice; guarding against mental defilement by catching and immediately dispelling any unwholesome train of thought in any posture; and guarding right effort by patiently enduring hardship and sustaining diligence in calm and insight without ever equating "I made no progress today" with permission to quit. He closes with the story of a monk who, discouraged after seven fruitless years of practice, was about to descend the mountain to claim treasure abandoned by merchants killed in an accident, and was turned back only by the Buddha's own timely, disguised rebuke — shame at being scolded by what he took for an improperly dressed nun restored his resolve, and returning to the breath-based six-gate method (counting, following, stilling, observing, returning, purifying) he eventually reached arhatship, illustrating that the whole difference between endless death and its ending lies simply in whether heedfulness is sustained or abandoned partway through.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi works through the verse's four stages as a single escalating chain. First, simply dwelling in memory on past pleasures — recalling a once-enjoyed sight, taste, or companionship without examining that memory at all — already sets unwholesome thought in motion. Second, some individuals carry unusually fierce craving from countless past lives' habituation, so that even sound reasoning in the present moment struggles against the sheer momentum of the seed. Third comes the habit of "seeing as fine": going out into the world without guarding the senses or maintaining mindfulness, so that whatever presents itself — a beautiful stranger, say — is taken at face value as genuinely lovely rather than examined as the impermanent, composite, and, looked at honestly, rather unglamorous thing it actually is. He illustrates this with the well-known story of a young monk so lovesick after glimpsing an attractive woman in the almsround village that he stopped eating entirely, whom the Buddha cured simply by taking him to see that same woman's corpse three days after her sudden death — bloated, discolored, already crawling with the insects that any body left unwashed for even a single day quickly attracts — after which the monk, seeing plainly that "beauty" had only ever been his own projection, needed no further argument and soon reached arhatship.

Fourth and worst is the doubling-down itself, which Cháng Bǎi identifies through five telling signs: being unable to let go even of trivial desires, let alone refined ones; acquiring wealth by improper or violent means rather than honest ones; committing real wrongdoing over even small sums, which only forecasts far worse for larger ones; having no genuine interest even in small acts of generosity or discipline, let alone sustained practice; and having no interest in even hearing about nirvāṇa, let alone seeking it. Whoever shows these signs, he notes, is a being whose craving is compounding rather than diminishing. The treatise then names three marks of the resulting "firm bondage": it is durable, hard to break because rooted in the three unwholesome roots of greed, hatred, and delusion which drive genuinely destructive action in this very life; it inevitably makes contact with suffering, since unwholesome action ripens into painful result; and it follows a being for a long time, since unwholesome karma from past lives keeps following until its result has been fully undergone. He closes by tracing this whole chain back to two root failures — failure in how a person pursues what they want (through improper or violent means) and failure in how they consume what they get (helplessly absorbed by whatever feeling, pleasant, painful, or neutral, that consumption produces, precisely the craving-and-clinging pattern this same fascicle examined at length under the "spring" and "the six feelings" earlier) — themselves driven by two underlying forces: present unwholesome thinking, and the accumulated momentum of past habituation. Both together, he stresses, are what a practitioner's mindfulness training exists to interrupt. The treatise then views the same bondage from another angle: seven kinds of defilement, all produced by craving — of recollection, of lack of freedom, of objects, of feverish torment, of association with favorable destinies, of association with unfavorable destinies, and of views.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies four distinct causes behind genuinely pure precepts, matched to the verse's own four phrases. "Abiding in the Dharma" is properly receiving ordination in the first place — going forth out of sincere confidence in the teaching, delighting in the noble eightfold path rather than in any counterfeit substitute. "Perfecting the precepts" is not then breaking what was received — keeping every rule intact, observing proper decorum, avoiding disreputable places, and treating even the smallest fault as genuinely alarming rather than trivial, since laxity toward small things is exactly what erodes the discipline needed for real concentration and insight to take root. "Possessing conscience" is feeling genuine shame at the causes that lead toward breaking precepts in the first place — noticing an unwholesome thought or a distorted perception of some tempting object the moment it arises, rather than only regretting the broken precept afterward. And "speaking only what is true" is not concealing a fault once one has actually slipped — disclosing it honestly to a wiser fellow practitioner and undertaking whatever remedy the precepts prescribe, rather than letting a small lapse fester into a larger one through silence.

He illustrates the value of the third factor with the story of two monks traveling to see the Buddha who found only insect-filled water to drink: one refused rather than break the precept against killing and died of thirst, arriving before the Buddha instantly as a celestial being reborn in the Heaven of the Thirty-Three; the other drank, arrived alive, and reported his companion's death with regret — only to be rebuked by the Buddha, who pointed out that his precept-keeping friend, now present as a god, had in fact already arrived first, while the surviving monk, despite being bodily present, had in an important sense not truly "seen" the Buddha at all. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it is not that death is preferable to drinking questionable water, but that genuine care for even a minor precept reflects something the treatise calls real self-respect — since only refraining from wrongdoing and cultivating what is wholesome actually protects a person, in a way indulging every impulse never does, however comfortable indulgence feels in the moment. He closes by noting that most practitioners will, through occasional ignorance or laziness rather than genuine corruption of intent, slip at some point — and that even here, the same principle applies: what safeguards purity is not a flawless record but the honest, prompt correction of whatever lapse occurs. With this, the treatise's exposition of pure precepts is complete, and it turns to its fifth sub-part: the actual working of karmic ripening — how the fruit of good and bad action comes due, and why so many spend their whole lives failing to see the connection.

The treatise then works through eight concrete patterns such an observer notices in the world, and reduces them to four basic relationships between an action and its fruit.

Ru-Yi Translation

Seeing another's wrongdoing, one who reflects on it with genuine, careful thought will never do the same themselves — knowing that action itself is what binds.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，云何見他惡業，審諦思惟？謂如有一，或善男子、或善女人，為性聰慧，成就如理諦觀法忍。見他現行惡行因故，便遭種種挫辱楚撻，又為王人執至王所，廣說如經，乃至斷命。見已便作如是思惟：觀觀是人，於現法中造作如是惡不善業，即於現法還受如是辛楚果報；乃至止止如是惡不善業，終不應為、終不應作、終不應行，終不應犯。即彼又見屠羊、雞、猪，廣說一切不律儀眾。不由如是作業伎能活命方術，而乘象馬車乘輦輿；又不因此能致廣大財寶庫藏，令不散失；然為世間之所訶毀。凡在傭俗，尚不以身暫相觸受，而遠避之，況餘賢哲。見已便作如是思惟，餘如前說。即彼又見他人巨富，饒大財寶；然由嬾惰多住縱逸，經過日夜，淹積歲月，所有珍財僮僕基業，及諸善法，漸漸衰退。見已便作如是思惟，餘如前說。即彼又見種種有情身相差別。或有生盲、生聾、生瘂、或瞎、或跛、或癬、或癩、或復短壽、或惡形色、或多疾病、或貧賤家、或少支屬、或弊惡慧、或扇宅迦、或半宅迦、或醜形類，餘即不爾。見已便作如是思惟：觀觀是人，先作種種惡不善業，今受如是苦惡果報；乃至止止如是惡不善業，餘如前說。即彼又見他人黠慧，無有嬾惰，具足翹勇。所謂能作營農、商賈、行船等業，及能正作言論事業。彼雖具足如是翹勇，所作事業數漸衰損，終無成辦。見已便作如是思惟，餘如前說。即彼又見二人出家，趣於非家，同修梵行。一、於衣服、飲食等利有所匱乏，一則不爾。見已便作如是思惟，餘如前說。即彼又見或有國王，或是王等，大地封疆咸皆克伏，堅著不捨。但為一身一具骸骨，唯為現在少小安樂，身語意門現行無量廣大惡行，損壞多生多身安樂，當受多生多身大苦。見已便作如是思惟：觀觀是王、或是王等甚為愚弊，唯知保愛一生一身，不知保愛多生多身，唯愛現在少時小樂，不愛當來多時大樂，亦非不愛多生重苦；乃至止止如是惡不善業，終不應為、終不應作、終不應行、終不應犯。復有或善男子、或善女人，為性聰慧，獲得天眼；用此天眼見諸有情死時、生時，如經廣說，乃至生在大那落迦中。見已便作如是思惟：觀觀是人、於現法中造作如是惡不善業，令受後法辛楚果報；乃至止止如是惡不善業。餘如前說。如是或善男子、或善女人，見他所作諸惡業已，由四種行，諦善思惟、諦善觀察。何等為四？一者、觀察或因違越，或邪活命，或放逸懈怠，於現法中造作種種惡不善業，即現法受非愛果報。二者、觀察或有有情依身差別，或有所作而不果遂，或有所求而不果遂，皆由先造惡不善業，故現法中各受如是非愛果報。三者、觀察或有國王或與王等，因現法中行諸惡業，比知當來定受種種非愛果報。四者、觀察諸有情類死時、生時，因現法中造作種種惡不善業，後法中受非愛果報。彼由如是如實知故，終不自作。云何業縛？謂樂諸業故，由業重故，於業果報不自在故。樂諸業者，謂如有一，串習惡故，愛樂諸惡。由此因緣，於諸善法心不能入。是初業縛。由業重者，謂如有一，於無間業，或有具造，或不具造。由此因緣，雖有欣樂於佛所證善說正法毘柰耶中，暫時出家尚不能得，況當能獲沙門果證。如是名為第二業縛。於業果報不自在者，謂如有一，由身語意惡行因緣，生諸惡趣。生彼處已，不得自在，不能自任長夜受苦；或生邊地，於彼絕無四賢善眾，所謂苾芻，廣說乃至鄔波斯迦。如是名為第三業縛。復次，今當略辨上所說義。謂薄伽梵於此略示，依諸有情業業果報，如理思惟；及顯如理思惟為先，法隨法行。當知是名此中略義。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, how does one, seeing others' evil deeds, reflect on them carefully? Someone, a son or daughter of good family, intelligent by nature and endowed with the acceptance of the Dharma that observes truly and appropriately, sees that others, because of the evil deeds they commit, suffer all kinds of humiliation and beatings, are seized by the king's men and taken to the king, and so on as explained at length in the sūtra, up to being put to death. Seeing this, they reflect: 'Look, look at this person: in this present life they did such evil, unwholesome deeds, and in this present life they receive such bitter results.' And so on up to: 'Stop, stop! Such evil, unwholesome deeds should never be done, never be made, never be practiced, never be committed.' They also see butchers of sheep, chickens, and pigs, and, as explained at length, all those bound to non-restraint. Through such work, skills, and means of livelihood they do not come to ride elephants, horses, carriages, or palanquins, nor do they gain vast stores of treasure that do not disperse; rather they are reviled by the world. Even common hired labourers will not let them touch them for a moment and keep away from them, let alone the wise. Seeing this, they reflect in this way; the rest is as before. They also see others who are very rich, with great treasure and wealth, but who, through laziness and much heedlessness, as days and nights pass and years accumulate, gradually lose their treasure, servants, estates, and wholesome qualities. Seeing this, they reflect in this way; the rest is as before. They also see the differences in the bodies of various beings: some born blind, born deaf, born mute; some one-eyed, lame, scabby, or leprous; some short-lived, of ugly appearance, or much afflicted by illness; some of poor and low family, with few relatives, or of bad understanding; some ṣaṇḍhakas or paṇḍakas, or of ugly form; while others are not so. Seeing this, they reflect: 'Look, look at these people: earlier they did all kinds of evil, unwholesome deeds, and now they receive such painful, bad results.' And so on up to: 'Stop, stop! Such evil, unwholesome deeds…', the rest as before. They also see others who are clever, not lazy, and full of energy, able to farm, trade, sail ships, and so on, and able to carry on the work of discussion properly. Yet although they have such energy, what they undertake gradually declines again and again and is never accomplished. Seeing this, they reflect in this way; the rest is as before. They also see two people who have gone forth from home into homelessness and practice the holy life together; one lacks gain in robes, food, and the like, and the other does not. Seeing this, they reflect in this way; the rest is as before. They also see a king, or one equal to a king, who has subdued all the lands of the great earth and clings to them firmly without letting go; for the sake of one body, one set of bones, for a little happiness in the present, through the doors of body, speech, and mind, he commits countless great evil deeds, destroys the happiness of many lives and many bodies, and will undergo great suffering in many lives and many bodies. Seeing this, they reflect: 'Look, look at this king, or this one equal to a king: how foolish and wretched he is! He knows only how to cherish one life and one body, and does not know how to cherish many lives and many bodies; he loves only a little happiness for a short time in the present and does not love great happiness for a long time in the future, nor does he shrink from heavy suffering in many lives.' And so on up to: 'Stop, stop! Such evil, unwholesome deeds should never be done, never be made, never be practiced, never be committed.' Further, a son or daughter of good family, intelligent by nature, who has gained the divine eye, sees with this divine eye beings dying and being born, as explained at length in the sūtra, up to being born in the great hells. Seeing this, they reflect: 'Look, look at this person: in this present life they did such evil, unwholesome deeds and so receive bitter results in a later life.' And so on up to: 'Stop, stop! Such evil, unwholesome deeds…', the rest as before. In this way, a son or daughter of good family, having seen the evil deeds done by others, reflects carefully and examines carefully in four ways. What are the four? First, they observe that through transgression, wrong livelihood, or heedlessness and laziness, people do various evil, unwholesome deeds in this present life and receive unwanted results in this present life. Second, they observe that differences in beings' bodies, undertakings that are not accomplished, and seeking that is not fulfilled are all due to evil, unwholesome deeds done before, and so each receives such unwanted results in this present life. Third, they observe that a king, or one equal to a king, through the evil deeds he does in this present life, will certainly receive various unwanted results in the future, as one infers. Fourth, they observe beings dying and being born, and that through the various evil, unwholesome deeds done in this present life they receive unwanted results in a later life. Because they know this as it really is, they never do such deeds themselves. What is the bondage of karma? It is delighting in karma, the weight of karma, and lack of mastery over the results of karma. Delighting in karma: someone, through habituation to evil, loves and delights in evil; for this reason their mind cannot enter into wholesome dharmas. This is the first bondage of karma. The weight of karma: someone has committed the uninterrupted deeds, in full or in part; for this reason, although they may delight in the well-taught true Dharma and Vinaya realized by the Buddha, they cannot go forth even for a while, let alone attain the fruits of the ascetic life. This is called the second bondage of karma. Lack of mastery over the results of karma: someone, because of bad conduct of body, speech, and mind, is born in bad destinies. Once born there, they have no mastery and cannot help themselves, suffering through the long night; or they are born in a border land where there are none of the four good assemblies, namely monks, and so on at length up to laywomen. This is called the third bondage of karma. Further, the meaning of what was said above will now be briefly set out. Here the Blessed One briefly shows reflecting appropriately on beings' karma and the results of karma, and shows that, with appropriate reflection first, one practices the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. It should be understood that this is the summary meaning here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes a genuinely wise observer as someone who, on seeing any of seven ordinary patterns in the world, does not simply note the fact and move on but traces the causal thread all the way back, then resolves never to repeat it: seeing someone punished, imprisoned, or executed for breaking the law; seeing butchers of sheep, chickens, and pigs, whose trade neither carries them in carriages nor secures great wealth, yet earns the world's censure; seeing someone of great wealth squander an entire fortune through sheer laziness and indulgence; seeing the vast range of bodily misfortune among otherwise equal human beings — blindness, deafness, deformity, chronic illness, short life, poverty — as the direct fruit of past unwholesome action; seeing intelligent, tireless people whose every honest venture somehow still fails, for no visible reason; seeing two monastics practicing side by side, one provided for and one constantly in want; and seeing kings and rulers who commit vast wrongdoing for the sake of one fleeting lifetime's comfort, oblivious to how much suffering across how many future lives it will cost them. An eighth pattern is available only to someone who has actually developed the divine eye through meditation: directly witnessing where beings are reborn after death, and seeing the unwholesome act and the resulting rebirth as one continuous, visible causal chain rather than an inference.

He then explains how these eight patterns reduce to four basic relationships between action and fruit: some wrongdoing ripens in this very life, as when a crime is punished immediately or a wasted fortune runs out within one lifetime; some present suffering is actually the ripening of wrongdoing from a past life, as with congenital disability, effort that inexplicably fails, or unequal monastic provision despite equally sincere practice; some wrongdoing committed now by someone currently too powerful to face consequences is nonetheless certain to ripen in a future life, as with an abusive ruler; and some present wrongdoing simply ripens as an unfavorable future rebirth, directly witnessed through the divine eye. Having genuinely understood this fourfold pattern, he stresses, the observer does not merely believe in karma abstractly but sees clearly enough that committing the same wrongdoing themselves becomes simply unthinkable — which is the verse's own point: sound reflection on cause and effect is what actually prevents wrongdoing, far more reliably than fear of punishment or mere rule-following ever could.

23. Fascicle 18 Complete: The Three Ways Karma Itself Becomes a Bond

The verse's final line — "action itself is what binds" — is unpacked into three distinct ways karma becomes a genuine obstacle to liberation, closing both the fifth sub-part and, with it, this fascicle's portion of the source text.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi names three forms this bondage takes. First, simple habituation: repeated wrongdoing breeds an actual taste for wrongdoing, the way any habit — good or bad — deepens through sheer repetition, until the mind finds it genuinely difficult to warm to wholesome practice at all. Second, weight: the five (or, in some enumerations, ten) acts of immediate retribution — killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, wounding a buddha, or causing schism in the monastic community — are so grave that a being who has committed them cannot even be ordained in this lifetime, let alone attain any fruit of the path; the door itself is closed. Third, loss of freedom in the very working-out of the result: rebirth in the lower realms leaves a being with no opportunity to practice at all — hell's suffering leaves no room for reflection, a hungry ghost's hunger leaves no room for anything else, an animal's confusion leaves no room for insight — and even a fortunate human rebirth can land in a "border region" lacking any of the fourfold assembly (monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen) capable of teaching the Dharma at all, leaving a being with no way to hear it.

He closes the sub-part, and this fascicle's stretch of the treatise, with the well-known story of King Virūḍhaka's massacre of the Buddha's own Śākya clan — the king's grudge dating to an earlier insult by Śākya monks, avenged only after he seized his father's throne; the Buddha's two attempts to turn back the invading army, and his refusal to attempt a third once he recognized the karma was beyond averting; Maudgalyāyana's well-meaning but overconfident proposal to save the clan by placing them in the sky, beneath the ocean, between two mountains, or in another world-system, and the Buddha's warning not to attempt it, since ripening karma cannot be averted once the causes and conditions are complete — a warning borne out when Maudgalyāyana, having nonetheless lifted five hundred clanspeople into the sky inside his alms bowl, opened it after the massacre to find only blood. Master Cháng Bǎi draws from the Buddha's own teaching on this occasion seven things no being can ultimately avoid — birth, aging, sickness, death, the ripening of wrongdoing, the ripening of merit, and the plain fact of causes and conditions running their course — closing with the reminder that the only genuinely wise response to another's wrongdoing is never to dwell on it with resentment, since resentment itself is simply the next unwholesome action waiting to happen, extending the very cycle the observer set out to escape.
