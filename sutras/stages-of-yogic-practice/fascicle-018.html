Fascicle 18 · 瑜伽師地論卷第十八 · The Essential-Body Verses Continue

The Superior Kind of Person Who Leads Others to Freedom

Fascicle 18 continues directly from fascicle 17's stopping point, taking up the sixth of fourteen topics in the essential-body verses: who is genuinely qualified to guide others toward liberation, what the path of release actually consists in, and where a practitioner should stand and what they should learn to never fear death again.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 18 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Topic Six Opens: Who Can Truly Guide Others to Freedom?

Continuing directly from fascicle 17, the sixth of fourteen topics opens with a deity's question about genuine spiritual leadership: who is actually qualified to guide the different classes of society toward liberation, and what does the path itself consist in? The Buddha's answer compresses the entire path — from his own long preparation through the stages of realization to final, fearless freedom — into two dense verses.

Chinese Source

誰獎勝類生，及開出離道？於何住何學，不懼後世死？

Ru-Yi Translation

Who guides the superior kinds of beings, and opens the path of release? In what should one abide, and what should one train in, so as not to fear death in a future life?

Chinese Source

戒慧自熏修，具定念正直，斷諸愁熾然，正念心解脫；能獎勝類生，及開出離道，住此於此學，不懼後世死。

Ru-Yi Translation

Precepts and wisdom, self-cultivated; possessed of concentration, mindfulness, and uprightness; having cut off sorrow's blaze; the mind liberated through right mindfulness — such a one truly guides the superior kinds of beings, and opens the path of release. Abiding in this, training in this, one no longer fears death in a future life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the Buddha's answer as tracing his own path from the very beginning: as a bodhisattva, he renounced the finest sensual pleasures and household life, kept pure bodily and verbal discipline, and reflected deeply on beings' universal suffering — cultivating the six perfections across three incalculable eons until, with no teacher needed, the mind of renunciation arose naturally and insight into the five aggregates' true nature followed. From there, resting on the four absorptions and eight attainments, he cultivated the four foundations of mindfulness and the full thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, generating the noble eightfold path that runs counter to saṃsāra's current and severing every affliction eliminated by the path of seeing — the attainment of stream-entry. Continued cultivation of the same factors then secured non-returner status, permanently free of sorrow's grief and craving's blaze; and continued cultivation once more secured arhatship, or, for one following the bodhisattva path, full buddhahood, mind liberated from both the afflictive and cognitive obstructions.

He treats this compressed account as establishing exactly why only the Buddha is qualified to answer the deity's opening question: having personally traversed every stage of the path from renunciation through complete liberation, he alone can guide beings of every social class toward the same release, and open the same path for others that he walked himself. Whoever, of any class, takes up this same threefold training — precepts, concentration, and wisdom — in genuine practice will, the verse promises, reach the same freedom from fear of future death. With the compressed answer given, the treatise turns to explaining who exactly these "superior kinds of beings" are, and what makes the Buddha uniquely their true guide.

2. The Four Classes, and Why Only the Buddha Is the True Guide

The treatise names the four classes of ancient Indian society the verse addresses, then explains precisely what genuine guidance toward liberation requires — and argues that only the Buddha, having realized every stage of the path himself, actually qualifies as both the true guide and the true teacher of the path.

Chinese Source

今此頌中，言勝類者，即是四種勝上姓類。一、婆羅門，二、剎帝利，三、吠舍，四、戍達羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

In this verse, "the superior kinds" refers to the four superior classes: first, brahmins; second, kṣatriyas; third, vaiśyas; and fourth, śūdras.

Chinese Source

以法、以正、以制、以導，教勝類生，故名為獎。此中顯示唯佛世尊能以法、以正、以制、以導，教勝類生。由此因緣，世尊自顯唯我獨為真獎導者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Teaching the superior kinds of beings through the Dharma, through right refuge, through established precepts, and through guidance — this is called "guiding." This shows that only the Blessed One is able to teach the superior kinds of beings through the Dharma, through right refuge, through established precepts, and through guidance. For this reason, the Blessed One reveals that he alone is the true guide.

Chinese Source

又唯世尊能為四種勝上類生，宣說出離一切眾苦聖八支道。此中世尊亦自顯示是真說者。

Ru-Yi Translation

And only the Blessed One is able to teach the four superior classes of beings the noble eightfold path that releases every kind of suffering. Here, the Blessed One likewise reveals himself as the true teacher.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the four classical Indian classes briefly: brahmins, the priestly and scholarly caste responsible for ritual and learning, traditionally held to wield influence exceeding even royalty; kṣatriyas, kings and warriors charged with governance and defense; vaiśyas, commoners engaged in farming, herding, and trade; and śūdras, the lowest and most impoverished class, historically bound to menial and degrading labor in service of the other three. He notes the treatise's point is not to endorse the caste hierarchy itself but simply to observe that all four classes, whatever their worldly station, are equally "superior" in one specific sense — capable, unlike beings in the lower realms, of actually learning and practicing the Dharma.

He unpacks "guiding" into four increasingly complete functions drawn from the Compendium of Establishments: expelling what should not be done, encouraging what should be done, clearly disclosing both, and — the fullest sense — dispelling every doubt a student might have, along with all remorse over past error and every affliction, gross or subtle. Only someone who has personally exhausted every doubt and every affliction in themselves can offer this fullest guidance to another, which is exactly why the treatise insists this designation belongs to the Buddha uniquely, not even to his most advanced disciples. He draws the same distinction for "teacher": the Compendium of Establishments names three roles — one who genuinely teaches from direct realization, one who transmits that teaching faithfully (Śāriputra, whom the Buddha often asked to teach in his place, is the classic example), and one who simply practices in accordance with what was taught — and only the first, belonging to the Buddha alone, counts as the "true teacher" this verse names. With guide and teacher both defined and reserved for the Buddha specifically, the treatise turns to explaining, one by one, the nine specific qualities that actually make him so.

3. Precepts, Wisdom, and Self-Cultivation: The First Three of Nine Qualities

The treatise begins its nine-part breakdown of the compressed verse with the first three qualities: the precepts kept as a bodhisattva, the wisdom that recognized suffering's universal reach, and the self-cultivation sustained across three incalculable eons that let renunciation and insight finally arise without any teacher's prompting.

Chinese Source

云何具戒？謂佛世尊昔菩薩時，棄上妙欲，捨離居家，受持身語所有律儀。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of precepts"? It means that the Blessed Buddha, when formerly a bodhisattva, gave up the finest sensual pleasures, left household life behind, and upheld all bodily and verbal discipline.

Chinese Source

云何具慧？謂即於彼受持身語律儀住者，起如是相，內正思惟，深心籌量，審諦觀察：今此世間多遭艱苦，所謂若生、若老，如經廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of wisdom"? It means that, abiding in this bodily and verbal discipline, such a mark arose: reflecting rightly within, weighing it deeply, examining it truly — recognizing that this world constantly meets with hardship, whether through birth, through aging, and so on, as the sutras explain at length.

Chinese Source

云何自熏修？謂於往昔無量餘生，經三大劫阿僧企耶，於六波羅蜜多修習、善修習；由彼因緣，今無師自然心趣出離，又於眾緣所生諸行，以微妙智能隨悟入。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "self-cultivated"? It means that, across countless former lives spanning three incalculable eons, the six perfections were cultivated, and cultivated well. By this cause, in this life, without any teacher, the mind naturally inclined toward renunciation — and, toward all conditioned things arising from many causes, subtle wisdom was able to penetrate and awaken accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first quality, precepts, as beginning with the bodhisattva's own great renunciation — leaving the finest palace pleasures and royal household behind for pure bodily and verbal conduct — the same concrete act of renunciation the treatise has held up as the model throughout this entire ground. The second quality, wisdom, he reads as the specific realization that followed directly from that same disciplined life: not abstract philosophy, but a settled recognition, arrived at through genuinely deep reflection, that birth, aging, and every stage of life bring hardship universally, to every being without exception — the same starting insight later systematized as the first noble truth.

The third quality, self-cultivation, he treats as the crucial explanation for why the Buddha needed no teacher where every other being does: three incalculable eons of sustained practice across the six perfections (generosity, precepts, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom) had already, by the time of his final life, brought the causal conditions to full ripeness — renunciation and insight into dependent origination's true nature (impermanent, suffering, empty, without self) arose naturally, the way fruit ripens and falls of its own accord once conditions are complete, needing no external prompting. Cháng Bǎi stresses this quality specifically distinguishes the Buddha's realization from a disciple's: a śrāvaka needs to hear the teaching from someone else, while the Buddha's own vast prior cultivation let the same insight arise entirely from within. With precepts, wisdom, and self-cultivation established as the foundation, the treatise turns to the next three qualities: the concentration, mindfulness, and stream-entry that follow from this foundation.

4. Concentration, Mindfulness, and Uprightness: Reaching the Stream

The fourth through sixth qualities trace the path from meditative mastery through the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment to the noble eightfold path itself — arising in direct opposition to saṃsāra's current — culminating in stream-entry: the first genuine foothold in the stream of noble ones.

Chinese Source

云何具定？謂能乃至離無所有處欲，證得非想非非想處。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of concentration"? It means being able to become free of desire even for the sphere of nothingness, and on to realizing the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

Chinese Source

云何具念？謂依如是所得勝定，為斷見斷諸煩惱故，修四念住。即以如是所修念住為其導首，乃至修習三十七種菩提分法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "possessed of mindfulness"? It means that, resting on this superior concentration attained, in order to sever the afflictions eliminated by the path of seeing, the four foundations of mindfulness are cultivated. Taking this cultivated mindfulness as the leading factor, the full thirty-seven factors of enlightenment are cultivated in turn.

Chinese Source

云何正直？謂彼生起逆流正直聖八支道，能斷見斷所有煩惱，於逆流道得預隨流。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "uprightness"? It means that the noble eightfold path arises, upright and running counter to the current of saṃsāra, able to sever every affliction eliminated by the path of seeing — and, on this path running counter to the current, one becomes established in the stream of the noble ones.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "possessed of concentration" as naming the full range of the eight meditative attainments, climbing from the first absorption through the four formless spheres to their very peak, neither-perception-nor-non-perception — the complete mastery of worldly meditation the Buddha had already achieved before his final breakthrough. "Possessed of mindfulness" then shows how that same concentration becomes the platform for actual liberating insight: the four foundations of mindfulness (observing body, feeling, mind, and mental objects) serve as the entry point for the full sequence of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment already introduced in earlier fascicles — the four right efforts, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of enlightenment, and the eightfold path itself, each building on the last until non-conceptual wisdom directly severs every affliction the path of seeing eliminates (the same 112 conceptually-produced afflictions across the three realms named in earlier fascicles).

"Uprightness," the sixth quality, he reads as the moment this cultivation actually bears fruit: the noble eightfold path arising in direct, active opposition to saṃsāra's own current — since ordinary craving and ignorance carry beings downstream into further rebirth, while the eightfold path alone runs the other way — and severing every seeing-path affliction in a single stroke secures a practitioner's first genuine, irreversible foothold in the stream of the noble ones: stream-entry itself. With concentration, mindfulness, and uprightness accounting for the path up through this first noble attainment, the treatise turns to the three qualities that carry a practitioner from stream-entry through to full and final liberation.

5. Topic Six Concludes: From Sorrow's End to Full Awakening

The final three qualities carry the path from stream-entry through non-returner status to complete liberation from both obstructions — culminating, for the bodhisattva specifically, in full buddhahood — before the treatise closes topic six with a summary confirming the Buddha alone as both true guide and true teacher.

Chinese Source

云何永斷一切愁憂熾然？謂從諦現觀俱得成不還者。又能永斷五下分結，瞋恚似順愁憂，貪欲似順熾然，於如是等皆已永斷。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "permanently cutting off all sorrow's blaze"? It means that, from direct realization of the truths, one becomes a non-returner, thereby permanently severing the five lower fetters. Aversion resembles and accords with sorrow; craving resembles and accords with blazing fire — both of these are now permanently severed.

Chinese Source

云何正念？謂為永斷上分諸結，復更修習四種念住，乃至修習三十七種菩提分法。云何心解脫？謂已永斷上分結故，於二種障心善解脫，謂煩惱障及所知障。其心如是善解脫故，得成如來應正等覺。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is "right mindfulness"? It means that, in order to permanently sever the higher fetters as well, the four foundations of mindfulness are cultivated once more, and the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment cultivated in turn. What is "mind liberated"? It means that, the higher fetters now permanently severed, the mind becomes genuinely liberated from both obstructions — the afflictive obstruction and the cognitive obstruction. Because the mind is thus genuinely liberated, one attains the state of the Tathāgata, worthy of offerings, perfectly and fully awakened.

Chinese Source

諸有四種勝類，隨一於此聖教愛樂正行，為欲證得聖八支道，於三學中勤修學者，彼定能證聖八支道及涅槃果。由證彼故，不懼當來生老病死。又復示現佛是天人無上大師。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whichever of the four superior classes delights in genuine practice within this teaching, and diligently trains in the three trainings in order to realize the noble eightfold path, will surely realize both the noble eightfold path and the fruit of nirvāṇa. Through this realization, one no longer fears future birth, aging, sickness, and death. This also reveals the Buddha as the unsurpassed teacher of gods and humans alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "sorrow's blaze" with the two afflictions among the five lower fetters that remain after stream-entry's first three are cut — aversion, whose felt quality resembles grief and sorrow, and craving, whose felt quality resembles a fire building in intensity — both fully extinguished only at non-returner status. He then distinguishes the five higher fetters targeted next: craving for the form realm, craving for the formless realm, the restlessness that arises from over-valuing meditative attainment, the pride of comparing one's own concentration favorably against others', and the subtle ignorance of still not recognizing that even the most refined absorption remains impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self — subtler afflictions than the lower five precisely because they arise from spiritual attainment itself rather than ordinary sense-craving.

Full severance of these five higher fetters, he notes, yields arhatship for a śrāvaka — but the treatise specifically frames this ninth and final quality around the bodhisattva path, where the same cultivation, carried further to include severance of the cognitive obstruction as well as the afflictive one, yields complete buddhahood; he cites the classical contrast between an arhat's limited claim to knowledge ("I will answer what I know") and the Buddha's unlimited one ("ask whatever you wish") as illustrating exactly this difference in scope. With all nine qualities named — precepts, wisdom, self-cultivation, concentration, mindfulness, uprightness, sorrow's end, right mindfulness, and mind liberated — the treatise closes topic six by confirming, once more, that only someone who has completed this entire nine-part path is qualified to be called the true guide of the four classes and the true teacher of the path that frees them, and turns to its seventh topic: what actually brings a person genuine renown, wealth, praise, and friendship.

6. Topic Seven Opens: What Actually Brings Renown, Wealth, Praise, and Friends

The seventh topic turns to entirely practical concerns — the very things most people spend their lives pursuing — and the Buddha's answer is disarmingly direct: precepts bring renown, generosity brings wealth, honesty brings praise, and giving brings genuine friendship. The treatise then illustrates the first with one of the tradition's most memorable stories about Mahākāśyapa.

Chinese Source

云何擅名譽？云何具珍財？云何獲美稱？云何攝親友？

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one come to command renown? How does one come to possess treasured wealth? How does one come to earn genuine praise? How does one come to gather true friends?

Chinese Source

持戒擅名譽，布施具珍財，諦實獲美稱，惠捨攝親友。

Ru-Yi Translation

Keeping precepts, one commands renown; giving generously, one possesses treasured wealth; speaking truthfully, one earns genuine praise; giving freely, one gathers true friends.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，或男或女，具足尸羅及賢善法，乃至命終斷除殺罪、遠離殺生，如經廣說……乃至十方所有沙門、婆羅門等，常所稱歎。由是因緣，為諸國王、群臣、長者，乃至城邑聚落人民，恭敬供養。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one, man or woman, who fully possesses moral discipline and the qualities of a genuinely good person — abandoning the fault of killing, keeping far from taking life, until the very end of their days, as the sutras explain at length... down to being constantly praised by ascetics and brahmins in every direction. For this reason, they receive the reverent support of kings, ministers, elders, and the people of every town and village alike.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the qualities of a "genuinely good person" as more than mere rule-following: born to either a noble or prosperous family and gone forth with genuine faith, such a person carries themselves with a naturally composed and dignified bearing, has genuinely studied the teaching, speaks skillfully and completely, and — however small the wisdom, insight, or peace so far attained — neither grows arrogant about it nor looks down on others still further behind, recognizing that only actually practicing the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma is genuinely worthwhile. This settled humility, sustained across the full ten wholesome courses of action rather than the precept against killing alone, is what the treatise means by "possessing precepts" in its fullest sense.

He illustrates renown's actual mechanism with the well-known story of Mahākāśyapa, famed above all disciples for the purity of his conduct: children who saw the Buddha and his monks pass by would offer only a passing greeting, still clutching their sweets — but the moment they spotted Mahākāśyapa among the group, they ran to offer him their treats directly. When Mahākāśyapa redirected them to make their offering to the Buddha and the wider community instead, the other monks asked the Buddha why the children had singled him out. The Buddha's answer was simple: Mahākāśyapa's precepts are so purely kept that anyone who merely sees him, monastic or lay, spontaneously feels joy. Cháng Bǎi treats the story as proof that renown built on genuine virtue requires no self-promotion at all — it simply radiates outward on its own, exactly as the tradition's saying has it, that precepts themselves carry their own fragrance. With renown's source explained, the treatise turns to the second question: what actually produces lasting wealth.

7. Topic Seven Concludes: Wealth, Praise, and Friendship's Real Sources

The remaining three questions close out with matching directness: wealth traces back to generosity practiced in past lives, praise to honest livelihood untainted by deception, and genuine friendship to present generosity that makes others glad to help in return. A closing summary sorts all four causes into three underlying forces.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，昔餘生中作及增長施福業事；由此因緣，今生巨富大財寶家，乃至眾多府庫盈積。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who, in some former life, performed and repeatedly cultivated meritorious acts of giving; by this cause, in this life, one is born into a family of vast wealth, its storehouses filled to overflowing.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，不以假偽斗稱函等，諂誑陵懱妄言等事，而致財寶；但以如法作業技能，依法不暴，而致財寶。彼既如是，眾咸唱言：賢哉儒士！乃能如法作業技能引致財寶。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who does not resort to false measures, deceptive documents, flattery, contempt, or falsehood to acquire wealth, but earns it only through lawful work and genuine skill, honestly and without violence. Such a person, everyone declares: what a worthy soul! — able to acquire wealth entirely through lawful labor and skill.

Chinese Source

謂如有一，現前多有種種家產，遠離慳垢，不吝資具，以正安樂而自歡娛，乃至友朋、親戚、耆長。彼諸人等便相佐助，引致財寶，守護滋息。

Ru-Yi Translation

It refers to one who, possessing considerable family wealth, remains free of the stain of stinginess, is never grudging with their resources, and takes wholesome joy in sharing it — with friends, relatives, and elders alike. Such people, in turn, gladly help them, bringing further wealth their way, protecting it, and making it grow.

Chinese Source

謂略顯示恭敬、利養二種因緣。持戒擅名譽者，顯恭敬因緣；所餘諸句，顯利養因緣。謂因力故，士用力故，助伴力故。

Ru-Yi Translation

This briefly reveals two kinds of cause: the cause of respect, and the cause of material gain. "Keeping precepts, one commands renown" reveals the cause of respect; the remaining lines reveal the causes of material gain — the power of karmic cause, the power of human effort, and the power of supportive companions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads wealth's cause as purely a matter of seeds already planted: generosity practiced and, crucially, sustained with genuine gladness (rather than followed by regret, which the treatise elsewhere notes actively diminishes the resulting merit) in a past life ripens as wealth in this one — a straightforward case of the "power of karmic cause." Praise's cause, by contrast, operates entirely within this present life: honest livelihood, free of false measures or deceptive dealing, earns spontaneous public respect through nothing more than consistent integrity, illustrating the "power of human effort." Friendship's cause he reads as a virtuous cycle: present-life generosity, freely shared with genuine joy rather than grudging obligation, naturally draws capable people to a person's side, and their combined support then compounds the very wealth that started the cycle — the "power of supportive companions."

He treats the three-force summary as the topic's real teaching: renown rests on respect (which precepts alone secure), while wealth, praise, and friendship all trace back to material gain through one of three distinct mechanisms — past karmic seed, present honest effort, or present generous cultivation of allies — a tidy causal map any practitioner can use to diagnose exactly which of these four goods they may be neglecting. With this topic complete, the treatise turns to its eighth: a spring and its water, a vivid extended metaphor for the six senses and the craving they continuously generate.

8. Topic Eight Opens: The Spring That Never Stops Flowing

The eighth topic poses three linked riddles through a single sustained image: when does the spring stop flowing, when does the path close, and where is all worldly suffering and pleasure extinguished without remainder? The Buddha's answer names a single place — the complete cessation of the six senses and the five aggregates — before the treatise unpacks exactly what the spring itself represents.

Chinese Source

齊何泉止息？於何徑不通？世間諸苦樂，何處無餘滅？

Ru-Yi Translation

At what point does the spring cease flowing? At what point does the path no longer run through? Where is the world's suffering and pleasure alike extinguished, without remainder?

Chinese Source

若於是處所，眼耳及與鼻，舌身意名色，永滅盡無餘；齊此泉止息，於斯徑不通，世間諸苦樂，是處無餘滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wherever eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind — together with mental and physical existence itself — are permanently and completely extinguished without remainder: precisely there does the spring cease flowing, precisely there does the path no longer run through, and precisely there is the world's suffering and pleasure alike extinguished without remainder.

Chinese Source

云何為泉？謂六觸處。何以故？譬如泉池能生諸水，水所繫屬，堪任觸用；又能存養男女大小，下及禽獸，乃至一切未盡枯竭。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the spring? It refers to the six fields of contact. Why is this so? Just as a spring or pool constantly generates water, water belonging to and drawn from it, fit for use and contact — sustaining men and women, young and old, and even animals — so long as it has not yet run dry.

Chinese Source

六內觸處亦復如是。一切愚夫六境界觸之所觸用；又能存養，乃至是中諸貪愛水未盡枯竭。

Ru-Yi Translation

The six internal fields of contact are just the same: every foolish, unlearned being makes use of contact with the six kinds of objects, and is sustained by it, so long as the water of craving within has not yet run dry.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the deity's three questions as three angles on a single destination: the spring's flowing, the path's passage, and the presence of worldly suffering and pleasure are simply three names for saṃsāra's continued operation, and the Buddha's answer names exactly one place where all three stop at once — the complete, remainderless cessation of the six senses and the aggregates of body and mind they depend on, which is to say, nirvāṇa itself.

He unpacks the spring metaphor with real precision: just as an actual spring continuously generates water that sustains every kind of creature for as long as it flows, the six sense-fields (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind) continuously generate contact with their objects, and that contact — for an ordinary, unenlightened being — continuously generates craving, the very "water" that sustains the ongoing life of affliction and further rebirth. The metaphor's power, he notes, lies in its passivity: a spring does not choose to flow, and an untrained sense faculty does not choose to generate craving upon contact — it simply does, automatically, moment after moment, for as long as ignorance keeps the source unblocked. Only when contact itself, and the aggregates that make contact possible, cease entirely does the spring finally run dry — precisely the state the deity's questions were pointing toward from the start. With the central image established, the treatise turns to unpacking the deity's second question: what exactly the "path" refers to.

9. Topic Eight Concludes: A Graduated Map From Ordinary Being to Final Nirvāṇa

Having explained the spring, the treatise defines the "path" as affliction and karma's own two-part road toward suffering, then closes topic eight with an unusually precise graduated map: exactly how much of the spring, the path, and suffering-and-pleasure remains present in an ordinary being, a trainee, an arhat still living, and an arhat who has passed into final nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

云何為徑？徑有二種。一、煩惱徑，二者、業徑。此中徑者，意明因義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the path? The path has two kinds: first, the path of affliction; and second, the path of karma. Here, "path" means precisely "cause."

Chinese Source

若諸異生，泉徑苦樂一切無闕，亦未有捨。若諸有學，闕而未捨。若諸無學，徑及當來所有苦樂亦闕亦捨，不復現行；泉及現法所有苦樂亦闕亦捨，有餘依故，猶復現行。是故無餘涅槃界中，說彼一切無餘盡滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

In ordinary beings, the spring, the path, and suffering-and-pleasure are all entirely present, without deficiency, and not yet abandoned. In trainees, they are deficient but not yet abandoned. In those beyond training, the path and all future suffering-and-pleasure are both deficient and abandoned, no longer manifesting; but the spring and the suffering-and-pleasure of the present life are deficient yet still manifest, since a remainder of past conditions still remains. Therefore, it is said that within the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder, all of this is extinguished without exception, leaving nothing behind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the "path" as simply naming causation itself in its two forms — affliction, the mental cause, and karma, the resulting action — together forming the road that carries a being from present affliction to future suffering. He then treats the fourfold graduated map as this topic's real payoff, tracking precisely how much of the spring (sense-contact generating craving), the path (affliction and karma), and suffering-and-pleasure remains at each stage: an ordinary being has all three fully intact, bound by unbroken affliction with nothing yet reduced; a trainee (any of the six graded stages from stream-enterer through the approach to arhatship) has begun reducing affliction's grip but has not yet abandoned it entirely, since the subtler afflictions eliminated only by the path of cultivation still occasionally manifest.

The most striking distinction, he notes, comes at the level of the arhat: one who has attained arhatship while still alive has genuinely severed the *path* — no further karma is being generated, and no future suffering-and-pleasure will ever arise again — yet the *spring* itself, ordinary sense-contact with a body still shaped by past karma, continues operating, since a residue of prior conditions (the physical aggregates themselves) remains until death. Crucially, he stresses, this continued sense-contact produces only bodily feeling, never mental affliction — an arhat eating, walking, or encountering pleasant and unpleasant circumstances experiences physical sensation exactly as anyone would, but without the reflexive craving, aversion, or confusion that would turn that sensation into fresh suffering for an unawakened mind. Only at the arhat's death, entering nirvāṇa without any remainder at all, does even the spring itself finally run dry — the single point where spring, path, and suffering-and-pleasure alike are extinguished without exception. With this precise map complete, topic eight closes, and the treatise turns to its ninth topic, addressing floods once more from a different angle.

10. Topic Nine Opens: Who Crosses the Flood?

The ninth topic returns to the image of the flood — the deity's four-part question of who can cross the flood, cross the great sea, relinquish all suffering, and attain purity, answered by the Buddha with a matching four-part reply naming faith, heedlessness, diligence, and wisdom as the respective means.

Chinese Source

誰能越暴流　誰能超大海　誰能捨眾苦　誰能得清淨

Ru-Yi Translation

Who can cross the flood? Who can cross the great sea? Who can relinquish all suffering? Who can attain purity?

Chinese Source

正信越暴流　無逸超大海　精進捨眾苦　智慧得清淨

Ru-Yi Translation

Right faith crosses the flood. Heedlessness-free diligence crosses the great sea. Vigor relinquishes all suffering. Wisdom attains purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through each of the four answers as a stage of practice. "Right faith crosses the flood": someone who, through hearing the Dharma, comes to see the danger in worldly desire develops genuine confidence in the Three Jewels, goes forth from household life to sever both circumstantial entanglements (family, property, obligation) and afflictive craving itself — this severing of desire for the six sense objects is what "crossing the flood" means. "Heedlessness-free diligence crosses the great sea": having gone forth, the practitioner does not rest content with the calm of concentration, but stays vigilant, using that stability to directly realize the Four Noble Truths and exhaust the corruptions rooted in form and formless-realm attachment together with ignorance and views — this is crossing the "great sea" of saṃsāra itself. "Vigor relinquishes all suffering": a trainee who has already attained stream-entry does not stop there, but presses on to purify the mind of the five hindrances (desire, ill will, dullness, restlessness, and doubt) that still veil the innate afflictions, thereby relinquishing the sorrow those hindrances generate. "Wisdom attains purity": relying on the same unbroken concentration and the direct realization already gained, the practitioner brings the Four Truths fully to bear until every corruption is exhausted without remainder — the fruit of full liberation.

He notes the Buddha closes this first half of the topic by summarizing it as two pairings: faith and heedlessness-free diligence describe how an ordinary being first leaves desire behind and then purifies through direct realization of the truths; vigor and wisdom describe how a trainee, already grounded in that direct realization, presses the same insight further until every subtler affliction is gone and arhatship is reached.

11. Topic Nine Continues: Crossing the Flood Without Drowsiness

A second set of three verses returns to the same theme with sharper focus: who crosses all four floods without a trace of dullness day or night, and who attains the deep, unimpaired state of being free of both mental grasping (攀) and mental lodging (住) — a state the treatise identifies specifically with cessation-attainment (滅盡定), the highest meditative attainment available to a living being.

Chinese Source

誰超越暴流　晝夜無惛昧　於無攀無住　甚深無減劣

Ru-Yi Translation

Who crosses beyond the flood, free of dullness day and night, abiding in that grasping-free, lodging-free state — deep, complete, and undiminished?

Chinese Source

圓滿眾尸羅　具慧善安定　內思惟繫念　能度極難度

Ru-Yi Translation

One who perfects the precepts entire, is endowed with wisdom and settled well in concentration, reflects inwardly with mindfulness fixed — such a one crosses what is hardest of all to cross.

Chinese Source

諸欲想離染　亦超色界結　彼無攀無住　甚深無減劣

Ru-Yi Translation

Free of the stain of desire's every notion, surpassing also the fetters of the form realm — such a one abides grasping-free and lodging-free, deep, complete, and undiminished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "four floods" named here as desire, existence, views, and ignorance — the desire-realm's pull, the deep undertow of craving for form and formless-realm rebirth and for concentration itself, the five wrong views, and the pervasive misconception that the five aggregates and all things are truly "I" and "mine." Being "grasping-free and lodging-free" he glosses two ways: afflictions actively manifesting are "grasping," their dormant seeds are "lodging" — full nirvāṇa is free of both; or, more precisely for this passage, "grasping" is the initial mental image-taking that starts any afflictive chain and "lodging" is the feeling that follows contact and settles into craving — and the one state where neither the taking of an image nor the settling into feeling occurs at all, for a being who is still alive, is cessation-attainment (滅盡定), where the six operating consciousnesses' feeling and perception, along with the afflicted seventh consciousness, temporarily cease altogether, as if entering nirvāṇa itself.

He then works through the second verse's own list of qualities as a single ascending path: perfecting the precepts (disciplined conduct and a purified livelihood) as the first step; being endowed with wisdom, meaning genuine realization of the Four Noble Truths as they truly are; settling well in concentration, meaning mastery running from the preparatory stage up through the fourth absorption and the formless attainments; reflecting inwardly by constantly examining, through the twenty-two aspects the *Ground of What Is Attained Through Cultivation* sets out, whether one's going forth, precepts, wholesome practice, and endurance of the four kinds of suffering are genuinely being lived out; and fixing mindfulness by letting the very identity of "one who seeks the path" stay constantly present in the mind, which alone furnishes the strength to press on rather than settling for stream-entry's first taste of relief. Only this sustained self-examination, he stresses, is what "crosses what is hardest of all to cross" — severing the innate self-grasping that clings even to the most rarefied, barely-there state of the peak of existence (非想非非想處), a grasping so subtle that no ordinary meditator, however accomplished in worldly concentration, could ever hope to sever it unaided.

The third verse's remaining phrases mark two further milestones on the same path, he explains: freedom from desire's every notion corresponds to the non-returner who has severed the five lower fetters (self-view, precept-attachment, doubt, desire-realm craving and ill will) entire; surpassing the form realm's fetters corresponds to that same practitioner's mastery extending upward through form-realm attachment as well. Because both non-returners and arhats alike are capable of entering cessation-attainment — though only the arhat has fully left formless-realm craving behind — the verse deliberately stops short of claiming that this stage surpasses the formless realm's own fetters, leaving that distinction for arhatship alone. With this, the treatise closes its account of "who crosses the flood," summarizing the two verse-sets as describing, respectively, the path to the ultimate goal of nirvāṇa and the path to cessation-attainment, the highest abiding available within a still-living body — and turns next to a new topic: the causes and conditions behind craving and aversion themselves.

12. Topic Ten Opens: Craving and Aversion, and the Six Feelings That Grow From Them

The tenth topic addresses a deity's question about six ordinary mental disturbances — craving, aversion, delight, sorrow, fear that raises the hair on the body, and restless mental rumination — comparing them to a child clinging to its wet-nurse, and the Buddha's answer, which traces all six back to the moisture of craving and to their own individual seeds, spreading as readily as a banyan tree and binding a being as tightly as jungle vines strangle the trees they climb.

Chinese Source

貪恚何因緣　由何故欣慼　毛豎意尋思　如孩依乳母

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the cause of craving and aversion? What gives rise to delight and sorrow, to hair standing on end, and to mental rumination — clinging as a child clings to its wet-nurse?

Chinese Source

潤所生自生　如諾瞿陀樹　別縛於諸欲　猶摩迦處林
是貪恚因緣　由斯故欣慼　毛豎意尋思　如孩依乳母
知彼彼因緣　生已尋除滅　超昔未超海　暴流無後有

Ru-Yi Translation

Born of moisture, and born of themselves — like the banyan tree. Bound each to its own desire, as the vine binds the forest. These are the causes of craving and aversion, and from them come delight and sorrow, hair standing on end, and mental rumination — as a child clings to its wet-nurse. Knowing each cause as it truly is, one seeks it out the moment it arises and extinguishes it. What was never crossed before is crossed at last — the flood, never to return.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the six feelings across three settings a practitioner passes through. In household life, craving arises on meeting a pleasing person or circumstance, and aversion on meeting an unpleasing one — the ordinary condition of anyone still living amid family and possessions. Among those who have gone forth, two further feelings can appear depending on why they left home in the first place: someone who ordained out of genuine faith but without careful reflection may still feel a pang of loss recalling family and wealth once mixing again with worldly company — that pang is "sorrow" — while someone who ordained less from faith than from being unable to survive in lay life, and who finds monastic life comfortable and well-provided-for, may feel a private, complacent satisfaction at how easy the robes have made things — that is "delight," and he notes it is no less a form of attachment than sorrow is, just wearing a happier face.

For the practitioner who has gone forth from genuine faith and then withdrawn into solitary retreat to actually practice, two further feelings can still arise precisely because withdrawal removes every distraction that used to occupy the mind: at night, unfamiliar sounds — thunder amplified by open mountain air, wild animals, bandits, or what feels like a non-human presence — can trigger real fear, hair standing on end, especially before a practitioner has attained enough stability to no longer be shaken by such things; and by day, the same undistracted mind can drift into vivid, absorbing rumination about attractive people or circumstances remembered or imagined, which the treatise treats as a genuine obstacle to practice — a "demonic" interference in the technical sense of anything that pulls the mind off its object, not necessarily anything supernatural. He offers, by way of illustration, the well-known story of a widower haunted nightly by what seemed to be his late wife's ghost commenting on his courtship of a new woman, until an adviser suggested he ask the apparition to count a handful of soybeans in his palm — a number he did not know himself, and so a question the "ghost" (his own projection) could never answer, at which point it stopped appearing entirely. The lesson he draws is that both night-fears and day-ruminations in solitary retreat are typically the mind's own unexamined projections rather than anything external, and the corrective is always the same: return immediately to the primary object of meditation — breath, recitation, whatever it is — rather than following the image wherever it leads.

13. Topic Ten Concludes: Common and Individual Causes, and How the Bond Is Finally Cut

Having named the six feelings, the treatise explains what actually produces them: a "common" cause shared by all six — the moisture of craving itself — and an "individual" cause specific to each — the particular seed each affliction carries in the mind — together with a threefold map of exactly which objects bind a person into craving, aversion, delight, sorrow, fear, and rumination. It closes by explaining how the bond is severed for good.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the "common cause" as craving's own moisture: whenever affection for oneself, for a loved object, or for future rebirth waters the mind, it can produce grasping and, through grasping, any of the six feelings — this is what all six share, regardless of who experiences them. The "individual cause" is each affliction's own seed, laid down by past instances of that very affliction manifesting and then fading back into a latent trace, ready to sprout again the moment the right condition appears — this is why one person's particular vulnerability (to fear, say, rather than delight) differs from another's, even though both share the same underlying moisture of craving.

He then works through the three kinds of binding object the treatise names, each splitting into two: circumstantial desire (事欲) binds a householder to craving or aversion depending on whether daily encounters through one's own effort are pleasant or not, and binds someone who ordained for survival to delight in present comforts, or someone who ordained on a passing impulse to sorrow over a remembered or imagined past and future; afflictive desire itself (煩惱欲) binds through simple attachment to one's own body and to the requisites that sustain it; and unexamined, still-unabandoned craving (妄分別貪) binds the solitary retreatant either to fear, when night brings unfamiliar sounds and shapes, or to rumination, when day brings attractive images the mind has not yet learned to release. All six feelings, he notes, actually reduce to just two underlying families — everything colored by delight, longing, or absorbed attachment belongs to the craving family; everything colored by aversion, sorrow, or fright belongs to the aversion family — a genuinely useful diagnostic for anyone examining their own mind in practice, in or out of retreat.

The verses' closing two lines explain how the bond ends: a practitioner — whether ordained or a learned lay brahmin — who has truly understood each of these causes, and who has additionally realized that everything arising from causes and conditions is impermanent, no longer grasps at whatever feeling arises; it is abandoned the instant it appears, its dormant seed is purged, and eventually nothing — neither manifest affliction nor latent seed — remains at all. Master Cháng Bǎi reads this threefold sequence (abandoning what has manifested, purging the seed, and finally exhausting both without remainder) as the standard pattern of the entire path of cultivation, applied here specifically to craving and aversion: by leaving behind attachment to the form and formless realms, one crosses the flood of existence itself; by leaving behind desire-realm craving, one crosses the flood of desire — and once genuinely crossed for the first time, this crossing, he stresses, never reverses. With this, the tenth topic — the fourteen-topic verse cycle's own halfway marker on craving — comes to a close, and the treatise turns to its eleventh topic: what a practitioner should actually strive to accomplish.

14. Topic Eleven Opens: Who Is the True Brahmin?

The eleventh topic stages a disagreement between a deity's Brahmanical assumptions and the Buddha's own redefinition of what a "brahmin" really is — not a matter of birth or of aspiring to rebirth in the Brahmā heavens, but a matter of having genuinely finished the task of liberation.

Chinese Source

應作婆羅門　謂斷無縱逸　求棄捨諸欲　不希望此有

Ru-Yi Translation

One should become a brahmin: cutting off all indulgence, seeking to relinquish every desire, hoping for no further existence here.

Chinese Source

若更有所作　非真婆羅門　當知婆羅門　於所作已辦
諸身分劬勞　未極底未度　已得度住陸　無勤到彼岸
天汝今當知　此喻真梵志　謂永盡諸漏　得常委靜慮
彼永斷一切　愁憂及熾然　恆住於正念　亦常心解脫

Ru-Yi Translation

One who still has more left to do is no true brahmin. Know that the brahmin is one whose task is already accomplished. Toiling still with every limb, not yet at the bottom, not yet across — such is the one who has crossed and stands now on dry land, needing no further effort to reach the far shore. Deity, know now: this is the true likeness of the genuine seeker of purity — one who has exhausted all corruptions forever, or who abides in constant, thorough diligence in meditation. Such a one has forever cut off all sorrow and burning, abides always in right mindfulness, and is forever liberated in mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first sets out what the deity actually believes: for the Brahmanical tradition, a "brahmin" is simply someone born into the priestly caste, and the highest aim available is rebirth in the Brahmā heavens of the first absorption — reached by renouncing desire, dwelling in seclusion, sleeping little, and cultivating stillness. He notes this is by definition still a return ticket into saṃsāra, however lofty the destination, since the aspirant explicitly still hopes for "existence" — just a more refined one. The Buddha's reply redefines the term entirely around actual attainment rather than birth or aspiration: one who still has more work left to do, however exalted the goal, is not yet a true brahmin; only one whose task is genuinely finished — for whom nothing further remains to be accomplished — deserves the name.

He unpacks the swimming image at length: the trainee still bound by desire-realm craving (stream-enterer and once-returner) is like a swimmer laboring with both feet and hands — the feet standing for effort toward calm-abiding (having only reached the preparatory stage of meditation, lacking full concentration), the hands for effort toward insight (having realized non-self in principle, but not yet uprooted the innate, wordless grip of self-clinging) — someone genuinely working, stroke by stroke, toward the "bottom" of stilled water and the far "shore" of liberation, neither yet reached. The trainee already free of desire-realm craving (the non-returner) has, by contrast, already touched that bottom — settled, clear water beneath — and no longer needs the strenuous limb-by-limb effort of the earlier stages, working instead simply and continuously to sever the five remaining higher fetters. And the arhat has fully crossed: standing on dry land, no further exertion required, because the work itself is entirely done. He closes by naming three grades folded into the phrase "true brahmin" this way — the trainee still in the water, the trainee already at the bottom, and the one already standing on the far shore — with the last two lines' description of "ceasing all sorrow and burning" and "abiding always in mindfulness, forever liberated" applying respectively to the non-returner's exhaustion of the desire realm's own craving and aversion, and to the arhat's complete and final freedom.

15. Topic Twelve Opens: The Buddha's Own Wasted Toil, and the Path That Finally Worked

The twelfth topic turns unusually personal: a deity questions the Buddha directly about his own austerities, and the Buddha answers with his own history — six years of self-mortification that led nowhere, followed by the middle way that led to full awakening — offered as a "black" and "white" pairing illustrating wasted effort against genuine progress.

Chinese Source

苾芻！苾芻！已度暴流耶？告言：如是，天！
無攀無住，已度暴流耶？告言：如是，天！
苾芻！汝今猶如何等無攀無住，已度暴流？

Ru-Yi Translation

"Monk! Monk! Have you crossed the flood?" "I have, deity." "Grasping-free and lodging-free — have you crossed the flood?" "I have, deity." "Monk, in what manner now are you grasping-free and lodging-free, having crossed the flood?"

Chinese Source

如如我劬勞　如是如是劣　如如我劣已　如是如是住
如如我住已　如是如是漂
天我如如捨劬勞　如是如是無減劣
如是廣說鮮白品　此中祇焰頌應知

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as I once toiled, so did I decline; just as I declined, so did I stop; just as I stopped, so was I again swept adrift. Deity, just as I then relinquished that toil, so did I cease declining at all — the bright, pure path that follows is fully told in the verses of restatement that accompany this teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this exchange as the Buddha candidly naming his own pre-awakening mistake: six years living on a single grain of sesame and a single grain of wheat a day, reduced to skin and bone, following non-Buddhist teachers' methods of extreme self-mortification — genuinely strenuous effort, but effort aimed the wrong way, so it produced no result, then decline, then abandonment, then only further drifting when he sought out other non-Buddhist teachers instead. He illustrates the futility of misapplied austerity with the well-known story of the brahmin Māyūra whose father was reborn as his own household dog after a lifetime of sincere but doctrinally confused fire-worship — the Buddha's remark to the growling dog that "you needn't bark, you barked just this way in your last life too" led, after the son tested and confirmed the dog's identity by having it locate his father's buried gold, to the son eventually understanding from the Buddha exactly why sincere effort in a mistaken framework still leads nowhere good.

Only when the Buddha gave up that "black" path — accepting the herdswoman's offering of milk, taking the middle way of neither indulgence nor self-torture — did his practice actually begin to progress, the "white" path where each stage genuinely built on the last until, sitting beneath the bodhi tree contemplating dependent origination, he attained unsurpassed awakening. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Dalai Lama has made exactly the same point about his own decades of disciplined practice still not yielding full realization — a corrective, he stresses, against the common and rather naive modern fantasy that a year or two of retreat should be enough to attain the path; genuine progress, whether the Buddha's own or anyone else's since, requires that the method itself be correct, not merely that the effort be sincere.

16. Topic Twelve Concludes: The Same Four Causes, Now Applied to Nirvāṇa and to Cessation-Attainment

The treatise then generalizes the black-and-white pattern just illustrated into two precise fourfold analyses — one explaining exactly what keeps an ordinary being from ever reaching nirvāṇa, the other explaining why a trainee who has already attained cessation-attainment can still lose it — with each "black" cause answered by its mirror-image "white" cause.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first fourfold analysis, on why beings fail to reach nirvāṇa at all: craving's own thought-pattern of savoring pleasant feeling is the root cause, which breeds laziness, which leaves a being simply parked in the ordinary unawakened condition, which finally surrenders the being entirely to saṃsāra's current, carried helplessly through the five realms of rebirth by the sheer momentum of craving. Each has its mirror: the thought-pattern of renunciation in place of savoring, diligence in place of laziness, genuine attainment of the noble path in place of staying parked as an ordinary being, and swimming against that same current via the eightfold noble path in place of being swept along by it — the same four-part shape, he notes, that structured the Buddha's own toil-decline-halt-adrift verse, now generalized to any being's relationship to liberation itself.

The second fourfold analysis addresses something more specific: why even a non-returner or arhat who has already tasted cessation-attainment (滅盡定) — the deepest meditative state available to a living being — can still lose it. The cause, he explains, is simple complacency: settling back into the ordinary joy-and-perception of the four absorptions rather than repeatedly returning to cessation itself, which lets the attainment decay, which leaves the practitioner once again dependent on rebirth-generating conditions belonging to the lower absorptions, which finally fixes their next rebirth in the form realm rather than carrying them onward to final nirvāṇa. He illustrates the stakes with the story of the "Black Ascetic" (黑氏梵志), a non-Buddhist teacher who had genuinely attained the four absorptions and five mundane superknowledges through non-Buddhist methods, was warned by Yama himself that a moment of hatred at his life's end would drop him straight into hell despite all that attainment, and who was saved only by seeking out the Buddha and being taught, through the repeated instruction to "put it down" — first the two branches he carried as an offering, then the six sense-faculties, six sense-objects, and six consciousnesses themselves — a release his existing concentration made him ready to receive almost instantly, reaching arhatship on the spot. Master Cháng Bǎi's point in citing it here is precise: mundane concentration alone, however deep, is never itself the safeguard; only genuine renunciation of self-clinging is. With the two fourfold analyses complete, the twelfth topic — on wasted toil versus toil rightly spent — closes, and the treatise turns to its thirteenth topic: the true benefit of solitary retreat.
