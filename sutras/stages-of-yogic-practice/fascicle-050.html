Fascicle 50 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十 · The Chapter on Establishment, Continued

Seven Angles on the Ten Powers

This fascicle opens with the second half of the chapter on establishment, first offering a general synthesis of the ten powers just explained in fascicle 49 — their essential nature, their threefold differentiation, why they belong to the Buddha alone, how all buddhas equally possess them yet each dwells predominantly in different ones, their function, their sequence, and the distinctions between each power and its neighbor — before continuing into the remaining exclusive buddha-qualities: the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, the three things requiring no guarding, great compassion, and beyond.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 50 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle 50 Opens — The Ten Powers Synthesized Through Seven Angles

Fascicle 50 opens with a note on its own place within the treatise: this is the second part of the fifth chapter, the chapter on establishment, within the third yoga foundation of ultimate practice. Based on both the abiding and the ultimate ground of the Tathāgata, the buddhas, blessed ones, possess one hundred forty exclusive qualities, explained across ten categories: the thirty-two marks of a great being, the eighty exemplary signs, the four kinds of utter purity, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, the three things requiring no guarding, great compassion, the dharma of never forgetting, and the permanent destruction of habitual tendencies, together with wisdom of every kind. The first three of these ten categories — the marks and signs, the four utter purities, and the individual explanation of each of the ten powers — were already explained in fascicle 49. This fascicle now explains the general synthesis of the ten powers, followed by the remaining seven categories, and will go on to explain the fourth yoga foundation of sequential practice's own chapter on arousing the mind of perfect awakening, together with the treatise's stateless-remainder and remainderless-remainder grounds — bringing to completion both the entire Bodhisattva Stage and the treatise's full account of all seventeen stages. The treatise then presents a summary verse gathering seven angles for synthesizing the ten powers as a whole: nature, differentiation, exclusivity, equality, function, sequence, and distinction, explained last.

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the basic section, the Bodhisattva Stage, the third yoga foundation of ultimate practice, the second part of the fifth chapter, the chapter on establishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi orients the reader to just how much ground this single fascicle covers: not only the remaining seven of the Buddha's ten categories of exclusive qualities, but also the entire chapter on arousing the mind of awakening and the treatise's own closing account of the stage with residue remaining and the stage with no residue remaining — meaning fascicle 50 completes not just the chapter on establishment, but the entire Bodhisattva Stage and the full seventeen-stage structure of the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra itself. He treats this as explaining why the treatise pauses here for a synthesizing overview of the ten powers before moving on: having explained each power individually in fascicle 49, the treatise now steps back to characterize them as a unified whole, through seven distinct angles, before continuing to the remaining unique qualities.

He also flags the practical value of this synthesis for ordinary practitioners, quoting the concern raised directly in the text: a practitioner still far from buddhahood who studies the ten powers without properly understanding their scope risks a subtle but serious error — mistaking some small meditative experience of their own for a share in the Buddha's own unsurpassed wisdom, and thereby falling into a kind of spiritual over-estimation. Careful study of exactly what makes these powers unique, he notes, is itself a safeguard against this danger. Having introduced the seven-angle framework, he notes, the treatise now explains the first angle: the very nature of the ten powers.

2. Milestone: The First Angle — Wisdom Alone Is Named the Very Nature of the Ten Powers

The treatise explains the first of the seven angles: the essential nature of the ten powers. Asked what this nature is, the answer explains that all five faculties — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — together constitute the nature of the ten powers; but because wisdom is foremost among them, the powers are said to have wisdom as their nature. This is why the very first power is named solely "the power of wisdom regarding what is possible and impossible," never "the power of faith," "the power of diligence," or any other faculty regarding the possible and impossible — and the same holds true for all the remaining nine powers as well. With this, the treatise concludes: this is called the nature of the Tathāgata's ten powers. The commentary clarifies further by drawing on the treatise's own Chapter on Resolving Difficulties: the nature described here belongs to what is called the "faculty of complete knowledge," the third and final of the three uncontaminated faculties, itself encompassing the mental faculty, the five faculties of faith and the rest, and the faculties of pleasure, joy, and equanimity — from which it follows that these wholesome faculties, taken together, form the ten powers' underlying nature.

By these seven characteristics, the essential meaning of the Tathāgata's ten powers should be known.

Second, explaining in turn; first, nature; first, posing the question. What is the nature of the Tathāgata's ten powers?

Second, explaining; first, stating the essential nature. In general, the five faculties constitute their nature; but because wisdom is foremost, it is said that wisdom is the nature of the ten powers.

Second, showing what is foremost; first, citing the first. This is why it is said only "the power of wisdom regarding what is possible and impossible," not "the power of faith" nor any other faculty's power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes how naturally this opening angle answers a question a careful reader might have carried since fascicle 49: why is each of the ten called specifically a power of "wisdom" — 智力 — rather than a power of faith, diligence, mindfulness, or concentration, when all five faculties clearly play some role in the path leading to buddhahood? The answer given here is refreshingly direct: all five faculties genuinely underlie the ten powers, but wisdom is simply the most excellent and decisive among them, and so it alone lends its name to each power's title.

He also draws on the commentary's own cross-reference to the doctrine of twenty-two faculties, identifying the ten powers' nature specifically with the faculty of complete knowledge — the most mature of the three uncontaminated faculties tracking a practitioner's progress from initial insight to full liberation — itself said to encompass mind, the five faculties, and the three feeling-faculties of pleasure, joy, and equanimity together. Having explained the ten powers' shared nature, he notes, the treatise turns to the second angle: their threefold differentiation across time, kind, and the beings they extend to.

3. Milestone: The Second Angle — Immeasurable Through Time, Through Kind, and Through Every Being's Own Continuity

The treatise now explains the second angle, the differentiation of the ten powers, showing why they should be understood as immeasurable. In brief, this immeasurability is established through three kinds of differentiation. First, differentiation by time: the ten powers penetrate and comprehend everything knowable falling within the past, the future, and the present alike. Second, differentiation by kind: the ten powers penetrate and comprehend every specific characteristic and every shared characteristic belonging to each and every conditioned phenomenon. Third, differentiation by continuity: the ten powers penetrate and comprehend every distinct being's own continuity, together with every matter pertaining to it, throughout the assemblies of beings in every direction. Through these three kinds of differentiation together, the Tathāgata's ten powers should be understood as truly immeasurable — this is called the differentiation of the Tathāgata's ten powers.

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, differentiation; first, raising the question. What is the differentiation of the Tathāgata's ten powers?

Second, briefly stating. In brief, through three kinds of differentiation, they should be known as immeasurable.

Third, listing and explaining; first, differentiation by time. First, differentiation by time: penetrating and comprehending everything knowable falling within the past, future, and present.

Second, differentiation by kind. Second, differentiation by kind: penetrating and comprehending the specific and shared characteristics of every conditioned phenomenon.

Third, differentiation by continuity. Third, differentiation by continuity: penetrating and comprehending every distinct being's own continuity and every matter pertaining to it, throughout every direction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the Yogâcārabhūmi's own commentary tradition to sharpen what each of these three dimensions actually means in practice: differentiation by time maps roughly onto three of the individual powers already explained — recollection of past abodes covering countless past eons, the divine eye covering future rebirths, and the power regarding exhaustion of outflows covering the present moment of liberation directly, with the remaining seven powers cutting across all three times at once. Differentiation by kind distinguishes knowledge of a phenomenon's own specific nature (such as knowing the specific unwholesome character of impurity) from knowledge of what is shared across all phenomena (such as knowing the selfless nature common to every dharma) — with the first power's own scope being unusually broad, comprehending both alike.

He treats differentiation by continuity as perhaps the most striking of the three: every one of the ten powers, without exception, extends to literally every being throughout every world-system in every direction, each considered individually rather than as an undifferentiated mass — a claim of comprehensiveness that itself explains why the powers are called immeasurable rather than simply extensive. Having explained the ten powers' threefold differentiation, he notes, the treatise turns to the third angle: why these powers belong to the Buddha exclusively.

4. Milestone: The Third and Fourth Angles — Belonging to the Buddha Alone, Yet Equally Shared by Every Buddha

The treatise now explains the third angle, exclusivity: asked what makes the ten powers exclusive, the answer is direct — only the Tathāgata possesses these ten powers; they are shared with no śrāvaka and no pratyekabuddha whatsoever. This alone is what is meant by the exclusivity of the Tathāgata's ten powers. The treatise then explains the fourth angle, equality, through two aspects. First, equality in attainment: all these ten powers are equally and completely accomplished by every single Tathāgata without exception, and so are said to be equal, without any difference between one buddha and another. Second, a difference in predominant abiding: although every buddha equally possesses all ten powers in full, when it comes to which power a given buddha dwells in most often, there is indeed a difference from buddha to buddha — one Tathāgata may abide predominantly in one particular power, while another Tathāgata abides predominantly in a different one. With this, the treatise concludes: this is called the equality of the Tathāgata's ten powers — equal in complete attainment, yet differing in which power each buddha most frequently dwells within, according to the specific causes and conditions by which each guides beings.

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, exclusivity. What makes the Tathāgata's ten powers exclusive? Only the Tathāgata possesses these ten powers, shared with no śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha; this is called the exclusivity of the Tathāgata's ten powers.

Fourth, equality; first, posing the question. What is the equality of the Tathāgata's ten powers?

Second, explaining; first, equal attainment without difference. These ten powers are equally and completely accomplished by every Tathāgata; hence they are said to be equal, without difference.

Second, a difference in predominant abiding. Yet with respect to which power a Tathāgata dwells in most, there is indeed a difference among Tathāgatas accordingly: one Tathāgata dwells predominantly in one power, another Tathāgata dwells predominantly in another.

Third, concluding. This is called the equality of the Tathāgata's ten powers.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the exclusivity angle as a simple but important boundary-marker, directly distinguishing the ten powers from qualities that arhats and pratyekabuddhas can also attain in some measure — the ten powers belong to buddhahood alone, full stop, with no partial version shared by the two lesser vehicles.

He treats the equality angle's second aspect as resolving what might otherwise look like a contradiction: how can every buddha equally possess all ten powers in full, and yet different buddhas seem, in the accounts of their teaching careers, to rely on or demonstrate certain powers more visibly than others? The answer is that full attainment and habitual reliance are two different things — every buddha holds the complete set, but which power comes most naturally into play depends on the specific causes and conditions, and the specific beings, each buddha is engaged in guiding. Having explained exclusivity and equality, he notes, the treatise turns to the fifth angle: the actual function each of the ten powers performs.

5. Milestone: The Fifth Angle Opens — What Each of the First Six Powers Actually Accomplishes

The treatise now explains the fifth angle, the function of each of the ten powers, through ten categories. The power of wisdom regarding what is possible and impossible truly knows cause as cause and fruit as fruit within every causal relation, and thereby subdues every rival argument among non-Buddhist ascetics and brahmins who claim there is no cause, or claim a false cause, for what occurs. The power of wisdom regarding karma truly knows the karma one performs and the fruit one experiences by one's own action, thereby subduing every rival argument claiming that merit gained through giving can simply be transferred to someone else. The power of wisdom regarding meditative absorptions, liberations, equipoise, and attainment can manifest three kinds of miraculous transformation — of miraculous power, of mind-reading discourse, and of instruction — teaching the beings to be transformed without any distortion, and thereby subduing every rival argument rooted in mutually hostile, contradictory positions among non-Buddhist ascetics and brahmins. The power of wisdom regarding superior and inferior faculties truly knows the distinctions among beings' inferior, middling, and superior faculties, and accordingly teaches the true dharma exactly as fits each. The power of wisdom regarding various resolute inclinations truly knows the distinctions among beings' inferior, middling, and superior grades of pure or impure resolute understanding, causing pure understanding to grow gradually while impure understanding is gradually abandoned. The power of wisdom regarding various elements truly knows the distinctions among beings' inferior, middling, and excellent elements — corresponding to the desire, form, and formless realms respectively — and, according to each being's own faculties, inclinations, and latent tendencies, provides undistorted instruction suited to whatever gateway of practice that being is entering, established exactly as is fitting.

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, function; first, posing the question. What is the function of the Tathāgata's ten powers?

Second, explaining; first, the power of wisdom regarding what is possible and impossible. The Tathāgata's power of wisdom regarding what is possible and impossible truly knows cause as cause and fruit as fruit within every cause; and it subdues every rival argument of no cause or false cause among all ascetics and brahmins.

Second, the power of wisdom regarding karma. The Tathāgata's power of wisdom regarding karma truly knows the karma one performs and the fruit one experiences by one's own action; and it subdues every rival argument of transferring the merit of giving among all ascetics and brahmins.

Third, the power of wisdom regarding meditative absorptions, liberations, equipoise, and attainment. The Tathāgata's power of wisdom regarding these can manifest three kinds of miraculous transformation, teaching the beings to be transformed without distortion; and it subdues every rival argument rooted in mutually hostile, contradictory positions among all ascetics and brahmins.

Fourth, the power of wisdom regarding superior and inferior faculties. The Tathāgata's power of wisdom regarding superior and inferior faculties truly knows the distinctions among beings' inferior, middling, and superior faculties, and accordingly teaches the true dharma exactly as is fitting.

The Tathāgata's power of wisdom regarding various resolute inclinations truly knows the distinctions among beings' inferior, middling, and superior, pure or impure resolute understanding; pure understanding is caused to grow gradually, impure understanding is gradually abandoned.

The Tathāgata's power of wisdom regarding various elements truly knows the distinctions among beings' inferior, middling, and excellent elements; and according to each being's faculties, inclinations, and latent tendencies, in whatever gateway of practice they are entering, it provides undistorted instruction, established as is fitting.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes how each power's function pairs an inward capacity (truly knowing something about beings or about karma) with an outward capacity (subduing a specific rival argument that denies this same truth) — the first power counters denial of causation itself, the second counters the transfer-of-merit theory (which he notes bears an uncomfortable resemblance to the folk saying "a family that accumulates goodness will have surplus blessing," properly understood only through shared karma rather than literal transfer), and the third counters hostile, contradictory doctrines through the direct demonstration of miraculous power.

He highlights the sixth power's own explanation of the three elements — inferior, corresponding to the desire realm's outward-flowing mind; middling, corresponding to the form realm's inward composure still bound to physical form; and excellent, corresponding to the formless realm's temporary suspension even of that attachment — as a genuinely useful diagnostic for understanding why beings gravitate toward different practices. Having briefly stated the sixth power's overall correspondence, he notes, the treatise now turns to its detailed application: first in teaching śrāvakas, then in teaching bodhisattvas at far greater length.

6. Milestone: Teaching Śrāvakas in Full, and the Eleven Provisions a Bodhisattva Needs Before Settling the Mind

The treatise now explains, in detail, how the power of wisdom regarding various elements teaches different beings. For śrāvakas, the Tathāgata provides correct instruction according to whatever gateway of practice each is entering, exactly as explained without interruption throughout the Śrāvaka Ground — revealing, clarifying, formulating in words, and disclosing every aspect of the path, from the two provisions for the path through the four noble truths to realizing the four fruits, leaving no doubt unresolved. The treatise then turns to bodhisattvas, posing the question: how does the Tathāgata instruct the assembly of bodhisattvas who are beginners, newly embarked on practice, gathering the provisions for meditative equipoise, and wishing to settle their own minds, so that their minds actually become settled? Such beginning bodhisattvas, newly turned toward the Great Vehicle and having aroused the aspiration for unsurpassed awakening, still have minds prone to distraction and views still tinged with exaggeration or denial regarding emptiness, and so must first cultivate the nine mental abidings — meaning they require the same provisions for the path that the Śrāvaka Ground itself details across fourteen items, summarized there in verse as: the perfection of oneself and others, wholesome desire, precepts, the senses guarded, moderation in food, wakefulness, correct knowledge, dwelling among good companions, learning, reflection, freedom from hindrance, and being adorned with generosity. Master Cháng Bǎi's own lecture further draws on the Yogâcārabhūmi's commentary tradition to summarize these same provisions in eleven points: purity of precepts; finding a good companion; guarding the sense-gates; moderation in food; reducing sleep; possessing wholesome reflection; possessing faith and understanding; possessing the qualities of a practitioner; possessing a basis for liberation; freedom from the three obstructions of affliction, karma, and retribution; and relinquishing every form of clinging attachment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own economy in handling śrāvaka instruction — a single sentence pointing back to the fourteen chapters of the Śrāvaka Ground already given at great length earlier in the treatise — compared to the far more extensive treatment bodhisattva instruction receives here, reflecting the Bodhisattva Stage's own natural emphasis on its primary audience.

He distinguishes the two kinds of "beginning practice" bodhisattva the commentary identifies: one still cultivating the preparatory concentration through the nine mental abidings in an ordinary, scattered state of mind, and a second who has already attained that preparatory concentration and now cultivates the four noble truths and the four types of examination and genuine understanding within concentration itself — both requiring, first and foremost, the same eleven or fourteen provisions for the path, since attempting deep meditation without this groundwork is like attempting a long journey without first checking one's fuel, water, and tires. Having listed these provisions, he notes, the treatise turns to how the Tathāgata actually begins instructing such a bodhisattva — starting, remarkably, with an inquiry into the bodhisattva's own given name.

7. Milestone: "Reflect on the Name Your Parents Gave You" — Contemplating the Self Through the Six Sense Bases

The treatise now presents the Tathāgata's own actual words of instruction to such a bodhisattva. First, in brief: for bodhisattvas free of flattery and deception, reverent and cherishing the provisions for meditative equipoise, whether beginners or newly embarked on practice, the Tathāgata first establishes undistorted instruction from the very outset. The instruction itself is then given directly: "Come, good son! You should dwell in a place removed from distraction, upon a simple seat, alone and undivided, your mind inwardly tranquil; reflect properly, as is fitting, upon your own name — the name your parents gave you when you were young, or the name given you by your preceptor or instructing teacher." The bodhisattva is then guided to reflect further: "Apart from the six sense-bases, does there exist some truly real, essential self — whether within, without, or somewhere between the two — to which this name, this notion, this provisional designation and manner of speech, actually refers and truly belongs?" Having reflected properly in this way, the bodhisattva comes to find that, in truth, nothing at all corresponding to such a self can be found there — life is simply the functioning and interaction of the six inner sense-bases, the six outer objects, and the six consciousnesses arising between them, with no independent self directing the process from within; the name given by one's parents or teacher is merely a convenient label for this ongoing process, a token for communication, no more referring to some truly existing person than any other name would.

Second, direct address; first, properly reflecting; first, on one's own name; first, stating the reflection. Thus it is said: "Come, good son! You should dwell in a secluded resting place, alone and undivided, inwardly tranquil, and properly reflect, as is fitting, upon the name your parents gave you, or the name given by your preceptor or instructing teacher."

Second, explaining the manner of the reflection. Reflect thus: "Is there, apart from the six sense-bases, some truly real, essential self — whether within, without, or in between — to which this name, this notion, this provisional designation and manner of speech, actually refers?"

Third, presenting the resulting understanding. "Having thus properly reflected, you should find that nothing at all corresponding to this can be found."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening instruction as strikingly practical and personal — rather than beginning with abstract doctrine, the Tathāgata directs the beginning bodhisattva to reflect on something utterly familiar and close at hand: one's own given name, the very first thing a person learns to answer to. He notes the commentary's own careful cross-reference to the four foundations of mindfulness already explained in the Śrāvaka Ground, confirming that this contemplation of the six sense-bases as empty of any abiding self is simply the same fundamental practice applied here at the very outset of bodhisattva training.

He highlights the practical genius of starting exactly here: a name feels so intimately "mine" that most people never think to question whether anything beyond the label actually exists to bear it — and yet, once a practitioner properly investigates, no such independently existing self can be located anywhere among the six inner sense-bases, the six outer objects, or the six consciousnesses arising between them. Having established this understanding that one's own name refers to no truly existing self, he notes, the treatise turns next to explaining how this same principle applies to what one calls "guest," or adventitious, phenomena.
