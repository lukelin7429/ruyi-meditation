Fascicle 26 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十六 · The Second Yogic Process Opens

Twenty-Eight Kinds of Practitioners

Fascicle 26 opens the second of four yogic processes within the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples: establishing categories, a systematic account spanning fascicles 26 through 29 of who practices, how they are classified, what they take as their object of meditation, and how they progress. It begins with seventeen questions and the first of these — the twenty-eight kinds of practitioners, from the dull-facultied to the fully liberated.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 26 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Second Yogic Process Opens — Seventeen Questions

Fascicle 26 marks a genuine structural turn: the first yogic process (fascicles 22–25), covering the fourteen provisions for the path, has closed in full. The second yogic process, establishing categories, now opens with seventeen questions that will structure fascicles 26 through 29 — beginning with how many kinds of practitioner can actually attain liberation.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之一……問：於如前所舉、所開示出離地中，有幾品類補特伽羅能證出離？云何建立補特伽羅？云何所緣？云何教授？云何學？云何隨順學法？云何瑜伽壞？云何瑜伽？云何作意？云何瑜伽師所作？幾種瑜伽師？云何瑜伽修？云何修果？幾種補特伽羅異門？幾種補特伽羅？幾種建立補特伽羅因緣？有幾種魔？幾種魔事？云何發趣空無有果？

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first part of the second yogic process … Question: Within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation as already raised and explained, how many kinds of practitioner are capable of realizing liberation? How are practitioners classified? What is their object of meditation? What is instruction? What is training? What is conduct in accordance with the training? What is the failure of yogic practice? What is yogic practice? What is mental engagement? What is the yoga master's own work? How many kinds of yoga master are there? What is the cultivation of yogic practice? What is its fruit? How many synonyms are there for a practitioner? How many kinds of practitioner are there? How many causes establish a practitioner as such? How many kinds of demonic force are there, and how many kinds of demonic activity? And what is it to set out toward liberation and yet arrive at no fruit at all?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi locates this fascicle precisely: the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples unfolds across four yogic processes — the first (fascicles 22–25) just closed, covering the fourteen provisions for the path; the second (fascicles 26–29), opening now, covers establishing categories; the third (fascicles 30–32) will cover the actual structuring of yogic practice; and the fourth (fascicles 33–34) will give a detailed account of cultivation itself. This second process opens with seventeen questions, immediately condensed into a single eight-line summary verse for memorization, covering: the varieties of practitioner; how they are classified, their object of meditation, and instruction; training and conduct in accordance with it; the failure of yogic practice, yogic practice itself, and mental engagement; the yoga master's work, how many kinds of yoga master exist, and cultivation itself; the fruit of cultivation, synonyms for "practitioner," and how many practitioners there are; the causes that establish practitioners as such, demonic forces and demonic activity, and finally what it means to set out toward liberation yet achieve nothing. Cháng Bǎi notes all seventeen will be unpacked in turn across the next four fascicles, beginning now with the first and most foundational: exactly how many kinds of practitioner exist within this path.

2. Twenty-Eight Kinds of Practitioners Named

Chinese Source

補特伽羅品類差別有二十八種。云何二十八？謂鈍根者、利根者、貪增上者、瞋增上者、癡增上者、慢增上者、尋思增上者、得平等者、薄塵性者、行向者、住果者、隨信行者、隨法行者、信勝解者、見至者、身證者、極七返有者、家家者、一間者、中般涅槃者、生般涅槃者、無行般涅槃者、有行般涅槃者、上流者、時解脫者、不動法者、慧解脫者、俱分解脫者。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are twenty-eight kinds of practitioner. What are the twenty-eight? The dull-facultied; the sharp-facultied; those dominated by desire; by aversion; by delusion; by pride; by discursive thought; those of balanced temperament; those of naturally light affliction; those approaching a fruit; those abiding in a fruit; faith-followers; dharma-followers; those liberated through resolute faith; those who have reached right view; witnesses through direct experience; those bound for at most seven more rebirths; those passing from family to family; those with a single remaining interval; those who attain final nirvana in the intermediate state; those who attain it upon rebirth; those who attain it without further effort; those who attain it through further effort; those who ascend to a higher realm before attaining it; those liberated in due season; those of unshakeable attainment; those liberated through wisdom; and those liberated in both respects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces this as the treatise's comprehensive taxonomy of practitioners, moving from the most basic temperamental distinctions to the most advanced stages of realization. The first nine sort practitioners by disposition rather than attainment: dull or sharp faculties (how readily one's roots of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom mature); domination by one of five specific afflictions — desire, aversion, delusion, pride, or discursive thought — each naming someone whose besetting tendency is unusually strong and persistent; a balanced temperament, in which no single affliction dominates but various afflictions surface depending on circumstance; and a naturally light-afflicted disposition, free of the intensity the prior categories describe. The next two describe general progress: approaching a fruit (actively practicing toward one of the four stages) and abiding in a fruit (having actually attained one). Two describe how someone first enters the path: faith-followers, of duller faculties, who advance largely on trust in what they've been taught, and dharma-followers, of sharper faculties, who advance through their own direct investigation of the teaching — the same two people, matured, becoming, respectively, "liberated through resolute faith" and "one who has reached right view." A witness through direct experience names someone who has personally tasted the deeper meditative absorptions on the way to liberation. The following seven name refinements specifically within the third fruit, non-returning, distinguished by how many further rebirths and what kind of effort remain before final nirvana; "ascending to a higher realm" names a non-returner who passes through successively higher heavens before attaining it. And the last four distinguish arhats by how their liberation was actually achieved: in due season through supportive conditions, unshakeably regardless of conditions, through wisdom alone, or through both wisdom and full meditative absorption together. Each of these twenty-eight will be defined individually in what follows.

3. Dull Faculties and Sharp Faculties: Innate Temperament and Cultivation

Chinese Source

云何鈍根補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，成就鈍根，於所知事遲鈍運轉、微劣運轉，如前已說。此復二種，應知其相。一者、本來鈍根種性，二者、未善修習諸根。云何利根補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，成就利根，於所知事不遲鈍運轉、不微劣運轉，如前已說。此亦二種，應知其相。一者、本來利根種性，二者、已善修習諸根。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a dull-facultied practitioner? It is one whose faculties operate slowly and weakly with regard to what should be known, as explained before. This is, moreover, of two kinds: one is dull-facultied by inherent disposition, and one has simply not cultivated the faculties well. What is a sharp-facultied practitioner? It is one whose faculties operate neither slowly nor weakly with regard to what should be known, as explained before. This too is of two kinds: one is sharp-facultied by inherent disposition, and one has cultivated the faculties well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "faculties" in question as the same five roots already introduced in fascicle 21 — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — whose relative strength determines how readily someone grasps and realizes the Dharma. A sharp-facultied practitioner holds unshakeable faith, sustains diligence for hours without fatigue, retains what's learned effortlessly, settles into concentration readily, and decides matters with clear, confident wisdom; a dull-facultied practitioner wavers at the first counterargument, tires quickly, forgets almost as fast as they learn, struggles to still a restless, fidgeting mind, and hesitates over decisions the sharp-facultied would resolve at once.

Crucially, Cháng Bǎi stresses both dullness and sharpness trace to two distinct causes rather than one: an inborn disposition carried over from cultivation (or its absence) in past lives, and simply how much effort has actually been invested in cultivating the five faculties in this present life. This means faculties are never fixed — the treatise's own teaching on "refining the faculties" holds that sustained effort can gradually sharpen even an originally dull disposition, exactly the kind of practical encouragement that matters more than debating exactly how naturally gifted one started out. Cháng Bǎi notes some classical sources also propose an intermediate category between dull and sharp, but the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā argues there is, strictly speaking, no genuine "middle" faculty — every stage of the path divides into exactly two paths (faith-follower and dharma-follower on the path of seeing, faith-liberated and view-attained on the path of cultivation, time-liberated and unshakeable on the path beyond training), with no room for a third, so any apparently "middling" case is simply classified as relatively sharper or duller than its immediate neighbors.

4. Dominated by Desire: How One Craving Becomes Deeply Entrenched

Chinese Source

云何貪增上補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，於貪煩惱已修、已習、已多修習；由是因緣，今此生中，於所愛事，有猛利貪、有長時貪。是名貪增上補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner dominated by desire? It is one who, in previous lives, has already cultivated, repeated, and extensively practiced the affliction of desire; through this cause, in the present life, toward objects of attachment, such a one has fierce desire and prolonged desire. This is called a practitioner dominated by desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats desire-domination as a habit deeply grooved by repetition across many lifetimes — not a single strong craving, but a settled tendency that resurfaces reliably whenever a desirable object appears, "fierce" in intensity and "prolonged" in how persistently it lingers once triggered. He cites the treatise's own finer analysis: desire's essential nature is the impulse to claim another's possessions as one's own; its object is any worldly wealth or resource; and its characteristic mental pattern is the reflexive thought "whatever belongs to them should belong to me." Genuinely fierce desire, Cháng Bǎi notes, shows itself precisely in how strongly it grips even trivial, low-value objects — someone who can't bear to part with something worthless has revealed a stronger underlying pattern than someone merely tempted by something genuinely valuable.

The same essential analysis — a habit cultivated repeatedly across past lives, now manifesting as a fierce and prolonged pattern in the present — applies identically to the next three categories the treatise names: domination by aversion (a settled tendency toward anger and ill-will), by delusion (a settled tendency toward confusion and wrong view), and by pride (a settled tendency toward self-exaltation), each defined by the treatise in exactly parallel language, substituting only the specific affliction involved.

5. Dominated by Aversion, Delusion, Pride, or Discursive Thought

Chinese Source

謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，於瞋煩惱已修、已習、已多修習；由是因緣，今此生中，於所憎事，有猛利瞋、有長時瞋。是名瞋增上補特伽羅。……於癡煩惱……有猛利癡、有長時癡。是名癡增上補特伽羅。……於慢煩惱……有猛利慢、有長時慢。是名慢增上補特伽羅。……於其尋思……有猛利尋思、有長時尋思。是名尋思增上補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is one who, in previous lives, has already cultivated, repeated, and extensively practiced the affliction of aversion; through this cause, in the present life, toward objects of hatred, has fierce and prolonged aversion. This is called a practitioner dominated by aversion. … The same applies to delusion … fierce and prolonged delusion. This is called a practitioner dominated by delusion. … The same applies to pride … fierce and prolonged pride. This is called a practitioner dominated by pride. … The same applies to discursive thought … fierce and prolonged discursive thought. This is called a practitioner dominated by discursive thought.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi again applies the treatise's essence/object/aspect analysis to each. Aversion's essence is the settled wish to harm another's body or possessions; its object is other beings and their property; its characteristic pattern is the reflexive wish to see them suffer loss and distress. Delusion here specifically means entrenched wrong view — the classic nihilistic denial that giving, moral cause and effect, this world or the next, or genuine liberated beings even exist; someone dominated by delusion readily embraces such views and actively propagates them to others, unable to enter genuine Buddhist practice as a result. Pride's essence is simply persistent self-exaltation — Cháng Bǎi notes a folk belief that habitual contempt for others is even said to shape one's stature in a future life, height instead following from habitual respect shown to others. And discursive thought names a mind that races even over trivial matters, scattering into restless mental chatter that specifically undermines seated meditation — someone dominated by it finds calming practice unusually difficult, the mind refusing to settle even when there's genuinely nothing worth thinking about.

6. Milestone: Balanced Temperament and the Opening of Light Affliction

The eighth type breaks the pattern just established: someone who never specifically cultivated any of the five afflictions into a dominant habit, yet also never cultivated their antidotes — so any of the five can still surface, just never with the fierce, entrenched intensity of the dominated types.

Chinese Source

云何得平等補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，雖於貪、瞋、癡、慢、尋思，不修、不習、不多修習，而於彼法未見過患、未能厭壞、未善推求。由是因緣，於所愛、所憎、所愚、所慢、所尋思事，無猛利貪、無長時貪，然如彼事，貪得現行。如貪，瞋、癡、慢、尋思亦爾。是名得平等補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner of balanced temperament? It is one who, in previous lives, did not cultivate, repeat, or extensively practice desire, aversion, delusion, pride, or discursive thought — yet also never saw their genuine dangers, never developed real revulsion toward them, and never skillfully investigated them. Through this cause, toward objects of attachment, hatred, delusion, pride, or discursive thought, such a one has no fierce or prolonged desire — yet when the occasion for desire actually arises, desire still manifests. The same holds for aversion, delusion, pride, and discursive thought. This is called a practitioner of balanced temperament.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as neither a good nor a bad starting point, but simply a neutral one: someone who happened not to entrench any single affliction into a dominant habit across past lives, yet also never took up the specific antidotes the treatise names elsewhere — the "purifying objects of meditation" (contemplating impurity against desire, loving-kindness against aversion, dependent origination against delusion, the elements against pride, and breath-counting against discursive thought) — nor investigated affliction's object, basis, mark, category, timing, and underlying principle closely enough to weaken it. The result is a mind where any of the five afflictions can still arise given the right trigger, just never with the fierce, self-reinforcing intensity the dominated types display — genuinely average, in other words, rather than either afflicted or purified. The ninth type, light affliction, opens immediately after as its natural counterpart: someone whose afflictions, whatever their type, are simply thin and weak across the board.

7. Milestone: Light Affliction Closes; Approaching a Fruit Opens the Four Stages

Light affliction closes the temperament-based categories: someone who has genuinely cultivated the antidotes to affliction, leaving only the faintest residue even toward attractive objects. The treatise then turns to attainment itself, opening with the four practitioners actively approaching — but not yet abiding in — one of the four fruits of the path.

Chinese Source

云何薄塵性補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，於貪煩惱不修、不習、不多修習，已能於彼多見過患，已能厭壞、已善推求。由是因緣，今此生中，於所愛事會遇現前，眾多美妙上品境中，起微劣貪；於其中品、下品境中，貪全不起。如貪，瞋、癡、慢、尋思應知亦爾。是名薄塵性補特伽羅。云何行向補特伽羅？謂行四向補特伽羅。何等為四？一、預流果向，二、一來果向，三、不還果向，四、阿羅漢果向。是名行向補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner of light affliction? It is one who, in previous lives, did not cultivate desire, but had already come to see its many dangers, developed genuine revulsion toward it, and skillfully investigated it. Through this cause, in the present life, when encountering even the most excellent and appealing objects, only the faintest, most minor desire arises — and toward middling or inferior objects, no desire arises at all. The same holds for aversion, delusion, pride, and discursive thought. This is called a practitioner of light affliction. What is a practitioner approaching a fruit? It is one practicing toward one of the four fruits. What are the four? Approaching the fruit of stream-entry, approaching the fruit of once-returning, approaching the fruit of non-returning, and approaching the fruit of arhatship. This is called a practitioner approaching a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts light affliction directly with balanced temperament: where the balanced type never took up affliction's antidotes, the light-afflicted type genuinely has — seeing desire's real dangers, cultivating revulsion toward it, and investigating it through the treatise's own sixfold analysis (its object, basis, mark, category, timing, and underlying principle) — so that even the most attractive object barely stirs a flicker of desire, and lesser objects stir nothing at all. Fascicle 29 identifies this type as the fastest to attain genuine meditative stability, since they carry none of the heavy karmic obstructions, fierce entrenched afflictions, or unfortunate rebirth circumstances that slow other practitioners down.

"Approaching a fruit" then opens the treatise's classical four-stage framework of the path itself. Someone "approaching stream-entry" has entered the path of seeing — the first direct realization of the Four Noble Truths — but not yet completed its full sixteen-moment sequence; traditionally, once a practitioner reaches this point they are guaranteed to complete stream-entry and will not die before doing so. "Approaching once-returning" and "approaching non-returning" name practitioners in the process of eliminating further grades of affliction bound to the desire realm, whether progressing step by step through each fruit in sequence or advancing more directly, having already weakened desire through worldly meditative attainment before ever entering the path of seeing. "Approaching arhatship" names a non-returner working to eliminate the subtlest remaining afflictions, bound to the very peak of conditioned existence, on the way to final liberation. Cháng Bǎi notes the classical six preparatory contemplations behind this progress — cultivating revulsion toward the desire realm's coarseness, suffering, and obstruction, and aspiration toward the first absorption's superior, subtle, and liberating qualities — as the meditative engine driving a practitioner from one stage toward the next.

8. Abiding in a Fruit: The Four Attainments Themselves

Chinese Source

云何住果補特伽羅？謂住四果補特伽羅。何等為四？一、預流果，二、一來果，三、不還果，四、阿羅漢果。是名住果補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner abiding in a fruit? It is one abiding in one of the four fruits. What are the four? The fruit of stream-entry, the fruit of once-returning, the fruit of non-returning, and the fruit of arhatship. This is called a practitioner abiding in a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi defines each fruit precisely. Stream-entry means having permanently eliminated every affliction removable through the path of seeing, entering the stream that runs against the current of ordinary existence — bound to at most seven more rounds of rebirth between the human and heavenly realms before arhatship becomes inevitable. Once-returning means having additionally eliminated the upper and middle grades of desire-realm affliction removable through cultivation, with at most one further rebirth in each of the human and heavenly realms remaining. Non-returning means having eliminated the desire-realm's remaining lower grades of affliction entirely, so that rebirth in the desire realm never occurs again. And arhatship means having permanently eliminated every affliction of every kind across all three realms, reaching the very limit of suffering, with no further rebirth of any kind to come — the "no further learning" fruit, since nothing remains to be studied or overcome once it's reached.

9. Milestone: Faith-Followers and Dharma-Followers, Renamed Upon Touching the Fruit

The treatise names two ways a practitioner first enters the path — relying on a teacher's direct guidance, or independently investigating the teaching oneself — then shows how each becomes a new designation the moment the practitioner actually touches the fruit of stream-entry.

Chinese Source

云何隨信行補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，從他求請教授教誡，由此力故，修證果行。……唯由隨他補特伽羅，信而修行。是名隨信行補特伽羅。云何隨法行補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，如其所聞、所受、所究竟、所思、所量、所觀察法，自有功能、自有勢力隨法修行；不從他求教授教誡，修證果行。是名隨法行補特伽羅。云何信勝解補特伽羅？謂即隨信行補特伽羅，因他教授教誡，於沙門果得觸證時，名信勝解補特伽羅。云何見至補特伽羅？謂即隨法行補特伽羅，於沙門果得觸證時，說名見至補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a faith-follower? It is one who seeks instruction and guidance from another, and, through that power, cultivates and realizes the fruit. … Relying solely on another, such a one practices through faith. This is called a faith-follower. What is a dharma-follower? It is one who, through what has been heard, received, thoroughly understood, reflected upon, weighed, and observed, has their own capability and their own strength to practice in accordance with the teaching, not seeking instruction and guidance from another. This is called a dharma-follower. What is one liberated through resolute faith? It is precisely the faith-follower who, through another's instruction and guidance, upon touching the fruit of renunciation, is called one liberated through resolute faith. What is one who has reached right view? It is precisely the dharma-follower who, upon touching the fruit of renunciation, is called one who has reached right view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats faith-followers and dharma-followers as naming the same fundamental duller-versus-sharper distinction already introduced through dull and sharp faculties, now specifically applied to how a practitioner first engages the teaching before reaching any fruit: a faith-follower advances by trusting and following a teacher's direct instruction, without yet having the independent capacity to investigate scripture — hearing, accepting, fully understanding, reflecting on, weighing, and observing its meaning — on their own; a dharma-follower has exactly that independent capacity, and needs no outside guidance to progress. The moment either actually touches the fruit of stream-entry — the direct, sixteen-moment realization of the Four Noble Truths — their designation changes: the faith-follower becomes "one liberated through resolute faith," since their liberation was reached primarily through faith in guidance received; the dharma-follower becomes "one who has reached right view," since theirs was reached through direct investigation arriving at genuine insight. Cháng Bǎi notes both new names share the same underlying event — the mind's release from the three fetters of self-view, attachment to wrong rules and rituals, and doubt — differing only in which capacity, faith or independent insight, led there first.

10. The Body-Witness: Mastery of the Eight Liberations

Chinese Source

云何身證補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，於八解脫順逆入出身作證，多安住，而未能得諸漏永盡。是名身證補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a body-witness? It is one who has personally realized, and dwells extensively within, the eight liberations, entering and emerging from them in forward and reverse order, yet who has not yet permanently exhausted every outflow. This is called a body-witness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes the eight liberations already introduced in the Ground of Meditative Equipoise: liberation through contemplating external forms as impure while still perceiving inner form; liberation through the same contemplation once inner perception of form has already been abandoned; liberation through directly witnessing the pure aspect of form once practice has fully matured; and the four formless liberations — infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception — culminating in the eighth, the cessation of feeling and perception itself. A body-witness moves freely through all eight, forward from the first to the last and backward again, entering whichever is wished at will — "witnessed by the body" meaning realized through the mind's own direct experience (the treatise's "body" here referring to mental rather than physical embodiment) rather than merely understood conceptually. This mastery marks a non-returner of unusually advanced meditative accomplishment, though — crucially — not yet an arhat, since the subtlest afflictions of the three realms remain to be eliminated even after such complete command of absorption.
