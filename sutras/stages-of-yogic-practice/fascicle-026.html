Fascicle 26 · 瑜伽師地論卷第二十六 · The Second Yogic Process Opens

Twenty-Eight Kinds of Practitioners

Fascicle 26 opens the second of four yogic processes within the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples: establishing categories, a systematic account spanning fascicles 26 through 29 of who practices, how they are classified, what they take as their object of meditation, and how they progress. It begins with seventeen questions and the first of these — the twenty-eight kinds of practitioners, from the dull-facultied to the fully liberated.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 26 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Second Yogic Process Opens — Seventeen Questions

Fascicle 26 marks a genuine structural turn: the first yogic process (fascicles 22–25), covering the fourteen provisions for the path, has closed in full. The second yogic process, establishing categories, now opens with seventeen questions that will structure fascicles 26 through 29 — beginning with how many kinds of practitioner can actually attain liberation.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地第二瑜伽處之一……問：於如前所舉、所開示出離地中，有幾品類補特伽羅能證出離？云何建立補特伽羅？云何所緣？云何教授？云何學？云何隨順學法？云何瑜伽壞？云何瑜伽？云何作意？云何瑜伽師所作？幾種瑜伽師？云何瑜伽修？云何修果？幾種補特伽羅異門？幾種補特伽羅？幾種建立補特伽羅因緣？有幾種魔？幾種魔事？云何發趣空無有果？

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first part of the second yogic process … Question: Within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation as already raised and explained, how many kinds of practitioner are capable of realizing liberation? How are practitioners classified? What is their object of meditation? What is instruction? What is training? What is conduct in accordance with the training? What is the failure of yogic practice? What is yogic practice? What is mental engagement? What is the yoga master's own work? How many kinds of yoga master are there? What is the cultivation of yogic practice? What is its fruit? How many synonyms are there for a practitioner? How many kinds of practitioner are there? How many causes establish a practitioner as such? How many kinds of demonic force are there, and how many kinds of demonic activity? And what is it to set out toward liberation and yet arrive at no fruit at all?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi locates this fascicle precisely: the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples unfolds across four yogic processes — the first (fascicles 22–25) just closed, covering the fourteen provisions for the path; the second (fascicles 26–29), opening now, covers establishing categories; the third (fascicles 30–32) will cover the actual structuring of yogic practice; and the fourth (fascicles 33–34) will give a detailed account of cultivation itself. This second process opens with seventeen questions, immediately condensed into a single eight-line summary verse for memorization, covering: the varieties of practitioner; how they are classified, their object of meditation, and instruction; training and conduct in accordance with it; the failure of yogic practice, yogic practice itself, and mental engagement; the yoga master's work, how many kinds of yoga master exist, and cultivation itself; the fruit of cultivation, synonyms for "practitioner," and how many practitioners there are; the causes that establish practitioners as such, demonic forces and demonic activity, and finally what it means to set out toward liberation yet achieve nothing. Cháng Bǎi notes all seventeen will be unpacked in turn across the next four fascicles, beginning now with the first and most foundational: exactly how many kinds of practitioner exist within this path.

2. Twenty-Eight Kinds of Practitioners Named

Chinese Source

補特伽羅品類差別有二十八種。云何二十八？謂鈍根者、利根者、貪增上者、瞋增上者、癡增上者、慢增上者、尋思增上者、得平等者、薄塵性者、行向者、住果者、隨信行者、隨法行者、信勝解者、見至者、身證者、極七返有者、家家者、一間者、中般涅槃者、生般涅槃者、無行般涅槃者、有行般涅槃者、上流者、時解脫者、不動法者、慧解脫者、俱分解脫者。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are twenty-eight kinds of practitioner. What are the twenty-eight? The dull-facultied; the sharp-facultied; those dominated by desire; by aversion; by delusion; by pride; by discursive thought; those of balanced temperament; those of naturally light affliction; those approaching a fruit; those abiding in a fruit; faith-followers; dharma-followers; those liberated through resolute faith; those who have reached right view; witnesses through direct experience; those bound for at most seven more rebirths; those passing from family to family; those with a single remaining interval; those who attain final nirvana in the intermediate state; those who attain it upon rebirth; those who attain it without further effort; those who attain it through further effort; those who ascend to a higher realm before attaining it; those liberated in due season; those of unshakeable attainment; those liberated through wisdom; and those liberated in both respects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces this as the treatise's comprehensive taxonomy of practitioners, moving from the most basic temperamental distinctions to the most advanced stages of realization. The first nine sort practitioners by disposition rather than attainment: dull or sharp faculties (how readily one's roots of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom mature); domination by one of five specific afflictions — desire, aversion, delusion, pride, or discursive thought — each naming someone whose besetting tendency is unusually strong and persistent; a balanced temperament, in which no single affliction dominates but various afflictions surface depending on circumstance; and a naturally light-afflicted disposition, free of the intensity the prior categories describe. The next two describe general progress: approaching a fruit (actively practicing toward one of the four stages) and abiding in a fruit (having actually attained one). Two describe how someone first enters the path: faith-followers, of duller faculties, who advance largely on trust in what they've been taught, and dharma-followers, of sharper faculties, who advance through their own direct investigation of the teaching — the same two people, matured, becoming, respectively, "liberated through resolute faith" and "one who has reached right view." A witness through direct experience names someone who has personally tasted the deeper meditative absorptions on the way to liberation. The following seven name refinements specifically within the third fruit, non-returning, distinguished by how many further rebirths and what kind of effort remain before final nirvana; "ascending to a higher realm" names a non-returner who passes through successively higher heavens before attaining it. And the last four distinguish arhats by how their liberation was actually achieved: in due season through supportive conditions, unshakeably regardless of conditions, through wisdom alone, or through both wisdom and full meditative absorption together. Each of these twenty-eight will be defined individually in what follows.

3. Dull Faculties and Sharp Faculties: Innate Temperament and Cultivation

Chinese Source

云何鈍根補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，成就鈍根，於所知事遲鈍運轉、微劣運轉，如前已說。此復二種，應知其相。一者、本來鈍根種性，二者、未善修習諸根。云何利根補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，成就利根，於所知事不遲鈍運轉、不微劣運轉，如前已說。此亦二種，應知其相。一者、本來利根種性，二者、已善修習諸根。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a dull-facultied practitioner? It is one whose faculties operate slowly and weakly with regard to what should be known, as explained before. This is, moreover, of two kinds: one is dull-facultied by inherent disposition, and one has simply not cultivated the faculties well. What is a sharp-facultied practitioner? It is one whose faculties operate neither slowly nor weakly with regard to what should be known, as explained before. This too is of two kinds: one is sharp-facultied by inherent disposition, and one has cultivated the faculties well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the "faculties" in question as the same five roots already introduced in fascicle 21 — faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom — whose relative strength determines how readily someone grasps and realizes the Dharma. A sharp-facultied practitioner holds unshakeable faith, sustains diligence for hours without fatigue, retains what's learned effortlessly, settles into concentration readily, and decides matters with clear, confident wisdom; a dull-facultied practitioner wavers at the first counterargument, tires quickly, forgets almost as fast as they learn, struggles to still a restless, fidgeting mind, and hesitates over decisions the sharp-facultied would resolve at once.

Crucially, Cháng Bǎi stresses both dullness and sharpness trace to two distinct causes rather than one: an inborn disposition carried over from cultivation (or its absence) in past lives, and simply how much effort has actually been invested in cultivating the five faculties in this present life. This means faculties are never fixed — the treatise's own teaching on "refining the faculties" holds that sustained effort can gradually sharpen even an originally dull disposition, exactly the kind of practical encouragement that matters more than debating exactly how naturally gifted one started out. Cháng Bǎi notes some classical sources also propose an intermediate category between dull and sharp, but the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā argues there is, strictly speaking, no genuine "middle" faculty — every stage of the path divides into exactly two paths (faith-follower and dharma-follower on the path of seeing, faith-liberated and view-attained on the path of cultivation, time-liberated and unshakeable on the path beyond training), with no room for a third, so any apparently "middling" case is simply classified as relatively sharper or duller than its immediate neighbors.

4. Dominated by Desire: How One Craving Becomes Deeply Entrenched

Chinese Source

云何貪增上補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，於貪煩惱已修、已習、已多修習；由是因緣，今此生中，於所愛事，有猛利貪、有長時貪。是名貪增上補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner dominated by desire? It is one who, in previous lives, has already cultivated, repeated, and extensively practiced the affliction of desire; through this cause, in the present life, toward objects of attachment, such a one has fierce desire and prolonged desire. This is called a practitioner dominated by desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats desire-domination as a habit deeply grooved by repetition across many lifetimes — not a single strong craving, but a settled tendency that resurfaces reliably whenever a desirable object appears, "fierce" in intensity and "prolonged" in how persistently it lingers once triggered. He cites the treatise's own finer analysis: desire's essential nature is the impulse to claim another's possessions as one's own; its object is any worldly wealth or resource; and its characteristic mental pattern is the reflexive thought "whatever belongs to them should belong to me." Genuinely fierce desire, Cháng Bǎi notes, shows itself precisely in how strongly it grips even trivial, low-value objects — someone who can't bear to part with something worthless has revealed a stronger underlying pattern than someone merely tempted by something genuinely valuable.

The same essential analysis — a habit cultivated repeatedly across past lives, now manifesting as a fierce and prolonged pattern in the present — applies identically to the next three categories the treatise names: domination by aversion (a settled tendency toward anger and ill-will), by delusion (a settled tendency toward confusion and wrong view), and by pride (a settled tendency toward self-exaltation), each defined by the treatise in exactly parallel language, substituting only the specific affliction involved.

5. Dominated by Aversion, Delusion, Pride, or Discursive Thought

Chinese Source

謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，於瞋煩惱已修、已習、已多修習；由是因緣，今此生中，於所憎事，有猛利瞋、有長時瞋。是名瞋增上補特伽羅。……於癡煩惱……有猛利癡、有長時癡。是名癡增上補特伽羅。……於慢煩惱……有猛利慢、有長時慢。是名慢增上補特伽羅。……於其尋思……有猛利尋思、有長時尋思。是名尋思增上補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is one who, in previous lives, has already cultivated, repeated, and extensively practiced the affliction of aversion; through this cause, in the present life, toward objects of hatred, has fierce and prolonged aversion. This is called a practitioner dominated by aversion. … The same applies to delusion … fierce and prolonged delusion. This is called a practitioner dominated by delusion. … The same applies to pride … fierce and prolonged pride. This is called a practitioner dominated by pride. … The same applies to discursive thought … fierce and prolonged discursive thought. This is called a practitioner dominated by discursive thought.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi again applies the treatise's essence/object/aspect analysis to each. Aversion's essence is the settled wish to harm another's body or possessions; its object is other beings and their property; its characteristic pattern is the reflexive wish to see them suffer loss and distress. Delusion here specifically means entrenched wrong view — the classic nihilistic denial that giving, moral cause and effect, this world or the next, or genuine liberated beings even exist; someone dominated by delusion readily embraces such views and actively propagates them to others, unable to enter genuine Buddhist practice as a result. Pride's essence is simply persistent self-exaltation — Cháng Bǎi notes a folk belief that habitual contempt for others is even said to shape one's stature in a future life, height instead following from habitual respect shown to others. And discursive thought names a mind that races even over trivial matters, scattering into restless mental chatter that specifically undermines seated meditation — someone dominated by it finds calming practice unusually difficult, the mind refusing to settle even when there's genuinely nothing worth thinking about.

6. Milestone: Balanced Temperament and the Opening of Light Affliction

The eighth type breaks the pattern just established: someone who never specifically cultivated any of the five afflictions into a dominant habit, yet also never cultivated their antidotes — so any of the five can still surface, just never with the fierce, entrenched intensity of the dominated types.

Chinese Source

云何得平等補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，雖於貪、瞋、癡、慢、尋思，不修、不習、不多修習，而於彼法未見過患、未能厭壞、未善推求。由是因緣，於所愛、所憎、所愚、所慢、所尋思事，無猛利貪、無長時貪，然如彼事，貪得現行。如貪，瞋、癡、慢、尋思亦爾。是名得平等補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner of balanced temperament? It is one who, in previous lives, did not cultivate, repeat, or extensively practice desire, aversion, delusion, pride, or discursive thought — yet also never saw their genuine dangers, never developed real revulsion toward them, and never skillfully investigated them. Through this cause, toward objects of attachment, hatred, delusion, pride, or discursive thought, such a one has no fierce or prolonged desire — yet when the occasion for desire actually arises, desire still manifests. The same holds for aversion, delusion, pride, and discursive thought. This is called a practitioner of balanced temperament.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as neither a good nor a bad starting point, but simply a neutral one: someone who happened not to entrench any single affliction into a dominant habit across past lives, yet also never took up the specific antidotes the treatise names elsewhere — the "purifying objects of meditation" (contemplating impurity against desire, loving-kindness against aversion, dependent origination against delusion, the elements against pride, and breath-counting against discursive thought) — nor investigated affliction's object, basis, mark, category, timing, and underlying principle closely enough to weaken it. The result is a mind where any of the five afflictions can still arise given the right trigger, just never with the fierce, self-reinforcing intensity the dominated types display — genuinely average, in other words, rather than either afflicted or purified. The ninth type, light affliction, opens immediately after as its natural counterpart: someone whose afflictions, whatever their type, are simply thin and weak across the board.

7. Milestone: Light Affliction Closes; Approaching a Fruit Opens the Four Stages

Light affliction closes the temperament-based categories: someone who has genuinely cultivated the antidotes to affliction, leaving only the faintest residue even toward attractive objects. The treatise then turns to attainment itself, opening with the four practitioners actively approaching — but not yet abiding in — one of the four fruits of the path.

Chinese Source

云何薄塵性補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，先餘生中，於貪煩惱不修、不習、不多修習，已能於彼多見過患，已能厭壞、已善推求。由是因緣，今此生中，於所愛事會遇現前，眾多美妙上品境中，起微劣貪；於其中品、下品境中，貪全不起。如貪，瞋、癡、慢、尋思應知亦爾。是名薄塵性補特伽羅。云何行向補特伽羅？謂行四向補特伽羅。何等為四？一、預流果向，二、一來果向，三、不還果向，四、阿羅漢果向。是名行向補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner of light affliction? It is one who, in previous lives, did not cultivate desire, but had already come to see its many dangers, developed genuine revulsion toward it, and skillfully investigated it. Through this cause, in the present life, when encountering even the most excellent and appealing objects, only the faintest, most minor desire arises — and toward middling or inferior objects, no desire arises at all. The same holds for aversion, delusion, pride, and discursive thought. This is called a practitioner of light affliction. What is a practitioner approaching a fruit? It is one practicing toward one of the four fruits. What are the four? Approaching the fruit of stream-entry, approaching the fruit of once-returning, approaching the fruit of non-returning, and approaching the fruit of arhatship. This is called a practitioner approaching a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts light affliction directly with balanced temperament: where the balanced type never took up affliction's antidotes, the light-afflicted type genuinely has — seeing desire's real dangers, cultivating revulsion toward it, and investigating it through the treatise's own sixfold analysis (its object, basis, mark, category, timing, and underlying principle) — so that even the most attractive object barely stirs a flicker of desire, and lesser objects stir nothing at all. Fascicle 29 identifies this type as the fastest to attain genuine meditative stability, since they carry none of the heavy karmic obstructions, fierce entrenched afflictions, or unfortunate rebirth circumstances that slow other practitioners down.

"Approaching a fruit" then opens the treatise's classical four-stage framework of the path itself. Someone "approaching stream-entry" has entered the path of seeing — the first direct realization of the Four Noble Truths — but not yet completed its full sixteen-moment sequence; traditionally, once a practitioner reaches this point they are guaranteed to complete stream-entry and will not die before doing so. "Approaching once-returning" and "approaching non-returning" name practitioners in the process of eliminating further grades of affliction bound to the desire realm, whether progressing step by step through each fruit in sequence or advancing more directly, having already weakened desire through worldly meditative attainment before ever entering the path of seeing. "Approaching arhatship" names a non-returner working to eliminate the subtlest remaining afflictions, bound to the very peak of conditioned existence, on the way to final liberation. Cháng Bǎi notes the classical six preparatory contemplations behind this progress — cultivating revulsion toward the desire realm's coarseness, suffering, and obstruction, and aspiration toward the first absorption's superior, subtle, and liberating qualities — as the meditative engine driving a practitioner from one stage toward the next.

8. Abiding in a Fruit: The Four Attainments Themselves

Chinese Source

云何住果補特伽羅？謂住四果補特伽羅。何等為四？一、預流果，二、一來果，三、不還果，四、阿羅漢果。是名住果補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner abiding in a fruit? It is one abiding in one of the four fruits. What are the four? The fruit of stream-entry, the fruit of once-returning, the fruit of non-returning, and the fruit of arhatship. This is called a practitioner abiding in a fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi defines each fruit precisely. Stream-entry means having permanently eliminated every affliction removable through the path of seeing, entering the stream that runs against the current of ordinary existence — bound to at most seven more rounds of rebirth between the human and heavenly realms before arhatship becomes inevitable. Once-returning means having additionally eliminated the upper and middle grades of desire-realm affliction removable through cultivation, with at most one further rebirth in each of the human and heavenly realms remaining. Non-returning means having eliminated the desire-realm's remaining lower grades of affliction entirely, so that rebirth in the desire realm never occurs again. And arhatship means having permanently eliminated every affliction of every kind across all three realms, reaching the very limit of suffering, with no further rebirth of any kind to come — the "no further learning" fruit, since nothing remains to be studied or overcome once it's reached.

9. Milestone: Faith-Followers and Dharma-Followers, Renamed Upon Touching the Fruit

The treatise names two ways a practitioner first enters the path — relying on a teacher's direct guidance, or independently investigating the teaching oneself — then shows how each becomes a new designation the moment the practitioner actually touches the fruit of stream-entry.

Chinese Source

云何隨信行補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，從他求請教授教誡，由此力故，修證果行。……唯由隨他補特伽羅，信而修行。是名隨信行補特伽羅。云何隨法行補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，如其所聞、所受、所究竟、所思、所量、所觀察法，自有功能、自有勢力隨法修行；不從他求教授教誡，修證果行。是名隨法行補特伽羅。云何信勝解補特伽羅？謂即隨信行補特伽羅，因他教授教誡，於沙門果得觸證時，名信勝解補特伽羅。云何見至補特伽羅？謂即隨法行補特伽羅，於沙門果得觸證時，說名見至補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a faith-follower? It is one who seeks instruction and guidance from another, and, through that power, cultivates and realizes the fruit. … Relying solely on another, such a one practices through faith. This is called a faith-follower. What is a dharma-follower? It is one who, through what has been heard, received, thoroughly understood, reflected upon, weighed, and observed, has their own capability and their own strength to practice in accordance with the teaching, not seeking instruction and guidance from another. This is called a dharma-follower. What is one liberated through resolute faith? It is precisely the faith-follower who, through another's instruction and guidance, upon touching the fruit of renunciation, is called one liberated through resolute faith. What is one who has reached right view? It is precisely the dharma-follower who, upon touching the fruit of renunciation, is called one who has reached right view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats faith-followers and dharma-followers as naming the same fundamental duller-versus-sharper distinction already introduced through dull and sharp faculties, now specifically applied to how a practitioner first engages the teaching before reaching any fruit: a faith-follower advances by trusting and following a teacher's direct instruction, without yet having the independent capacity to investigate scripture — hearing, accepting, fully understanding, reflecting on, weighing, and observing its meaning — on their own; a dharma-follower has exactly that independent capacity, and needs no outside guidance to progress. The moment either actually touches the fruit of stream-entry — the direct, sixteen-moment realization of the Four Noble Truths — their designation changes: the faith-follower becomes "one liberated through resolute faith," since their liberation was reached primarily through faith in guidance received; the dharma-follower becomes "one who has reached right view," since theirs was reached through direct investigation arriving at genuine insight. Cháng Bǎi notes both new names share the same underlying event — the mind's release from the three fetters of self-view, attachment to wrong rules and rituals, and doubt — differing only in which capacity, faith or independent insight, led there first.

10. The Body-Witness: Mastery of the Eight Liberations

Chinese Source

云何身證補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，於八解脫順逆入出身作證，多安住，而未能得諸漏永盡。是名身證補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a body-witness? It is one who has personally realized, and dwells extensively within, the eight liberations, entering and emerging from them in forward and reverse order, yet who has not yet permanently exhausted every outflow. This is called a body-witness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes the eight liberations already introduced in the Ground of Meditative Equipoise: liberation through contemplating external forms as impure while still perceiving inner form; liberation through the same contemplation once inner perception of form has already been abandoned; liberation through directly witnessing the pure aspect of form once practice has fully matured; and the four formless liberations — infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception — culminating in the eighth, the cessation of feeling and perception itself. A body-witness moves freely through all eight, forward from the first to the last and backward again, entering whichever is wished at will — "witnessed by the body" meaning realized through the mind's own direct experience (the treatise's "body" here referring to mental rather than physical embodiment) rather than merely understood conceptually. This mastery marks a non-returner of unusually advanced meditative accomplishment, though — crucially — not yet an arhat, since the subtlest afflictions of the three realms remain to be eliminated even after such complete command of absorption.

11. Milestone: Bound for at Most Seven More Rebirths — The Classic Stream-Enterer

The treatise names the stream-enterer proper: someone who has permanently severed three specific fetters, guaranteeing they can never again fall into a lower rebirth and will inevitably, eventually, reach full awakening.

Chinese Source

云何名為極七返有補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，已能永斷薩迦耶見、戒禁取、疑三種結故，得預流果，成無墮法，定趣菩提。……極七返有天人往來。極至七返，證苦邊際。如是名為極七返有補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by one bound for at most seven more rebirths? It is one who has permanently severed the three fetters — self-view, attachment to wrong rules and rituals, and doubt — thereby attaining the fruit of stream-entry, becoming incapable of falling into a lower rebirth, and certain to reach awakening. … Bound for at most seven more rebirths between the heavens and the human world, such a one reaches the very limit of suffering. This is called one bound for at most seven more rebirths.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "fetters" as afflictions that bind beings to the three realms, unite them with suffering, and poison body and mind at the root — and notes a natural question the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā addresses at length: since a stream-enterer actually eliminates all eighty-eight conceptually-based afflictions, why does the treatise single out only these three? Twenty separate reasons are offered there, boiling down to a few key ideas: these three are the most representative, since every other affliction ultimately traces back to one of them (wrong views branching from self-view, indulgent asceticism from attachment to rules and rituals, and further confusion from doubt); they are the most stubborn, persisting even in subtle form as habitual residue long after being formally cut (an arhat, for instance, might still say "my bowl" or "my robe" out of old habit, even with no genuine belief in a self left to possess anything); and naming them specifically matches how directly they obstruct the three approaches to liberation through emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness. Cutting all three guarantees a stream-enterer can never again descend to an unfortunate rebirth, and guarantees eventual arhatship — at the very most, seven more round trips between the human and heavenly realms, fourteen lives in total, before reaching the final limit of suffering.

12. Family-to-Family: A Stream-Enterer Advancing Toward the Second Fruit

Chinese Source

云何家家補特伽羅？謂有二種家家。一、天家家，二、人家家。天家家者，謂於天上，從家至家，若往、若來，證苦邊際。人家家者，謂於人間，從家至家，若往、若來，證苦邊際。當知此二，俱是預流補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a family-to-family practitioner? There are two kinds: the heavenly family-to-family, and the human family-to-family. The heavenly family-to-family is one who, passing from family to family in the heavens, going and coming, reaches the very limit of suffering there. The human family-to-family is one who, passing from family to family in the human world, going and coming, reaches the very limit of suffering there. Know that both of these are, in terms of fruit, still stream-enterers.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "family-to-family" as a stream-enterer who, rather than waiting passively through as many as seven remaining rebirths, actively continues cultivating the path and eliminates three or four of the nine grades of affliction still binding them to the desire realm, leaving only three or two further rebirths — passing, as the name suggests, from one rebirth "household" to the next — before reaching arhatship. Two dimensions distinguish the type: where final liberation occurs (the heavenly family-to-family reaches it among the gods, the human family-to-family among human beings) and how many further lives remain (three-life or two-life, depending on exactly how many grades of affliction have been eliminated). A further, more technical distinction the treatise develops at length divides each of these by whether the practitioner's remaining lives alternate evenly between the two realms or unevenly, driven by how strongly they still wish to shorten the remaining cycle — yielding eight named sub-patterns in total, each simply tracking a slightly different sequence of human and heavenly rebirths converging on the same eventual arhatship. Together with the stream-enterer bound for at most seven lives and the stream-enterer who attains arhatship in this very life, this brings the classical count of stream-entry's own internal varieties to ten, all sharing the same fundamental fruit while differing in how quickly and by what path they complete the journey to full liberation.

13. Milestone: One Interval Remaining, and Three Sparks of Nirvana in the Between-State

The treatise names a non-returner with only one rebirth left before arhatship, then names three vivid gradations of a rarer attainment: reaching final nirvana in the very interval between death and rebirth, illustrated through the image of a spark struck from iron.

Chinese Source

云何一間補特伽羅？謂即一來補特伽羅，行不還果向，已能永斷欲界煩惱上品、中品，唯餘下品。唯更受一欲界天有，即於彼處得般涅槃，不復還來生此世間。是名一間補特伽羅。云何中般涅槃補特伽羅？謂有三種……一、有一種中般涅槃補特伽羅，從此沒已，中有續生；中有生已，便般涅槃。如小札火，微星纔舉，即便謝滅。二、……中有生已，少時經停，未趣生有，便般涅槃。如鐵摶鋌，炎熾赫然，鎚鍛星流，未下便滅。三、……往趣生有，未得生有，便般涅槃。如彼熱鐵，鎚鍛星流，下未至地，即便謝滅。如是三種中般涅槃補特伽羅，總說為一中般涅槃補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner with one interval remaining? It is precisely the once-returner, now approaching non-returning, who has permanently eliminated the upper and middle grades of desire-realm affliction, leaving only the lower grade — needing only one further rebirth among the gods of the desire realm, attaining final nirvana there, never again returning to be reborn in this world. This is called one with one interval remaining. What is a practitioner attaining final nirvana in the intermediate state? There are three kinds. … One such practitioner, having just died here, no sooner has the intermediate-state body arisen than final nirvana occurs — like a tiny spark struck from flint, extinguished the very instant it appears. … A second, having remained briefly in the intermediate state, attains final nirvana before proceeding toward rebirth — like sparks flying from hammered iron glowing in the fire, vanishing before they fall. … A third, having proceeded toward rebirth but not yet arrived at it, attains final nirvana then — like those same sparks from the hammered iron, vanishing just before they reach the ground. These three kinds together are called, as one, a practitioner attaining final nirvana in the intermediate state.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "one interval remaining" as a once-returner who has pressed on to eliminate all but the very last, lowest grade of desire-realm affliction, leaving only a single further rebirth among the desire-realm gods (or, in a parallel human variant, a single further rebirth among humans) before reaching arhatship in that very life — the "interval" being that one remaining birth standing between the practitioner and final liberation.

He then unpacks the three gradations of nirvana reached in the intermediate state itself — the transitional consciousness-body traditionally understood to exist between death and the next rebirth. All three describe a non-returner who died in the desire realm carrying unusually strong momentum from prior practice, and who completes the elimination of the remaining subtle afflictions bound to the two higher realms sometime during that in-between state rather than waiting for rebirth to actually occur. The three differ only in exactly how much of that interval elapses first: the fastest attains it the instant the intermediate-state body arises, like a spark from flint gone before it's even fully visible; the middle speed attains it after some time has passed but before rebirth is actively underway, like sparks thrown from a red-hot iron under the hammer, extinguished before they can fall; and the slowest attains it only once already moving toward the next rebirth, not yet arrived, like those same sparks vanishing just before striking the ground. All three, different only in timing, are together classed as one single type: parinirvana in the intermediate state.

14. Nirvana Upon Rebirth, and the Opening of Nirvana Without Effort

Chinese Source

云何生般涅槃補特伽羅？謂纔生彼已，便般涅槃。是名生般涅槃補特伽羅。云何無行般涅槃補特伽羅？謂生彼已，不起加行、不作功用、不由勞倦，道現在前而般涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner attaining final nirvana upon rebirth? It is one who, no sooner having been reborn there, attains final nirvana. This is called a practitioner attaining final nirvana upon rebirth. What is a practitioner attaining final nirvana without effort? It is one who, having been reborn there, without arousing special exertion, without applying particular effort, without any real toil, has the path manifest before them and attains final nirvana.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes these from the three intermediate-state types by timing: rather than completing the journey before or during the transition itself, these practitioners actually complete rebirth in the form realm first. Nirvana upon rebirth means the remaining afflictions are eliminated very shortly after arrival, with minimal delay. Nirvana without effort goes further still: once reborn, the path simply manifests on its own, the remaining subtle afflictions of the two higher realms falling away without the practitioner needing to exert any special striving at all — momentum from prior cultivation carrying the work to completion almost automatically, in contrast with the next type the treatise is about to name, where genuine sustained effort is still required even after rebirth.

15. Milestone: Nirvana Through Genuine Effort, and Ascending Through the Heavens

Nirvana with effort closes the four ways a non-returner reborn in the form realm reaches arhatship. The treatise then names a non-returner who ascends progressively through successive heavens — sometimes leaping directly to the highest, sometimes passing through every level in turn — before finally attaining liberation.

Chinese Source

云何有行般涅槃補特伽羅？謂彼生已，發起加行、作大功用、由極勞倦，道現在前而般涅槃。是名有行般涅槃補特伽羅。云何上流補特伽羅？謂有不還補特伽羅，從此上生初靜慮已，住於彼處，不般涅槃。從彼沒已，展轉上生諸所生處，乃至或到色究竟天，或到非想非非想處。是名上流補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner attaining final nirvana through effort? It is one who, having been reborn there, arouses real exertion, applies great effort, and, through genuine toil, has the path manifest before them and attains final nirvana. This is called a practitioner attaining final nirvana through effort. What is a practitioner who ascends to a higher realm? It is a non-returner who, having been reborn in the first absorption, dwells there without attaining final nirvana, and who, upon dying there, passes progressively upward through successive rebirths, until reaching either the Highest Form Heaven or the Sphere of Neither Perception nor Non-Perception. This is called a practitioner who ascends to a higher realm.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts nirvana with effort against its counterpart: here the remaining afflictions yield only through genuinely sustained, effortful practice after rebirth, not automatically. He then describes the ascending type as a non-returner who, rather than completing the journey in the first absorption, dies there and rises through each successive level — the second, third, and fourth absorptions, through the four "pure abodes" at the peak of the form realm, and, for a duller variant, on through the formless absorptions to the very summit of conditioned existence — reaching final liberation only at whichever level marks the top of their particular ascent. Two temperaments distinguish this type: those who love wisdom, who practice absorption mixed with insight and reach liberation at the form realm's highest peak, and those who love concentration, who practice absorption alone and reach liberation only at the very peak of the formless realm. Each further divides into three, by how much of the intervening ascent is actually traversed: a "full leap" bypasses every intermediate level entirely, going directly from the first absorption to the final peak; a "half leap" skips some levels but not all; and a "traversal of every level" passes through each heaven in strict sequence, omitting none. Together with the earlier three-fold intermediate-state nirvana, nirvana upon rebirth, nirvana without effort, and nirvana with effort, this ascending type completes what the treatise calls the "seven destinies of the good person" — seven distinct routes by which a non-returner reaches final arhatship.

16. Milestone: Time-Liberated — Five Grades of Arhat Whose Freedom Depends on Conditions

The treatise turns from non-returners to arhats themselves, opening with the "time-liberated" — duller-facultied arhats whose meditative attainment remains genuinely vulnerable to relapse, and whose liberation depends on six specific favorable conditions actually being present.

Chinese Source

云何時解脫補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，鈍根種性，於諸世間現法樂住容有退失，或思自害、或守解脫，勵力勤修不放逸行。……是名時解脫補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a time-liberated practitioner? It is one of dull-facultied disposition, whose meditative ease in this very life remains genuinely susceptible to relapse — who may, in response, contemplate self-harm out of frustration, or simply guard what liberation has been attained, striving diligently and without heedlessness. This is called a time-liberated practitioner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies five distinct grades within this dull-root arhat type, distinguished by how they relate to the ever-present risk of losing their meditative attainment: the retrogressive type, prone to genuinely losing it when distracted by excessive activity, idle talk, quarreling, travel, or illness; the thought-prone type, who, fearing that same loss, contemplates ending their own life rather than risk falling back under affliction's power; the guardian type, who actively and successfully protects what's been attained through vigilant effort; the abiding type, who — while free of the strongest causes of relapse — simply maintains a stable plateau without further advancing, lacking the extra effort needed for genuine progress; and the progressive type, whose real capability lets them push forward through refining their own faculties toward the next classification, unshakeable attainment. Cháng Bǎi explains why this whole grade is called "time-liberated": their freedom manifests only when six specific favorable conditions actually converge — fine clothing, excellent food, comfortable bedding, a genuinely quiet dwelling, teaching precisely suited to their needs, and companionship with virtuous, easy-to-live-with fellow practitioners. Lacking any of these six, genuine liberation simply doesn't manifest for this type — hence "liberated only at the right time," when conditions happen to align.

17. Unshakeable Attainment: Liberation Independent of Circumstance

Chinese Source

云何不動法補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，與上相違，當知是名不動法補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a practitioner of unshakeable attainment? It is one who is the direct opposite of what was just described — know that this is called a practitioner of unshakeable attainment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this sharp-root arhat as the mirror image of the time-liberated in every respect: none of the six favorable conditions are actually required. Given coarse, discarded-rag robes rather than fine ones, plain and unappetizing food, rough bedding on bare rock, a noisy or unsuitable dwelling, teaching poorly matched to their needs, or company with difficult, virtue-lacking companions — liberation still manifests readily regardless, since it no longer depends on external circumstance at all. This type is also called "liberated beyond time," precisely because their freedom isn't conditioned on favorable timing the way the five time-liberated grades' freedom is.

18. Milestone: Wisdom-Liberated and Both-Liberated Complete All Twenty-Eight Types

The treatise closes its full taxonomy of practitioners with the two ways an arhat's liberation can be complete: through wisdom alone, having ended every affliction but not yet mastered the eight liberations in full meditative absorption, or through both wisdom and absorption together.

Chinese Source

云何慧解脫補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，已能證得諸漏永盡，於八解脫未能身證具足安住。是名慧解脫補特伽羅。云何俱分解脫補特伽羅？謂有補特伽羅，已能證得諸漏永盡，於八解脫身已作證具足安住，於煩惱障分及解脫障分心俱解脫。是名俱分解脫補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a wisdom-liberated practitioner? It is one who has already permanently exhausted every outflow, yet has not yet fully realized and dwelt within the eight liberations through direct experience. This is called a wisdom-liberated practitioner. What is a both-liberated practitioner? It is one who has already permanently exhausted every outflow, and has fully realized and dwells within the eight liberations through direct experience — a mind liberated with respect to both the portion belonging to the obstruction of affliction and the portion belonging to the obstruction of concentration. This is called a both-liberated practitioner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two final arhat types by exactly what has and hasn't been overcome: a wisdom-liberated arhat has genuinely ended every affliction — the obstruction to liberation itself — through the power of wisdom alone, but hasn't necessarily mastered the eight liberations' full meditative absorption, the deepest levels of concentration up through cessation itself; a both-liberated arhat has accomplished both, ending affliction through wisdom and also mastering the complete nine successive stages of absorption, including the cessation attainment, so that both the obstruction of affliction and the separate obstruction to deep concentration are fully overcome. The both-liberated arhat, Cháng Bǎi notes, is also the type most naturally associated with fully developed supernormal powers, since complete mastery of absorption is what supports their fullest expression.

With this, the treatise's complete taxonomy of twenty-eight practitioner types — the very first of the second yogic process's seventeen questions — closes in full, spanning every stage from the most basic temperamental distinctions through the subtlest refinements of full liberation.

19. Milestone: Establishing Practitioners — Eleven Principles Behind the Twenty-Eight Types

The treatise's second question opens: by what underlying principles were the twenty-eight types actually derived? Eleven distinct principles are named, each generating one or more of the categories just completed.

Chinese Source

云何建立補特伽羅？謂由十一差別道理，應知建立補特伽羅。云何十一差別道理？一、根差別故；二、眾差別故；三、行差別故；四、願差別故；五、行跡差別故；六、道果差別故；七、加行差別故；八、定差別故；九、生差別故；十、退不退差別故；十一、障差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

How are practitioners classified? By eleven distinct principles should the classification of practitioners be understood. What are the eleven distinct principles? First, difference of faculty; second, difference of assembly; third, difference of temperament; fourth, difference of vow; fifth, difference of practical approach; sixth, difference of path-fruit; seventh, difference of effort; eighth, difference of concentration; ninth, difference of rebirth; tenth, difference of retrogression versus non-retrogression; eleventh, difference of obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews how each of the eleven principles maps back onto categories already given: faculty-difference produced the dull- and sharp-facultied pair; assembly-difference sorts practitioners across the sevenfold monastic and lay community; temperament-difference produced the seven types dominated by desire, aversion, delusion, pride, discursive thought, balanced temperament, and light affliction; vow-difference distinguishes those who have taken up the hearer-disciple's aspiration, the solitary-realizer's, or the bodhisattva's; practical-approach-difference produces the four classical combinations of painful-slow, painful-fast, pleasant-slow, and pleasant-fast progress toward realization; path-fruit-difference produced the eight types approaching or abiding in each of the four fruits; effort-difference produced faith-followers and dharma-followers; concentration-difference distinguishes non-returners who master the eight liberations from arhats who do; rebirth-difference produced the eight non-returner sub-types tracking exactly how many further rebirths remain; retrogression-difference produced time-liberated and unshakeable arhats; and obstruction-difference produced wisdom-liberated and both-liberated arhats. Every one of the twenty-eight types just completed, in other words, traces back to exactly one of these eleven organizing principles — the treatise now turns to explain each principle in its own right, beginning with the difference of faculty.

20. Faculty and Assembly Close Briefly; Temperament Reopens With the Desire-Type's Visible Signs

Chinese Source

云何由根差別建立補特伽羅？謂根差別故，建立二種補特伽羅。一者、鈍根，二者、利根。云何由眾差別建立補特伽羅？謂眾差別故，建立七種補特伽羅。謂苾芻、苾芻尼、式叉摩那、勞策男、勞策女、近事男、近事女。……問：貪行補特伽羅應知何相？答：貪行補特伽羅，於諸微劣所愛事中，尚能生起最極、厚重、上品貪纏，何況中品、上品境界。又此貪纏住在身中，經久相續，長時隨縛。由貪纏故，為可愛法之所制伏，不能制伏彼可愛法。諸根悅懌、諸根不彊、諸根不澀、諸根不麤，為性不好以惡身語損惱於他，難使遠離、難使厭患，下劣勝解，事業堅牢、事業久固，禁戒堅牢、禁戒久固，能忍能受，於資生具為性耽染，深生愛重，多喜多悅，遠離顰蹙，舒顏平視，含笑先言。如是等類，應知是名貪行者相。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does difference of faculty classify practitioners? By difference of faculty, two kinds are established: the dull-facultied and the sharp-facultied. How does difference of assembly classify practitioners? By difference of assembly, seven kinds are established: monks, nuns, female probationers, male and female novices, and male and female lay disciples. … Question: What marks should one recognize in a desire-type practitioner? Answer: A desire-type practitioner, even toward the most trivial objects of attachment, can still generate the most extreme, heavy, superior-grade entanglement of desire — how much more so toward middling or superior objects. This entanglement, moreover, lodges within them, continuing without break, binding them over a long time. Through this entanglement, they are overcome by whatever is attractive, unable to master it in turn. Their faculties are pleasant and at ease, neither rigid, nor coarse, nor harsh; they are, by nature, disinclined to harm others through unwholesome speech or action; they are difficult to separate from desire, difficult to bring to genuine disgust with it, holding a settled, if inferior, conviction about it; their undertakings are steady and enduring, their discipline steady and enduring; they are able to bear hardship and endure it; toward the necessities of life they are, by nature, deeply attached, holding them dear; they are frequently joyful and cheerful, free of a furrowed brow, open-faced, direct in gaze, smiling and speaking first. All such traits together should be recognized as the marks of a desire-type.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats faculty and assembly as brief recaps — faculty simply restating dull versus sharp already covered, and assembly simply naming the sevenfold community of monks, nuns, female probationers, male and female novices, and male and female lay disciples already familiar from earlier fascicles. Temperament then reopens the same seven dispositional types from the original taxonomy, but this time with genuinely new content: concrete, observable behavioral signs a teacher could actually use to diagnose a student's dominant tendency at a glance.

For the desire-type specifically, Cháng Bǎi draws out the somewhat counterintuitive portrait the treatise paints: not someone visibly agitated or grasping, but someone outwardly pleasant — soft-eyed, easygoing, reluctant to cause others harm through word or deed, steady and persistent in both work and discipline (since desire itself sustains follow-through), patient under hardship, warm, quick to smile, and the first to greet someone — precisely because desire, unlike aversion, doesn't naturally produce hostility toward others. The real diagnostic sign is instead the difficulty of separating such a person from what they're attached to, and a settled, if ultimately mistaken, conviction that indulging sensory pleasure is worthwhile — visible less in surface irritability than in a persistent, cheerful clinging to comfort and possessions that resists any suggestion of letting go.

21. Milestone: The Visible Signs of Anger and Delusion Types

Where the desire-type's signs were surprisingly pleasant, the anger-type and delusion-type each carry their own distinctive, readily observable behavioral profile — one harsh and combative, the other dull and easily led.

Chinese Source

諸根枯槁、諸根剛彊、諸根疏澀、諸根麤燥，為性好樂以惡身語損惱於他，易令遠離、易令厭患……喜生忿怒，眉面顰蹙，恆不舒顏，邪睛下視，於他榮利多憎多嫉。如是等類，應知是名瞋行者相。……諸根闇鈍、諸根愚昧、諸根羸劣，身業慢緩、語業慢緩，惡思所思、惡說所說、惡作所作，懶惰懈怠……緣所牽纏，他所引奪，他所策使。如是等類，應知是名癡行者相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Their faculties are withered, rigid, alienating, and coarse; they are, by nature, prone to harming others through harsh speech and action; they are easily separated from what provokes them and easily brought to disgust with it … given to sudden anger, brow perpetually furrowed, face never open, gaze askance and downward, deeply envious and resentful of others' honor and gain. All such traits are the marks of an anger-type. … Their faculties are dull, clouded, and feeble; their bodily and verbal actions sluggish; their thoughts, words, and deeds unwholesome; given to laziness and idleness … entangled by circumstance, easily led astray by others, easily ordered about by others. All such traits are the marks of a delusion-type.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the anger-type as the desire-type's near-total opposite: withered, rigid features rather than soft ones; a harsh, combative manner rather than an easygoing one; genuine willingness to harm others through word or deed; work and discipline that can't hold steady, precisely because irritation keeps disrupting follow-through; a perpetually furrowed brow, a face that never opens, a downward, sidelong glare, and real envy at others' good fortune. The one advantage Cháng Bǎi notes: anger, once recognized, is comparatively easy to develop genuine revulsion toward — its ugliness is obvious even to the person feeling it, unlike desire's more disguised, pleasant-seeming grip.

The delusion-type, by contrast, is marked less by hostility than by a kind of pervasive dullness: slow reactions, slow speech, habitual laziness, tasks left unfinished, weak eloquence, poor memory, little genuine self-awareness, and views easily bent toward whatever's plausible-sounding rather than actually examined — someone genuinely attached to indulgence yet unable to articulate why, easily swept along by others' influence and direction since their own convictions were never firmly grounded to begin with. Cháng Bǎi notes this type faces the hardest road of the three: not lacking desire or anger's obvious energy to redirect, but lacking the basic clarity needed to even recognize what needs redirecting.
