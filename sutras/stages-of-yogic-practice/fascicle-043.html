Fascicle 43 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十三 · Meditation, Wisdom, and the Four Means of Gathering Beings

Stillness, Wisdom, and Kind Speech

Continuing directly from fascicle 42's completion of discipline, patience, and diligence, the treatise closes out the six perfections with meditative concentration and wisdom, then carries the four means by which a bodhisattva actually gathers and guides other beings — kind speech, beneficial conduct, and working alongside others — fully through to the concrete karmic fruits this entire body of practice produces.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation complete Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 43 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Perfection of Meditative Concentration Opens — a Lamp in the Wind, a Moon on Rippling Water

Fascicle 43 opens the thirteenth chapter of the Bodhisattva Stage's foundational section: the perfection of meditative concentration, immediately explaining why it is indispensable — the supernormal powers a bodhisattva needs to genuinely help beings never arise without it, and the undefiled wisdom that actually liberates never arises without it either — before laying out the same familiar ninefold verse already used for generosity, discipline, patience, and diligence.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處靜慮品第十三。云何菩薩靜慮波羅蜜多？嗢柁南曰：自性一切難　一切門善士　一切種遂求　二世樂清淨　如是九種相　名略說靜慮。謂九種相靜慮，名為菩薩靜慮波羅蜜多。一者、自性靜慮，二者、一切靜慮，三者、難行靜慮，四者、一切門靜慮，五者、善士靜慮，六者、一切種靜慮，七者、遂求靜慮，八者、此世他世樂靜慮，九者、清淨靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter thirteen: meditative concentration. What is a bodhisattva's perfection of meditative concentration? A summary verse states: "Intrinsic nature, comprehensive scope, difficult practice; comprehensive gateways, virtuous persons; comprehensive kinds, fulfilling requests; both-worlds happiness, purity — these nine kinds of mark are, in brief, called meditative concentration." These nine marks of meditative concentration together constitute a bodhisattva's perfection of meditative concentration: first, the meditative concentration of intrinsic nature; second, comprehensive meditative concentration; third, the meditative concentration of difficult practice; fourth, comprehensive-gateway meditative concentration; fifth, virtuous-person meditative concentration; sixth, comprehensive-kind meditative concentration; seventh, fulfilling-requests meditative concentration; eighth, the meditative concentration of happiness in this world and the next; ninth, purity meditative concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why meditative concentration occupies its particular place in the sequence of six perfections: generosity, discipline, and patience are all, at root, forms of worldly merit — genuinely valuable, but not yet capable on their own of producing either the supernormal abilities a bodhisattva needs to work effectively across countless world-systems, or the undefiled wisdom that actually cuts through affliction at its root. Both of those depend on genuine stillness first being established; as the classical saying goes, "gathering the mind into one place is itself a forest of merit, while scattering it even briefly is already a demon of affliction" — a mind that has learned to settle produces virtue the way a forest produces trees, densely and continuously, while a mind left to scatter, even for a short time, easily slides into the agitation from which affliction springs.

He passes along a vivid classical image for why an unsettled mind cannot support genuine wisdom: a scattered mind is like a lamp exposed to wind, its light guttering and unsteady, or like the moon's reflection on rippling water, breaking apart into scattered, indistinct fragments no matter how bright the actual moon remains overhead — in neither case can anything be seen clearly, however much genuine light or clarity might be present in principle. A mind this unsettled cannot reflect reality accurately regardless of how much doctrinal understanding it holds; only once the water itself grows still does the moon's reflection actually become visible. This is precisely why the treatise insists a bodhisattva must cultivate meditative concentration, and precisely why it now organizes that cultivation through the same ninefold structure already applied to every perfection so far. This closes the chapter's opening; the treatise turns next to meditative concentration's own intrinsic nature.

2. Milestone: The Intrinsic Nature of Meditative Concentration — Genuinely Refined, Never Merely Absorbed in Its Own Pleasure

What meditative concentration actually is at its root: a bodhisattva's genuinely excellent, one-pointed mind, whether worldly or transcendent, grounded first in hearing and reflecting on the great vehicle's own teaching, settled correctly in calming practice, insight practice, or their genuine union — deliberately excluding any concentration merely absorbed in its own pleasurable taste, since such attachment, however pleasant, is precisely what this perfection must rise above.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩自性靜慮？謂諸菩薩，於菩薩藏聞思為先，所有妙善世出世間心一境性，心正安住，或奢摩他品、或毗鉢舍那品、或雙運道俱通二品。當知即是菩薩所有靜慮自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's meditative concentration of intrinsic nature? Grounded first in hearing and reflecting on the bodhisattva canon, bodhisattvas possess a genuinely excellent, one-pointed mind, whether worldly or transcendent, correctly and settledly established — whether within the domain of calming practice, within the domain of insight practice, or within the joined path uniting both together. One should understand this to be a bodhisattva's own intrinsic nature of meditative concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the treatise's own careful qualifier here — "genuinely excellent" (妙善) — as deliberately excluding a common trap: ordinary meditative absorption, wherever it is pursued without genuine dharma grounding, easily becomes an object of craving in its own right, the practitioner growing attached to its pleasurable stillness the way anyone might grow attached to any other pleasant experience. Genuinely excellent concentration, by contrast, is free of this attachment from the very start, precisely because it is grounded first in hearing and reflecting on genuine teaching rather than pursued for its own pleasurable taste — the same danger already flagged in fascicle 41's own precept against becoming attached to the flavor of absorption.

He distinguishes worldly from transcendent one-pointedness along two possible lines: worldly concentration remains tainted by residual affliction even while genuinely settled, while transcendent concentration is joined with genuine liberating wisdom and free of affliction entirely; alternatively, concentration taking conventional truth as its object may be called worldly, while concentration taking ultimate truth, genuine suchness, as its object may be called transcendent. Either way, he stresses, the passage names three specific modes this settled mind may take: calming practice alone, insight practice alone, or the joined path where both operate together as a single unified process — precisely the same three modes already introduced in fascicle 42's own account of what genuinely mature diligence in meditation looks like. This closes intrinsic nature; the treatise turns next to comprehensive meditative concentration and its own three-part structure.

3. Milestone: Comprehensive Meditation Opens — Dwelling in Present Ease, Free of Pride, Free of Attachment

Comprehensive meditative concentration first divides into worldly and transcendent, each further unfolding into three: meditation dwelling in present ease, meditation drawing forth a bodhisattva's own uncommon meditative attainments, and meditation benefiting sentient beings. The first of these, dwelling in present ease, is defined with particular care: free of all conceptual discrimination, producing genuine bodily and mental ease, entirely free of pride in one's own attainment, and — most strikingly — a bodhisattva willing to set the pleasure of that very attainment aside and return to the desire realm rather than cling to it.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切靜慮？謂此靜慮略有二種。一者、世間靜慮，二者、出世間靜慮。當知此二，隨其所應復有三種。一者、現法樂住靜慮，二者、能引菩薩等持功德靜慮，三者、饒益有情靜慮。若諸菩薩所有靜慮，遠離一切分別，能生身心輕安，最極寂靜，遠離憍舉，離諸愛味，泯一切相，當知是名菩薩現法樂住靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive meditative concentration? This meditative concentration is, in brief, of two kinds: first, worldly meditative concentration; second, transcendent meditative concentration. One should know that each of these two, as appropriate, has three further kinds: first, meditative concentration dwelling in present ease; second, meditative concentration drawing forth a bodhisattva's own meditative attainments; third, meditative concentration benefiting sentient beings. The meditative concentration bodhisattvas possess that is free of all conceptual discrimination, that produces genuine bodily and mental ease, that is utterly and completely still, entirely free of pride, free of any attachment to its own pleasurable taste, and that dissolves every fixed characteristic whatsoever — this should be understood as a bodhisattva's meditative concentration dwelling in present ease.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the treatise's own gloss on each quality named here. "Free of all conceptual discrimination" means a bodhisattva, relying on this meditative concentration, observes every phenomenon as genuinely free of any fixed, nameable essence, resting the mind correctly in that recognition rather than in the endless conceptual overlay ordinary perception applies to experience. "Free of pride" means holding no doubt whatsoever about one's own genuine attainment — since real doubt-free confidence has nothing left to prove, and pride only ever arises from an underlying insecurity about one's own standing that genuine realization simply does not carry.

The most striking quality, he stresses, is "free of attachment to its own pleasurable taste," glossed by the treatise as a bodhisattva's genuine willingness to release the pleasure of a higher meditative state and consciously choose rebirth back in the desire realm rather than remain in the more refined, more pleasant heavens that deep concentration alone could otherwise produce — since remaining in genuine equanimity there would mean abandoning contact with the beings a bodhisattva has vowed to help, most of whom remain in the desire realm's own more turbulent, more suffering-filled conditions. And "dissolving every fixed characteristic" means recognizing that all apparent characteristics derive from conceptual naming in the first place; once the underlying naming is seen through as empty, the "characteristics" built on top of it dissolve along with it, and the mind settles into the signless absorption proper to genuine noble attainment. This closes meditation dwelling in present ease; the treatise turns next to the second kind — meditative concentration that draws forth a bodhisattva's own distinctive, uncommon attainments.

4. Milestone: Meditation Benefiting Beings Closes Comprehensive Meditation — the Same Eleven Activities, Now With Real Depth Behind Them

The third kind of comprehensive meditation: meditative concentration in benefiting sentient beings, which the treatise defines through the identical eleven activities already familiar from the discipline and diligence chapters — lending a hand, teaching the dharma, repaying kindness, protecting from fear, consoling grief, giving material support, properly leading a following, accommodating genuine needs, praising real virtue, correcting real wrongdoing, and displaying supernormal power — closing all three kinds of comprehensive meditation together.

Chinese Source

菩薩饒益有情靜慮有十一種，如前應知。謂諸菩薩依止靜慮，於諸有情能引義利彼彼事業，與作助伴，於有苦者能為除苦；於諸有情能如理說；於有恩者知恩知惠，現前酬報；於諸怖畏能為救護；於喪失處能解愁憂；於有匱乏施與資財；於諸大眾善能匡御；於諸有情善隨心轉；於實有德讚美令喜；於諸有過能正調伏；為物現通，恐怖引攝。如是一切總名菩薩一切靜慮。此外無有若過若增。

Ru-Yi Translation

A bodhisattva's meditative concentration in benefiting sentient beings is of eleven kinds, as should be understood from what was already described earlier: relying on meditative concentration, bodhisattvas lend a hand in whatever undertakings genuinely benefit beings, and relieve those in suffering of their suffering; correctly teach beings the dharma; recognize and openly repay those who have shown them kindness; rescue beings from every kind of fear; relieve grief in those who have suffered loss; give material support to those in need; skillfully lead and guide the larger community; skillfully accommodate beings' genuine needs; praise and delight in genuine virtue; correctly correct genuine wrongdoing; and, for the sake of beings, display supernormal powers, whether to inspire fear or to draw beings in. All of this together is called a bodhisattva's comprehensive meditative concentration — there is nothing beyond this, and nothing to add to it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this is the third time this exact eleven-fold catalogue has appeared across the treatise — first as a precept in fascicle 41's discipline chapter, then as an effort in fascicle 42's diligence chapter, and now as a meditative capacity here — and stresses that the repetition is deliberate rather than redundant: the identical activity of, say, protecting a frightened being from danger looks genuinely different depending on whether it is undertaken as a rule to be kept, an effort to be sustained, or a capacity flowing from genuine meditative depth. A bodhisattva who has actually cultivated real stillness brings a settled, undistracted presence to each of these eleven activities that mere discipline or mere diligence alone cannot fully supply — the same outward action, carried out with a mind genuinely unclouded by agitation.

He treats the treatise's own closing line — "there is nothing beyond this, and nothing to add to it" — as a deliberate echo of the identical claim already made for the entire structure of the bodhisattva precepts in fascicle 42, applied now to comprehensive meditation specifically: dwelling in present ease, drawing forth uncommon attainments, and benefiting beings together constitute the complete scope of what "comprehensive meditation" actually covers. This closes comprehensive meditative concentration; the treatise turns next to the meditation of difficult practice.

5. Milestone: The Meditation of Difficult Practice — Choosing the Desire Realm Over Heaven, Uncommon Absorption, and Speed to Awakening

The third meditation-category names three genuinely difficult achievements: a bodhisattva who has already mastered vast, excellent, thoroughly matured meditative attainment deliberately setting that very attainment's pleasure aside to be reborn in the desire realm out of compassion for beings there; drawing forth meditative attainments so far beyond anything a śrāvaka or solitary realizer could access that they cannot even imagine them; and meditative concentration so genuinely deep it brings supreme awakening within swift reach.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩難行靜慮？謂此靜慮略有三種。若諸菩薩已能安住廣大、殊勝、極善成熟、多所引發諸靜慮住，隨自欲樂，捨彼最勝諸靜慮樂，愍有情故，等觀無量利有情事，為諸有情義利成熟，故意思擇還生欲界。當知是名菩薩第一難行靜慮。若諸菩薩依止靜慮，能發種種無量無數不可思議，超過一切聲聞、獨覺所行境界菩薩等持。當知是名菩薩第二難行靜慮。若諸菩薩依止靜慮，速證無上正等菩提。當知是名菩薩第三難行靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's meditation of difficult practice? This meditation is, in brief, of three kinds. Bodhisattvas who have already come to abide in vast, excellent, thoroughly matured meditative attainments capable of drawing forth much further ability, yet who, according to their own genuine wish, set aside even the most excellent pleasure of that meditation, and, out of compassion for beings, contemplate with genuine equanimity the immeasurable work of benefiting them, deliberately choosing rebirth back in the desire realm for the sake of beings' genuine welfare and maturation — this is called a bodhisattva's first meditation of difficult practice. Bodhisattvas who, relying on meditative concentration, are able to draw forth countless, immeasurable, inconceivable attainments genuinely surpassing anything within a śrāvaka's or solitary realizer's own reach — this is called a bodhisattva's second meditation of difficult practice. Bodhisattvas who, relying on meditative concentration, swiftly realize supreme, complete awakening — this is called a bodhisattva's third meditation of difficult practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first difficulty as directly echoing the striking quality already named under "dwelling in present ease" — freedom from attachment to meditation's own pleasurable taste — but now emphasizing just how difficult that release genuinely is: a bodhisattva who has actually reached vast, thoroughly matured meditative attainment, capable of producing real supernormal ability, faces a genuine and rare temptation to simply remain in the refined peace such attainment makes available, and choosing instead to return to the far more turbulent, far more suffering-filled desire realm purely to remain within reach of beings still there is precisely why this qualifies as difficult practice rather than a simple continuation of prior effort.

The second difficulty he ties to the four distinctive powers of bodhisattva meditative absorption already introduced earlier in this fascicle — enabling every perfection's completion, drawing forth supernormal powers to mature beings, purifying entire buddha-fields, and completing the ten powers and other unique buddha-qualities — none of which a śrāvaka or solitary realizer's own meditation, however genuinely accomplished within its own scope, can access at all. The third difficulty simply names the treatise's own confidence that sufficiently deep meditative concentration functions as a direct accelerant toward the ultimate goal itself — genuine stillness is not merely a supporting practice alongside the path but one of its most direct, most effective routes. This closes the meditation of difficult practice; the treatise turns next to comprehensive-gateway meditation, naming the four classical absorptions themselves.

6. Milestone: Comprehensive-Gateway Meditation — the Four Classical Absorptions, From Discursive Thought to Perfect Equanimity

The fourth meditation-category names the four classical meditative absorptions themselves: the first absorption, still accompanied by directed and sustained discursive thought; the second, marked by genuine joy once that discursive activity has settled; the third, marked by a subtler, steadier pleasure once even joy's excitement has been released; and the fourth, marked by perfect equanimity once even pleasure itself has been set aside.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切門靜慮？謂此靜慮略有四種。一者、有尋有伺靜慮，二者、喜俱行靜慮，三者、樂俱行靜慮，四者、捨俱行靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-gateway meditative concentration? This meditation is, in brief, of four kinds: first, meditation accompanied by directed and sustained discursive thought; second, meditation accompanied by joy; third, meditation accompanied by pleasure; fourth, meditation accompanied by equanimity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the four classical absorptions and their defining supporting factors, worth knowing in outline for anyone engaged in serious meditation practice. The first absorption is reached by working through directed and sustained discursive thought to withdraw from the desire realm's own craving, aversion, and harmful intention, and carries five supporting factors: directed thought, sustained thought, joy, pleasure, and one-pointedness of mind. The second absorption is reached by observing the very drawbacks of that discursive activity — its inherent restlessness — until it settles into a genuinely still, unified clarity free of both directed and sustained thought, carrying four supporting factors: inner clarity, joy, pleasure, and one-pointedness; though the first absorption already includes some degree of joy and pleasure, the second cultivates both far more fully, which is why it earns the specific name "accompanied by joy."

The third absorption is reached by observing the drawbacks of joy's own excited, effortful quality, releasing the joy of the second absorption and settling into a subtler, steadier pleasure sustained through genuine mindfulness and clear awareness, carrying five supporting factors: mindfulness, clear awareness, equanimity, pleasure, and one-pointedness. And the fourth absorption is reached by observing the drawbacks even of that subtler pleasure — recognizing that any residual pleasant sensation still carries some subtle disturbance — and releasing it entirely to reach a perfectly settled equanimity, carrying four supporting factors: purified equanimity, purified mindfulness, neither-painful-nor-pleasant sensation, and one-pointedness. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this entire progression — from discursive thought through joy and pleasure to final equanimity — is detailed at much greater length in an earlier chapter of the treatise devoted specifically to meditative absorption, worth consulting for anyone wanting the complete causal and structural picture behind each stage. This closes the four classical absorptions; the treatise turns next to virtuous-person meditation, unfolding the four immeasurables.

7. Milestone: Virtuous-Person Meditation — Freedom From Attachment, and the Four Immeasurable States

The fifth meditation-category names five kinds: meditation entirely free of attachment to its own pleasurable taste, and the four classical immeasurable states cultivated within meditative absorption — loving-kindness, wishing beings genuine happiness; compassion, wishing suffering beings genuine relief; sympathetic joy, genuinely rejoicing in others' good fortune; and equanimity, wishing beings release from the craving, aversion, and confusion that attachment, aversion, and indifference respectively produce.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩善士靜慮？謂此靜慮略有五種。一者、無愛味靜慮，二者、慈俱行靜慮，三者、悲俱行靜慮，四者、喜俱行靜慮，五者、捨俱行靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's virtuous-person meditative concentration? This meditation is, in brief, of five kinds: first, meditation free of attachment to its own pleasurable taste; second, meditation accompanied by loving-kindness; third, meditation accompanied by compassion; fourth, meditation accompanied by sympathetic joy; fifth, meditation accompanied by equanimity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first category through its own classical counterpart named in an earlier chapter: a practitioner who has heard about the first absorption's genuine benefits but not yet learned how to move beyond it can easily become attached, clinging fixedly to that first taste of settled stillness rather than continuing to progress — freedom from this attachment is precisely what distinguishes genuinely virtuous meditation from meditation that has quietly become just another object of craving.

He walks through the four immeasurables as together forming the emotional core of a bodhisattva's own meditative cultivation: loving-kindness actively wishes happiness upon beings who currently have neither notable suffering nor notable joy, extending genuine warmth rather than mere neutrality; compassion actively wishes relief upon beings already suffering, the wish to remove pain rather than merely observe it; sympathetic joy takes active, genuine delight in the happiness and virtue beings already possess, the opposite of the quiet resentment or indifference such fortune can otherwise provoke; and equanimity releases the practitioner's own reactive tendency to feel craving toward pleasant beings, aversion toward unpleasant ones, and simple indifference toward neutral ones, cultivating instead a genuinely even regard for all. He notes the treatise's own cross-reference to a fuller account elsewhere of the three ways these four immeasurables can be cultivated — taking sentient beings themselves as the object, taking phenomena as the object once a bodhisattva has directly realized their empty nature, or taking no fixed object at all, the most complete and advanced mode — worth consulting for the full doctrinal picture. This closes virtuous-person meditation; the treatise turns next to the thirteen comprehensive-kind categories.

8. Milestone: Comprehensive-Kind Meditation's Thirteen Categories — By Ethical Quality, and By Meditative Function

The sixth meditation-category, comprehensive-kind meditation, names thirteen categories in two sets: six describing meditation's own ethical and functional quality — wholesome meditation, karmically neutral meditation used in supernormal display, calming-based meditation, insight-based meditation, meditation devoted to careful reflection on genuine benefit, and meditation that draws forth supernormal power — and seven describing meditation by its object or role within practice — meditation taking a name as its object, meditation taking the corresponding meaning as its object, and meditation organized around the specific signs of calming, rousing, equanimity, present ease, and benefiting others.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩一切種靜慮？謂此靜慮，六種、七種，總十三種。言六種者，一者、善靜慮，二者、無記變化靜慮，三者、奢摩他品靜慮，四者、毗鉢舍那品靜慮，五者、於自他利正審思惟靜慮，六者、能引神通威力功德靜慮。言七種者，一者、名緣靜慮，二者、義緣靜慮，三者、止相緣靜慮，四者、舉相緣靜慮，五者、捨相緣靜慮，六者、現法樂住靜慮，七者、能饒益他靜慮。如是十三種，名為菩薩一切種靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-kind meditative concentration? This meditation is, in brief, of thirteen kinds altogether — six of one sort and seven of another. The six are: first, wholesome meditation; second, karmically neutral meditation used in transformation; third, calming-based meditation; fourth, insight-based meditation; fifth, meditation devoted to careful reflection on genuine benefit to self and others; sixth, meditation that draws forth supernormal power. The seven are: first, meditation taking a name as its object; second, meditation taking the corresponding meaning as its object; third, meditation organized around the sign of calming; fourth, meditation organized around the sign of rousing; fifth, meditation organized around the sign of equanimity; sixth, meditation dwelling in present ease; seventh, meditation benefiting others. These thirteen together are called a bodhisattva's comprehensive-kind meditative concentration.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first six, organized by ethical quality and functional role: wholesome meditation is genuinely free of affliction, accompanying wholesome states such as the four immeasurables already discussed; karmically neutral meditation covers supernormal display undertaken for its own sake — practice or simple play — rather than deliberately to guide or benefit beings, which the treatise elsewhere classifies as wholesome specifically because of that compassionate intention; calming-based and insight-based meditation name the two foundational modes already introduced under intrinsic nature; careful reflection on genuine benefit names meditation devoted specifically to working out, through sustained contemplation, what actually helps oneself and others rather than merely settling the mind; and meditation drawing forth supernormal power covers both the abilities shared with śrāvakas and those unique to bodhisattvas alone.

The seven organized by object, he explains, trace the actual internal structure of meditative technique: taking a name as the object means resting attention on a word or phrase itself — a buddha's name, a mantra — without necessarily unpacking its full meaning discursively; taking the corresponding meaning as the object means instead holding the actual content that name points toward, visualizing a lotus, for instance, rather than merely repeating its name. The sign of calming, rousing, and equanimity repeat the same three technical meditative signs already discussed under fascicle 42's own account of purity diligence — resting on an object without further discursive elaboration, deliberately rousing a dulled mind with an inspiring object, and settling into effortless evenness once the mind no longer needs active adjustment in either direction. Present-ease meditation and benefiting-others meditation close the set by naming, once more, the actual fruit meditation produces — genuine ease in this very life, and the concrete capacity to help other beings — tying this thirteen-part breakdown back to the very same core concerns this whole meditation-category has explored from the start. This closes the thirteen comprehensive-kind categories; the treatise turns next to the eight fulfilling-requests categories, including the striking claim that a bodhisattva's meditative power can make even an otherwise powerless mantra genuinely effective.

9. Milestone: Fulfilling-Requests Meditation — Curing Disease, Calling Down Rain, and Making an Otherwise Powerless Mantra Work

The seventh meditation-category names eight genuinely practical powers meditative concentration makes available: neutralizing poison, curses, and demonic affliction, and making even an otherwise ineffective mantra genuinely work; curing disease caused by the body's own elemental imbalance; summoning rain during famine and drought; rescuing beings from danger on water or land, whether from human or non-human threats; conjuring food for those stranded without it; providing wealth to those in genuine need; admonishing careless beings across every world; and correctly acting on whatever a situation genuinely calls for.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩遂求靜慮？謂此靜慮略有八種。一者、於諸毒藥、霜雹、毒熱、鬼所魅等種種災患能息，能成呪術所依靜慮；二者、於界互違所生眾病能除靜慮；三者、於諸饑饉大災旱等現在前時，興致甘雨靜慮；四者、於其種種人非人作水陸怖畏，能正拔濟靜慮；五者、於乏飲食墮在曠野諸有情類，能施飲食靜慮；六者、於乏財位所化有情，能施種種財位靜慮；七者、於十方界放逸有情，能正諫誨靜慮；八者、於諸有情隨所生起所應作事，能正造作靜慮。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's fulfilling-requests meditative concentration? This meditation is, in brief, of eight kinds. First, meditation capable of dispelling every kind of disaster — poison, hail, deadly fever, demonic affliction — and of making mantras genuinely effective in doing so. Second, meditation capable of eliminating illness arising from imbalance among the body's own elements. Third, meditation capable of summoning genuine rain when famine or severe drought is genuinely present. Fourth, meditation capable of correctly rescuing beings from every kind of danger on water or on land, whether from human or non-human threat. Fifth, meditation capable of providing food to beings stranded in the wilderness without any. Sixth, meditation capable of providing every kind of wealth to beings in genuine need of it. Seventh, meditation capable of correctly admonishing and instructing careless beings throughout the worlds of the ten directions. Eighth, meditation capable of correctly carrying out whatever task genuinely arises for beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the six as the classical undefiled knowledges corresponding directly to the four noble truths and their completion: the knowledge of suffering, seeing the five aggregates through impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness; the knowledge of origin, seeing how afflicted existence is gathered together through craving and clinging as its cause; the knowledge of cessation, seeing the genuine ending of afflicted existence; and the knowledge of the path, seeing discipline, concentration, and wisdom as what actually carries a practitioner through to that cessation — the same four truths explored at the very foundation of the entire Buddhist path, now viewed specifically as forms of wisdom. The final two belong specifically to complete liberation itself: the knowledge that affliction is genuinely exhausted, confirming directly that suffering has been fully seen, its origin fully abandoned, its cessation fully realized, and the path fully cultivated; and the knowledge that no further rebirth in bondage will occur, confirming that this seeing, abandoning, realizing, and cultivating need never be repeated again — together marking an arhat's own complete attainment.

The seven he treats more briefly, given their technical density: direct knowledge and inferential knowledge distinguish realization of what is immediately observable — the desire realm's own five aggregates — from realization extended by inference to what is not directly observable — the subtler aggregates of the form and formless realms; conventional knowledge names the Buddha's own skillful use of ordinary, world-accommodating language to teach beings according to their actual capacity; the knowledge of supernormal powers spans the six classical abilities — divine sight, divine hearing, knowledge of past lives, mind-reading, miraculous ability, and the knowledge that ends all defilement; the knowledge of characteristics penetrates the three classical natures of phenomena detailed in the *Sutra Explaining the Profound Secrets* — the merely imagined nature ordinary confusion projects onto things, the dependently arisen nature phenomena actually have, and the perfectly accomplished nature that is simply reality as it is once confusion is removed; the knowledge preceding the ten powers names the seven preparatory practices — broad resolute understanding, seeking the true teaching, teaching it to others, practicing in accordance with it, correctly instructing others, correctly correcting them, and skillfully applying body, speech, and mind together — that mature into a buddha's own unique ten powers; and the knowledge of correct reasoning draws on the four classical kinds of valid reasoning — reasoning from observed regularity, reasoning that proves a thesis, reasoning from function, and reasoning from the simple nature of things — as the genuine logical foundation beneath correct understanding itself. This closes all thirteen comprehensive-kind categories; the treatise turns next to the eight fulfilling-requests categories of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the classical four unimpeded understandings as together constituting complete communicative mastery of the teaching: unimpeded wisdom regarding the teaching itself covers every synonym and alternate name a concept might carry; unimpeded wisdom regarding genuine meaning covers the actual reality any name points toward, not merely its surface label. 22. Milestone: The Six Perfections as One — a Great White Ocean of Virtue, a Gushing Spring of Treasure

The treatise steps back to view all six perfections as a single whole: everything the Buddha ever taught about them, scattered across countless individual sutras according to each audience's own capacity, is here deliberately gathered into this one unified ninefold framework — and the same framework, the treatise notes, applies equally to the four means of gathering beings still to come. He treats the three underlying marks as the more practically useful summary: words that please genuinely bring the listener joy and satisfaction, never spoken with the reluctant, burdensome tone of an unwelcome duty; words free of taint are genuinely true, spoken with a mind clean enough that speaking them never compromises the speaker's own discipline in the slightest, neither a lesser nor a graver violation; and words that are wholesome through and through — the dharma-aligned words and the benefit-drawing words together — genuinely lead a listener out of the confusion, harmful action, and suffering that afflict ordinary existence and into a place of settled goodness, bringing real benefit and ease both now and in lives to come. Bodhisattvas, through that same kind speech, reveal correct principles to beings, and, according to what is appropriate, guide them toward genuinely beneficial practice within what they have undertaken to learn, practicing in accordance with the teaching itself. In this conduct, bodhisattvas abide in genuine compassion, a mind free of any self-interested attachment, actively encouraging, correcting, settling, and establishing beings in practice. This is, in brief, called the intrinsic nature of beneficial conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the genuinely useful distinction the classical commentators offer here: kind speech is broadly explanation, laying out the teaching's own content clearly; beneficial conduct is broadly practice, actively moving a listener from hearing that explanation into genuinely undertaking it. The two share the same nine-category architecture and much of the same underlying material precisely because genuine explanation and genuine encouragement to practice are, at bottom, simply two inseparable aspects of the very same compassionate act — understanding and practice must advance together, neither complete without the other.

He unpacks the four classical modes of reasoning the treatise names as the actual tool of this guidance: the reasoning of dependence, recognizing that every phenomenon arises only in dependence on its own causes and conditions; the reasoning of function, recognizing that each thing has its own specific role — the eye sees, the ear hears, earth is characteristically solid, water characteristically flows; the reasoning of valid proof, establishing a claim's genuine truth through direct observation, inference, or reliable testimony; and the reasoning of the nature of things, simply the given, self-evident way things already are, such as fire's own heat. A bodhisattva skilled in weaving all four together can genuinely reach beings of very different temperaments and levels of doubt, since some listeners are moved most by seeing a cause explained, others by seeing a function demonstrated, others by seeing a claim rigorously proven, and others simply by having something's own evident nature pointed out plainly. This closes beneficial conduct's intrinsic nature; the treatise turns next to comprehensive beneficial conduct, opening with the crucial distinction between maturing a being not yet ready and liberating one who already is.

33. Milestone: Comprehensive Beneficial Conduct — Benefit Now, Benefit Later, and Benefit Spanning Both, Beginning With Honest Livelihood

Comprehensive beneficial conduct first distinguishes maturing beings not yet ready from liberating those already mature, then unfolds into three further modes matched to when the benefit actually lands: guidance toward benefit in this very life, guidance toward benefit in lives to come, and guidance toward benefit spanning both — the first of these opening with strikingly concrete, practical encouragement toward honest livelihood, built on genuine reasoning, genuine effort, and genuine skill.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know this conduct is, in brief, of two kinds: first, beneficial conduct that matures beings not yet mature; second, beneficial conduct that liberates beings already mature. Guidance toward benefit in this present life means correctly guiding beings, through sound reasoning, genuine effort, and genuine skill, to gather, protect, and increase their own wealth and standing — this is called guidance toward benefit in this present life. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the opening two-fold division as tracking a being's own actual readiness: someone whose faculties and wholesome roots are not yet mature needs guidance that gradually ripens them further, while someone already genuinely mature needs guidance that actually completes their liberation — the same basic distinction, he notes, as a teacher recognizing when a student needs more foundational work versus when they are ready to be pushed toward final mastery.

He treats the first of the three temporal modes as genuinely striking in its concreteness: rather than treating worldly prosperity as spiritually irrelevant or even suspect, the treatise instructs a bodhisattva to actively encourage beings toward it, provided it is built honestly — through sound, correct reasoning about cause and effect, through the bodhisattva's own or the being's own genuine effort and hard work, and through genuine skill rather than deception or exploitation. He glosses the three specific verbs carefully: "gathering" wealth through this honest combination of reasoning, effort, and skill; "protecting" it once gathered, through prudent care that keeps it from being carelessly lost; and "increasing" it further through the same combination sustained over time. The resulting benefit named — a genuinely good reputation and genuine ease in this very life — is, he stresses, itself the fruit of a specific kind of meditative or samādhi-adjacent contentment made possible by having one's basic material needs actually met, not a lesser, merely worldly consolation prize compared to spiritual attainment, but a genuine good in its own right that a bodhisattva has real reason to encourage. This closes the first temporal mode; the treatise turns next to the second — guidance toward benefit specifically in lives still to come.

34. Milestone: Encouraging Renunciation for Future Benefit, and Gradual Cultivation Toward Both — Comprehensive Beneficial Conduct Closes

The second and third temporal modes of comprehensive beneficial conduct: guidance toward benefit specifically in lives to come, encouraging a being to set aside worldly wealth and standing altogether and take up genuine renunciation, sustained through simple, humble alms rather than material accumulation; and guidance toward benefit spanning both this life and the next, gradually leading a being — whether lay or ordained — step by step toward genuine freedom from craving, yielding both settled ease of body and mind here and now, and either rebirth in a genuinely pure realm or complete liberation itself in the life to come.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Guidance toward benefit spanning both this life and the next means correctly guiding beings, whether lay or ordained, to gradually and progressively practice, moving toward genuine freedom from craving — this is called guidance toward benefit spanning both this life and the next. Through this, beings attain genuine bodily and mental ease in this very life, dwelling in settled peace; and in the life to come, they are reborn in a genuinely pure heaven, or enter complete liberation within the realm of nirvana without any residue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second mode as the direct counterpart to the first: where the first mode encourages honest worldly prosperity, this second mode encourages its deliberate release, taking up ordination and depending entirely on others' generosity rather than accumulated wealth — the treatise's own careful framing being that this path is assured of genuine future benefit "whether or not" present-life comfort follows, since renunciation trades immediate material ease for a far more valuable, far more certain future good.

He treats the third mode as the fullest and most complete of the three, spelling out its precise cross-reference: for lay practitioners, taking up the basic precepts of a devoted follower and gradually working to reduce unwholesome conduct and worldly craving, yielding no wrongdoing in this life and rebirth in a genuinely pure heavenly realm in the next; for ordained practitioners, taking up the full monastic precepts and gradually working through both the worldly path of releasing craving — yielding settled ease in this very life and rebirth in the form or formless heavens in the next — and the transcendent path of releasing craving, yielding, in the life to come, complete liberation within nirvana itself, free of any further residue of conditioned existence. With this, all three temporal modes, and with them both kinds of comprehensive beneficial conduct — maturing and liberating — reach their complete close. The treatise turns next to the difficult forms of beneficial conduct.

35. Milestone: Difficult-Practice Beneficial Conduct — Guiding Those With No Foothold, the Comfortably Careless, and the Firmly Mistaken

The third beneficial-conduct category names three genuinely difficult undertakings: guiding beings who have never yet cultivated any superior wholesome root to draw on; guiding beings so comfortably absorbed in vast wealth and every material luxury that genuine carelessness has become their default state; and guiding beings deeply committed to rival, non-Buddhist teachings, whose loyalty to their own tradition runs so deep it shades into open hostility toward the genuine teaching.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's difficult-practice beneficial conduct? One should know this conduct is, in brief, of three kinds. Bodhisattvas who are able to guide beings who have never yet developed any superior wholesome root — this is called the first difficult-practice beneficial conduct. Why is this so? Because such beings are genuinely difficult to guide. Bodhisattvas who are able to guide beings so absorbed in vast wealth and every kind of material fulfillment, already present before them, that genuine carelessness has become their settled state — this is called the second difficult-practice beneficial conduct. Why is this so? Because such beings remain absorbed in the vast places and habits of their own extreme carelessness. Bodhisattvas who are able to guide beings deeply committed to non-Buddhist teachings, holding wrong views and wrong conduct rooted in a rival tradition — this is called the third difficult-practice beneficial conduct. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first difficulty with beings entirely lacking the faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, or wisdom needed to even begin genuine practice — without any of these as a starting foothold, there is genuinely little for encouragement to take hold of. He identifies the second difficulty with what the classical teacher Zhiyi called "inverted good fruit" — genuinely good karmic results, wealth and comfort earned through real past merit, that paradoxically become an obstacle precisely because they are so comfortable, absorbing a being so completely in enjoying them that no motivation remains to look further; he distinguishes the classical terms for where such carelessness plays out — the actual places of amusement someone habitually frequents, and the physical residences, however grand, that make such comfort possible.

The third difficulty he treats as particularly stubborn precisely because it is not mere ignorance but genuine, committed loyalty — a follower of a rival tradition is often sincerely devoted, which makes their resistance to a different teaching a matter of settled conviction rather than simple unfamiliarity, considerably harder to shift than genuine open-mindedness would be. All three difficulties, he notes, share the same underlying structure already seen throughout this whole section of the treatise: genuine difficulty arises wherever an external circumstance — lack of foothold, comfortable privilege, or firm prior commitment — actively works against the very receptivity that guidance depends on. This closes difficult-practice beneficial conduct; the treatise turns next to comprehensive-gateway beneficial conduct, matched to the four classical roots still lacking in a given being.

36. Milestone: Comprehensive-Gateway Beneficial Conduct — Building Faith, Discipline, Wisdom, and Generosity Wherever Each Is Missing

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-gateway beneficial conduct? One should know this conduct is, in brief, of four kinds. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold structure as covering the essential foundation a genuinely balanced practice requires — faith to actually begin, discipline to sustain clean conduct, wisdom to see clearly, and generosity to genuinely open outward toward others — and notes the treatise's own repeated emphasis on carrying guidance "all the way to actually establishing it," not stopping at mere encouragement or correction. A bodhisattva may need to praise, encourage, gently correct, or even firmly reprimand and discipline a given being depending on what actually moves them, but the standard of success in every case is the same: the missing quality genuinely, lastingly established, not simply pointed out and left for the being to pursue alone. This closes comprehensive-gateway beneficial conduct; the treatise turns next to virtuous-person beneficial conduct.

37. Milestone: Virtuous-Person Beneficial Conduct — Five Qualities of Genuinely Skillful Guidance, and Comprehensive-Kind Conduct Opens

The fifth beneficial-conduct category names five qualities of genuinely skillful guidance: grounded in ultimate truth itself, correctly timed to a being's actual readiness, aimed at genuinely supreme benefit rather than anything lesser, delivered gently even when correcting real fault, and motivated throughout by real compassion. The sixth category, comprehensive-kind beneficial conduct, then opens with six further kinds tracing a being's entire arc from first contact with the teaching through to complete liberation.

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's comprehensive-kind beneficial conduct? One should know this conduct is, in brief, of thirteen kinds altogether — six of one sort and seven of another. What are the six? Bodhisattvas correctly gather in beings who should be gathered in; correctly tame beings who should be tamed; dispel the hostility of beings who resent and turn away from the sacred teaching; bring beings who remain genuinely undecided into the sacred teaching; correctly mature beings who have already entered the teaching within whichever of the three vehicles suits them; and bring beings who have already matured to genuine liberation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five virtuous-person qualities as together describing the difference between guidance that merely sounds wise and guidance that genuinely works: grounding in ultimate truth means teaching content that is actually, doctrinally sound rather than merely comforting; correct timing means teaching what a specific being can actually absorb right now, whether that is basic generosity and discipline for a beginner or the four noble truths for someone already more settled; aiming at supreme benefit means never settling for a lesser goal when a greater one is genuinely reachable; gentleness means that even necessary correction of real fault is delivered without harshness, never using rough language when raising a genuine problem; and compassion names the underlying motive running through all of it — guidance offered because the guide genuinely wishes the other person well, not from any other motive.

He treats the six comprehensive-kind categories as tracing a single being's entire possible journey, start to finish: gathering in those ready to be gathered, taming the afflictions of those already gathered, specifically dispelling the resentment of those actively hostile to the teaching (a harder case than mere unfamiliarity), drawing in those genuinely undecided rather than either committed or opposed, maturing those already inside the teaching according to whichever of the three vehicles genuinely fits their own disposition, and finally bringing those already mature all the way to actual liberation — six stages spanning the complete arc from a being's very first contact with the dharma to its final fruition. This closes virtuous-person beneficial conduct and opens the six qualities of comprehensive-kind conduct; the treatise turns next to its remaining seven categories.

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes these seven around five classical "places" a bodhisattva helps a being properly settle into, drawn from the Śrāvaka Stage's own detailed account: the place of guarding and nurturing the resources for concentration, ensuring sustained, undiminished effort; the place of genuine solitude, itself requiring a genuinely suitable physical setting, properly composed bodily posture during practice, and both bodily and mental withdrawal from distraction; the place of one-pointedness of mind, the settled, unbroken absorption built through the nine classical stages of mental abiding; the place of freedom from the obstructions that block concentration — timidity, the five hindrances, distracting thought, and restless self-satisfaction — each requiring its own specific antidote; and the place of cultivating the four mental applications that actually produce genuine absorption: training the mind to turn away from what should be renounced, nourishing the mind toward what is genuinely worth seeking, settling the body in genuine ease once training and nourishment are complete, and, on that foundation, cultivating the genuinely undefiled insight of the four noble truths.

Genuine skillful guidance, he stresses, respects a being's actual capacity rather than insisting everyone follow an identical route. With this, all thirteen comprehensive-kind categories of beneficial conduct reach their complete close. The treatise turns next to the eight fulfilling-requests categories, grounded in the classical eight afflictions that most commonly entangle a practitioner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the classical eight, worth recognizing individually since together they cover the most common ways practice quietly stalls. Shamelessness and lack of conscience — the first lacking any inner sense of having done wrong, the second lacking any concern for how that wrong reflects on one's teachers, family, or fellow practitioners — together corrode discipline at its root, since genuine restraint depends on caring that wrongdoing occurred at all. Torpor, the heavy dullness that leaves body and mind unable to engage a meditation object clearly, and drowsiness, the deeper collapse into actual sleep-seeking, both directly block concentration — the classical antidotes involve properly guarding the senses, eating in moderation, and maintaining the disciplined wakefulness already detailed in fascicle 41, together with visualizing light and reflecting on sleep's own genuine drawbacks.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the classical division of these eight across the three trainings: shamelessness and lack of conscience obstruct discipline; torpor, drowsiness, restlessness, and regret obstruct concentration; and jealousy and stinginess obstruct the shared harmony that makes genuine communal practice function at all — together, all eight stand between a sincere practitioner and genuine progress, which is exactly why a bodhisattva's skillful attention to untangling them, one at a time, counts as fulfilling-requests beneficial conduct. The ninth and final category, purity beneficial conduct, then opens with ten kinds divided into five addressing external, visible conduct and five addressing the bodhisattva's own internal motivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this ninefold category rests on the classical, deeply detailed teaching of karmic cause and effect underlying each of the ten unwholesome deeds and their wholesome opposites — killing ripens into short life and frequent illness, its opposite into long life; stealing into poverty, its opposite into genuine wealth; sexual misconduct into scandal and disloyalty, its opposite into being genuinely cherished; lying into being disbelieved even when honest, its opposite into being trusted; harsh speech into constant conflict, its opposite into a genuinely pleasant voice; divisive speech into fractured relationships, its opposite into harmonious family; idle talk into being ignored even in earnest, its opposite into being genuinely heard; craving into poverty and frustrated desire, its opposite into a healthy, sufficient body; aversion into being habitually resented, its opposite into a genuinely pleasing appearance and settled mind; and wrong view into duplicity and being born among those with false views, its opposite into a genuinely gentle nature and settled faith. He introduces the treatise's own division of purity beneficial conduct into external and internal halves through the classical commentary's own gloss: the five external categories concern how a bodhisattva's guidance actually appears and functions in the world, keeping those being guided genuinely free of fault; the five internal categories concern the purity of the bodhisattva's own motivating heart throughout — genuine compassion, sustained without fatigue, without any trace of arrogance, and entirely free of self-interest — since guidance that looks correct externally but is driven by a contaminated inner motive has not genuinely achieved this final, most demanding category. This closes both-worlds-happiness beneficial conduct and opens purity beneficial conduct's own two-part structure; the treatise turns next to its five external marks in detail.

41. Milestone: Purity Beneficial Conduct's Five External Marks — Freedom From Fault, Correcting False Paths, Gradual Teaching, Universal Reach, and Fitting Guidance

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the vajra-solid body as the fruit of the diamond-like concentration reached at the very threshold of buddhahood, where the seeds of both afflictive obstruction and obstruction to complete knowledge are simultaneously and permanently eliminated, transforming the eight consciousnesses into the four wisdoms of a fully awakened buddha — a transformation the treatise calls "vajra-solid" precisely because nothing can ever again disturb or unravel it. The lasting endurance of the true teaching names the simple historical fact that each buddha's own teaching continues to genuinely help beings reach liberation for a substantial span after that buddha's own passing, exactly as Śākyamuni's own teaching has continued to do.

He treats the second fruit, matchless and wondrous qualities, as the natural reward for undertaking what was genuinely difficult in life — a buddha's own qualities stand utterly without peer precisely because the practice that produced them was itself genuinely without peer in its difficulty. The third fruit, worthiness of universal offering, names one of the ten classical epithets of a buddha, "worthy of offerings," earned specifically through comprehensive practice that left no gateway of cultivation untouched. And the fourth fruit, supremacy among literally every kind of being across every possible category of existence — footless creatures, four-legged animals, formless beings, beings without perception at all, and beings dwelling in the very subtlest state of neither perception nor its absence — Master Cháng Bǎi treats as the natural completion of virtuous-person practice's own comprehensive reach, extended in this fruit to cover the complete taxonomy of conditioned existence itself, leaving no category of being outside a buddha's own supremacy. This closes the first four karmic fruits; the treatise turns next to the remaining four, culminating in the complete catalogue of a buddha's own unique qualities.

48. Milestone: The Final Fruits — the Marks of a Great Being, Invincibility at the Bodhi Seat, and Every Unique Quality of a Buddha — Fascicle 43 Closes

The final four karmic fruits, and with them the close of this entire fascicle: comprehensive-kind practice ripens into the thirty-two major marks and eighty minor marks adorning a buddha's own body; fulfilling-requests practice ripens into complete invincibility at the moment of awakening itself, unshaken by any of the four classical maras; both-worlds-happiness practice ripens into the very deepest meditative bliss a buddha alone can access; and purity practice ripens into the complete catalogue of a buddha's own wholly unique qualities — the four complete purities, freedom from ever needing to guard body, speech, or mind, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, and the eighteen qualities shared with no other being whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through this final, richest cluster of fruits with real care. The thirty-two major marks and eighty minor marks together constitute the complete physical splendor traditionally attributed to a buddha's own body, the coarser thirty-two visible even to ordinary observers and the subtler eighty apparent only to those with sufficiently refined perception. Invincibility at the seat of awakening recalls the classical account of the Buddha's own final battle against the four maras — the mara of the aggregates, of affliction, of death, and the heavenly mara — on the very night of enlightenment, remaining entirely unshaken by any of them. Supreme meditative bliss names the four form-realm absorptions, the eight liberations, and the various classical concentrations and attainments, experienced by a buddha in their most complete and refined form.

The final fruit, purity, he treats as the treatise's own grand summary of everything unique to complete buddhahood: the four complete purities — purified basis (the six sense faculties themselves), purified object (the six sense objects), purified mind, and purified wisdom; freedom from ever needing to guard body, speech, or mind, since a buddha's conduct in all three is already so completely free of fault that no special vigilance is ever required; the ten powers, spanning perfect knowledge of cause and effect, of karma and its ripening, of every meditative attainment, of beings' own relative faculties, of their varied inclinations, of the different realms of existence, of where every path ultimately leads, of past lives without limit, of beings' future rebirths through the divine eye, and of the complete and final exhaustion of every trace of affliction; the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness through which a buddha remains genuinely equanimous toward disciples who practice well, those who practice poorly, and those who do both by turns; and the eighteen qualities, among a traditionally counted one hundred forty unique buddha-qualities altogether, shared with absolutely no other being — not śrāvaka, not solitary realizer, not even an advanced bodhisattva still short of complete awakening.

He closes with the treatise's own explanation for a detail attentive readers might otherwise puzzle over: of the nine categories organizing every perfection and every means of gathering beings throughout fascicles 39 through 43, this closing section names karmic fruits for only eight, conspicuously omitting intrinsic nature. The reason, he explains, is structural rather than an oversight: intrinsic nature is the whole from which the other eight are drawn as its own specific aspects — reuniting all eight is simply intrinsic nature restated in full, so a separate fruit for intrinsic nature alone would be redundant, the part standing in for a whole that was never actually separate from it. With this, the treatise's complete account of the six perfections and the four means of gathering beings — every practice, every category, every fruit — reaches its final close, and with it, fascicle 43 in its entirety: opening with the perfection of meditative concentration, completing the perfection of wisdom and, with it, all six perfections together for the first time in this project, then carrying kind speech, beneficial conduct, and working alongside others each through their own rich detail, and closing with this final, comprehensive account of what all of it, sustained and purified, actually produces — nothing less than complete buddhahood itself.
