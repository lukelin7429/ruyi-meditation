How to Rightly Explain a Sutra

With the Determinations now closed in fascicle 80, the treatise opens its third of five major divisions: the Compendium of Explanations (攝釋分), spanning fascicles 81 and 82, on how a teacher should properly interpret and expound a sūtra. A summary verse previews its whole scope — the sūtra's own body of text and meaning, the method of explanation through five or six aspects, the qualities of a genuine teacher, the assembly and its listening, and praising the Buddha before teaching begins. Fascicle 81 opens with the sūtra's own body: text (the six kinds of what is spoken) and meaning (the ten kinds of what is meant), and the method of drawing out that meaning through summary, occasion, purport, resolving objections, and sequence.

1. Section: The Compendium of Explanations Opens — Closing the Determinations, and a Summary Verse Previewing Its Whole Scope

The Compendium of Explanations, part one. Thus has the explanation of the Determinations already been spoken. What, now, is the Compendium of Explanations? The overall summary verse says: Body, explanation, text, meaning, dharma; occasion, meaning, objection, sequence, teacher; the speaker, the listener, and praising the Buddha; and, in brief and in detail, the benefit of study.

Chinese Source

攝釋分之上。如是已說釋攝決擇。云何攝釋？總嗢柁南曰：體釋文義法，起義難次師，說眾聽讚佛，略廣學勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Compendium of Explanations, part one.
Thus has the explanation of the Determinations already been spoken. What, now, is the Compendium of Explanations?
The overall summary verse says: Body, explanation, text, meaning, dharma; occasion, meaning, objection, sequence, teacher; the speaker, the listener, and praising the Buddha; and, in brief and in detail, the benefit of study.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise now opens its third of five major divisions. A sūtra is called "contractual" (契, qì) because it accords both with ultimate truth and with the capacities of its hearers — a teaching that is at once true and rightly fitted to its audience. This Compendium exists to teach exactly how such a text should be understood and explained, spanning two fascicles (81–82), and the summary verse previews the whole of it. "Body" names the sūtra's own text and meaning; "explanation," together with the four terms that follow (occasion, meaning, objection, sequence), names the five aspects a teacher draws out in expounding it — what is to be taught, why it arose, what it means, how objections to it are resolved, and in what order it unfolds. "Teacher" names the ten qualities required of a genuine expounder. "The speaker, the listener, and praising the Buddha" cover, respectively, the eight qualities of skillful speech suited to five different kinds of listener, the ten-point standard for how a listener ought to attend, and the practice of praising the Buddha, briefly or at length, before teaching begins. And the verse closes by promising a demonstration, through an actual sūtra passage, of how to explain a text in practice, and the benefit that genuine study brings — the complete program for both fascicle 81 and fascicle 82 together.

2. Section: The Sutra's Body — Text as What Is Depended Upon, Meaning as What Depends on It

What is the body? It is the body of a sūtra. In brief, it is of two kinds: first, text; and second, meaning. Text is what is depended upon; meaning is what depends on it. These two together are called, in sum, the entire domain of what is known.

Chinese Source

云何為體？謂契經體。略有二種。一、文，二、義。文是所依，義是能依。如是二種，總名一切所知境界。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the body? It is the body of a sūtra. In brief, it is of two kinds: first, text; and second, meaning. Text is what is depended upon; meaning is what depends on it.
These two together are called, in sum, the entire domain of what is known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Following the summary verse's own order, the treatise now unfolds "body" first: the very substance of what a sūtra is. A sūtra ("contractual scripture") is composed of two elements at once. Text is the actual body of names, phrases, and syllables strung together into an ordered scripture — the tool a teaching depends on to be conveyed at all. Meaning is the principle or reality those words point toward — what depends on the text to become manifest, since meaning cannot show itself without some vehicle of expression. Considered as what supports and what is supported, text is the support and meaning what rests upon it; considered instead as what expresses and what is expressed, text is the expressing agent and meaning the content expressed. Text alone, without meaning, could never produce genuine understanding in a listener; meaning alone, without text, could never be conveyed at all — a sūtra's body is complete only when both are present together, and it is precisely this combination that constitutes the entire domain a listener comes to know through study.

3. Section: Explanation's Five Aspects — What Kind of Teaching, Why It Arose, What It Means, Resolving Objections, and Its Sequence

What is explanation? In brief, it is of five kinds. First, the dharma; second, the occasion; third, the meaning; fourth, resolving objections; and fifth, the sequence.

Chinese Source

云何為釋？謂略有五。一者、法，二者、等起，三者、義，四者、釋難，五者、次第。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is explanation? In brief, it is of five kinds. First, the dharma; second, the occasion; third, the meaning; fourth, resolving objections; and fifth, the sequence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having named the sūtra's body, the treatise now names the five aspects of genuinely explaining it. The dharma identifies which of the twelve divisions of scripture the text being explained belongs to. The occasion traces the specific causes and conditions — who asked, in what circumstance, at what time — that gave rise to this particular teaching being spoken at all. The meaning draws out the principle the text conveys, itself analyzable through ten further categories to be explained shortly. Resolving objections means posing, and then answering, the difficulties a passage might raise — whether self-posed or drawn from another's actual question — so that whatever remains unclear is brought fully into the light. And the sequence means explaining the text in its proper order, one point building on the last. These five aspects, briefly named here, will each receive extended treatment as the fascicle proceeds.

4. Section: Detailed Explanation Begins — Six Kinds of Text: Names, Phrases, Syllables, Speech, Mode of Address, and the Occasion for Asking

What is text? It is of six kinds. First, a body of names; second, a body of phrases; third, a body of syllables; fourth, speech; fifth, mode of address; and sixth, the occasion for asking.

Chinese Source

云何為文？謂有六種。一者、名身，二者、句身，三者、字身，四者、語，五者、行相，六者、機請。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is text? It is of six kinds. First, a body of names; second, a body of phrases; third, a body of syllables; fourth, speech; fifth, mode of address; and sixth, the occasion for asking.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise now turns from the brief overview to a detailed, separate explanation of the sūtra's body — text first, then meaning. Text, it turns out, is not simply the names, phrases, and syllables of ordinary grammar; it comprises six elements together. A body of names, a body of phrases, and a body of syllables together constitute the raw linguistic material of any teaching. Speech names the actual spoken quality of the teaching. Mode of address names the manner or angle from which a teaching approaches its subject. And the occasion for asking names the specific request — from one of twenty-seven distinguishable kinds of listener — that prompted the teaching to be given. Each of these six will now be explained in turn, beginning with the body of names.

5. Section: A Body of Names — the Commonly Known Designation Superimposed Upon What Has No Words of Its Own

A body of names is what is called a commonly known designation.

Chinese Source

名身者，謂共知增語。

Ru-Yi Translation

A body of names is what is called a commonly known designation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A single name is simply a name; two or more together form a "body" of names. "Commonly known" means the shared understanding by which a community jointly recognizes a given word; "designation" (literally, "added-on speech") marks that every dharma is, in its own true nature, without any inherent verbal expression — language is something superimposed upon it after the fact, never the thing itself. The *Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra* teaches that dharmas have no own-nature and no verbal expression either, and that those who fail to see this empty of even emptiness wander on in confusion — yet the Buddha still chose to speak, comparing himself to a king who first grants a prince some token gift before conferring the real reward of land and subjects: words first point toward the deep truth so listeners can get some purchase on it, after which what has actually been personally realized can be conveyed more directly. Language conveyed this way remains only an approximation of what is truly profound, since the real nature of things can only be known by directly, non-conceptually realizing it within, free of all discursive elaboration — yet because that direct realization cannot otherwise be communicated to another, provisionally-posited names remain the necessary means by which a listener is first brought toward it.

6. Section: Twelve Kinds of Name — Imputed Names for the Self and Real-Thing Names for What Genuinely Exists

This, in brief, is of twelve kinds. First, imputed names; second, real-thing names; third, names correlated by common kind; fourth, names correlated by distinct kind; fifth, names following a quality; sixth, arbitrarily-assigned names; seventh, commonly-understood names; eighth, not-commonly-understood names; ninth, self-evident names; tenth, non-self-evident names; eleventh, abbreviated names; and twelfth, expanded names. An imputed name is: with respect to what is internal, the imputed designation of "I," "sentient being," "one possessed of a life-span," and the like; with respect to what is external, the imputed designation of "vase," "garment," and the like.

Chinese Source

此復略說有十二種。一者、假立名，二者、實事名，三者、同類相應名，四者、異類相應名，五者、隨德名，六者、假說名，七者、同所了名，八者、非同所了名，九者、顯名，十者、不顯名，十一者、略名，十二者、廣名。假立名者，謂於內假立我及有情、命者等名，於外假立瓶、衣等名。

Ru-Yi Translation

This, in brief, is of twelve kinds. First, imputed names; second, real-thing names; third, names correlated by common kind; fourth, names correlated by distinct kind; fifth, names following a quality; sixth, arbitrarily-assigned names; seventh, commonly-understood names; eighth, not-commonly-understood names; ninth, self-evident names; tenth, non-self-evident names; eleventh, abbreviated names; and twelfth, expanded names.
An imputed name is: with respect to what is internal, the imputed designation of "I," "sentient being," "one possessed of a life-span," and the like; with respect to what is external, the imputed designation of "vase," "garment," and the like.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Maitreya Bodhisattva's own great wisdom classifies every "commonly known designation" into twelve categories, all of them superimposed labels laid over things, yet still capable of picking out an object or a concept and making communication possible. A closely parallel twelfth-fold list of "modes of names" appears earlier in the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, with largely matching content under slightly different labels, useful for cross-reference. The first, imputed names, covers exactly the kind of naming already flagged as problematic throughout the treatise's earlier discussions of selflessness: internally, designating the aggregated body-and-mind as "I," "a sentient being," "one possessed of a life-span" (or, elsewhere, "agent," "person," "one who repeatedly takes rebirth," "ordinary being," "young one" — or simply "unenlightened" versus "noble"); externally, designating the aggregated elements and their derived forms as "vase," "garment," and so on. Every name a sentient being ordinarily uses to pick out anything, inner or outer, is in this sense imputed — projected onto a composite that has no such fixed identity in itself.

7. Section: Real-Thing Names for What Genuinely Functions, and Names Shared by an Entire Kind

A real-thing name is: with respect to the meaning of the eye and the other faculties, and of form and the other objects, positing names such as "eye" and the rest. A name correlated by common kind is: names such as "sentient being," "form," "feeling," and "great element."

Chinese Source

實事名者，謂於眼等、色等諸根義中，立眼等名。同類相應名者，謂有情、色、受、大種等名。

Ru-Yi Translation

A real-thing name is: with respect to the meaning of the eye and the other faculties, and of form and the other objects, positing names such as "eye" and the rest.
A name correlated by common kind is: names such as "sentient being," "form," "feeling," and "great element."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Real-thing names differ from imputed names in what they are attached to: where "I" names a mere aggregation with no such single referent findable within it, "eye" and the other sense-faculties are seeds-produced dharmas with genuine function — the eye genuinely, functionally exists, even though "I" does not. This is a relative, not an absolute, reality — still relative to the further distinction, drawn later, between merely provisional designation and what a noble one's wisdom directly realizes — but it marks a real difference nonetheless: names for the composite whole (an imputed vase) versus names for the functioning constituents that compose it (the real earth, water, fire, air, and sense-data that make up the vase). Among physical dharmas specifically, only fourteen count as strictly real-thing names — the five sense-faculties, the five corresponding sense-objects, and the four great elements — everything else being, strictly speaking, an imputed name for their composite. Names correlated by common kind, meanwhile, group many individual things under one shared label according to a common property: every sentient being, whatever the individual, shares the name "sentient being"; every solid, liquid, warm, or mobile element shares the name "earth," "water," "fire," or "wind" respectively; internal phenomena are similarly grouped as "form," "feeling," and so on — a name applied by kind rather than to any single individual.

8. Section: Names Distinguishing One Individual From Another, and Names That Simply Follow a Thing's Own Function

A name correlated by distinct kind is: names such as "Buddhadatta," "Guṇamitra," "blue," and "yellow." A name following a quality is: because it obstructs and can be broken, it is named "form"; because it receives and takes in, it is named "feeling"; because it emits light, it is named "sun" — names such as these.

Chinese Source

異類相應名者，謂佛授、德友、青、黃等名。隨德名者，謂變礙故名色，領納故名受，發光故名日，如是等名。

Ru-Yi Translation

A name correlated by distinct kind is: names such as "Buddhadatta," "Guṇamitra," "blue," and "yellow."
A name following a quality is: because it obstructs and can be broken, it is named "form"; because it receives and takes in, it is named "feeling"; because it emits light, it is named "sun" — names such as these.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Common-kind and distinct-kind names are correlative opposites: common-kind names group by the shared, general characteristic ("human being"), while distinct-kind names pick out the particular that distinguishes one member of a kind from another within it ("Buddhadatta" as this particular human, "blue" as this particular color among colors) — every human shares "human," yet each has a personal name marking individual distinction, just as the general category "color" resolves into the distinct names "blue" and "yellow." Names following a quality, the fifth kind, are named directly for a thing's own characteristic function rather than by any conventional or arbitrary label: matter is called "form" because it is subject to obstruction and to being broken apart; feeling is called "feeling" because its very function is to receive and register agreeable or disagreeable contact — without genuine receiving there is no feeling at all, exactly as words spoken to someone not listening produce no felt response; and the sun is named for its capacity to emit light, just as a lamp might be. In each case the name simply follows what the thing actually does.

9. Section: A Poor Man Called "Rich" — Names Assigned Without Any Regard for What They Actually Denote, and Names Everyone or Only Some Understand

An arbitrarily-assigned name is: calling a poor man "rich," or any other name posited without regard to its meaning. A commonly-understood name is: a shared, agreed-upon understanding. The reverse of this is a not-commonly-understood name.

Chinese Source

假說名者，謂呼貧名富，若餘所有不觀待義，安立其名。同所了名者，謂共所解想。與此相違，是非同所了名。

Ru-Yi Translation

An arbitrarily-assigned name is: calling a poor man "rich," or any other name posited without regard to its meaning.
A commonly-understood name is: a shared, agreed-upon understanding. The reverse of this is a not-commonly-understood name.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Arbitrarily-assigned names differ from imputed names in a crucial way: an imputed name (like "vase") still rests on some genuine composite basis, while an arbitrarily-assigned name has no such correspondence at all — calling a poor man "rich," giving a child born under an inauspicious sign a name meant to compensate for it, or naming a person "monk" though no ordination was ever taken, are all names assigned with no regard whatsoever for what they actually denote, sometimes even directly contradicting it; the tortoise and the hare are themselves imputed names for real animals, but "the tortoise's fur" and "the hare's horn" — things that do not exist — are arbitrarily-assigned names with nothing real behind them at all. Commonly-understood and not-commonly-understood names, by contrast, concern only the breadth of a shared community's familiarity: a landmark's name known throughout a whole region, like a great mountain range, is commonly understood there; the same landmark's name in a language or convention foreign to that community remains, for them, not commonly understood — the same distinction separating, for instance, ordinary vocabulary from a given profession's own specialized terminology, well understood by insiders and opaque to everyone else.

10. Section: A Meaning Hard to Grasp, a Name of a Single Syllable, and a Name of Many — Closing All Twelve Kinds of Name Entirely

A non-self-evident name is: one whose meaning is difficult to grasp, such as the Draviḍa mantras and the like. An abbreviated name is: a name of a single syllable. An expanded name is: a name of many syllables.

Chinese Source

不顯名者，謂其義難了，如達羅弭荼明呪等。略名者，謂一字名。廣名者，謂多字名。

Ru-Yi Translation

A non-self-evident name is: one whose meaning is difficult to grasp, such as the Draviḍa mantras and the like.
An abbreviated name is: a name of a single syllable. An expanded name is: a name of many syllables.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where a self-evident name's meaning is grasped immediately, a non-self-evident name's meaning resists understanding — mantras from the Draviḍa region of ancient India, whose sounds are recognized without their sense being known, exemplify names known by form alone. Abbreviated and expanded names then close the twelvefold list on a simpler note: "I" is a single-syllable, abbreviated name, while "person" (pudgala) strings together many syllables into an expanded one. With this, all twelve kinds of name — imputed, real-thing, common-kind, distinct-kind, quality-following, arbitrarily-assigned, commonly-understood, not-commonly-understood, self-evident, non-self-evident, abbreviated, and expanded — are fully explained, closing the body of names entirely. A sūtra draws on all twelve as needed — self-evident here, non-self-evident there, commonly understood in one place and not in another — which is exactly why each requires its own explanation. The treatise turns next to the body of phrases.

11. Section: A Body of Phrases — Names Brought to Completion, in Six Kinds: Incomplete and Complete, Established and Establishing, Topic and Explanation

A body of phrases is what is called the completion of names. This, further, is of six kinds. First, an incomplete phrase; second, a complete phrase; third, an established phrase; fourth, an establishing phrase; fifth, a topic phrase; and sixth, an explanatory phrase.

Chinese Source

句身者，謂名字圓滿。此復六種。一者、不圓滿句，二者、圓滿句，三者、所成句，四者、能成句，五者、標句，六者、釋句。

Ru-Yi Translation

A body of phrases is what is called the completion of names.
This, further, is of six kinds. First, an incomplete phrase; second, a complete phrase; third, an established phrase; fourth, an establishing phrase; fifth, a topic phrase; and sixth, an explanatory phrase.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where a name expresses only a thing's own bare identity, a phrase — the *Establishment of Consciousness-Only* teaches that "a name expresses own-nature, a phrase expresses distinction" — is needed to express the fuller relations and distinctions between things; one phrase, or two or more together, brings a name's meaning to its completion. Six kinds are named in three natural pairs: incomplete versus complete (a matter of whether the sense is finished or not), established versus establishing (a matter of what supports what logically), and topic versus explanation (a matter of what is announced versus what unpacks it) — each pair now to be illustrated with an example.

12. Section: "Refrain From Every Evil" — an Incomplete Phrase Needing Its Partner, and the Complete Phrase It Becomes

An incomplete phrase is one whose text is not yet finished, and whose meaning is not yet finished; know that it is only through a second phrase that completion is reached. As when it is said: "Refrain from every evil; uphold every good; well subdue one's own mind — this is the teaching of every buddha." If one says only "every evil," the text is not finished. If one says "every evil ones," the meaning is not finished. Only by further adding "refrain from" does it reach completion — this is then a complete phrase.

Chinese Source

不圓滿句者，謂文不究竟，義不究竟。當知復由第二句故，方得圓滿。如說：諸惡者莫作，諸善者奉行，善調伏自心，是諸佛聖教。若唯言諸惡，則文不究竟。若言諸惡者，則義不究竟。更加莫作，方得圓滿，即圓滿句。

Ru-Yi Translation

An incomplete phrase is one whose text is not yet finished, and whose meaning is not yet finished; know that it is only through a second phrase that completion is reached.
As when it is said: "Refrain from every evil; uphold every good; well subdue one's own mind — this is the teaching of every buddha." If one says only "every evil," the text is not finished. If one says "every evil ones," the meaning is not finished. Only by further adding "refrain from" does it reach completion — this is then a complete phrase.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This famous stanza — refraining from evil, upholding good, and subduing one's own mind as the essence of every buddha's teaching — illustrates the distinction precisely. "Every evil" alone is not even grammatically finished, leaving a listener with no idea what is being said about evil at all. "Every evil ones" completes the grammar but leaves the meaning hanging — what about them? Only "refrain from every evil" finishes both text and meaning together, becoming a genuinely complete phrase; everyday speech, when too clipped or elliptical, often falls into exactly this kind of incompleteness and invites misunderstanding as a result.

13. Section: "All Formations Are Impermanent" — What Is Established and What Establishes It, and a Topic Followed by Its Explanation — Closing the Body of Phrases Entirely

An established phrase is one where the earlier phrase is established only through the later phrase. As when it is said: "All formations are impermanent, subject to the dharma of arising and ceasing; because whatever is born must perish, their very quiescence is bliss." Within this, in order to establish that all formations are impermanent, it is then said that they are subject to the dharma of arising and ceasing. What precedes is what is established — the established phrase; what follows establishes it — the establishing phrase. A topic phrase is as when it is said "wholesome by nature"; an explanatory phrase is as when it is said "one who rightly proceeds toward being a good person."

Chinese Source

所成句者，謂前句由後句方得成立。如說：諸行無常，有起盡法，生必滅故，彼寂為樂。此中為成諸行無常，故次說言有起盡法。前是所成，即所成句；後是能成，即能成句。標句者，如言善性。釋句者，謂正趣善士。

Ru-Yi Translation

An established phrase is one where the earlier phrase is established only through the later phrase. As when it is said: "All formations are impermanent, subject to the dharma of arising and ceasing; because whatever is born must perish, their very quiescence is bliss." Within this, in order to establish that all formations are impermanent, it is then said that they are subject to the dharma of arising and ceasing. What precedes is what is established — the established phrase; what follows establishes it — the establishing phrase.
A topic phrase is as when it is said "wholesome by nature"; an explanatory phrase is as when it is said "one who rightly proceeds toward being a good person."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This well-known verse illustrates established and establishing phrases directly: "all formations are impermanent" is the claim to be established, and "subject to the dharma of arising and ceasing" is what establishes it, explaining precisely why formations count as impermanent — subject to birth, they are necessarily subject to death, so quiescence, once birth and death themselves cease, is genuine bliss. Topic and explanatory phrases work similarly but more simply: "wholesome by nature" merely announces a topic, while "one who rightly proceeds toward being a good person" — someone genuinely capable of cultivating wholesome dharmas — explains what that topic actually means; a summary verse functions as a topic phrase in just this way, with the prose exposition that follows serving as its explanatory phrase. With this, all six kinds of phrase — incomplete and complete, established and establishing, topic and explanatory — are fully explained through their three natural pairs, closing the body of phrases entirely. The treatise turns next to the body of syllables.

14. Section: A Body of Syllables — the Forty-Nine Letters Underlying Every Name and Phrase, Whether Finished or Not

A body of syllables is what is called the forty-nine letters on which names and phrases depend, whether finished or unfinished.

Chinese Source

字身者，謂若究竟、若不究竟，名句所依四十九字。

Ru-Yi Translation

A body of syllables is what is called the forty-nine letters on which names and phrases depend, whether finished or unfinished.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A syllable is language's smallest unit, and every name or phrase, complete or incomplete, ultimately rests on a basic set of forty-nine such units in Sanskrit. The *Establishment of Consciousness-Only* identifies text itself with syllable, the shared support underlying both names and phrases; syllables are fixed and do not further change, and it is only by resting on this fixed foundation that whatever a name or phrase expresses can become manifest at all. Lü Cheng's own summary of the science of phonetics distinguishes the three levels concretely: an isolated sound or letter is itself called a syllable ("a," "i," and the like); syllables strung together to denote a single thing form a name ("person," "eye," and the like); and names strung together to explain a single action or principle form a phrase ("refrain from every evil," "every sentient being must die," and the like) — and "body," in each case, simply means a plurality, two or more of the unit in question gathered together, never a single isolated instance alone.

15. Section: Overall Analysis — Desire Heads a Name, a Name Heads a Phrase, and a Single Syllable Alone Can Never Form One

Within this, desire is the head of a name, and a name is the head of a phrase. A phrase must have a name; a name must have a syllable. If there is only a single syllable, it does not form a phrase. Further, if there is a syllable not subsumed under any name, there is only the syllable, without any name.

Chinese Source

此中欲為名首，名為句首。句必有名，名必有字。若唯一字，則不成句。又若有字名所不攝，唯字無名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, desire is the head of a name, and a name is the head of a phrase. A phrase must have a name; a name must have a syllable. If there is only a single syllable, it does not form a phrase. Further, if there is a syllable not subsumed under any name, there is only the syllable, without any name.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained names, phrases, and syllables individually, the treatise now clarifies their relative scope. Desire — whose nature is to hope for an object and whose function is to drive diligent effort toward it — comes first of all: wishing to communicate, to make oneself understood, a being first forms the intention behind speech, and only then, through the mental factors of wish and applied thought, settles on a name to express what is intended. A name in turn heads a phrase, since phrases are simply names strung together — "every evil," "every good," and "subdue" combine to form the finished stanza already cited. And every phrase requires a name, every name requires a syllable, though the reverse does not hold: a single isolated syllable can never by itself form a phrase, and some syllables — an interjection like "ah" or "oh," used only to color a sentence's tone rather than to denote anything — never rise to the status of a name at all, remaining mere syllables without semantic content. Phrase is broadest in scope, syllable the smallest unit, and name stands between the two.

16. Section: Why Names, Phrases, and Syllables Are Posited at All — So That the Feeling Born of Contact With Designated Language Can Be Received

Question: For what cause and condition are these three bodies — of names and the rest — posited at all? Answer: So that the feeling born of contact with every kind of designated language may be received.

Chinese Source

問：何因緣故，施設名等三種身耶？答：為令領受諸增語觸所生受故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: For what cause and condition are these three bodies — of names and the rest — posited at all? Answer: So that the feeling born of contact with every kind of designated language may be received.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained names, phrases, and syllables individually and their relative scope, the treatise now asks why they exist at all — and answers with a single purpose: communication. When the mind-consciousness, accompanying the five sense-consciousnesses, meets an object, a feeling arises; that feeling is then registered and interpreted through the mind's own conceptual naming, and it is only this naming that makes genuine communication between beings possible at all. "Designated-language contact" is established, the *Determinations on the Ground of the Five Consciousness-Groups and the Ground of Mind* explains, precisely because the mind-consciousness discriminates its object through language — unlike the five sense-consciousnesses, which can directly apprehend an object's own intrinsic nature, the mind-consciousness knows its object only by way of concepts, since dependently-arisen phenomena have no inherent verbal nature of their own; language is what is added on afterward. Desire comes first, moving the mind toward conceptual construction; conceptual construction comes first, moving the mind toward speech — without names to construct with, there would be no way to register or share what contact with the world has produced.

17. Section: What a Name Is For — Shared Understanding, Forming a Mental Image, and Being Called By Speech

Question: What is the meaning of a name? Answer: Because it enables various things to be commonly understood together, it is called a name. Further, because it enables the mind to form various mental images, it is called a name. Further, because it is what speech calls out to summon, it is called a name.

Chinese Source

問：名是何義？答：能令種種共所了知，故名為名。又能令意作種種相，故名為名。又由語言之所呼召，故名為名。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: What is the meaning of a name? Answer: Because it enables various things to be commonly understood together, it is called a name.
Further, because it enables the mind to form various mental images, it is called a name.
Further, because it is what speech calls out to summon, it is called a name.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three reasons together explain why names exist. First, shared understanding: saying "a cart" lets every listener grasp at once that a vehicle for carrying people along a road is meant — communication itself depends on this common recognition. Second, forming a mental image: the mind-consciousness apprehends an object only by way of a name first — seeing a tree, the mind can only take hold of "tree" as a mental image once the name "tree" is available to organize the perception around; conceptual construction always follows naming, never precedes it. And third, being called by speech: without names, there would simply be no way for one being to call out to, address, or summon another through spoken words at all — ordinary conversation itself depends on this function. Each reason names a distinct, necessary function; together they explain why a name is indispensable.

18. Section: What a Phrase Is For, and What Text Is For — Bringing an Unseen Meaning Fully Into View, and Letting Names and Phrases Themselves Appear

By gathering in various names to bring an unseen meaning fully, clearly into view, it is called a phrase. By that through which names and phrases themselves come to appear, it is called text.

Chinese Source

攝受諸名，究竟顯了不現見義，故名為句。隨顯名句，故名為文。

Ru-Yi Translation

By gathering in various names to bring an unseen meaning fully, clearly into view, it is called a phrase.
By that through which names and phrases themselves come to appear, it is called text.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where earlier the treatise explained a phrase as "gathered names," it now explains the reverse relation: it is precisely by gathering many names together that a phrase can bring some object never directly seen — a place, a scene, a whole complex of circumstance — fully and clearly into a listener's understanding. A description of a mountain resort, strung together from many individual names (Taiwan, the county, the elevation, the sheep grazing the highland meadow, the seasonal fruit), lets a listener who has never visited genuinely grasp what the place is like, purely through the gathered names forming complete phrases. Text, in turn, is what lets names and phrases themselves appear at all: without the individual syllables combining first into names and then into phrases, nothing could be expressed — reciting a Buddha's name letter by isolated letter conveys nothing, but strung together as a name and invoked as a phrase, it evokes genuine reverence in a listener's heart. The *Illuminating the Sagely Teaching Treatise* names this precisely: text is called such because it lets name and phrase come forth — text is the very ground on which both depend.

19. Section: Explaining the Sutra's Own Words — Designated Language and Its Wellspring, Named Directly by the World-Honored One

Within this, designated language is what is called every name correlated by common kind, shared by an entire class of beings. The wellspring of designated language is what is called the shared desire of that entire class, able to give rise to it.

如世尊說：增語、增語路，乃至廣說。此中增語者，謂一切眾同類相應名。增語路者，謂并眾同類欲，能起彼故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the World-Honored One said: designated language, and the wellspring of designated language — and so on, at length.
Within this, designated language is what is called every name correlated by common kind, shared by an entire class of beings.
The wellspring of designated language is what is called the shared desire of that entire class, able to give rise to it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained names, phrases, and syllables in the treatise's own systematic vocabulary, the discussion now turns to explain how the sūtras themselves speak of language, using a different but related set of terms — designated language, expression, and posited description, each with its own "wellspring" or generative source. Designated language ("increased-upon speech") is exactly what was called, earlier, a name correlated by common kind: only a noble one truly understands that language is superimposed on things that have, in themselves, no verbal nature at all — an ordinary being who takes an insult like "you're a fool" as expressing some real, substantial quality reacts with anger, while one who sees it as mere designated language, empty of any fixed referent, does not. Its wellspring is simply the shared desire among beings of one class to communicate about their shared objects: desire is the cause, designated language the effect it gives rise to.

20. Section: Expression and Posited Description — Each With Its Own Wellspring in Fitting Speech and in Setting Things Out in Order

Expression is the speech corresponding to that entire class; and this same speech, in this place or that, either topically stated or explained, its very support is called the wellspring of expression. Posited description is the establishing and setting out of each individual thing, one by one; its very support is called the wellspring of posited description.

Chinese Source

詞者，謂彼相應語。又即此語，各別於彼彼處若標、若釋，彼所依處名為彼路。施設者，謂一一分別施設建立。彼所依處名為彼路。

Ru-Yi Translation

Expression is the speech corresponding to that entire class; and this same speech, in this place or that, either topically stated or explained, its very support is called the wellspring of expression.
Posited description is the establishing and setting out of each individual thing, one by one; its very support is called the wellspring of posited description.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Two further terms, broader in scope than designated language alone. Expression is speech genuinely fitted to what it describes — saying "a cup is a vessel for drinking" expresses the cup through a genuine, apt phrase, broader than the bare designated-language units ("cup," "drinking," "vessel") that compose it; its wellspring, per the *Compendium of Explanations*, is the discipline of phonetics and grammar itself, the traditional study governing how words are properly used, the very source from which fitting expression is drawn. Posited description is broader still: setting out an entire matter's full context and particulars, one by one, until a listener genuinely understands where something came from and what makes it distinctive — describing not just that a cup exists but where it was bought, how well it serves, how it improves the taste of water — and its wellspring is simply expression together with the desire that motivates it, since only through fitting words, born of genuine intent to communicate, can such a full description ever be assembled.

21. Section: Desire Is Itself Expression

Desire is itself expression; there is no separate desire apart from it. This is itself the wellspring of designated language and of posited description.

Chinese Source

欲即是詞，無有別欲。此即增語施設之路。

Ru-Yi Translation

Desire is itself expression; there is no separate desire apart from it. This is itself the wellspring of designated language and of posited description.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A final clarifying line draws the whole set of terms together. Designated language names an individual thing; expression gathers designated-language units into a genuinely fitting phrase describing that thing; posited description gathers expression, one item at a time, into a full account of a whole matter — three concentric circles of increasing scope, designated language the innermost and narrowest, posited description the outermost and broadest. Their respective wellsprings collapse into one another in just the same way: the wellspring of designated language is desire, the wellspring of expression just is that same desire once it has already become fitting speech — so desire and expression are, at root, one and the same thing under two names, "no separate desire apart from it" — and the wellspring of posited description is simply expression together with desire together. Every level, in the end, traces back to a single source: the wish to communicate. With this, the entire body of names, phrases, and text — their individual kinds, their relative scope, why they are posited at all, what each is for, and the sūtra's own parallel vocabulary of designated language, expression, and posited description together with their respective wellsprings — is fully explained. One further matter remains before this first of the six kinds of text is fully closed: where names, phrases, and syllables actually come to rest.

22. Section: Six Grounds Where Names, Phrases, and Syllables Come to Rest — Closing the Body of Names, Phrases, and Text Entirely

Chinese Source

又名身等略有六種依處。一者、法，二者、義，三者、補特伽羅，四者、時，五者、數，六者、處所。彼廣分別，當知已如聞所成地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, a body of names and the rest, in brief, rests upon six grounds. First, dharma; second, meaning; third, person; fourth, time; fifth, number; and sixth, place.
Their detailed analysis should be known just as already given in the Stage Formed by Hearing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise closes its account of text's first kind by pointing back to its own earlier, detailed treatment of the science of phonetics in the Stage Formed by Hearing, which names the same six grounds on which naming rests: the dharma-ground (the construction of names, phrases, and syllables themselves, along with speech possessing five virtuous qualities — refined, effortless, resonant, fitting, and rightly meant); the meaning-ground (ten categories of what is designated — the faculties, the great elements, action, seeking, unwholesome and wholesome conduct, flourishing, decline, use, and protection); the person-ground (grammatical gender — masculine, feminine, and neuter — and grammatical person — first, second, and third); the time-ground (verb tense across past, present, and future); the number-ground (singular, dual, and plural); and the ground of place and its root treatises (the foundational and derivative grammatical works of Sanskrit scholarship). Language and literature can never be separated from names, phrases, and text; these six grounds are simply the different places or occasions where different bodies of names, phrases, and syllables actually arise, depending on the object described, the person speaking, the time referenced, and the count involved. In sum: a name is designated language; a phrase brings a name to completion; text is what supports both. With this, the entire account of the body of names, phrases, and text — their kinds, their relative scope, their purpose, the sūtra's own parallel terms, and now the grounds on which they rest — is fully explained, closing the first of the six kinds of text entirely. The treatise turns next to the second kind: speech.

23. Section: Speech Opens — Eight Qualities by Which a True Teacher's Words Are Able to Proclaim the Genuine Dharma

Speech, know, in brief, possesses eight parts: namely, leading toward the highest, being sweet-sounding, and the rest. Because that speech's own words and phrases correspond to what they express — and so on, up through possessing the resource of constant, thorough application — one is thereby able to proclaim the true Dharma.

Chinese Source

語者，當知略具八分。謂先首、美妙等。由彼語文句等相應，乃至常委分資糧故，能說正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Speech, know, in brief, possesses eight parts: namely, leading toward the highest, being sweet-sounding, and the rest.
Because that speech's own words and phrases correspond to what they express — and so on, up through possessing the resource of constant, thorough application — one is thereby able to proclaim the true Dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise turns to the second of the six kinds of text: speech itself, the actual spoken quality of a teaching, distinct from its mere words. Eight qualities together mark speech capable of genuinely proclaiming the Dharma — leading toward the highest and being sweet-sounding are named as the first two examples, with six more to follow — and these same eight, the treatise notes, also correspond to eight further marks of how well speech's own words and phrases actually fit what they express, ranging from basic correspondence up through what is elsewhere called the resource of constant and thorough application. Every quality named here, spoken of at length already in the Grounds With and Without Investigation and Deliberation, describes not what a teacher says but how well it is said — the difference between speech that merely conveys information and speech genuinely able to move a listener toward liberation.

24. Section: Leading Toward the Highest, and Sweet as the Kalaviṅka Bird's Own Song

Leading-toward-the-highest speech is speech that takes proceeding toward the palace of nirvāṇa as what leads. Sweet-sounding speech is speech whose very sound is clear and lovely, like the song of the kalaviṅka bird.

Chinese Source

先首語者，趣涅槃宮為先首故。美妙語者，其聲清美，如羯羅頻迦音故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Leading-toward-the-highest speech is speech that takes proceeding toward the palace of nirvāṇa as what leads.
Sweet-sounding speech is speech whose very sound is clear and lovely, like the song of the kalaviṅka bird.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of the eight qualities: genuine Dharma-speech always leads a listener toward nirvāṇa, free of craving, hatred, and delusion, as its ultimate destination — whatever the immediate subject, the teaching's direction never loses sight of this final aim. The second: the very sound of such speech is clear and beautiful, likened to the kalaviṅka bird — a legendary bird whose song, scripture says, surpasses every other creature's even before it hatches from the egg, so lovely that gods, humans, and kiṃnaras alike cannot match it. Genuine Dharma-teaching is compared to this bird's voice because it is elegant and gentle rather than harsh, dignified and correct rather than frivolous, its very tone in accord with the teaching, the practice, and the fruit it conveys — delighting the listener, keeping the mind awake rather than dulled, correct rather than deviant, pure rather than defiled, and drawing the whole assembly toward nirvāṇa together.

25. Section: Clear, Easily Grasped, Delightful to Hear, and Free of Any Wish for Personal Gain

Self-evident speech is speech in which every word, phrase, and syllable is genuinely skillful. Easily-grasped speech is speech skillfully reasoned and expressed. Delightful-to-hear speech is speech that draws out the Dharma's own meaning. Speech without dependence is speech not resting on any wish for another's trust in oneself.

Chinese Source

顯了語者，謂詞句文皆善巧故。易解語者，巧辯說故。樂聞語者，引法義故。無依語者，不依希望他信己故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Self-evident speech is speech in which every word, phrase, and syllable is genuinely skillful.
Easily-grasped speech is speech skillfully reasoned and expressed.
Delightful-to-hear speech is speech that draws out the Dharma's own meaning.
Speech without dependence is speech not resting on any wish for another's trust in oneself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four more qualities. Self-evident speech uses vernacular that is genuinely accessible — plain rather than obscure — at every level, from word choice through phrasing. Easily-grasped speech reasons skillfully and illustrates its points vividly, often through concrete example, so a listener readily follows along. Delightful-to-hear speech draws a listener genuinely toward the Dharma's own meaning, not toward admiration of the speaker. And speech without dependence — perhaps the most demanding of the eight — is entirely free of any wish, however subtle, that the listener come away trusting or admiring the teacher personally; genuine Dharma-speech is offered from a mind of unconditioned compassion, seeking nothing for the speaker at all, neither reputation, flattery, nor personal advantage.

26. Section: Neither Too Much Nor Too Little, and Boundless in Its Resourceful Reach — Closing the Eight Qualities Entirely

Speech that does not provoke resistance is speech that knows its measure in speaking. Boundless speech is speech vast and skillful.

Chinese Source

不違逆語者，知量說故。無邊語者，廣大善巧故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Speech that does not provoke resistance is speech that knows its measure in speaking.
Boundless speech is speech vast and skillful.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh quality, speech that does not provoke resistance, means knowing exactly the right measure — neither so brief that a listener feels shortchanged nor so prolonged that patience wears thin; the *Lotus Sūtra* itself teaches that the Dharma should be taught equally to all beings, neither too much nor too little, not even multiplying words for one who deeply loves the teaching. The eighth, boundless speech, means that the meaning conveyed is itself vast and inexhaustibly skillful, capable of unfolding into ever more resourceful elaboration without ever running dry. Such speech, fully perfected in all eight qualities together, belongs properly only to bodhisattvas from the tenth ground upward and to the Buddha in complete fullness — even a genuine effort by an ordinary teacher, given how much depends on factors like the voice itself, a fixed karmic result difficult to change, and given that no unenlightened being possesses the four unobstructed forms of eloquence, is already a rare accomplishment if it manages not to stray from the Dharma at all. With this, all eight qualities of genuine Dharma-speech — leading toward the highest, sweet-sounding, self-evident, easily grasped, delightful to hear, free of dependence, measured, and boundless — are fully explained.

27. Section: Three Virtues Underlying the Eight Qualities — Orientation, Its Own Substance, and Applied Function — Closing the Eightfold Achievement Entirely

Chinese Source

如是八種語，當知略具三德。一者、趣向德，謂初一種。二者、自體德，謂次二種。三者、加行德，謂所餘種。

Ru-Yi Translation

These eight kinds of speech, know, in brief, possess three virtues.
First, the virtue of orientation — namely, the first kind. Second, the virtue of own substance — namely, the next two kinds. Third, the virtue of applied function — namely, all the remaining kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eight qualities just explained now resolve into three underlying virtues. The virtue of orientation belongs to leading-toward-the-highest speech alone, since it alone names the destination — nirvāṇa — toward which all genuine Dharma-speech points. The virtue of own substance belongs to the next two, sweet-sounding and self-evident speech, since these describe qualities intrinsic to the speech itself: its sound (clear and lovely as the kalaviṅka's song) and its supporting words and phrases (skillfully chosen at every level). The virtue of applied function belongs to the remaining five — easily grasped, delightful to hear, without dependence, non-resistance-provoking, and boundless — since these describe the actual work the speech performs in practice: reasoning skillfully, drawing out meaning, remaining free of self-interest, staying rightly measured, and unfolding inexhaustibly. With this, the eightfold achievement of genuine Dharma-speech, now organized under these three underlying virtues, is fully explained, closing it entirely. The treatise turns next to the eight further marks of perfected verbal expression.

28. Section: Perfected Expression Opens — Correspondence Through Well-Ordered Sequence and the Four Kinds of Reasoning

Correspondence is: because the body of names, phrases, and text is well established in proper sequence; and further, because it corresponds to the four kinds of reasoning.

Chinese Source

相應者，謂名句文身次第善安立故，又依四種道理相應故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Correspondence is: because the body of names, phrases, and text is well established in proper sequence; and further, because it corresponds to the four kinds of reasoning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond the eightfold speech just explained, a further eight marks characterize genuinely perfected verbal expression, beginning with correspondence, itself twofold. First, structural correspondence: a well-taught teaching orders its names, phrases, and text with real architecture — stating the general principle, then the particulars, then summarizing, then compressing the whole into a memorable verse, exactly as this treatise itself so often proceeds. Second, correspondence with the four kinds of reasoning, already explained at length in the Śrāvaka Ground: reasoning by dependence (every dharma arises only relative to its causes and conditions, and every designation only relative to what it designates); reasoning by function (the eye's function is to see, water's is to moisten, wholesome causes yield pleasant fruit); reasoning by proof (established through scriptural authority, through inference — coarse impermanence observed directly implies subtler momentary impermanence — and through direct perception, whether ordinary sensory perception or, at its purest, the meditative and finally the non-conceptual wisdom that sees selflessness directly); and reasoning by the nature of things as they simply are (a mountain is high, water is wet, fire is hot — such facts require no further "why," being simply how reality is constituted). Together these four kinds of reasoning cover the whole of dependent origination's true nature, and genuinely correspondent speech is speech that accords with them throughout.

29. Section: A Helpful Companion, a Fitting Sequel, Crystal Clarity, and What Purely Assists a Listener's Heart

助伴者，能成次第故。隨順者，謂解釋次第故。清徹者，文句顯了故。清淨資助者，善入眾心故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A helpful companion is that which is able to accomplish sequence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Four more marks. A helpful companion presents a matter's causal side in proper sequence, laying out how one point genuinely leads to the next. A fitting sequel then builds on this, explaining in fuller sequence the actual relation between cause and its resulting fruit — accomplishing sequence lays the groundwork, and explaining sequence fills it in with detail. Crystal clarity means the words and phrases used are themselves self-evident, letting a listener see straight through to the meaning without obstruction. And what purely assists means the teaching skillfully reaches into each listener's own heart, drawing that heart toward correspondence with the Dharma and, ultimately, toward nirvāṇa itself.

Ru-Yi Translation

Being fitting is as with the assembly.
Being suited is: because it accords with the Dharma, because it draws out its benefit, and because it accords with the occasion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The sixth mark, being fitting, differs subtly from "what purely assists": where the latter reaches into a listener's heart generally, being fitting means the teaching genuinely matches the specific capacities gathered in the actual assembly — householders, renunciants, the faithful and pure, the evil-inclined, and those in between, five broad kinds of listener the treatise will detail further on. The seventh, being suited, has three aspects: according with the Dharma (the words chosen genuinely match the twelve divisions of scripture they draw from); drawing out benefit (aiming the teaching clearly at what genuinely benefits hearers, whether worldly or supramundane); and according with the occasion (timely in every sense, including such matters as a teacher's proper bearing — robed and seated appropriately — that inspires reverence and purity of mind in speaker and listener alike). And the eighth, the resource of constant and thorough application, names speech that inspires beings to take up the thirty-seven factors of awakening with both thoroughgoing care and unbroken constancy, ultimately drawing forth the noble eightfold path's own correct view and its companions — genuinely skillful Dharma-speech is thus the very resource, the supporting condition, from which the noble path and true liberation are drawn. With this, all eight marks of perfected verbal expression — correspondence, a helpful companion, a fitting sequel, crystal clarity, what purely assists, being fitting, being suited, and the resource of constant and thorough application — are fully explained, closing this account, and with it the entire second kind of text, speech itself, entirely. The treatise turns next to the third kind: mode of address.

The third of the six kinds of text is comparatively brief: mode of address simply names the particular doctrinal framework a given passage of speech addresses itself through — the five aggregates, the eighteen elements, the sense-bases, the chain of dependent origination, the distinction between what is possible and what is not, or the four foundations of mindfulness — whichever a noble teacher, śrāvaka, bodhisattva, or Tathāgata alike, has chosen as the lens through which to correspond with and express a given truth. With this, mode of address is fully explained, closing the third kind of text entirely. The treatise turns next to the fourth kind: the occasion for asking.

32. Section: The Occasion for Asking Opens — Speech Arising From a Question, and Twenty-Seven Kinds of Listener Beginning With Dull and Sharp Faculties

Within this, by the distinction of faculty, there are two kinds: first, dull faculties; and second, sharp faculties.

Chinese Source

機請者，謂因機請問而起言說。此復根等差別，當知有二十七種補特伽羅。此中由根差別，故成二種。一者、鈍根，二者、利根。

Ru-Yi Translation

The occasion for asking is: speech that arises because of a question posed by a listener suited to be taught.
This, further, differs by faculty and the rest; know that there are twenty-seven kinds of person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth and final kind of text names the actual occasion prompting speech: a suitable listener's own question. What follows classifies such listeners into twenty-seven kinds across seven categories (the Śrāvaka Ground elsewhere counts a closely related twenty-eight), beginning with faculty. Dull faculties describe a being whose five faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom operate sluggishly and weakly with respect to whatever object is contemplated, lacking the capacity or force to penetrate the Dharma's meaning quickly. Sharp faculties describe the reverse: faculties that are keen and forceful, responding readily and swiftly, genuinely capable of penetrating meaning and realizing the truth without delay. Both kinds arise from two further causes each — an innate disposition present from birth, and a disposition shaped in this very life by diligence or its lack: neglecting cultivation of the good roots makes one dull, while diligent cultivation makes one sharp.

33. Section: Seven Dispositions of Mind — Dominant Greed, Hatred, Delusion, Conceit, and Discursiveness, Balanced Affliction, and Thin Defilement

By the distinction of disposition, there are seven kinds: namely, greed-dominant conduct and the rest, just as already explained in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Chinese Source

由行差別，故成七種。謂貪等行，如聲聞地已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the distinction of disposition, there are seven kinds: namely, greed-dominant conduct and the rest, just as already explained in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second sevenfold set classifies listeners by which mental disposition dominates their conduct. Greed-dominant conduct marks one with intense, long-standing craving, often outwardly cheerful and glowing. Hatred-dominant conduct marks one prone to intense, sustained anger, habitually frowning and displeased. Delusion-dominant conduct marks one confused about cause and effect, unclear about how things actually work. Conceit-dominant conduct marks one with an intensely inflated sense of self. Discursiveness-dominant conduct marks one whose mind churns every matter into needless complexity, scattered and restless — elsewhere named the most difficult type to guide toward either concentration or wisdom. Balanced conduct marks one free of any single dominant affliction, all five present but none clearly stronger than the rest. And thin-defilement conduct marks one in whom greed, hatred, delusion, conceit, and discursiveness alike are all comparatively faint. The Buddha taught accordingly: contemplating the impure for the greed-dominant, loving-kindness for the hatred-dominant, dependent origination for the deluded, the elements' analysis for the conceited, and breath-counting for the discursive — while the balanced and thin-defiled may enter through whichever approach best fits their own prior habituation.

34. Section: Householder or Renunciant, and Aspiration Toward One of Three Vehicles

By the distinction of assembly, there are two kinds: first, the householder assembly; and second, the renunciant assembly. By the distinction of aspiration, there are three kinds: first, śrāvakas; second, solitary-awakened ones; and third, bodhisattvas.

Chinese Source

由眾差別，故成二種。一者、在家眾，二者、出家眾。由願差別，故成三種。一者、聲聞，二者、獨覺，三者、菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the distinction of assembly, there are two kinds: first, the householder assembly; and second, the renunciant assembly.
By the distinction of aspiration, there are three kinds: first, śrāvakas; second, solitary-awakened ones; and third, bodhisattvas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third category, assembly, distinguishes only householder from renunciant — for lay followers the Buddha teaches first the dharma of upright conduct (giving, precepts, and the four immeasurables), then the essential teaching of the four noble truths that severs self-grasping; for the five kinds of renunciant (monks, nuns, and the rest), discipline differs by ordination while the deeper teaching on concentration and wisdom differs according to each one's own underlying aspiration. That underlying aspiration is the fourth category: a śrāvaka, hearing the Buddha's voice, cultivates the four noble truths, realizes emptiness, and attains a fruit up through arhatship; a pratyekabuddha, arising in an age without a living buddha, cultivates the thirty-seven factors of awakening without a teacher and likewise reaches arhatship; and a bodhisattva arouses the unsurpassed resolve, practices the six perfections, and works to liberate beings broadly — each receiving teaching suited to that aspiration: the four truths, the twelve links, or the six perfections, respectively.

35. Section: Those Who Can Be Saved and Those Who Cannot, and Nine Kinds by Degree of Application

By the distinction of application, there are nine kinds: first, already entered correct dharma; second, not yet entered correct dharma; third, having obstruction; fourth, without obstruction; fifth, already matured; sixth, not yet matured; seventh, fully bound; eighth, not fully bound; and ninth, unbound.

Chinese Source

由可救、不可救差別，故成二種。謂般涅槃法、不般涅槃法。由加行差別，故成九種。一、已入正法，二、未入正法，三、有障礙，四、無障礙，五、已成熟，六、未成熟，七、具縛，八、不具縛，九、無縛。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the distinction of whether one can be saved or cannot, there are two kinds: namely, those bound for final nirvāṇa, and those not bound for final nirvāṇa.
By the distinction of application, there are nine kinds: first, already entered correct dharma; second, not yet entered correct dharma; third, having obstruction; fourth, without obstruction; fifth, already matured; sixth, not yet matured; seventh, fully bound; eighth, not fully bound; and ninth, unbound.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth category asks simply whether the seed for any of the three vehicles' noble path is present at all: a being bound for final nirvāṇa carries such a seed and, meeting a suitable condition, can be led to it — genuinely "savable"; one not so bound lacks it, and can be taught only the wholesome conduct that secures a favorable human or heavenly rebirth, never liberation itself. The sixth category, application, then distinguishes nine further stages of practical progress among those savable beings. Already entered correct dharma names one who has drawn near good teachers, gained genuine faith in the Buddha's word and discipline, undertaken pure precepts, and softened wrong views — itself subdividing into those not yet freed from affliction and those who have already attained the fruit; not yet entered names one carrying the seed but lacking every one of these steps so far. Having obstruction names one hindered by either of two well-known sets of three obstacles — application, withdrawal, and quietude on one accounting (illness, attachment to worldly activity or company, or restlessness and self-satisfaction blocking calming and insight respectively), or karmic, afflictive, and retributive obstruction on another (grave prior karma ripening to block the path, intense ongoing affliction resisting purification even with effort, or birth into one of the eight unfavorable conditions, such as the lower realms or a lifespan with no buddha present, where the Dharma cannot be heard or understood at all); without obstruction names freedom from both sets, health and right view intact, free to practice well. Already matured and not yet matured mark whether the five faculties have ripened enough, in this very life, to reach a genuine fruit or not yet. And fully bound, not fully bound, and unbound mark an ordinary being still gripped entirely by affliction, a learner-stage noble one who has cut off some part of it, and an arhat who has cut off all of it, respectively.

36. Section: Human or Non-Human — Closing All Twenty-Seven Kinds of Listener Entirely

By the distinction of kind, there are two kinds: first, human; and second, non-human.

Chinese Source

由種類差別，故成二種。一者、人，二者、非人。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the distinction of kind, there are two kinds: first, human; and second, non-human.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh and final category simply distinguishes human listeners from every non-human kind of being — gods, hell-beings, hungry ghosts, and animals alike. The *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra* offers several accounts of why humans are so named: because, as a legendary wheel-turning king once instructed, humans alone reflect, weigh, and examine before acting, thereby growing skillful in every craft; because humans alone excel in three things beyond even the gods — courage (undertaking austerities without seeing the result in advance), memory (recalling distant past events clearly), and pure conduct (cultivating the roots that lead toward liberation and undertaking the precepts of individual liberation). With this, all twenty-seven kinds of listener — faculty (2), disposition (7), assembly (2), aspiration (3), savability (2), application (9), and kind (2) — are fully explained, closing this detailed classification entirely. The treatise turns next to draw out the overall significance of this fourfold occasion for asking.

37. Section: The Six Kinds of Text Reduced to Four Marks — What Is Spoken, Whom It Is For, What Speaks It, and Who Speaks

First, the mark of what is spoken — namely, the body of names and the rest, with mode of address coming last. Second, the mark of whom it is for — namely, the twenty-seven kinds of person comprised within the occasion for asking. Third, the mark of what speaks it — namely, speech. Fourth, the mark of who speaks — namely, śrāvakas, bodhisattvas, and the Tathāgata.

Chinese Source

如是六文，總有四相，說名為文。一、所說相，謂名身等，行相為後。二、所為相，謂機請攝二十七種補特伽羅。三、能說相，謂語。四、說者相，謂聲聞、菩薩及與如來。

Ru-Yi Translation

These six kinds of text, taken together, have four marks in all, and are called, in sum, text.
First, the mark of what is spoken — namely, the body of names and the rest, with mode of address coming last. Second, the mark of whom it is for — namely, the twenty-seven kinds of person comprised within the occasion for asking. Third, the mark of what speaks it — namely, speech. Fourth, the mark of who speaks — namely, śrāvakas, bodhisattvas, and the Tathāgata.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained all six kinds of text individually — the body of names, phrases, and syllables; speech; mode of address; and the occasion for asking — the treatise now shows how they reduce to four underlying marks. What is spoken combines names, phrases, and text (the substance) together with mode of address (their function put to use) — substance first, function following. Whom it is for is simply the twenty-seven kinds of listener already classified in such detail, the actual audience a teaching is shaped to reach. What speaks it is speech itself, the sound through which names, phrases, and syllables — themselves merely provisional designations resting on sound — are actually voiced. And who speaks covers every qualified teacher: śrāvaka, bodhisattva, or Tathāgata (and, more broadly still, even gods and ordinary humans capable of expounding the Dharma). Substance and function, audience, medium, and speaker — the whole of what makes a sūtra's text what it is, in four marks rather than six.

38. Section: Why All Six Are Called "Text" — None Can Be Missing Without Meaning Failing to Appear — Closing the Body of Text Entirely

Thus these six kinds all serve to make the text manifest; were even one of them lacking, meaning could not be made manifest. Because they are able to make meaning manifest, they are therefore all called text.

Chinese Source

如是六種皆顯於文，若闕一種，不能顯義。由能顯義，是故名文。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus these six kinds all serve to make the text manifest; were even one of them lacking, meaning could not be made manifest.
Because they are able to make meaning manifest, they are therefore all called text.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A closing summary explains why all six — seemingly quite different in kind — properly share the single name "text": the body of names, phrases, and syllables is text's own substance; mode of address is that substance's function; the occasion for asking is the causal condition prompting text to arise at all; speech is what text depends on to be voiced; and the speaker is who actually brings the text forth. Remove any single one of the six and a sūtra's meaning simply fails to become manifest — no substance without names and phrases, no function without a mode of address to put them to use, no arising without a listener's question to prompt it, no expression without speech to carry it, no delivery without a qualified speaker to voice it. Because all six together, and only together, make meaning genuinely manifest, all six rightly share the name "text." With this, the entire account of text — the sūtra's own body of names, phrases, and syllables, speech, mode of address, and the occasion for asking, together with the four marks and single purpose underlying all six — is fully explained, closing text, the first component of the sūtra's body, entirely. The treatise turns next to meaning, the second component, in ten kinds beginning with the meaning of ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having closed the entire account of text, the treatise turns to meaning — the principle text conveys — organized, per Maitreya's own analysis, into ten headings (compressed into eight sections below, since the seventh and eighth, and the ninth and tenth, are each treated as a single pair). The first heading, the meaning of ground, names the very stages of practice a sūtra's teaching addresses. In brief, five stages span the entire path: the ground of accumulation (an ordinary being outside the path proper, gathering the merit and wisdom that support genuine practice, as a traveler first gathers provisions); the ground of application (an ordinary being who has attained at least proximate concentration and begun cultivating insight, progressing through the four roots of goodness — heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma — a śrāvaka practicing the four truths' sixteen aspects and a Consciousness-Only practitioner the four kinds of investigation and as-it-is wisdom, in each case suppressing the conceptually-constructed afflictive and cognitive obstructions); the ground of seeing (the path of seeing itself, attaining the first fruit or the first ground, where a śrāvaka cuts off the 112 conceptually-constructed afflictions and a bodhisattva cuts off the manifest activity, seeds, and habituated traces of both obstructions at their conceptually-constructed level); the ground of cultivation (continuing on from there to cut off the innate, deeply rooted seeds of both obstructions); and the ultimate ground (arhatship for a śrāvaka, pratyekabuddhahood for a solitary awakener, buddhahood itself for a bodhisattva). Analyzed in full detail, these same five stages expand into the seventeen grounds structuring the entire *Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra* itself, summarized in its own opening verse: the five consciousness-groups and mind; the three grounds with and without investigation and deliberation; the two grounds of concentrated and unconcentrated states; the two grounds with and without mind; the three grounds formed by hearing, reflection, and cultivation; the three vehicle-specific grounds of śrāvaka, bodhisattva, and pratyekabuddha; and finally the two grounds with and without residue — the very seventeen grounds whose Determinations the treatise itself only just finished closing in fascicle 80.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth and fifth of the first five characteristics complete this opening set. Agreeable cause names wholesome, contaminated dharmas — the five precepts and the ten wholesome deeds an ordinary being keeps — that yield pleasant human and heavenly fruit. Disagreeable cause names unwholesome dharmas whose fruit is unwelcome. Nourishing cause names how wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral dharmas already arisen, through repeated and thorough cultivation, cause dharmas of the same kind to arise ever more strongly afterward — correct hearing, reflection, and cultivation nourished this way eventually yield the acceptance that no dharma truly arises. The cause of circling on names the twelve links of dependent origination themselves — ignorance planting the seeds of the three realms, karma's habituation, and craving and grasping's nourishment together driving birth, aging, and death around again without end. And the cause of reversal names every path capable of quieting affliction and moving toward awakening — the paths of seeing, cultivation, and no-more-learning, the accumulations for both worldly and supramundane paths, and the four roots of goodness that prepare the way — essentially the truth of the path itself. Characteristic as effect then simply names whatever these five causes, once conditions are complete, actually produce: birth into or out of the three realms, the attainment of nirvāṇa, the accomplishment of the Buddha's teaching, the completion of any skillful undertaking, or the functioning of any dharma's own proper activity — the fruit that appears once worldly or supramundane cultivation bears result. With this, the first five characteristics — own, common, imputed, cause, and effect — are fully explained, closing this set entirely. The treatise turns next to a second set of five.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The characteristic of alternate approach names how one and the same teaching can be explained through different angles that all lead toward the same nirvāṇa — Madhyamaka, Consciousness-Only, and the teaching of the eternal true mind, for instance, or the treatise's own later chapter explicitly devoted to "alternate approaches." The characteristic of yogic practice names the sequential stages of calming-and-insight cultivation, counted either as four factors (faith, aspiration, diligence, and skillful means) or as nine stages of the path (the mundane and supramundane paths; the preparatory path; the uninterrupted path that actually cuts off affliction; the path of liberation that follows; the path of further advancement; and paths of inferior, middling, and superior strength). The characteristic of change names the transformation a being undergoes circling through the three realms and five destinies, taking one rebirth after another. The characteristic of defilement covers the three defilements of affliction, karma, and rebirth — or, put otherwise, the truths of suffering and its origin. And the characteristic of purity covers the truths of cessation and the path — or, as the *Buddha-Ground Sūtra Treatise* puts it, the pure dharma-realm a buddha has fully realized, true suchness itself, obscured only as clear sky is obscured by passing smoke and cloud, until the diamond-like concentration finally disperses every last seed of both obstructions and true suchness stands as the ultimate transformed dependence, complete and pure.

There are further five characteristics: first, what is expressed; second, what expresses; third, the correspondence of these two; fourth, grasping; and fifth, non-grasping.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third and final set of five characteristics turns from doctrinal content to the very relationship between language and what it describes — a fitting culmination given this whole Compendium's concern with proper interpretation. What is expressed comprises the five factors already treated at length in the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground: characteristic (whatever basis any statement addresses); name (the designation superimposed upon that basis by the discriminating sixth consciousness, since the dependently-arisen basis itself, whether physical or mental, has no name of its own until conceptual construction adds one); discrimination (the mental factor of apprehending an object, active throughout the eight consciousnesses); true suchness (what the selflessness of dharmas discloses, the domain of a noble one's uncontaminated non-discriminating wisdom, never itself the basis for any verbal designation); and correct wisdom (twofold — the purely supramundane root wisdom, called non-discriminating wisdom, by which śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas realize personal emptiness and bodhisattvas realize both emptinesses directly; and the worldly-and-supramundane subsequently-attained wisdom that, arising after this direct realization, discriminates dependently-arisen phenomena as dreamlike without falling back into confusion about self or dharmas). The first three of these five factors are the defiled marks of ordinary saṃsāra; the last two are the pure marks of the supramundane — together, everything any sūtra ultimately expresses.

Ru-Yi Translation

Such kinds of distinction as these should be known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where what is expressed names the five factors as objects of speech, what expresses names the speech itself — before writing existed, purely spoken language, seeking to convey a thing's own-nature and its distinctions — and identifies this speech directly with the imagined own-nature already familiar from the treatise's earlier discussions of the three natures: language is precisely the ground on which beings project a fixed, substantial nature onto what is, in truth, empty of any such thing. Eight names trace this same imagined nature from different angles. It is imagined because, per the *Compendium of the Great Vehicle*, the sixth consciousness's limitless discriminating activity gives rise to a distorted appearance with no real own-nature of its own — only the imagining itself can be found, never any genuine referent. It is formed through mere aggregation because what the mind imagines as "self" is merely the coincidental gathering of the five aggregates, and what it imagines as any "dharma" is merely the coincidental gathering of causes and conditions — dreamlike, with no real own-nature to be found beneath the aggregate. It is a superimposed mark because the true, inexpressible nature of both conditioned and unconditioned dharmas gets language added onto it after the fact, and an ordinary being then mistakes that addition for something genuinely real. It is falsely grasped because the mind clings to what has no substance at all as though it were substantial — a self imagined to reside permanently within the body where none can be found. It is what verbal expression discloses because it is only ever a name-disclosed semblance of meaning, with no genuine object behind the name. It is the application of letters because it exists only through the power of written or spoken language giving it apparent form. It is mere sound because, examined closely, nothing at all can be found within spoken language beyond the sound itself — empty as an echo in a valley. And it possesses no substantial mark at all because, if there is no substance to what language designates, there can be no mark of that substance either. As Master Yinshun observes, what we take ourselves to be perceiving has, by the time discriminating consciousness gets hold of it, already become entirely this imagined nature — an ordinary being's whole understanding of the world through language is, in this sense, wholly a matter of grasping.

44. Section: Correspondence — Where What Expresses and What Is Expressed Meet, the Very Ground on Which Grasping at a Fixed Own-Nature Rests

The correspondence of these two is: what is expressed and what expresses, mutually corresponding with each other — this is itself the very support for grasping at the imagined own-nature.

Chinese Source

此二相應相者，謂所詮、能詮更互相應，即是遍計所執自性執所依止。

Ru-Yi Translation

The correspondence of these two is: what is expressed and what expresses, mutually corresponding with each other — this is itself the very support for grasping at the imagined own-nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Neither what is expressed nor what expresses, taken alone, produces the grasping mind — it is specifically their mutual correspondence, taking hold together, on which the sixth consciousness's grasping actually rests: consciousness perceiving a flower relies on both the expressing name "flower" and the expressed appearance together, and though neither the name nor the appearance has any substance of its own, their correspondence still gives rise to the mind's false conviction that a real flower is there. The *Compendium of the Great Vehicle*'s own verse names this precisely: the meaning of correspondence is nothing other than what is discriminated through the mutual entwining of syllables into meaning, letter building upon letter — wisdom never operates on what is expressed independently of what expresses it, since the two are never actually separable this way, which is exactly why the true nature of things, unlike this correspondence, admits no expression in words at all. A noble one's non-discriminating wisdom is free of precisely this correspondence; an ordinary being's cognition never is.

45. Section: Grasping — the Ignorant Being's Endless Circling, Fed by Both the Active Grip and Its Dormant Seed

Grasping is: the grasping at the imagined own-nature, together with its latent tendency, by which every ignorant, unlearned being has continuously circled on since beginningless time.

Chinese Source

執著相者，謂諸愚夫，無始時來相續流轉，遍計所執自性執及彼隨眠。

Ru-Yi Translation

Grasping is: the grasping at the imagined own-nature, together with its latent tendency, by which every ignorant, unlearned being has continuously circled on since beginningless time.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Grasping names the fourth characteristic in this final set: relying on the correspondence between what expresses and what is expressed just explained, an unenlightened mind's discriminating activity takes every dharma's imagined own-nature to be genuinely real, and this grasping — both its active manifestation and the latent seed it leaves behind, habituating the mind toward grasping again — is exactly what keeps beings circling through the three realms and five destinies without end, seed producing manifest grasping and manifest grasping in turn depositing further seed, an unbroken circle with no discernible beginning or end.

46. Section: Non-Grasping — the Noble One Who Sees the Imagined Mark Exactly as It Is, and Is Freed Even From Its Habituated Trace — Closing All Fifteen Characteristics Entirely

Non-grasping is: one who has already seen the truth, correctly understanding the mark of the imagined own-nature exactly as it is, and thereby freed even from its habituated trace.

Chinese Source

不執著相者，謂已見諦者，如實了知遍計所執相，及彼習氣解脫。

Ru-Yi Translation

Non-grasping is: one who has already seen the truth, correctly understanding the mark of the imagined own-nature exactly as it is, and thereby freed even from its habituated trace.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth and final characteristic of this last set of five stands opposite grasping: a noble one who has genuinely seen the truth — attained both the uncontaminated root wisdom and the subsequently-attained wisdom — correctly understands that what expresses and what is expressed are both merely names, mere conceptual construction with no substance behind either, and, seeing this exactly as it is, no longer deposits fresh seeds of grasping and is progressively freed even from the habituated trace such grasping leaves behind. Correct wisdom directly realizing true suchness is precisely what non-grasping means. With this, all fifteen characteristics — the first five (own, common, imputed, cause, and effect), the second five (alternate approach, yogic practice, change, defilement, and purity), and this final five (what is expressed, what expresses, their correspondence, grasping, and non-grasping) — are fully explained, closing the concise account of the meaning of characteristic entirely. The treatise turns next to point toward its fuller treatment.

47. Section: For the Fuller Account, Turn to the Stage Formed by Reflection — Closing the Meaning of Characteristic Entirely

If one wishes to reflect correctly, its characteristics should be known just as in the Stage Formed by Reflection.

Chinese Source

若正分別，如思所成地，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one wishes to reflect correctly, its characteristics should be known just as in the Stage Formed by Reflection.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The treatise closes this account of characteristic by pointing to its own earlier, fuller treatment in the Stage Formed by Reflection, which names three marks of genuinely correct reflection. Purity of own-nature names nine supporting conditions for reflection free of defilement: dwelling alone in quiet and reflecting carefully on what has genuinely been heard; setting aside altogether what cannot fruitfully be pondered (a hare's horn, endlessly speculated upon, yields only fantasy, never truth); knowing to distinguish a teaching's deeper, definitive sense from its merely provisional expression, and relying on the former; attending to meaning rather than clinging to the letter; simply trusting what remains beyond one's present depth rather than presuming to refute it, while examining with wisdom what can genuinely be investigated now; and reflecting with firmness, stability, continuity, and a thoroughness that never grows discouraged partway through. Investigating what is known means skillfully discerning presence and absence of characteristic, and of nature, in what is examined. And investigating the teachings themselves means examining both the meaning of the scriptures directly and the meaning of their summary verses — verses of ultimate significance (correct reflection on selflessness), of intended purport (locating the teaching's aim in discipline, concentration, and wisdom), and of essential content (the many verses expressing, in different words, refraining from evil, upholding good, purifying one's own mind, and departing from craving for the three realms). Guided by a noble one's wisdom and pursued through study, training, and reflection along these lines, a practitioner comes to correctly know what the Buddha means to convey — closing the meaning of characteristic, the second of the ten kinds of meaning, entirely. The treatise turns next to the third kind: the meaning of attention and the rest.

48. Section: The Meaning of Attention Opens — Seven Stages From Grasping the Mark Through the Fruit of Applied Practice Fully Complete

The meaning of attention and the rest is: the seven kinds of attention — grasping the mark, and the rest — just as already explained in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Chinese Source

作意等義者，謂七種作意。即了相等，如前聲聞地已說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of attention and the rest is: the seven kinds of attention — grasping the mark, and the rest — just as already explained in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third of the ten kinds of meaning traces the sequence of attention a practitioner actually cultivates in meditation, using the four noble truths as illustration. Attention grasping the mark first learns the coarse features of what is contemplated. Attention of resolute understanding then penetrates both the full extent and the true nature of every mark more deeply. Attention of withdrawal, once resolute understanding has grown strong enough, cuts off the coarsest grade of affliction. Attention of gathering joy continues on to cut off the middling grade, delighting increasingly in the practice itself. Attention of examination then checks whether every affliction has genuinely been removed. Attention of application's culmination presses on, through renewed resolute understanding, wherever affliction still remains. And attention of application's resultant culmination completes the application once even the subtlest grade is cut off, yielding release through either the worldly or the supramundane path. These seven attentions together trace the entire arc from a meditation's first grasp of its object to the liberation it finally secures.

49. Section: Ten Kinds of Wisdom — From Knowing Suffering and Its Origin Through the Exhaustion of Affliction and Certainty of No Further Birth

There are further ten kinds of wisdom: first, the wisdom of suffering; second, the wisdom of origin; third, the wisdom of cessation; fourth, the wisdom of the path; fifth, wisdom regarding dharmas; sixth, wisdom by inference; seventh, wisdom knowing another's mind; eighth, conventional wisdom; ninth, the wisdom of exhaustion; and tenth, the wisdom of no further arising. This too is just as discussed in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Chinese Source

復有十智。一者、苦智，二者、集智，三者、滅智，四者、道智，五者、法智，六者、種類智，七者、他心智，八者、世俗智，九者、盡智，十者、無生智。此亦如前聲聞地辯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are further ten kinds of wisdom: first, the wisdom of suffering; second, the wisdom of origin; third, the wisdom of cessation; fourth, the wisdom of the path; fifth, wisdom regarding dharmas; sixth, wisdom by inference; seventh, wisdom knowing another's mind; eighth, conventional wisdom; ninth, the wisdom of exhaustion; and tenth, the wisdom of no further arising. This too is just as discussed in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Ten kinds of uncontaminated wisdom, arranged partly by object and partly by scope. The first four correspond directly to the four noble truths: the wisdom of suffering contemplates contaminated formations as suffering, empty, impermanent, and selfless; the wisdom of origin contemplates their cause, accumulation, arising, and condition; the wisdom of cessation contemplates cessation, peace, excellence, and release from those same formations; and the wisdom of the path contemplates the path, its correctness, its practice, and its power to lead beyond. Wisdom regarding dharmas and wisdom by inference then distinguish where this same realization operates: the former, applied directly to the desire realm's four truths, cuts off the views to be abandoned by seeing there; the latter, applying that same understanding by analogy to the form and formless realms, cuts off the corresponding views there. Wisdom knowing another's mind reads whether another being's mind is pure or defiled — contaminated in an ordinary being or a learner, uncontaminated in one beyond learning. Conventional wisdom, shared by ordinary beings and noble ones alike, understands worldly matters correctly through conventional designation — what is wholesome or not, blameworthy or not — and moves skillfully through the world accordingly. And the wisdom of exhaustion and the wisdom of no further arising both belong to the arhat's final, complete realization: the former knowing directly that every affliction of the three realms has been exhausted, the latter knowing that no future rebirth from it will ever arise again — one the root wisdom of that direct realization, the other the subsequently-attained wisdom confirming it, according to whether discrimination is absent or present in the knowing.

50. Section: The Six Consciousness-Groups — the Very Basis on Which a Sentient Being's Whole World of Experience Turns

There are further six consciousness-groups: namely, eye-consciousness through mind-consciousness. This too is just as already discussed in the Ground of the Five Consciousness-Groups and the Ground of Mind.

Chinese Source

復有六識身，所謂眼識乃至意識。此亦如前五識身地、意地已辯。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are further six consciousness-groups: namely, eye-consciousness through mind-consciousness. This too is just as already discussed in the Ground of the Five Consciousness-Groups and the Ground of Mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Eye-consciousness, resting on the eye faculty, discerns form; ear-consciousness, resting on the ear, discerns sound; nose-consciousness discerns smell, tongue-consciousness discerns taste, and body-consciousness, resting on the body, discerns tactile contact; mind-consciousness, the sixth, is where mind, intellect, and consciousness itself converge — the treatise's very opening fascicle analyzes each through own-nature, support, object, accompanying factors, and function. A sentient being's whole world of experience turns entirely on these six — understanding one's own physiology and the working of consciousness is what allows a practitioner, in the midst of ordinary seeing, hearing, and knowing, to observe rather than react, subduing craving and aversion before they take hold. Though this is conventional wisdom rather than ultimate insight, it is the necessary ground on which ultimate wisdom is eventually built.

51. Section: Nine Kinds of Full Understanding — Cutting Off Every Affliction Bound to the Three Realms Through Seeing and Through Cultivation

There are further nine kinds of full understanding: first, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing suffering and its origin, bound to the desire realm; second, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing suffering and its origin, bound to the form and formless realms; third, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing cessation, bound to the desire realm; fourth, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing cessation, bound to the form and formless realms; fifth, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing the path, bound to the desire realm; sixth, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing the path, bound to the form and formless realms; seventh, full understanding that cuts off the fetters conforming to the lower portion; eighth, full understanding of the exhaustion of craving for form; and ninth, full understanding of the exhaustion of craving for the formless. Their characteristics have already been discussed in the Ground of Concentrated States.

Chinese Source

復有九種遍知。一者、欲界繫見苦集所斷斷遍知；二者、色無色界繫見苦集所斷斷遍知；三者、欲界繫見滅所斷斷遍知；四者、色無色界繫見滅所斷斷遍知；五者、欲界繫見道所斷斷遍知；六者、色無色界繫見道所斷斷遍知；七者、順下分結斷遍知；八者、色貪盡遍知；九者、無色貪盡遍知。如三摩呬多地已辯其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are further nine kinds of full understanding: first, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing suffering and its origin, bound to the desire realm; second, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing suffering and its origin, bound to the form and formless realms; third, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing cessation, bound to the desire realm; fourth, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing cessation, bound to the form and formless realms; fifth, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing the path, bound to the desire realm; sixth, full understanding that cuts off what is to be cut off by seeing the path, bound to the form and formless realms; seventh, full understanding that cuts off the fetters conforming to the lower portion; eighth, full understanding of the exhaustion of craving for form; and ninth, full understanding of the exhaustion of craving for the formless.
Their characteristics have already been discussed in the Ground of Concentrated States.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Nine stages of full understanding trace an arhat's complete cutting-off of affliction, established on two grounds: the first six through penetrating the truths directly at the path of seeing, the last three through utterly transcending each realm at the path of cultivation. The first six, taken in three pairs (desire-realm and form-formless-realm versions of the same cutting), sever the conceptually-constructed afflictions bound to the truths of suffering-and-origin, of cessation, and of the path respectively — twenty afflictions in the desire realm's first pair (the five sharp views and five dull afflictions applied to each of the two truths), eighteen in each form-and-formless-realm pair (the same ten minus aversion, which does not arise in those higher realms, applied across the pair of realms together). The seventh, cutting off the fetters conforming to the lower portion, completes the path of cultivation's work within the desire realm — severing the innate craving and aversion still remaining after stream-entry, secured only at the non-returner's fruit. And the eighth and ninth complete the parallel work in the form and formless realms respectively, exhausting whatever subtle craving for meditative absorption or higher rebirth still remains. Achieving all nine together constitutes arhatship in full.

52. Section: The Three Gates of Liberation — Emptiness, Wishlessness, and Signlessness — Closing All Five Approaches to Contemplation Entirely

There are further three gates of liberation: emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness. Know that these too are just as already discussed in the Ground of Concentrated States.

Chinese Source

復有三解脫門。謂空、無願、無相。當知亦如三摩呬多地已辯其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are further three gates of liberation: emptiness, wishlessness, and signlessness. Know that these too are just as already discussed in the Ground of Concentrated States.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where the three concentrations named elsewhere in the treatise may be either contaminated or uncontaminated depending on the practitioner, these three gates of liberation, detailed separately in the Śrāvaka Ground, are specifically the uncontaminated contemplations that actually cut off affliction. The gate of emptiness observes that every conditioned dharma, born from the coincidence of causes and conditions, contains no genuine being, life, or self at all — whether sought within or apart from the aggregates, no such self can be found, and thoroughly penetrating this brings mastery over every dharma. The gate of wishlessness observes the manifold faults and dangers pervading the five contaminated aggregates in every possible rebirth throughout the three realms, and, seeing this, no longer wishes for any further such rebirth to come. And the gate of signlessness arises from a deep aspiration toward nirvāṇa's unborn, unceasing peace — seeing it as the most ultimate, tranquil state, forever beyond the three realms' suffering and free of every characteristic whatsoever. Broadly: emptiness leans toward personal selflessness, wishlessness toward freedom from craving for further existence, and signlessness toward the markless nature of nirvāṇa itself — three distinct doors onto the same liberation. With this, all five approaches to contemplation — the seven attentions, ten wisdoms, six consciousness-groups, nine full understandings, and three gates of liberation — are fully explained, closing this account entirely.

53. Section: A Summary of the Whole Method — Applying Every Approach Again and Again to Contemplate All Dharmas — Closing the Meaning of Attention Entirely

Within this, one should distinguish, of any dharma: by how many kinds of attention it is contemplated; by how many kinds of wisdom it is known; by how many consciousnesses it is discerned; by how many kinds of full understanding it is fully understood; and by how many gates of liberation it is liberated. Through immeasurable approaches to contemplation such as these, one should contemplate every dharma.

Chinese Source

此中應當分別諸法，幾種作意之所思惟，幾智所知，幾識所識，幾種遍知之所遍知，幾解脫門之所解脫。以如是等無量觀門，應觀諸法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, one should distinguish, of any dharma: by how many kinds of attention it is contemplated; by how many kinds of wisdom it is known; by how many consciousnesses it is discerned; by how many kinds of full understanding it is fully understood; and by how many gates of liberation it is liberated. Through immeasurable approaches to contemplation such as these, one should contemplate every dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This closing instruction draws the five approaches together into a single method: a practitioner should learn to ask, of any conditioned or unconditioned dharma under examination, which of the seven attentions applies to it, which of the ten wisdoms knows it, which of the six consciousness-groups discerns it, which of the nine full understandings cuts off affliction connected to it, and which of the three gates of liberation opens through it — and should take up whichever approach fits, again and again, as an ongoing practice of contemplation. With this, the meaning of attention and the rest, the third of the ten kinds of meaning, is fully explained, closing it entirely. The treatise turns next to the fourth kind: the meaning of basis.

54. Section: The Meaning of Basis Opens — Every Sutra Rests on Some Matter, Some Time, and Some Person

The meaning of basis is, in brief, of three kinds: first, basis in a matter; second, basis in a time; and third, basis in a person.

Chinese Source

依處義者，略有三種。一者、事依處，二者、時依處，三者、補特伽羅依處。

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of basis is, in brief, of three kinds: first, basis in a matter; second, basis in a time; and third, basis in a person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth kind of meaning identifies what any given teaching in a sūtra actually rests on. Basis in a matter itself subdivides into three: the fundamental matter concerning a being's own condition, the matter of the means for attaining it, and the matter of compassion for others — each to be unfolded shortly. Basis in a time covers matters spoken of as past, future, or present. And basis in a person covers the twenty-seven kinds of listener already classified in such detail under the occasion for asking, since the Buddha addresses each kind of being with teaching suited to that being specifically. Every sūtra's content, in the end, comes down to some person, some matter, and some time — this threefold basis is simply what any passage of scripture is actually about.

The fundamental matter is, further, of six kinds: first, a fortunate destiny; second, an unfortunate destiny; third, decline; fourth, advancement; fifth, birth-and-death; and sixth, nirvāṇa. The matter of the means for attaining it is, further, of twelve kinds — namely, twelve kinds of conduct: first, conduct of desire; second, conduct of departure; third, wholesome conduct; fourth, unwholesome conduct; fifth, painful conduct; sixth, non-painful conduct; seventh, conduct conforming to decline; eighth, conduct conforming to advancement; ninth, defiled conduct; tenth, pure conduct; eleventh, conduct for one's own benefit; and twelfth, conduct for others' benefit. The matter of compassion for others is, further, of five kinds: first, causing freedom from craving; second, demonstrating; third, instructing; fourth, encouraging; and fifth, rejoicing together.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Basis in a matter, the largest of the three bases, unfolds into three further layers. The fundamental matter — "fundamental" naming the five aggregates as the very ground of a being's existence and practice — covers six conditions any being's aggregates may be found in, from fortunate and unfortunate rebirth through decline, advancement, ordinary birth-and-death, and finally nirvāṇa itself, each to be detailed shortly. The matter of the means for attaining these conditions covers twelve kinds of conduct, really six pairs of contrasting reasoning-by-dependence applied to how a being actually acts — desire versus departure from it, wholesome versus unwholesome, painful versus not, conforming to decline versus to advancement, defiled versus pure, and self-benefiting versus other-benefiting conduct. And the matter of compassion for others names five concrete ways of helping beings: freeing them from craving, demonstrating the teaching directly, instructing them further, encouraging their effort, and rejoicing together in whatever progress is made — the practical shape genuine compassion takes.

56. Section: A Fortunate Destiny, an Unfortunate One, and Fourteen Ways a Being Can Decline

Chinese Source

Just as with the decline of lifespan, so too should be known the decline of form, of strength, of wealth, of comfort, of renown, and of eloquence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A fortunate destiny simply names rebirth as a human or a god, drawn by wholesome karma, wholesome intention, and correct view; an unfortunate destiny names rebirth in hell, as a hungry ghost, or as an animal, drawn by their opposites. Decline, the third condition, then unfolds in seven ways not measured against anyone else — one's own lifespan, physical appearance, strength, wealth, comfort, renown, and eloquence, each simply compared against one's own earlier state, whether through natural exhaustion of karma, exhaustion of merit, or premature death through carelessness — and seven ways measured specifically against another being: being born into a lower caste than someone else, losing a concentration once possessed while another retains it, having a thin family line where another's flourishes, speaking without the authority that commands respect, possessing duller wisdom, being unable to obtain the fine sense-pleasures another enjoys, and, even where enjoyment is available, taking no genuine delight in it at all — fourteen concrete ways, in total, that a being's condition can decline.

57. Section: Advancement as Decline's Reverse, Birth-and-Death as Their Whole Cycle, and the Two Realms of Nirvana — Closing the Fundamental Matter Entirely

Kinds of this sort are called decline. The reverse of this, in each respective way, is called advancement. Birth-and-death is: precisely fortunate destiny, unfortunate destiny, decline, and advancement together. Nirvāṇa is: the two realms of nirvāṇa, with residue and without residue.

Chinese Source

如是等類，名為退墮。與此相違，隨其所應，名為昇進。生死者，謂即善趣惡趣退墮昇進。涅槃者，謂有餘依及無餘依二涅槃界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Kinds of this sort are called decline. The reverse of this, in each respective way, is called advancement.
Birth-and-death is: precisely fortunate destiny, unfortunate destiny, decline, and advancement together.
Nirvāṇa is: the two realms of nirvāṇa, with residue and without residue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Advancement is simply the mirror image of decline just explained — lengthening rather than shortening lifespan, gaining rather than losing status, both without comparison to another and measured against another. Birth-and-death, the fifth condition, is then shown to be nothing other than the first four conditions taken together: fortunate and unfortunate destiny, decline and advancement, cycling endlessly — the *Connected Discourses* likens it to a stick thrown into the air, bound to land somewhere, on clean ground or foul, exactly as one who does not truly know the four noble truths must land in some destiny or other, favorable or not. Nirvāṇa, the sixth and final condition, names the two realms already explored at length in fascicle 80: nirvāṇa with residue, where the *Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra* and the *Establishment of Consciousness-Only* both confirm that an arhat's outflows are forever exhausted even while the physical body, inherited from prior karma, still continues — every affliction-based support gone, though the bare support of life itself remains, so that the fetters are truly and completely ended even now; and nirvāṇa without residue, where even that residual bodily support finally ceases, every trace of suffering forever stilled. With this, all six conditions of the fundamental matter — fortunate destiny, unfortunate destiny, decline, advancement, birth-and-death, and nirvāṇa — are fully explained, closing the fundamental matter entirely. The treatise turns next to the matter of the means for attaining these conditions, in twelve kinds of conduct.

58. Section: Conduct of Desire — Ten Kinds of Person, From Wealth Ill-Gotten and Hoarded to Wealth Rightly Earned and Rightly Used

Conduct of desire is: just as discussed among the ten kinds of one who enjoys the objects of desire.

Chinese Source

欲行者，謂如十種受用欲中說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct of desire is: just as discussed among the ten kinds of one who enjoys the objects of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Drawing on a teaching the Buddha gave the householder Anāthapiṇḍika in the *Madhyama-āgama*, the treatise's own opening Ground of Mind distinguishes ten kinds of person by how wealth is earned and then used, moving from worst to best. Some earn wealth unlawfully (through killing, arms-dealing, prostitution, gambling, or the like) and then neither enjoy it themselves, support their family, nor give toward merit; some earn it unlawfully and enjoy it alone, without supporting others or giving; some earn it unlawfully yet still enjoy it, support their family, and give toward merit. The same three patterns of use then repeat for wealth earned partly lawfully and partly not, and again for wealth earned entirely lawfully — except that the lawfully-earned case adds a further, more refined distinction: even one who earns lawfully, enjoys the wealth, supports family, and gives toward merit may still not understand the value of renunciation in how it is used, while the tenth and most complete kind does understand this, setting some portion of rightly-earned, rightly-shared wealth toward genuine spiritual practice rather than mere indulgence. Of the ten, only this last is fully rounded — earning rightly, using rightly, sharing rightly, and understanding rightly all at once.

59. Section: Departure, Wholesome Conduct, and Unwholesome Conduct — Renouncing the World, Cultivating Merit, and the Three Poisons' Own Deeds

Conduct of departure is: having understood, regarding whatever is enjoyed, its impermanence and the rest, becoming weary of it and going forth, upholding the precepts, guarding the sense-doors, and the rest. Wholesome conduct is: giving, discipline, and cultivating wholesome, contaminated conduct. Unwholesome conduct is: the three kinds of evil conduct.

Chinese Source

離行者，謂即於彼所受用事知無常等已，厭而出家，受持禁戒，守根門等。善行者，謂施、戒、修善有漏行。不善行者，謂三種惡行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct of departure is: having understood, regarding whatever is enjoyed, its impermanence and the rest, becoming weary of it and going forth, upholding the precepts, guarding the sense-doors, and the rest.
Wholesome conduct is: giving, discipline, and cultivating wholesome, contaminated conduct.
Unwholesome conduct is: the three kinds of evil conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Conduct of departure names the practitioner's path directly opposite conduct of desire: seeing clothing, food, medicine, and every other object of enjoyment as impermanent, suffering, empty, and selfless, one grows weary of the world and goes forth, taking up the precepts, guarding the senses, eating in moderation, and remaining wakeful in practice — the whole apparatus of renunciation that leads out of saṃsāra. Wholesome conduct names giving, discipline, and the four immeasurables cultivated as contaminated good, yielding favorable human and heavenly fruit. And unwholesome conduct names the three evil deeds of body, speech, and mind driven by greed, hatred, and delusion — or, counted differently, the ten unwholesome deeds — which, left unconfessed and grown weighty, draw rebirth in one of the three lower destinies.

60. Section: Fruitless Austerity, the Middle Way's Ease, and the Deeds That Draw Beings Toward Decline or Advancement

Painful conduct is: going naked without clothing, and kinds such as this, and so on, at length. Non-painful conduct is: not discarding whatever ease is lawfully obtained, departing from the two extremes — namely, the extreme of indulging in the pleasures of desire, and the extreme of indulging in self-inflicted austerity — and, relying on the middle way, lawfully seeking and correctly using clothing and the rest. Conduct conforming to decline is: whatever conduct is able to obstruct the advancement of one's lifespan and the rest. The reverse of this should be known as conduct conforming to advancement, just as discussed in the Parrot Sūtra.

Chinese Source

苦行者，謂露形無衣，如是等類乃至廣說。非苦行者，謂不棄捨如法所得所有安樂。遠離二邊，所謂受用欲樂行邊，及與受用自苦行邊；依止中道，如法追求及正受用衣服等事。順退分行者，謂所有行能障壽等諸昇進事。與此相違，當知即是順進分行，如鸚鵡經說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Painful conduct is: going naked without clothing, and kinds such as this, and so on, at length.
Non-painful conduct is: not discarding whatever ease is lawfully obtained, departing from the two extremes — namely, the extreme of indulging in the pleasures of desire, and the extreme of indulging in self-inflicted austerity — and, relying on the middle way, lawfully seeking and correctly using clothing and the rest.
Conduct conforming to decline is: whatever conduct is able to obstruct the advancement of one's lifespan and the rest. The reverse of this should be known as conduct conforming to advancement, just as discussed in the Parrot Sūtra.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Painful conduct names the extreme ascetic practices of certain non-Buddhist practitioners — going naked, starving themselves, or otherwise inflicting needless suffering in the mistaken belief that pain itself purchases liberation, since without genuinely cutting off affliction, such suffering accomplishes nothing. Non-painful conduct is the Buddha's own middle way: neither indulging desire nor punishing the body, but lawfully obtaining and rightly using life's necessities with mindfulness and moderation, free of greed, hatred, and delusion alike. Conduct conforming to decline names any deed that obstructs one's own lifespan or other advancement — while its reverse, conduct conforming to advancement, is illustrated in the *Parrot Sūtra*, where the householder Śuka questions the Buddha at length about exactly which causes and conditions lead to long life or short, prosperity or poverty, laying out the full causal picture of what draws a being toward advancement rather than decline.

61. Section: Defiled Conduct — Three Defilements Unfold Into Nine Root Terms, From the Three Poisons to the Four Inversions

Defiled conduct is, in brief, of three kinds: first, karmic defilement; second, afflictive defilement; and third, defilement of circling on. Know that within this there are nine root terms. Karmic defilement has three terms: first, greed; second, hatred; and third, delusion. Afflictive defilement has four terms — namely, the four inversions. Defilement of circling on has two terms — namely, ignorance and craving for existence.

Chinese Source

雜染行者，略有三種。一者、業雜染，二者、煩惱雜染，三者、流轉雜染。當知此中，有九根本句。謂業雜染有三句，一、貪欲，二、瞋恚，三、愚癡；煩惱雜染有四句，即四顛倒；流轉雜染有二句，謂無明及有愛。

Ru-Yi Translation

Defiled conduct is, in brief, of three kinds: first, karmic defilement; second, afflictive defilement; and third, defilement of circling on.
Know that within this there are nine root terms. Karmic defilement has three terms: first, greed; second, hatred; and third, delusion. Afflictive defilement has four terms — namely, the four inversions. Defilement of circling on has two terms — namely, ignorance and craving for existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Defiled conduct — every contaminated dharma not yet freed from distortion or affliction's seeds — traces back to ignorance, which breeds affliction, which drives karma, which produces the circling of birth-and-death. Karmic defilement covers the contaminated bodily, verbal, and mental karma (meritorious, demeritorious, and unmoving) that affliction produces, itself rooted in greed, hatred, and delusion. Afflictive defilement covers every root and secondary affliction, all of them ultimately accompanied by ignorance, and is here represented by the four inversions: the false view of self mistaking what is selfless for a self; the extremist view of permanence mistaking what is impermanent for permanent; the view holding an inferior view as supreme mistaking what is impure for pure; and attachment to wrong precepts and vows mistaking what is suffering for bliss (craving itself spans both the impure-as-pure and the suffering-as-bliss inversions). And defilement of circling on — the very continuation of a defiled life through defiled karma — is represented by its two deepest roots, ignorance and craving for existence, whether self-love for one's own aggregates or craving for pleasant objects.

62. Section: Why Three, Four, and Two — Renunciants Driven by Ignorance, Householders by Craving — Closing Defiled Conduct Entirely

Why is this? Because the three unwholesome roots give rise to manifold karmic defilement. Because the four inversions are able to arouse manifold afflictive defilement. Once affliction has arisen, it is through the gate of ignorance that renunciants give rise to manifold defilement of circling on, and through the gate of craving for existence that householders give rise to manifold defilement of circling on.

Chinese Source

所以者何？由三不善根，生起種種業雜染故。由四顛倒，能發種種煩惱雜染故。煩惱生已，由無明門，諸出家者能生種種流轉雜染；由有愛門，諸在家者能生種種流轉雜染。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why is this? Because the three unwholesome roots give rise to manifold karmic defilement. Because the four inversions are able to arouse manifold afflictive defilement.
Once affliction has arisen, it is through the gate of ignorance that renunciants give rise to manifold defilement of circling on, and through the gate of craving for existence that householders give rise to manifold defilement of circling on.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The reasoning behind each count is now made explicit. Greed, hatred, and delusion — the three unwholesome roots — directly give rise to every karmic defilement, which is why karmic defilement is represented by exactly these three terms. The four inversions likewise directly arouse afflictive defilement in its many forms — mistaking the impermanent aggregates as permanent breeds the extremist view; mistaking austerity as the cause of liberation breeds attachment to wrong precepts; mistaking the selfless as self breeds the false view of self; and mistaking these very impure views as supremely pure breeds the view holding an inferior view as supreme — which is why afflictive defilement is represented by these four. And once affliction has arisen, renunciants — who have left behind the ordinary concerns of desire, so that whatever affliction remains tends to be rooted in mistaken views — circle on chiefly through the gate of ignorance, while householders — still immersed in worldly desire, so that affliction tends to be rooted in craving — circle on chiefly through the gate of craving for existence: craving conditions grasping, grasping conditions becoming, and becoming unfolds into birth, aging, and death, the whole mass of suffering. With this, defiled conduct, its nine root terms, and the reasoning behind each, is fully explained, closing it entirely. The treatise turns next to pure conduct.

63. Section: Pure Conduct — the Three Trainings Mapped Onto Five Stages, From Freedom From Greed to Final Liberation

Pure conduct is, in brief, the three trainings and the five stages — the ground of accumulation through the ultimate ground, just as already discussed. Know that training and the rest have nine root terms: the training in higher discipline and the training in higher mind have freedom from greed, from hatred, and from delusion, at the ground of accumulation and the ground of application; the training in higher wisdom has the four freedoms from inversion, clarity, and liberation, at the ground of seeing, the ground of cultivation, and the ultimate ground.

Chinese Source

清淨行者，略有三學、五地。謂資糧地乃至究竟地，如先已說。當知學等，有九根本句。謂增上戒學及增上心學，有無貪、無瞋、無癡，在資糧地及加行地；增上慧學，有四無顛倒明及解脫，在見地、修地及究竟地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Pure conduct is, in brief, the three trainings and the five stages — the ground of accumulation through the ultimate ground, just as already discussed.
Know that training and the rest have nine root terms: the training in higher discipline and the training in higher mind have freedom from greed, from hatred, and from delusion, at the ground of accumulation and the ground of application; the training in higher wisdom has the four freedoms from inversion, clarity, and liberation, at the ground of seeing, the ground of cultivation, and the ultimate ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Pure conduct — the tenth of the twelve — maps the three trainings directly onto the five stages already explained under the meaning of ground, again in nine root terms. Discipline cannot arise without some foundation, mind cannot settle without discipline, and wisdom cannot arise without a settled mind — the three depend on each other in strict order. The training in higher discipline, paired with the ground of accumulation, and the training in higher mind, paired with the ground of application (where concentration first becomes possible and insight can be cultivated within it), are each represented by freedom from greed, hatred, and delusion — six terms in total. The training in higher wisdom, spanning the ground of seeing, the ground of cultivation, and the ultimate ground, is represented by three further terms: freedom from the four inversions (the uncontaminated right view attained at the path of seeing, where conceptually-constructed distortion no longer arises); clarity (ignorance progressively dispelled through the path of cultivation); and liberation (the ultimate ground's final freedom, wisdom's own work brought to completion). Though each pairing highlights one stage in particular, all three qualities are, in truth, present in some degree at every one of the three later stages together — the pairing simply names where each becomes most prominent.

64. Section: Conduct for One's Own Benefit and Conduct for Others' — Closing All Twelve Kinds of Conduct, and the Means of Attainment, Entirely

Conduct for one's own benefit is: conduct for self-benefit. As with a śrāvaka or a solitary awakened one — though they may, at times, give rise to conduct benefiting others, their fundamental aspiration is not oriented solely toward benefiting others, and so their conduct is called conduct for one's own benefit. Conduct for others' benefit is: conduct benefiting others. As with a buddha or a bodhisattva, who, wishing to benefit immeasurable sentient beings, wishing to bring ease to immeasurable sentient beings — and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

自義行者，謂自利行。如聲聞、獨覺，彼雖或時起利他行，然本期願不唯利他，是故所行名自義行。他義行者，謂利他行。如佛菩薩為欲利益無量眾生，為欲安樂無量眾生，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct for one's own benefit is: conduct for self-benefit. As with a śrāvaka or a solitary awakened one — though they may, at times, give rise to conduct benefiting others, their fundamental aspiration is not oriented solely toward benefiting others, and so their conduct is called conduct for one's own benefit.
Conduct for others' benefit is: conduct benefiting others. As with a buddha or a bodhisattva, who, wishing to benefit immeasurable sentient beings, wishing to bring ease to immeasurable sentient beings — and so on, at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The final pair of the twelve kinds of conduct distinguishes not what is done but why. A śrāvaka may teach, and a pratyekabuddha may display a wondrous transformation, each thereby benefiting other beings incidentally — yet the deep aspiration behind their practice was never fundamentally oriented that way, viewing the three realms as a prison and birth-and-death as an enemy, emphasizing personal selflessness through the four truths and dependent origination, cutting off the afflictions of view and cultivation, and inclining toward solitary practice over active engagement with the world; their compassion, though genuine, remains comparatively slight beside a bodhisattva's. A buddha or bodhisattva, by contrast, though also delighting in nirvāṇa and wary of affliction — even more so than a śrāvaka — takes compassionate engagement with the world as the very point of practice from the start: self-benefit and benefiting others together, awakening oneself and awakening others together, emphasizing dharma-emptiness, cultivating the six perfections and the non-dual gate to reality as the very provisions of the path, seeing worldly and ultimate truth as inseparable and saṃsāra as nirvāṇa itself, and thereby able to labor for beings without limit across all future time. With this, all twelve kinds of conduct — desire, departure, wholesome, unwholesome, painful, non-painful, conforming to decline, conforming to advancement, defiled, pure, self-benefiting, and other-benefiting — are fully explained, closing the matter of the means of attainment entirely. The treatise turns next to the matter of compassion for others, in five concrete acts.

65. Section: Compassion for Others Opens — Freeing Beings From Craving by Exposing Six Dark Faults, and Guiding Them Toward Bright Conduct Through Four True Principles

Causing freedom from craving is: reproving the six kinds of dark conduct, displaying their danger, so as to free beings from craving. Demonstrating is: in order to cause beings to take up and study bright conduct, displaying the four kinds of true principle.

Chinese Source

令離欲者，謂訶責六種黑品諸行，示現過患，令離愛欲。示現者，謂為令受學白品行故，示現四種真實道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Causing freedom from craving is: reproving the six kinds of dark conduct, displaying their danger, so as to free beings from craving.
Demonstrating is: in order to cause beings to take up and study bright conduct, displaying the four kinds of true principle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The matter of compassion for others, the third and final layer of basis in a matter, unfolds in five stages of guidance, beginning with these two. Causing freedom from craving reproves six dark faults the Śrāvaka Ground names in beings who mistake what is impure, within or without, for something genuinely lovely: such delusion is itself contentious, painful, harmful, disaster-prone, and consumed by burning craving, and it draws forth future birth, aging, sickness, death, and every attendant sorrow — seeing this danger clearly is what first moves a being to wish for freedom from craving at all. Demonstrating then guides that same being toward bright, wholesome conduct by displaying the four kinds of true principle already explained earlier in this fascicle: reasoning by dependence, by function, by proof, and by the nature of things as they simply are — the very framework by which a teaching's correspondence with reality is established, now offered as the positive path once craving's danger has been seen.

66. Section: Guiding, Encouraging, and Rejoicing — the Final Three Acts of Compassion, From First Discipline to a Student's Own Request for Further Teaching

Because such a one has thereby attained mastery over that practice, that one then asks: "What should I now do? I wish only for further teaching" — and is then told: "In such and such a matter, you should now correctly act and further train." Encouraging is: when such a being's mind grows discouraged regarding what is to be known, practiced, or attained, at that time praising and spurring on that mind, enabling it to find the strength for that very matter. Rejoicing is: when such a being courageously practices correctly in accordance with the Dharma, genuinely praising and delighting in this, so as to bring forth that being's joy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The final three acts of compassion complete a natural progression. Guiding places a student who has come to genuine resolute understanding into the discipline suited to a beginner — good conduct comes first, before concentration and wisdom — and once that student has mastered ordinary conduct well enough to ask, of their own accord, "what should I do next?", the teacher then guides them toward the further trainings of concentration and wisdom, exactly as the Śrāvaka Ground describes at length. Encouraging responds to a student whose confidence has flagged partway through — a well-timed word of genuine praise restores the strength to continue. And rejoicing celebrates a student's genuine, courageous progress along the path toward nirvāṇa, and the joy such celebration brings becomes itself the fuel for continuing further. With this, all five acts of compassion for others — causing freedom from craving, demonstrating, guiding, encouraging, and rejoicing, unfolding in their natural sequence — are fully explained. The treatise turns next to the specific ways these acts combine, and the benefits rejoicing in particular brings.

67. Section: Three Ways Guidance Toward Freedom From Craving and Demonstration Combine — Bare Instruction, Bare Explanation, or Both Together

Further, regarding causing freedom from craving and demonstrating: there is causing freedom from craving without demonstrating — as when one guides another toward freedom from craving by simply saying, "As has been said, this should not be done, so you now must certainly not do it again"; or by intimidation, saying, "If you do this, I will certainly respond in such and such a way"; or by appeal, saying, "If you are truly my beloved good friend, you certainly should not do this." There is also demonstrating without causing freedom from craving — as with one of middling capacity, to whom the merits and faults are displayed, yet who is not yet capable of being restrained from the fault. And there is both causing freedom from craving and demonstrating together — as when the fault is shown, and freedom from craving is thereby brought about.

Chinese Source

復次，令離欲示現者，或有令離欲而不示現。如教導他令其離欲，而謂彼曰：如某所言不應作者，汝今必定不應復作。或怖彼言：汝若作者，我必當作如是如是。或復求彼：汝若是我親愛善友，必不應作。或有示現，不令離欲。如處中者，示現功德及與過失，而未堪遮令離過失。或有令離，欲亦示現。如示彼過，令其離欲。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, regarding causing freedom from craving and demonstrating: there is causing freedom from craving without demonstrating — as when one guides another toward freedom from craving by simply saying, "As has been said, this should not be done, so you now must certainly not do it again"; or by intimidation, saying, "If you do this, I will certainly respond in such and such a way"; or by appeal, saying, "If you are truly my beloved good friend, you certainly should not do this."
There is also demonstrating without causing freedom from craving — as with one of middling capacity, to whom the merits and faults are displayed, yet who is not yet capable of being restrained from the fault.
And there is both causing freedom from craving and demonstrating together — as when the fault is shown, and freedom from craving is thereby brought about.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

These first two acts of compassion do not always occur together, and the treatise now distinguishes three combinations. Bare instruction toward freedom from craving — through direct command, through warning of consequence, or through personal appeal to affection — changes behavior without necessarily explaining why, useful for a student not yet ready to absorb the underlying reasoning. Bare demonstration without insisting on immediate change suits a student of middling capacity, who benefits from seeing both the merit of restraint and the fault of indulgence laid out clearly, even though not yet capable of actually being restrained — left, instead, to reflect and awaken to it in their own time. And the fullest approach combines both: showing the fault and, through that very demonstration, bringing about actual freedom from craving. A skillful teacher reads which combination a given student actually needs.

68. Section: Guiding a Beginner to Study, Encouraging One Who Has Stalled, and Five Benefits Rejoicing Brings the Whole Community

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Guiding and encouraging, paired together here, mark two distinct moments: guiding a complete beginner into study for the first time, and encouraging one already studying but currently stalled, toward renewed advancement. Rejoicing then closes the sequence with five concrete benefits reaching well beyond the individual celebrated. A teacher's genuine celebration of a student's attainment confirms that student's own confidence in what has actually been realized; inspires the surrounding community to aspire toward the same realization for themselves; softens the mind of anyone who had been slandering that student, once the genuine merit becomes plain; leaves one not yet inclined either way settled in a wholesome neutrality rather than tipping toward doubt; and deepens the faith of those already well-disposed all the more. A single act of rightly-placed rejoicing thus ripples outward through an entire community.

If any person rejoices in another's good deed, know that this constructs and increases karma capable of drawing forth a pleasing rebirth among the gods. Beyond the five benefits rejoicing brings the surrounding community, the one who rejoices reaps a direct karmic reward as well: genuinely celebrating another's worldly or supramundane good deed itself constructs the projecting and completing karma of a pleasing heavenly rebirth, so that in whatever future life follows, that person constantly encounters pleasing sound and delightful circumstance in every direction — a fitting reward, since a mind that takes joy in others' goodness rather than begrudging it is itself already a wholesome, expansive state of mind. With this, all five acts of compassion for others — causing freedom from craving, demonstrating, guiding, encouraging, and rejoicing, together with how the first two combine and the benefits rejoicing brings both to others and to oneself — are fully explained, closing this entire matter of compassion entirely. The treatise turns next to comparing all twelve kinds of conduct against one another directly.

70. Section: Comparing the Twelve Kinds of Conduct Begins — Desire and Departure Can Each Lead Either Way, While Good and Evil Conduct Cannot

Further, conduct of desire may sometimes draw forth a fortunate destiny, as when unlawful karma is performed for the sake of a future desire; or it may draw forth an unfortunate destiny, as when the objects of desire are seized through unlawful means. Conduct of departure, if it involves transgression, draws forth an unfortunate destiny; if it is genuinely accomplished, it draws forth a fortunate destiny, and is also able to serve as a provision for nirvāṇa. Wholesome conduct draws forth a fortunate destiny, and is also able to serve as a provision for nirvāṇa; unwholesome conduct draws forth an unfortunate destiny.

復次，欲行或有能感善趣，如為欲故造後善業；或有能感惡趣，如以非法攝受諸欲。離行若有毀犯，能感惡趣；若能成辦，能感善趣，及能作涅槃資糧。善行能感善趣，及作涅槃資糧；不善行能感惡趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, conduct of desire may sometimes draw forth a fortunate destiny, as when unlawful karma is performed for the sake of a future desire; or it may draw forth an unfortunate destiny, as when the objects of desire are seized through unlawful means.
Conduct of departure, if it involves transgression, draws forth an unfortunate destiny; if it is genuinely accomplished, it draws forth a fortunate destiny, and is also able to serve as a provision for nirvāṇa.
Wholesome conduct draws forth a fortunate destiny, and is also able to serve as a provision for nirvāṇa; unwholesome conduct draws forth an unfortunate destiny.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having explained all twelve kinds of conduct individually, the treatise now compares them directly to bring out how each actually functions. Conduct of desire cuts both ways depending on method: cultivating the five precepts and ten wholesome deeds for the sake of a favorable future draws a fortunate destiny, while seizing desire's objects through killing, theft, or deceit draws an unfortunate one. Conduct of departure likewise cuts both ways depending on integrity: taking up precepts toward renunciation yet transgressing them without confession draws an unfortunate destiny, while genuinely keeping those precepts draws both a fortunate destiny and, further cultivated through concentration and wisdom, becomes an actual provision toward nirvāṇa. Wholesome and unwholesome conduct, by contrast, run in only one direction each: giving, precepts, and the four immeasurables draw a fortunate destiny and can themselves become a provision toward nirvāṇa once cultivated further, while the ten unwholesome deeds draw only an unfortunate destiny — no ambiguity in either case.

71. Section: Fruitless Austerity Draws Only Suffering, While the Middle Way and Rightly-Aimed Conduct Draw Toward Advancement

Painful conduct draws forth an unfortunate destiny, since it relies on wrong view to inflict suffering on the body; non-painful conduct is able to serve as a provision for nirvāṇa. Conduct conforming to decline and conduct conforming to advancement, in each respective way, lead to decline or to advancement.

Chinese Source

苦行能感惡趣，由依邪見自苦身故；非苦行能作涅槃資糧。順退分行、順進分行，隨其所應，退墮、昇進。

Ru-Yi Translation

Painful conduct draws forth an unfortunate destiny, since it relies on wrong view to inflict suffering on the body; non-painful conduct is able to serve as a provision for nirvāṇa.
Conduct conforming to decline and conduct conforming to advancement, in each respective way, lead to decline or to advancement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Painful conduct — the non-Buddhist ascetic's self-inflicted suffering, going naked or otherwise punishing the body — draws only an unfortunate destiny, since it rests on the mistaken view that pain itself purchases liberation, with no genuine insight established to make it fruitful; non-painful conduct, the Buddha's own middle way of lawfully obtaining and rightly using life's necessities, becomes instead a genuine provision toward nirvāṇa. Conduct conforming to decline and conduct conforming to advancement then simply lead where their names suggest: the ten unwholesome deeds are traditionally said to draw specific corresponding losses — killing draws short life and frequent illness, stealing draws frost and drought, sexual misconduct draws scandal and an unfaithful spouse, false speech draws foul breath and ill repute, divisive speech draws discord among one's own family, harsh speech draws bitter and stunted harvests, idle chatter draws a thorny, neglected orchard, greed draws an empty granary, hatred draws an ugly and full-of-thorn harvest, and wrong view draws small and unpleasant fruit — while their reverses draw the corresponding advancement: health and long life, wealth, a dignified family and good reputation, fragrant breath, harmonious kin, sweet harvests, a flourishing and trusted orchard, a full granary, a well-formed body, and fragrant, wholesome fruit.

72. Section: Defilement Draws Birth-and-Death While Purity Realizes Nirvana, and Self-Benefit Reaches Only Oneself While Other-Benefit Reaches Both — Closing the Comparison Entirely

Defiled conduct draws forth birth-and-death; pure conduct is able to realize nirvāṇa. Conduct for one's own benefit causes only oneself to go toward a fortunate destiny, reach advancement, and realize nirvāṇa; conduct for others' benefit causes both oneself and others to go toward a fortunate destiny, reach advancement, and realize nirvāṇa.

Chinese Source

雜染行能感生死，清淨行能證涅槃。自義行唯令自身往善趣，逮昇進，證涅槃；他義行俱令自他往善趣，逮昇進，證涅槃。

Ru-Yi Translation

Defiled conduct draws forth birth-and-death; pure conduct is able to realize nirvāṇa.
Conduct for one's own benefit causes only oneself to go toward a fortunate destiny, reach advancement, and realize nirvāṇa; conduct for others' benefit causes both oneself and others to go toward a fortunate destiny, reach advancement, and realize nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The final two comparisons close this whole survey cleanly. Defiled conduct — affliction, karma, and rebirth together — simply continues the suffering of birth-and-death; pure conduct — the three trainings across all five stages — is what actually realizes nirvāṇa; there is no ambiguity in either direction. And self-benefiting conduct, however genuinely it secures a fortunate destiny, advancement, and eventually nirvāṇa for the practitioner alone, reaches only that one person, while other-benefiting conduct — the bodhisattva's own aspiration — brings both self and others together toward the very same fortunate destiny, advancement, and nirvāṇa, the scope of benefit itself marking the deepest difference between the two paths. With this, the direct comparison of all twelve kinds of conduct — which draw favorable results, which unfavorable, which cut both ways, and which reach only the self or reach others as well — is fully explained, closing this comparison entirely. The treatise turns next to an overall summary of basis in a matter as a whole.

73. Section: An Overall Summary — the Fundamental Matter Sets the Aim, and Compassion Draws Beings Toward It Through the Twelve Kinds of Conduct — Closing Basis in a Matter Entirely

Thus, within these three matters: the fundamental matter has six kinds, the first being a fortunate destiny and the last being nirvāṇa; the matter of the means of attainment has twelve kinds — namely, the twelve kinds of conduct; and the matter of compassion for others has five kinds — namely, the five ways of showing compassion to sentient beings. Within this, it is through the empowering force of the fundamental matter that, relying on the twelve kinds of conduct, one causes others, as is fitting, to be freed from craving, and so on up through rejoicing.

Chinese Source

如是三事中，根本事有六種，謂初善趣乃至涅槃為後；得方便事有十二種，謂十二行；悲愍他事有五種，謂由五種悲愍眾生。此中由根本事增上力故，依十二行，如其所應，令他離欲乃至慶喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, within these three matters: the fundamental matter has six kinds, the first being a fortunate destiny and the last being nirvāṇa; the matter of the means of attainment has twelve kinds — namely, the twelve kinds of conduct; and the matter of compassion for others has five kinds — namely, the five ways of showing compassion to sentient beings.
Within this, it is through the empowering force of the fundamental matter that, relying on the twelve kinds of conduct, one causes others, as is fitting, to be freed from craving, and so on up through rejoicing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This closing summary shows how the three layers of basis in a matter actually work together as a single system, not three independent lists. The fundamental matter's six conditions set the very goal a being aspires toward — moving from an unfortunate state up through a fortunate destiny, advancement, and finally nirvāṇa itself; it is precisely the empowering pull of this goal that gives the twelve kinds of conduct their direction and force. And it is by drawing on those twelve kinds of conduct — departure, wholesome conduct, non-painful conduct, conduct conforming to advancement, pure conduct, and conduct benefiting both self and others among them — that the five acts of compassion actually guide a being, step by step from freedom from craving through rejoicing, toward whatever fortunate destiny or ultimate realization that being is capable of reaching. Goal, method, and guidance together: this is the complete architecture of basis in a matter. With this, the entire matter of basis — the fundamental matter, the means of its attainment, and compassion for others, together with their inner workings and this closing summary — is fully explained, closing basis in a matter entirely. The treatise turns next to basis in a time.

74. Section: Basis in a Time, and Basis in a Person — Past, Future, and Present Matters, Addressed to the Twenty-Seven Kinds of Listener

Basis in a time is, in brief, of three kinds of matter spoken of: first, matters spoken of the past; second, matters spoken of the future; and third, matters spoken of the present, just as extensively discussed in the sūtras. Basis in a person is: the twenty-seven kinds of person, of dull faculties and the rest, whose characteristics should be known.

Chinese Source

時依處者，謂略有三種言事。一者、過去言事，二者、未來言事，三者、現在言事。如經廣說。補特伽羅依處者，謂軟根等二十七種補特伽羅，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Basis in a time is, in brief, of three kinds of matter spoken of: first, matters spoken of the past; second, matters spoken of the future; and third, matters spoken of the present, just as extensively discussed in the sūtras.
Basis in a person is: the twenty-seven kinds of person, of dull faculties and the rest, whose characteristics should be known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second and third bases are comparatively brief, each pointing back to material already established elsewhere. Basis in a time simply sorts any given teaching by whether it addresses past matters (accounts of the Buddha's or noble disciples' former lives, as in the divisions of scripture devoted to past lives and past events), future matters (predictions of what is to come), or present matters (the immediate teaching of birth-and-death, nirvāṇa, defilement, purity, discipline, concentration, and wisdom as they stand right now). Basis in a person simply names, once again, the twenty-seven kinds of listener already classified in full detail under the occasion for asking — faculty, disposition, assembly, aspiration, savability, application, and kind — since it is always to some specific kind of listener that any given teaching is actually addressed.

75. Section: Why It Is Called Basis — the Buddha's Teaching Always Rests on Some Matter, Some Time, Some Person — Closing the Meaning of Basis Entirely

It is precisely in dependence on matter, time, and person, just as discussed above, that the buddhas, the world-honored ones, propagate the noble teaching — and this is why these are called basis.

Chinese Source

即依如是如上所說若事、若時、若補特伽羅故，諸佛世尊流布聖教，是故說彼名為依處。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is precisely in dependence on matter, time, and person, just as discussed above, that the buddhas, the world-honored ones, propagate the noble teaching — and this is why these are called basis.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This closing line names why these three — matter, time, and person — properly share the single term "basis": every teaching the buddhas propagate rests on some particular matter (the fundamental condition addressed, the means toward it, or compassionate guidance), occurs at some particular time (past, future, or present), and is addressed to some particular person (one of the twenty-seven kinds of listener) — with no teaching ever floating free of all three at once. With this, the meaning of basis — matter, time, and person together, matter itself unfolding through the fundamental condition, its means of attainment, and compassion for others — is fully explained, closing the fourth of the ten kinds of meaning, and one of its two largest, entirely. The treatise turns next to the fifth kind: the meaning of danger.

76. Section: Danger and Benefit — Rightly Condemning What Deserves Condemnation, and Rightly Praising What Deserves Praise

The meaning of danger is, in essence: with respect to a dharma or a person that genuinely deserves condemnation and aversion, giving rise to condemnation and aversion accordingly. The meaning of benefit is, in essence: with respect to a dharma or a person that genuinely deserves praise, giving rise to praise accordingly.

Chinese Source

過患義者，以要言之，於應毀厭義而起毀厭，或法、或補特伽羅。勝利義者，以要言之，於應稱讚義而起稱讚，或法、或補特伽羅。

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of danger is, in essence: with respect to a dharma or a person that genuinely deserves condemnation and aversion, giving rise to condemnation and aversion accordingly.
The meaning of benefit is, in essence: with respect to a dharma or a person that genuinely deserves praise, giving rise to praise accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth and sixth kinds of meaning are brief mirror images of each other. The meaning of danger names the Buddha's own warnings throughout the sūtras against whatever dharma or person genuinely warrants condemnation and aversion — craving, unwholesome dharmas, and those who commit evil deeds are all rightly held at a distance, not out of arbitrary judgment but because their actual danger has been seen clearly. The meaning of benefit names the reverse: wherever a dharma or person genuinely deserves praise — the qualities of buddhahood, or a being genuinely diligent in hearing, reflection, cultivation, and the three trainings — the Buddha praises it accordingly. Both meanings rest on the same principle: praise and condemnation alike should track what is actually deserved, neither more nor less.

77. Section: What Is Counteracted and What Counteracts It — Every Defiled Conduct Answered by Its Matching Pure Remedy

The meaning of what is to be counteracted is, in essence: every kind of defiled conduct. The meaning of what counteracts is, in essence: every kind of pure conduct. As greed is what is to be counteracted, and the contemplation of impurity is what counteracts it; as hatred is what is to be counteracted, and loving-kindness is what counteracts it — kinds such as these should all be known accordingly.

Chinese Source

所治義者，以要言之，一切雜染行。能治義者，以要言之，一切清淨行。如貪是所治，不淨為能治；瞋是所治，慈為能治；如是等，盡當知。

Ru-Yi Translation

The meaning of what is to be counteracted is, in essence: every kind of defiled conduct. The meaning of what counteracts is, in essence: every kind of pure conduct.
As greed is what is to be counteracted, and the contemplation of impurity is what counteracts it; as hatred is what is to be counteracted, and loving-kindness is what counteracts it — kinds such as these should all be known accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The seventh kind of meaning applies the general categories of defiled and pure conduct, already explained under basis in a matter, specifically as diagnosis and remedy. What is to be counteracted is affliction, karma, and rebirth in every form; what counteracts it is discipline, concentration, and wisdom across all five stages. The Buddha then prescribes specific remedies to specific afflictions exactly as a physician matches medicine to disease: greed is countered by contemplating the body's impurity, hatred by cultivating loving-kindness, excessive discursiveness by counting the breath, heavy karmic obstruction by mindfulness of the Buddha, and thick delusion by contemplating dependent origination — each affliction named in the earlier sevenfold classification of listeners by disposition now paired with its own matching antidote.

78. Section: Brief and Detailed Meaning Opens — Grouping Dharmas by Their Common Kind or Unpacking Every Distinction, and Teachings Provisional or Definitive

Brief meaning is: proclaiming dharmas together according to their common kind. Detailed meaning is: proclaiming dharmas according to their distinct kinds. Further, it is speaking of a non-definitive scripture, and it is speaking of a definitive scripture.

Chinese Source

略義者，謂宣說諸法同類相應。廣義者，謂宣說諸法異類相應。復次，說不了義經故，說了義經故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Brief meaning is: proclaiming dharmas together according to their common kind. Detailed meaning is: proclaiming dharmas according to their distinct kinds.
Further, it is speaking of a non-definitive scripture, and it is speaking of a definitive scripture.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eighth and final kind of meaning opens with two different ways of understanding "brief" and "detailed." First, by kind of dharma: speaking of a person's whole embodiment simply as "form and mind" together is brief, grouping every constituent under two broad headings without further distinction, while unpacking that same embodiment into its many particulars — the five faculties, the five viscera, the four great elements, and so on — is detailed, spelling out every distinction one by one. Second, by kind of scripture: a scripture that leaves a teaching's deeper principle unclear is, in this sense, "brief," while one that spells the principle out fully is "detailed" — provisional teaching corresponding to brevity, definitive teaching to full elaboration. The same terms, brief and detailed, thus carry two related but distinct senses depending on whether one is classifying dharmas or classifying scripture itself.

79. Section: Two Kinds of Brief, Two Kinds of Detailed — Brevity and Elaboration in Name Alone, and in Meaning Itself

Further, there are two kinds of brief meaning: first, brief in name; and second, brief in meaning. Just as with brief meaning, so too detailed meaning is likewise of two kinds: first, detailed in name; and second, detailed in meaning.

Chinese Source

復次，有二種略義。一者、名略，二者、義略。如略義，如是廣義亦有二種。一者、名廣，二者、義廣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, there are two kinds of brief meaning: first, brief in name; and second, brief in meaning.
Just as with brief meaning, so too detailed meaning is likewise of two kinds: first, detailed in name; and second, detailed in meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

A finer distinction now separates brevity and elaboration of mere terminology from brevity and elaboration of the underlying sense conveyed. Brief in name means a short designation is used regardless of how much meaning it actually carries; brief in meaning means the content itself, whatever words convey it, remains compressed and undeveloped. Detailed in name and detailed in meaning mirror this: a lengthy designation, or content genuinely unpacked at length. The two pairings do not always align — a short name can sometimes carry richly elaborated meaning, and a lengthy name can sometimes convey very little — which is exactly why the treatise distinguishes them as four separate possibilities rather than collapsing brief and detailed into a single scale.

80. Section: An Example From Scripture — Brief Words, Vast Meaning, and Genuine Understanding Rarely Attained — Closing Brief and Detailed Meaning, and All Ten Kinds of Meaning, Entirely

Just as the World-Honored One said: "Śāriputra, the dharma I teach is sometimes brief, sometimes detailed — yet one who genuinely comprehends it is exceedingly hard to find," spoken at length as in the sūtra. Know that this shows how, within a sūtra proper, the World-Honored One's text is extensive while the meaning is brief, whereas within a verse, the meaning is extensive while the text is brief.

Chinese Source

如世尊言：舍利子！我所說法或略、或廣，然悟解者甚難可得。廣說如經。當知此中，顯示世尊於契經中文廣義略，於伽陀中義廣文略。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as the World-Honored One said: "Śāriputra, the dharma I teach is sometimes brief, sometimes detailed — yet one who genuinely comprehends it is exceedingly hard to find," spoken at length as in the sūtra.
Know that this shows how, within a sūtra proper, the World-Honored One's text is extensive while the meaning is brief, whereas within a verse, the meaning is extensive while the text is brief.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha's own words to Śāriputra illustrate everything just explained about brief and detailed meaning, while adding a further, genuinely important point: whichever mode is used, genuine comprehension remains rare — brevity and elaboration are simply different vehicles for the same difficult truth, neither one guaranteeing that a listener actually grasps it. The treatise then draws out one final distinction: an ordinary prose sūtra typically has extensive text conveying, in the end, a fairly compact core meaning (the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s vast text ultimately concerns the ten grounds; the *Lotus Sūtra*'s seven fascicles ultimately concern opening beings' eyes to the Buddha's own insight), while a verse compresses vast meaning into very few words — brief and detailed thus trading places depending on whether prose or verse is being considered. With this, brief and detailed meaning — by kind of dharma, by kind of scripture, by name versus meaning, and now illustrated directly from the Buddha's own words — is fully explained, closing the eighth and final kind of meaning entirely. With it, all ten kinds of meaning — ground, characteristic, attention, basis, danger, benefit, what is counteracted, what counteracts, brief meaning, and detailed meaning — are now completely explained.

Chinese Source

為攝十義，故說中間嗢柁南曰：諸地相作意，依處德非德，所對治能治，廣略義應知。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to gather in the ten meanings, the following intervening summary verse is spoken: Grounds, marks, attentions; basis, virtue, and fault; what is to be counteracted, what counteracts; brief and detailed meaning — thus should they be known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This compact verse compresses all ten kinds of meaning just explained at such length into four short lines, a memory aid meant to be carried away and recalled at will: "grounds" for the meaning of ground, "marks" for the meaning of characteristic, "attentions" for the meaning of attention and the rest, "basis" for the meaning of basis, "virtue" and "fault" for the meanings of benefit and danger respectively, "what is to be counteracted" and "what counteracts" naming themselves directly, and "brief and detailed meaning" closing the list. A practitioner who has genuinely worked through the full exposition can, from now on, simply hold this one verse in mind and reconstruct the entire teaching from it — precisely the practice of compressing a long, carefully studied passage into a single memorable line of one's own devising, the very skill this whole account of meaning has been modeling throughout.

Further, having thus briefly explained the ten meanings that constitute the very body of the Buddha's teaching, every teacher of the Dharma should, in dependence on the noble teaching, seek out these ten — whether fully or only in part. Having personally sought them out in this way, one should then teach them to others.

Chinese Source

復次，如是略說佛教體性十種義已，諸說法者，應依聖教尋求十種，若具不具。既自求已，應為他說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, having thus briefly explained the ten meanings that constitute the very body of the Buddha's teaching, every teacher of the Dharma should, in dependence on the noble teaching, seek out these ten — whether fully or only in part. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This closing instruction underscores that the ten meanings are not merely an academic classification but a practical discipline: any teacher approaching a passage of scripture should actively search it for however many of these ten meanings genuinely apply — not every passage will yield all ten — and only after personally working through this search, deepening one's own understanding in the process, should that understanding then be passed on to others; teaching another is itself one of the ten methods of Dharma practice, and personal investigation always precedes it. With this, the sūtra's entire body — text, in six kinds, and meaning, in ten kinds — together with the method for any teacher to search it out, is fully explained, closing the sūtra's whole body of text and meaning entirely. The treatise turns next to the second of the five aspects promised at the very opening of this Compendium: explanation itself, beginning with the twelve divisions of scripture.

83. Section: Explanation Opens — Five Aspects for Rightly Expounding Any Sutra, From Its Essential Teaching to Its Very Sequence

Having thus established the body of every sūtra's text and meaning, every teacher of the Dharma should, through five aspects, explain any Buddhist sūtra in accordance with it: first, one should briefly state the essential teaching; next, one should proclaim its occasion; next, one should proclaim its meaning; next, one should resolve objections to it; and finally, one should clarify its sequence.

Chinese Source

如是建立諸經文義體已。諸說法者，應以五相，隨順解釋一切佛經。謂初應略說法要，次應宣說等起，次應宣說其義，次應釋難，後應辯次第。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having thus established the body of every sūtra's text and meaning, every teacher of the Dharma should, through five aspects, explain any Buddhist sūtra in accordance with it: first, one should briefly state the essential teaching; next, one should proclaim its occasion; next, one should proclaim its meaning; next, one should resolve objections to it; and finally, one should clarify its sequence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Having fully established what a sūtra's body actually consists of, the treatise turns to the second of the ten items previewed in the very opening summary verse: explanation itself, the second-longest topic of this whole Compendium. Any teacher expounding any sūtra should proceed through five ordered steps. First, briefly state the essential teaching — the *Amitābha Sūtra*'s essential teaching, for instance, is simply exhorting beings to seek rebirth in the Pure Land. Second, proclaim the occasion — who requested the teaching, when, on what matter, through what encouragement, toward what practice — essentially the basis already explained under the meaning of basis. Third, proclaim the meaning — both the overall sense and the particular points, general first and then specific. Fourth, resolve objections — posing, whether self-generated or drawn from an actual questioner (a Consciousness-Only teacher might anticipate a Madhyamaka objection, for instance), whatever difficulty a passage's meaning might otherwise leave unclear, and then answering it directly from scripture and reasoning. And fifth, clarify the sequence — the order in which the sūtra's own content actually unfolds. This fivefold method, now to be unpacked at length beginning with the essential teaching, is the very heart of the Compendium's practical instruction for anyone who would teach the Dharma.

84. Section: The Essential Teaching Opens — Twelve Divisions of Scripture, Beginning With the Sutra Proper

The essential teaching is, in brief, of twelve kinds — namely, the sūtra proper and the other eleven divisions of scripture. The sūtra proper is: what threads meaning together, spoken directly in prose, largely comprising the intended purport and own-nature of the teaching.

Chinese Source

法者，略有十二種，謂契經等十二分教。契經者，謂貫穿義長行直說，多分攝受意趣體性。

Ru-Yi Translation

The essential teaching is, in brief, of twelve kinds — namely, the sūtra proper and the other eleven divisions of scripture.
The sūtra proper is: what threads meaning together, spoken directly in prose, largely comprising the intended purport and own-nature of the teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first of the five aspects, the essential teaching, now unfolds into the twelve traditional divisions of Buddhist scripture — knowing which division a given passage belongs to is itself the first step toward grasping its essential point. The sūtra proper ("threaded scripture," from which the very word for scripture derives) is prose that threads its meaning together directly, without relying on verse, using elegant and abundant language to convey what leads toward genuine benefit and toward pure conduct, and to establish the nature of the aggregates, elements, sense-bases, and other such categories of dharma. Great-vehicle sūtras of this kind mostly guide disciples toward unsurpassed awakening; śrāvaka-vehicle sūtras of this kind mostly guide disciples toward liberation from the three realms — in either case, the sūtra proper conveys the teaching's core intent and essential nature most directly of all twelve divisions.

85. Section: Restatement in Verse, and Prediction — Recapping the Prose in Song, and Foretelling Where a Departed Disciple Will Be Reborn

Restatement in verse is: proclaiming a verse after the prose, and, further, briefly marking out the non-definitive scripture already spoken. Prediction is: extensively distinguishing the meaning already briefly marked out, and predicting where a departed disciple will be reborn.

Chinese Source

應頌者，謂長行後宣說伽陀，又略標所說不了義經。記別者，謂廣分別略所標義，及記命過弟子生處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Restatement in verse is: proclaiming a verse after the prose, and, further, briefly marking out the non-definitive scripture already spoken.
Prediction is: extensively distinguishing the meaning already briefly marked out, and predicting where a departed disciple will be reborn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Restatement in verse follows a lengthy prose passage with a verse recapping its content in brief — fearing a point made only in prose might not be fully grasped, the Buddha restates it in memorable verse form, as the *Lotus Sūtra* so often does after its own long expositions; unlike a freestanding verse, restatement in verse is always tied directly to the prose it follows, recapping rather than introducing new content. Prediction has two related senses: extensively unpacking a meaning only briefly indicated before, and — its more familiar sense — foretelling where a disciple will be reborn after death, including the many predictions of future buddhahood the Buddha gives throughout the *Lotus Sūtra*, naming the future buddha-name, buddha-land, and beings to be liberated for disciples such as Śāriputra.

86. Section: Spontaneous Verse, and Unprompted Teaching — Standalone Song Unlinked to Any Prose, and the Buddha Speaking Before Anyone Asks

Spontaneous verse is: teaching through verse — whether in two lines, or in three, four, five, or six lines. Unprompted teaching is: teaching without having been asked, in order to bring a disciple to genuine resolute understanding; and, in order to establish a being of superior capacity in the excellent principle, teaching spontaneously — as a sūtra says, "The World-Honored One now spontaneously proclaims."

Chinese Source

諷頌者，謂以句說。或以二句，或以三、四、五、六句說。自說者，謂無請而說，為令弟子得勝解故。為令上品所化有情安住勝理，自然而說。如經言：世尊今者自然宣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

Spontaneous verse is: teaching through verse — whether in two lines, or in three, four, five, or six lines.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Spontaneous verse, unlike restatement in verse, stands entirely on its own — self-contained, whether two lines or up to six, needing no accompanying prose passage to make sense, as with the *Dharmapada* or Aśvaghoṣa's own verses in praise of the Buddha. Unprompted teaching then covers two related situations, distinguished by the capacity of the audience: teaching given though no one asked, aimed at bringing an ordinary disciple to genuine resolute understanding through hearing and reflection — the *Amitābha Sūtra* itself is a well-known example, spoken though unrequested — and teaching aimed specifically at a being of superior faculties, establishing that being directly in the profound principle of true suchness or nirvāṇa, suited to one whose cultivated wisdom is already close to direct realization, a final decisive word offered at exactly the right moment rather than the more gradual instruction resolute understanding requires. Both count as "unprompted" since neither was requested, yet they serve disciples at quite different stages of readiness.

87. Section: Causal Occasion — Teaching Given at a Listener's Request, Discipline Established Through a Transgression, and Any Word Traced to Its Own Cause — Closing This Division Entirely

Causal occasion is: teaching given at another's request — as a sūtra says, "The World-Honored One, on one occasion, in dependence on Kṛṣṇa-mṛgadāraka, proclaimed the essential teaching for the monks." Further, it is every word comprised within monastic discipline that arises from the causal occasion of individual liberation. Further, wherever it is said: "The World-Honored One, in dependence on such-and-such a cause and condition, in dependence on such-and-such a matter, spoke such-and-such words" — this too is included.

Chinese Source

緣起者，謂有請而說。如經言：世尊一時依黑鹿子，為諸苾芻宣說法要。又依別解脫因起之道，毗奈耶攝所有言說。又於是處說如是言：世尊依如是如是因緣，依如是如是事，說如是如是語。

Ru-Yi Translation

Causal occasion is: teaching given at another's request — as a sūtra says, "The World-Honored One, on one occasion, in dependence on Kṛṣṇa-mṛgadāraka, proclaimed the essential teaching for the monks."
Further, it is every word comprised within monastic discipline that arises from the causal occasion of individual liberation.
Further, wherever it is said: "The World-Honored One, in dependence on such-and-such a cause and condition, in dependence on such-and-such a matter, spoke such-and-such words" — this too is included.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Causal occasion, in its broadest sense, covers any scripture that arose from some identifiable cause — three specific senses are given here. First, teaching given at a listener's request, illustrated by the Buddha's teaching to the monks prompted by the monk Kṛṣṇa-mṛgadāraka's own question — whenever a bodhisattva, elder disciple, or other follower asks, and the Buddha answers with teaching on renunciation, concentration, or wisdom, this is causal occasion in this first sense. Second, monastic discipline established in response to a specific transgression: when a monk's conduct threatened pure conduct or drew public criticism, the Buddha established a corresponding precept in response — the *Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya*'s account of Kṛṣṇa-mṛgadāraka's own fondness for matchmaking, which occasioned the precept against it, is a specific example. And third, the broadest sense of all, covering both dharma and discipline together: any passage stating simply that the Buddha spoke certain words in dependence on certain causes and certain matters, whatever the specific content. Whether teaching prompted by request or discipline prompted by transgression, causal occasion stands in contrast to unprompted teaching, which arose from the Buddha's own initiative alone. With this, causal occasion, the sixth division, is fully explained, closing it entirely. The treatise turns next to the seventh division: simile.

Past-life accounts are: proclaiming the Buddha's own past-life matters, from when he himself, in a former age, practiced the bodhisattva path.

譬喻者，謂有譬喻經，由譬喻故，隱義明了。本事者，謂除本生，宣說前際諸所有事。本生者，謂宣說己身，於過去世行菩薩行時，自本生事。

Ru-Yi Translation

Simile is: sūtras that contain similes, by which what was hidden in meaning becomes clear.
Prior accounts are: setting aside past-life accounts of the Buddha himself, proclaiming every other matter belonging to the past.
Past-life accounts are: proclaiming the Buddha's own past-life matters, from when he himself, in a former age, practiced the bodhisattva path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Simile, the seventh division, names scriptures such as the *Sūtra of Dharma-Verses and Similes*, rich with parables and stories that make an otherwise obscure teaching vivid and clear, awakening the wish for renunciation in a being with genuine wholesome aspiration. Prior accounts, the eighth division, cover past-life matters generally — of the Buddha's disciples or of anyone else — excluding specifically the Buddha's own past lives, which are reserved for the ninth division. And past-life accounts, illustrated by the *Jātaka* collection, cover exactly this: the Buddha's own countless past lives across all six destinies, practicing the bodhisattva path long before this present buddhahood.

89. Section: Extended Teaching — the Bodhisattva Path Through Seven Grounds, a Buddha's Hundred and Forty Exclusive Qualities, and Why It Is Called "Vast, Square, and Extended"

Extended teaching is: teaching the bodhisattva path. As with teaching the seven grounds and the four kinds of bodhisattva conduct, and teaching the buddhas' one hundred and forty qualities exclusive to them — the fourfold purity of every kind, up through the wondrous wisdom of every kind — just as extensively taught in the Bodhisattva Ground. Further, this teaching is called extended because it is vast, because it is abundant, because it is exceedingly lofty and great, and because it spans a vast span of time — since only through the most courageous diligence, over three great incalculable eons, is it brought to completion.

Chinese Source

方廣者，謂說菩薩道。如說七地四菩薩行，及說諸佛百四十種不共佛法。謂四一切種清淨，乃至一切種妙智，如菩薩地已廣說。又復此法，廣故、多故、極高大故、時長遠故，謂極勇猛，經三大劫阿僧企耶方得成滿，故名方廣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Extended teaching is: teaching the bodhisattva path. As with teaching the seven grounds and the four kinds of bodhisattva conduct, and teaching the buddhas' one hundred and forty qualities exclusive to them — the fourfold purity of every kind, up through the wondrous wisdom of every kind — just as extensively taught in the Bodhisattva Ground.
Further, this teaching is called extended because it is vast, because it is abundant, because it is exceedingly lofty and great, and because it spans a vast span of time — since only through the most courageous diligence, over three great incalculable eons, is it brought to completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Extended teaching, the tenth division, names great-vehicle scriptures such as the *Flower Ornament Sūtra* and the *Lotus Sūtra* — the Bodhisattva Basket itself, and this very treatise among them. The seven grounds (lineage, resolute-conduct, purely superior resolve, correct-conduct, the determined ground, the ground of determined conduct, and the ultimate ground) and the four kinds of bodhisattva conduct (the perfections, the factors of awakening, spiritual powers, and ripening beings) trace the whole bodhisattva path already explained at length in the Bodhisattva Ground's own chapter on the grounds; the one hundred and forty qualities exclusive to a buddha (the thirty-two marks, the eighty minor marks, the fourfold purity, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, the three things not needing to be guarded, great compassion, the dharma of never forgetting, the permanent destruction of habituated traces, and wisdom of every mode) were likewise already unfolded in full in that same ground's chapter on establishment. Why call this teaching "extended"? Because its scope is vast, its content abundant, its aim exceedingly lofty, and its timespan immense — brought to completion only through the most courageous, sustained diligence across three great incalculable eons — a fitting name for the teaching addressed to those who would walk the entire bodhisattva path to its very end.

90. Section: The Unprecedented Dharma — Marvels Spoken of Buddhas, Renunciants, and Householders Alike

The unprecedented dharma is: the marvelous dharma spoken of the Tathāgatas, of the śrāvakas, and of householders — as in every sūtra where speech arises on account of some rare and marvelous matter.

Chinese Source

未曾有法者，謂諸如來，若諸聲聞，若在家者說希奇法。如諸經中因希有事起於言說。

Ru-Yi Translation

The unprecedented dharma is: the marvelous dharma spoken of the Tathāgatas, of the śrāvakas, and of householders — as in every sūtra where speech arises on account of some rare and marvelous matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The eleventh division, also called the marvelous dharma, covers three kinds of rare event: matters of ultimate truth unlike anything in ordinary experience; inconceivable events of supernatural power; and wondrous, awe-inspiring phenomena of the natural world. The Śrāvaka Ground fills this out further: it proclaims the exceptional, supreme qualities of buddhas and their disciples — monks, nuns, novices of either sex, and lay men and women alike — qualities that surpass ordinary beings and the rest of the world, whether shared with or exclusive to that world, and universally acknowledged as truly rare and marvelous wherever they appear.

91. Section: Discussion Opens — the Mother of Categories, Spoken Both by the World-Honored One Himself and by Noble Disciples Who Have Directly Realized the Truth

Discussion is: every scripture that repeatedly examines and investigates the mother of categories. Thus, every definitive sūtra is itself called the mother of categories, since, within it, the World-Honored One himself extensively distinguishes the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma. Further, wherever noble disciples, having already seen the trace of the truth, distinguish, in dependence on their own direct realization and without distortion, the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma — this too is called the mother of categories.

Chinese Source

論議者，謂諸經典循環研覈摩怛理迦，且如一切了義經，皆名摩怛理迦。謂於是處，世尊自廣分別諸法體相。又於是處，諸聖弟子已見諦跡，依自所證，無倒分別諸法體相，此亦名為摩怛理迦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Discussion is: every scripture that repeatedly examines and investigates the mother of categories. Thus, every definitive sūtra is itself called the mother of categories, since, within it, the World-Honored One himself extensively distinguishes the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma.
Further, wherever noble disciples, having already seen the trace of the truth, distinguish, in dependence on their own direct realization and without distortion, the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma — this too is called the mother of categories.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The twelfth and final division, discussion, names scripture devoted to repeated, careful investigation of the "mother of categories" (mātṛkā) — texts that thoroughly work through a teaching's meaning again and again until it becomes fully clear. Any single sūtra may in fact combine several of the twelve divisions at once, or feature only one or two — the twelve are simply different modes of expression, not twelve mutually exclusive sets of separate texts. Two sources of such discussion are named here: definitive sūtras where the World-Honored One himself extensively unpacks the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma directly, and treatises composed by noble disciples who, having already directly witnessed the trace of the truth at the path of seeing, distinguish those same characteristics without distortion, drawing on their own genuine realization rather than mere secondhand report — this very treatise itself stands as a prime example of the second kind. As the scholar Lü Cheng notes, "mother of categories" differs from the more familiar term "abhidharma" in three ways: it includes teaching spoken by the Buddha himself as well as by disciples, where abhidharma is chiefly disciples' own composition; it encompasses commentary on both sūtra and monastic discipline together, where abhidharma chiefly addresses sūtra alone; and it works by drawing out a teaching's overarching intent as a generative "mother," where abhidharma works instead by analyzing dharmas into their many particular categories, a more derivative undertaking.

92. Section: Why the Mother of Categories Is Also Called Abhidharma — Repeated Examination, Correct Establishment, and Freedom From Confusion

This very mother of categories is also called abhidharma. Just as every treatise of writing, arithmetic, poetry, or logic found in the world has its own mother of categories, know that repeated examination and investigation of every dharma's own-nature and characteristics within a sūtra is likewise so. Further, just as, without the mother of categories, the letters themselves would remain unclear, so too, without correctly establishing the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma, the sūtra and the other eleven divisions of the noble teaching would remain unclear; once these are correctly established, clarity is attained. Further, it proclaims the characteristics of dharmas without any confusion — and it is for this reason, too, that this very mother of categories is also called abhidharma.

Chinese Source

即此摩怛理迦，亦名阿毗達磨。猶如世間一切書算詩論等，皆有摩怛理迦，當知經中循環研覈諸法體相，亦復如是。又如諸字，若無摩怛理迦，即不明了。如是契經等十二分聖教，若不建立諸法體相，即不明了；若建立已，即得明了。又無雜亂宣說法相，是故即此摩怛理迦，亦名阿毗達磨。

Ru-Yi Translation

This very mother of categories is also called abhidharma. Just as every treatise of writing, arithmetic, poetry, or logic found in the world has its own mother of categories, know that repeated examination and investigation of every dharma's own-nature and characteristics within a sūtra is likewise so.
Further, just as, without the mother of categories, the letters themselves would remain unclear, so too, without correctly establishing the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma, the sūtra and the other eleven divisions of the noble teaching would remain unclear; once these are correctly established, clarity is attained.
Further, it proclaims the characteristics of dharmas without any confusion — and it is for this reason, too, that this very mother of categories is also called abhidharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Three reasons explain why the mother of categories is also rightly called abhidharma ("facing the dharma," or "corresponding to the truth"). First, by repeated examination: just as every worldly discipline — grammar, mathematics, poetics, logic — rests on some foundational primer that organizes and clarifies its subject matter, a sūtra's own repeated, careful investigation of dharmas' own-nature and characteristics serves exactly this organizing, clarifying role. Second, by correct establishment: without such a mother of categories, even individual letters would remain obscure, and without correctly establishing the own-nature and characteristics of every dharma, the entire teaching contained in the sūtra proper and the other eleven divisions together would remain unclear — establishing them correctly is what makes the whole teaching finally legible. And third, by freedom from confusion: unlike a scattered treatment that might blur one dharma's characteristics into another's, the mother of categories proclaims the distinguishing marks of each dharma with genuine clarity and order, without any mixing or muddling — and it is precisely because a student, relying on such treatises rather than on personal habituation, affliction, or unexamined background assumptions, is kept from imposing merely private interpretation onto the sūtras, that reliance on genuine treatises composed by noble ones remains the safest and most reliable way to study the Buddha's own teaching at all.

93. Section: Later Commentaries Too Are Called Discussion — Closing All Twelve Divisions of Scripture, and the Essential Teaching, Entirely

Further, whatever else, resting on this very mother of categories, explains the meaning of the sūtras, is likewise called discussion.

Chinese Source

又即依此摩怛理迦，所餘解釋諸經義者，亦名論議。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, whatever else, resting on this very mother of categories, explains the meaning of the sūtras, is likewise called discussion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Beyond the mother of categories spoken by the Buddha or by noble disciples who directly witnessed the truth, later treatises that rest on and explain that same foundation — Asaṅga's own *Diamond Sūtra Treatise*, for instance, which organizes its explanation of the sūtra around seven headings — are likewise rightly called discussion, since they continue the same basic work of drawing out a sūtra's meaning through careful, ordered investigation. With this, discussion, the twelfth and final division, is fully explained, closing it entirely. With it, all twelve divisions of scripture — the sūtra proper, restatement in verse, prediction, spontaneous verse, unprompted teaching, causal occasion, simile, prior accounts, past-life accounts, extended teaching, the unprecedented dharma, and discussion — are now completely explained, closing the essential teaching, the first of the five aspects of explanation, in its entirety. The treatise turns next to the second aspect: the occasion.

94. Section: The Occasion — Matter, Time, and Person Together Determine Exactly How a Teaching Unfolds — Closing the Second Aspect Entirely

The occasion is: proclaiming, as is fitting, in dependence on the three bases of matter, time, and person — that such-and-such a person having such-and-such conduct, one thereby causes that person to be freed from craving, and so on up through rejoicing.

Chinese Source

等起者，謂由三種若事、若時、若補特伽羅依處故，隨應當說。謂如是補特伽羅有如是行，為令離欲乃至慶喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

The occasion is: proclaiming, as is fitting, in dependence on the three bases of matter, time, and person — that such-and-such a person having such-and-such conduct, one thereby causes that person to be freed from craving, and so on up through rejoicing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second aspect, the occasion, applies directly the threefold basis already explained at length under the meaning of basis: matter, time, and person together are what actually occasion a given sūtra's arising. When the Buddha meets a particular kind of person — one of the twenty-seven kinds classified earlier — exhibiting some particular kind of conduct — one of the twelve kinds also explained earlier — he responds by drawing on whichever of the five acts of compassion is fitting, from causing freedom from craving through rejoicing, until that person is guided toward the noble path. Grasping a sūtra's occasion in this sense means seeing exactly which person, exhibiting which conduct, prompted which specific response — the concrete circumstance from which the teaching itself first arose. With this, the occasion, the second of the five aspects, is fully explained, closing it entirely. The treatise turns next to the third aspect: meaning.

95. Section: Meaning Opens — General and Specific, and Four Marks of the General Sense: What It Draws From, What It Fully Determines, Conduct, and Fruit

The occasion has now been explained; next, meaning should be explained. Meaning is, in brief, of two kinds: first, general meaning; and second, specific meaning. Through four marks, the general meaning should be stated: first, because it draws upon a definitive sūtra; second, because it fully determines its matter; third, because of conduct; and fourth, because of fruit.

Chinese Source

已說等起。次應說義。義者，略有二種。一者、總義，二者、別義。由四種相，當說總義。一者、引了義經故；二者、分別事究竟故；三者、行故；四者、果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The occasion has now been explained; next, meaning should be explained. Meaning is, in brief, of two kinds: first, general meaning; and second, specific meaning.
Through four marks, the general meaning should be stated: first, because it draws upon a definitive sūtra; second, because it fully determines its matter; third, because of conduct; and fourth, because of fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The third aspect, meaning, is the richest of the five and divides into two: general meaning, grasping a sūtra's overall sense, and specific meaning, unpacking its individual passages — general first, specific after, following the treatise's own consistent method throughout. General meaning is itself drawn out through four marks. First, drawing on a definitive sūtra: a treatise's general meaning is established by citing scripture that states a teaching's true significance without ambiguity, guiding beings to realize the true nature of dependently-arisen dharmas directly. Second, fully determining the matter: distinguishing a dharma's full extent (its complete range, exhaustively considered) together with its true nature (its reality as it actually is) — the two together sometimes called the very limit of the matter — brings understanding of dependently-arisen phenomena to its ultimate ground. Third and fourth, conduct and fruit, are named here only briefly and unfolded next: whether a passage concerns correct or wrong conduct, and whether it concerns the fruit that correct or wrong conduct respectively yields.

96. Section: Wrong Conduct and Correct Conduct, and Their Matching Fruits — the Five Kinds of Suffering Wrong Conduct Itself Produces

Conduct is, further, of two kinds: first, wrong conduct; and second, correct conduct. Fruit is likewise of two kinds: first, the fruit of correct conduct; and second, the fruit of wrong conduct.

Chinese Source

行復二種，一者、邪行，二者、正行。果亦二種，一者、正行果，二者、邪行果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Conduct is, further, of two kinds: first, wrong conduct; and second, correct conduct. Fruit is likewise of two kinds: first, the fruit of correct conduct; and second, the fruit of wrong conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Wrong conduct names bodily, verbal, and mental deeds that are crooked and incorrect — the eight wrong paths (wrong view, wrong intention, wrong speech, and the rest opposite the noble eightfold path), the ten unwholesome deeds, or, per the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground's own extended account, the eight ways a bodhisattva specifically fails to act in accordance with the true meaning of the teaching, such as drawing near unworthy companions; the *Compendium of Determinations* elsewhere characterizes it more broadly still as failing to understand one's own defiled karma, failing to understand its counteragent, and placing groundless faith in an unqualified teacher while presuming genuine understanding of the noble teaching without it. Correct conduct is simply its reverse: the noble eightfold path, hearing-reflection-cultivation, the six perfections and four means of attraction, or, as the *Compendium* puts it, extensive learning within the correct teaching, correctly discerning defilement and its purification, abandoning the unwholesome and cultivating the wholesome. The fruit of correct conduct is the resulting purity, ease, and eventual attainment of the noble path for oneself and others, moving toward a fortunate destiny and ultimately buddhahood; the fruit of wrong conduct is suffering, unpacked in the Bodhisattva Ground into five specific kinds — suffering arisen in this very life from harming another and meeting that being's retaliation; suffering from consuming unsuitable food and thereby disturbing the body's own elements; suffering that spontaneously compounds once already pressed by present suffering; suffering from afflictions and secondary afflictions that take hold through habitually dwelling in incorrect attention; and suffering in destinies to come, drawn by repeated unwholesome deeds of body, speech, and mind.

Ru-Yi Translation

Through four marks, the specific meaning should be stated: first, distinguishing the various names applied; second, distinguishing its own particular nature; third, glossing its actual wording; and fourth, its distinctions of meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Where general meaning grasped a sūtra's overall sense, specific meaning works through it sentence by sentence, phrase by phrase, using four further marks. Distinguishing the various names applied means recognizing that a single dharma may bear several different names, each offering its own angle — the imagined own-nature, for instance, was earlier shown to carry eight further names (formed through mere aggregation, a superimposed mark, and so on), each name illuminating that same nature from a different approach. Distinguishing its own particular nature means stating a dharma's actual defining characteristic directly, as when the imagined own-nature was defined as whatever own-nature is established resting on mere conventional, verbal designation. Glossing its actual wording and its distinctions of meaning, the two richest of the four, are each unfolded next in turn.

98. Section: Glossing Wording — Five Expedients by Resemblance, From Shared Appearance and Substance to Shared Function, Method, and Cause-and-Effect

Glossing wording proceeds, further, through five kinds of skillful means: first, by resemblance in mark; second, by resemblance in own-nature; third, by resemblance in function; fourth, by resemblance in method; and fifth, by resemblance in cause and effect.

Chinese Source

訓釋言詞，復由五種方便。一、由相故；二、由自性故；三、由業故；四、由法故；五、由因果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Glossing wording proceeds, further, through five kinds of skillful means: first, by resemblance in mark; second, by resemblance in own-nature; third, by resemblance in function; fourth, by resemblance in method; and fifth, by resemblance in cause and effect.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Glossing wording — explaining what a term in a sūtra actually means — draws on five kinds of resemblance, each functioning as a form of inference already explained in the Stage Formed by Hearing. Resemblance in mark reasons from an outward sign to what it implies, just as seeing smoke lets one infer fire must be present. Resemblance in own-nature reasons from a directly-observed portion of a thing's nature to the rest of that same nature, just as tasting one noodle from a pot lets one infer whether the whole potful is cooked. Resemblance in function reasons from one thing's known effect to a similar effect in something functionally alike, just as a sharp blade wounds the body and a sharp tongue, by the same kind of resemblance, is said to wound the heart. Resemblance in method reasons from one teaching to a closely related one, as impermanence naturally suggests suffering. And resemblance in cause and effect reasons from an observed cause to its likely future effect, as seeing someone diligently cultivating the noble path now suggests that path will eventually be accomplished. Together these five expedients let a teacher explain an unfamiliar or difficult term by drawing out its resemblance to something the listener already understands.

The fourth mark of specific meaning, distinctions of meaning, names five further lenses through which any dharma discussed in a sūtra can be further differentiated, each now to be unfolded in turn: by its own particular nature, by which of the three realms it belongs to, by which of the three times it occurs in, by which stage or position of the path it corresponds to, and by which kind of person it applies to. Together, glossing wording and distinctions of meaning give a teacher the complete toolkit for unpacking any specific passage of scripture down to its finest detail.

此中自性差別者，謂色自性有十色處差別，受自性有三受差別，想自性有六想差別，行自性有三行差別，識自性有六識差別。如是等類，當知諸法自性差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this, distinction in own-nature is: form's own-nature has ten distinctions as sense-fields; feeling's own-nature has three distinctions; perception's own-nature has six distinctions; formation's own-nature has three distinctions; and consciousness's own-nature has six distinctions. Kinds such as these should be known as the own-nature distinctions of every dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The first mark of distinction in meaning is illustrated through the five aggregates, each unfolding into its own further kinds according to its defining function. Form, whose nature is to obstruct and be broken, resolves into ten sense-fields: the five physical faculties and their five corresponding objects. Feeling, whose nature is to receive, resolves into pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling. Perception, whose nature is to apprehend a mark, resolves into the six perceptions arising from contact through each of the six sense-faculties. Formation, whose nature is volitional construction, resolves into wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral formations. And consciousness, whose nature is discernment, resolves into the six consciousnesses corresponding to the six sense-faculties. Recognizing which particular own-nature a given passage addresses, and into which further kinds that nature resolves, is the first step toward fully unpacking its specific meaning.

101. Section: Distinction in Realm and in Time — Desire, Form, and Formlessness, and Past, Future, and Present

Distinction in realm is: distinction belonging to the desire realm, distinction belonging to the form realm, and distinction belonging to the formless realm. Distinction in time is: distinction belonging to the past, distinction belonging to the future, and distinction belonging to the present.

Chinese Source

界差別者，謂欲界差別故，色界差別故，無色界差別故。時差別者，謂過去時差別故，未來時差別故，現在時差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Distinction in realm is: distinction belonging to the desire realm, distinction belonging to the form realm, and distinction belonging to the formless realm.
Distinction in time is: distinction belonging to the past, distinction belonging to the future, and distinction belonging to the present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second mark, distinction in realm, asks which of the three realms a given teaching addresses — the ten unwholesome or wholesome deeds belonging to the desire realm, the four form-realm absorptions, or the four formless attainments (the sphere of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception) — each realm's teaching addressing that realm's own distinct concerns. The third mark, distinction in time, is a key to interpretation in its own right: the Buddha's own past-life accounts and disciples' prior accounts address the past; predictions of future buddhahood address the future; and the great bulk of ordinary teaching, spoken directly to a present audience, addresses the present. Recognizing which realm and which time a passage concerns keeps its meaning from being misapplied to the wrong context.

102. Section: Distinction in Position — Twenty-Five Kinds, From High, Middling, and Low to Cause and Effect

Distinction in position: know that there are twenty-five kinds of distinction by stage. Namely: distinction into low, middling, and high; distinction into painful, pleasant, and neutral; distinction into wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral; distinction into hearing, reflection, and cultivation; distinction into higher discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom; distinction into internal and external; distinction into what is grasped and what grasps; distinction into what is counteracted and what counteracts; distinction into manifest and non-manifest; and distinction into cause and effect.

Chinese Source

位差別者，當知有二十五種分位差別。謂下中上三位差別故，苦、樂、不苦不樂三位差別故，善、不善、無記三位差別故，聞思修三位差別故，增上戒、增上心、增上慧三位差別故，內外二位差別故，所取、能取二位差別故，所治、能治二位差別故，現前、不現前二位差別故，因果二位差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Distinction in position: know that there are twenty-five kinds of distinction by stage. Namely: distinction into low, middling, and high; distinction into painful, pleasant, and neutral; distinction into wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral; distinction into hearing, reflection, and cultivation; distinction into higher discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom; distinction into internal and external; distinction into what is grasped and what grasps; distinction into what is counteracted and what counteracts; distinction into manifest and non-manifest; and distinction into cause and effect.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth mark, distinction in position, gathers ten separate three-fold and two-fold classifications — three each for the first five (totaling fifteen) and two each for the remaining five (totaling ten), twenty-five in all — through which any passage's specific standing can be located. Three grades of rank (low, middling, high); the three kinds of feeling; wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral quality; the three wisdoms of hearing, reflection, and cultivation; and the three trainings each offer a threefold scale. Internal versus external (a passage addressing one's own body, or the outer world's arising and passing); grasped versus grasping (an object versus the faculty apprehending it); counteracted versus counteracting (affliction versus its remedy); manifest versus non-manifest (as with the wisdom of dharmas contemplating the present aggregates directly, against the wisdom by inference contemplating the form and formless realms' aggregates not directly present); and cause versus effect (the five precepts and ten wholesome deeds as cause of a fortunate rebirth, evil karma as cause of an unfortunate one, and the five kinds of fruit — ripened, outflowing, freed-from-bondage, personally-produced, and dominant — that such causes yield) round out the list with five further two-fold pairs. Reflecting on which position a passage occupies along any of these twenty-five scales is itself, the treatise notes, one genuine path toward understanding a teaching's definitive meaning.

103. Section: Distinction in Person — the Twenty-Seven Kinds Already Classified — Closing All Distinctions of Meaning, and the Third Aspect of Explanation, Entirely

Distinction in person is: know the distinctions among the twenty-seven kinds of person already discussed above.

Chinese Source

補特伽羅差別者，如前所說二十七種補特伽羅，應知差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Distinction in person is: know the distinctions among the twenty-seven kinds of person already discussed above.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fifth and final mark of distinction returns once more to the twenty-seven kinds of listener classified in full detail under the occasion for asking — a passage addressed to one kind of person may not carry the same weight when applied to another, so recognizing exactly which of the twenty-seven a given teaching is speaking to remains essential to grasping its specific meaning correctly. With this, general meaning (through drawing on definitive scripture, fully determining the matter, conduct, and fruit) and specific meaning (through distinguishing names, own-nature, glossing wording, and these five distinctions of own-nature, realm, time, position, and person) are both fully explained, closing meaning, the third of the five aspects of explanation, entirely. The treatise turns next to the fourth aspect: resolving objections.

104. Section: Resolving Objections Opens — Self-Posed or Another's, Five Kinds of Difficulty, Beginning With Clarifying an Unclear Meaning

Resolving objections is: whether an objection is posed by oneself or posed by another, both should be resolved. Know that such objections, in brief, are of five kinds. First, so that an unclear meaning may be made clear — as when it is asked: "What is the meaning of this passage?"

Chinese Source

釋難者，若自設難、若他設難皆應解釋。當知此難略由五相。一者、為未了義得顯了故。如言：此文有何義耶？

Ru-Yi Translation

Resolving objections is: whether an objection is posed by oneself or posed by another, both should be resolved.
Know that such objections, in brief, are of five kinds. First, so that an unclear meaning may be made clear — as when it is asked: "What is the meaning of this passage?"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth aspect, resolving objections, addresses genuine difficulties a teaching may raise — whether the teacher poses the objection to himself in order to draw out a point more clearly, or an actual listener raises it — and both kinds equally deserve a real answer. Five kinds of difficulty are named, the first being simple unclarity: a passage whose meaning is not yet evident invites the plain question "what does this mean?" — the Determinations on the Ground of the Five Consciousness-Groups and the Ground of Mind, for instance, ask directly what is meant by faculties left unregulated, unguarded, unprotected, and uncultivated, precisely this first kind of clarifying question.

105. Section: An Apparent Contradiction in Words, and a Conflict With the Four Kinds of Reasoning

Second, because of an apparent verbal contradiction — as when it is asked: "Why does what the World-Honored One taught before differ from what he now teaches?" Third, because of conflict with reasoning — as when a meaning is shown to conflict with the four kinds of reasoning.

Chinese Source

二者、語相違故。如言：何故世尊先所說異，今所說異？三者、道理相違故。如有顯示與四道理相違之義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, because of an apparent verbal contradiction — as when it is asked: "Why does what the World-Honored One taught before differ from what he now teaches?"
Third, because of conflict with reasoning — as when a meaning is shown to conflict with the four kinds of reasoning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The second kind of difficulty arises when the Buddha's own words appear to contradict themselves across different occasions — the bodhisattva Paramārthasamudgata's own question, recorded in the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, exemplifies this precisely: having heard the Buddha teach at length on the aggregates' own marks, arising, and cessation, he later heard the same Buddha teach that all dharmas are without own-nature, unborn, unceasing, originally quiescent, and by nature nirvāṇa — and asked what hidden intent could reconcile such apparently opposite teachings. The third kind arises when some claim appears to conflict with one or more of the four kinds of reasoning already explained (dependence, function, proof, and the nature of things as they simply are) — the Grounds With and Without Investigation and Deliberation raise exactly this kind of objection against a theory of a permanent self, asking whether the sense of "I" arises with respect to the very thing observed or something apart from it: if the self is truly permanent and unchanging, it should not depend on anything, yet every dharma arises only in dependence on conditions, so a theory of self already conflicts with reasoning by dependence — and, once dependence fails, the self's supposed power to govern also collapses, conflicting with reasoning by function; noble ones' own direct realization of selflessness conflicts with the theory under reasoning by proof; and, whether grasped at or not, beings are simply, naturally selfless all along, so clinging to a fixed self conflicts even with the nature of things as they simply are — a teaching may raise this kind of objection against an outside view specifically to expose exactly where it breaks down.
