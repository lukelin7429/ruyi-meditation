Fascicle 59 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十九 · Karma Defilement Opens and Its Ten Paths Worked Out in Full

Three Conditions That Decide a Fall to the Evil Destinies

Continuing directly from fascicle 58's foundational account of affliction defilement's five establishing aspects, the treatise completes its extensive analysis of how affliction actually operates, closes the entire topic with its own summary verse, and then opens karma defilement, working through all ten unwholesome karma-paths in exhaustive detail, their defining factors, their concrete methods, and their underlying motives, from a bodhisattva's compassionate exception to killing down to why one particular denial alone is singled out and named wrong view.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 59 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Which Afflictions Generate Karma, and the Three Conditions for a Fall

Fascicle 58 closed having established affliction defilement through five aspects, its intrinsic nature, its specific varieties, its defiled-and-pure distinction, its confusion-and-severance distinction, and its antidote distinction, and having correlated this entire account against the sutras' own varied imagery. The treatise now turns to a considerably more extensive analysis of affliction defilement's own detailed workings, opening with a direct question: among the ten root afflictions, greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, doubt, self-view, view-attachment, precept-attachment, extreme-view, and wrong view, how many are actually capable of generating karma, and how many are not? The answer is that every one of them can. Yet a crucial qualification follows: only when these afflictions, whether craving-type or view-type, manifest with real force and vehemence are they capable of generating karma strong enough to carry a being down into the evil destinies, never when they manifest merely through a passing lapse of mindfulness, since even a lapse of mindfulness that generates some affliction is comparatively weak, easily noticed, and easily corrected, and the karma it generates lacks the power to bring about such a fall. Karma capable of leading to the evil destinies, moreover, arises specifically from afflictions generated through discrimination, the very afflictions severed at the path of seeing, rather than from innate afflictions arising spontaneously and severed only gradually through cultivation. As fascicle 58 already explained, three conditions together determine whether such discrimination-arisen affliction in the desire realm will definitely lead to the evil destinies: cultivating it extensively, taking it seriously and continuously as though it were a virtue, never perceiving its faults or dangers, and indulging it without restraint; using this very affliction as the basis for unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental conduct, both committing it and causing it to grow; and using this affliction to cut off another being's own wholesome roots while implanting unwholesome ones in their place — when an affliction is accompanied by all three conditions together, it becomes forceful enough to determine a fall into the evil destinies and the ripening of its corresponding painful fruit.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: among the ten afflictions, greed and the rest, how many can generate karma, and how many cannot? Answer: all can generate it. Only when these afflictions manifest with force and vehemence are they able to generate karma leading to the evil destinies, never when they manifest merely through a lapse of mindfulness. Also, it is discrimination-arisen affliction that can generate this karma, not affliction arising spontaneously.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between forceful manifestation and a mere lapse of mindfulness as genuinely reassuring for any sincere practitioner prone to occasional lapses: an isolated moment of irritation or careless craving, quickly noticed and quickly released, simply does not carry the same karmic weight as affliction cultivated deliberately, indulged without restraint, and used as a platform for harming others' own wholesome growth. He highlights the three conditions determining a fall to the evil destinies as this section's own practical checklist, less a doctrine about cosmic bookkeeping than a description of what habitual, self-justifying, other-harming affliction actually does to a person over time, and by extension a clear indication of exactly what sincere practice is meant to interrupt before it ever reaches that point.

2. Section: Three Marks Every Affliction Shares

The treatise next asks how many marks belong to affliction in general, answering that there are three in brief: its own particular mark, its mark held in common with every other affliction, and its distinguishing mark. Its own particular mark is whatever nature belongs specifically to greed, to hatred, and so forth, each affliction possessing a character entirely its own — greed, for instance, arising through associating with unwholesome companions, hearing improper teaching, and attending incorrectly, or else through a mere lapse of mindfulness, taking as its very essence a defiled attachment, whether discriminated or undiscriminated, to agreeable objects both external and internal; hatred arising through these same conditions, taking as its essence instead a defiled aversion toward disagreeable objects both external and internal. Its mark held in common with every other affliction is that all afflictions, without exception, share the single character of inner unrest: wherever affliction is genuinely present, the mind cannot be still, its own thoughts scattering and proliferating, unable to settle into concentration, and thereby obstructed from ever accomplishing the noble path — this shared quality of restlessness being the one mark every affliction, however different their own particular natures, ultimately holds in common.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the shared mark of inner unrest as a genuinely useful diagnostic tool for a practitioner uncertain whether some particular mental state actually counts as affliction: rather than needing to correctly classify every subtle mind-state into its proper technical category first, one can simply notice whether the mind, right now, has lost its own stillness, since that single quality, present without exception across every named affliction from the crudest to the subtlest, quietly gives the whole game away regardless of which particular affliction happens to be responsible. He notes that the treatise's own next step, the distinguishing mark that sets each affliction apart from the others despite this shared restlessness, is still to come.

3. Section: Twenty-Six Names, Seven Angles — Affliction's Distinguishing Marks

The treatise turns to affliction's third mark, its distinguishing mark, itself of two further kinds. The gate-distinguishing mark refers to the very door, so to speak, through which affliction enters and troubles a being, and accounts for why the same underlying affliction receives so many different names across the twenty-six terms already catalogued in an earlier stage of the treatise: it is called a knot because it binds a being together with suffering; a bond because, once present, it keeps a being from freely practicing virtue as they wish; a latent tendency because its own seed follows a being everywhere, ready to bring about rebirth's fruit throughout the three realms and five destinies; a secondary affliction because it inverts and defiles the very mind it touches; and an entanglement because it repeatedly manifests and coils itself around a being, robbing them of their own freedom — each name capturing a different functional angle on what is, at root, the very same affliction. The turning-distinguishing mark, by contrast, refers to the many different ways affliction actually arises and unfolds: through the turning of its own latent seed, through the turning of whatever object it takes, through the turning of its active manifestation, through the turning of its own grade, whether mild, moderate, or intense, through the turning of whether it arises forcefully through discrimination or merely spontaneously, through the turning of cause and effect, its seed giving rise to manifestation or its manifestation giving rise to further habituated result, and through the turning of confused conduct, the very way a deluded mind moves through its own activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the twenty-six names for affliction as proof that the tradition never regarded naming as a merely academic exercise: each term, knot, bond, latent tendency, secondary affliction, entanglement, isolates one specific consequence affliction produces, so that a practitioner encountering any one of these words in the sutras can immediately recognize which particular danger is being pointed to, binding to suffering, restricting freedom of action, or persistently coiling around the mind, rather than treating them as interchangeable synonyms. He notes that the seven angles of turning-distinguishing mark, still to be explained one by one, promise an even more detailed picture of affliction's own internal mechanics, tracking not just what affliction is called but exactly how it moves from dormant seed to active manifestation and back again.

4. Section: Eighteen Kinds of Latent Tendency, and Who Can Never Be Severed of Them

The treatise now details the turning of latent tendency itself, naming eighteen kinds in brief, arranged as eight paired or grouped contrasts. Latent tendency pursuing one's own realm belongs to whichever of the three realms a being is presently born into, while latent tendency pursuing another realm follows a being even after they have been reborn into a higher or lower realm, the seeds of the realm left behind still trailing along. Damaged latent tendency has been weakened and suppressed, unable to manifest, through a mundane path of meditative cultivation that has freed a being from desire for some lower ground; undamaged latent tendency, whether belonging to a realm already freed of desire or one not yet freed, remains fully intact. Increasing latent tendency belongs to one's own present realm, its own seed growing ever stronger through repeated habituation in that realm's own activity; non-increasing latent tendency belongs to some other realm one is not presently reborn into, and so is never repeatedly habituated or strengthened. Complete latent tendency is what an ordinary, unawakened being still carries in full, every seed entirely intact and unsevered; incomplete latent tendency is what a noble learner, from the first fruit up through the approach to the fourth, still carries, having already severed a portion while some innate affliction still remains. Destructible latent tendency belongs to those of the three vehicles' own uncontaminated potential, capable of reaching final nirvana and thereby capable of severing every one of their own latent tendencies; indestructible latent tendency belongs instead to a certain class of beings lacking any wholesome root of that uncontaminated potential at all, whose latent tendencies, the treatise states plainly, cannot be severed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the pairing of complete and incomplete latent tendency as a precise map of the spiritual path's own progress: an ordinary being carries every seed entirely intact, while a noble learner, having already broken through at the path of seeing, carries a steadily shrinking remainder, the very shape of the path itself traceable in how much of this eighteen-fold inventory still applies to a given practitioner at a given stage. He treats the distinction between destructible and indestructible latent tendency as one of the treatise's own most doctrinally weighty and carefully guarded statements, since it touches directly on the difficult question of whether every being without exception possesses the potential for final liberation, and notes that later Yogācāra masters would debate this very point at great length, some reading it as describing only a temporary, not a permanent, condition of certain beings.

5. Section: Waking and Sleeping Seeds — Completing the Eighteen

The treatise completes its eighteenfold catalogue of latent tendency. Superior latent tendency belongs to whichever affliction dominates a given person's own temperament, some beings carrying an especially forceful seed of craving and so being called craving-natured, others carrying an especially forceful seed of hatred, delusion, discursive restlessness, conceit, or doubt, and so being called by that affliction's own name in turn. Equal latent tendency belongs instead to those whose various afflictions all carry roughly the same, unremarkable strength, no single one standing out above the rest. Inferior latent tendency belongs to those whose afflictions overall are simply faint, thin as dust rather than thick. Awakened latent tendency is the very seed that has, upon meeting suitable conditions, actually given rise to its own active manifestation, precisely as a seed sprouting into a sapling might be likened to something finally waking up; unawakened latent tendency, by contrast, is the seed still slumbering, unmanifested, within the storehouse consciousness, its own causal power fully intact even while dormant. Latent tendency capable of generating much suffering belongs to the desire realm, productive of the suffering of pain, the suffering of change, and the suffering of formations alike; latent tendency capable of generating only little suffering belongs to the form and formless realms, whose own afflictions, faint and largely inert, generate only the suffering of change within pleasant feeling and the suffering of formations within neutral feeling, far milder than the desire realm's own outright pain. And latent tendency incapable of generating suffering at all belongs to bodhisattvas of the eighth ground and beyond, who, having attained forbearance with respect to the birthlessness of all things, have permanently suppressed every affliction's own manifestation, its power to draw them back into the cyclic suffering of the three realms entirely spent, sustained instead only by their own great vow of compassion until the very last instant before buddhahood, within diamond-like absorption, finally severs every remaining seed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the image of a seed "waking up" into its own manifestation as a genuinely precise way of describing something practitioners can actually observe in their own minds: an old, familiar pattern of craving or irritation that seemed dormant for months can suddenly stir the instant a suitable trigger appears, exactly as though it had merely been asleep rather than gone. He highlights the eighth-ground bodhisattva's suffering-free latent tendency as the treatise's own remarkable description of a being who still, technically, carries affliction, yet whose own great compassionate vow has so thoroughly repurposed whatever residual seed remains that it no longer has any power left to draw them back into ordinary cyclic suffering at all, a condition he notes as pointing toward buddhahood's own final, complete severing still to come.

6. Section: Why an Arhat's Body Still Differs From Their Own Severed Seeds

The treatise now turns from latent tendency itself to the broader notion of coarse-heaviness, the very quality of incapacity that latent tendency's own intrinsic nature is said to bear, asking first whether affliction-class coarse-heaviness actually differs from the aggregates and other conditioned phenomena it accompanies, or is simply one and the same thing. The answer is that they do indeed differ, and the reason is direct: an arhat has permanently destroyed every coarse-heaviness belonging to affliction, every discrimination-arisen and every innate affliction-seed alike entirely severed, and yet that very arhat's own continuing stream of aggregates, form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, has not thereby been cut off at all, continuing on exactly as before until the natural end of that lifetime — proof enough that affliction's own coarse-heaviness and the conditioned aggregates it once accompanied are genuinely two different things, not a single, inseparable whole. Turning next to coarse-heaviness in its own right, the treatise names eighteen kinds in brief: intrinsic ripening-coarseness, intrinsic affliction-coarseness, intrinsic karma-coarseness, affliction-hindrance coarseness, karma-hindrance coarseness, ripening-hindrance coarseness, hindrance-coarseness of the five obstructions, coarseness of improper discursive thought, coarseness of sorrow and distress, coarseness of fear, coarseness of toilsome exertion, coarseness of food and drink, coarseness of sleep and dreams, coarseness of sexual desire, coarseness of the body's own elemental imbalance, coarseness of the changes brought by time, coarseness of dying itself, and coarseness that pervades everything without exception — a rich and wide-ranging catalogue the treatise now proceeds to unpack one by one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the arhat's still-continuing body as this section's own clearest possible proof that severing affliction and dissolving the body are two entirely separate accomplishments: a being can genuinely finish the deepest work there is, every affliction-seed permanently gone, and still spend the remainder of an ordinary lifetime eating, aging, and eventually dying exactly like anyone else, the difference invisible from the outside and entirely a matter of what no longer stirs within. He notes that this newly opened eighteenfold catalogue of coarse-heaviness reaches considerably further than the earlier, narrower discussion of affliction's own coarse-heaviness in fascicle 58, extending now into ordinary bodily and circumstantial burdens, food, sleep, fear, toil, even the sheer passage of time, as areas the treatise is about to show are themselves shaped by coarse-heaviness in their own right.

7. Section: From the Five Heinous Crimes to "No Samadhi in Old Age"

The treatise defines its remaining kinds of coarse-heaviness, ranging from the gravest to the most ordinary. Karma-hindrance coarseness belongs specifically to the five heinous deeds, killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, causing schism in the harmonious monastic community, or shedding a buddha's blood, deeds so grave that they leave a being simply incapable of cultivating calming and insight or attaining the noble path at all, unlike the far lighter karma an ordinary person commits. Ripening-hindrance coarseness is the incapacity built directly into one's own present result-body, an innate dullness of mind or weakness of the senses inherited from past karma that leaves a family, sometimes, devoting its entire life merely to managing an inherited condition rather than to study or practice. Hindrance-coarseness of the five obstructions is the covering cast by craving, hatred, torpor and sleepiness, restlessness and worry, and doubt over the mind's own capacity for wholesome states. Coarseness of improper discursive thought is the disturbed, defiling movement of the eight kinds of wrong thinking, thoughts of desire, harm, kinsfolk, homeland, and the rest, unsettling the mind's own stillness. Sorrow, fear, toilsome exertion, and the extremes of over- or under-eating each, in their own way, leave body and mind without the capacity to sustain practice; sleep and dreams leave the body outside one's own control and the mind dulled; sexual desire exhausts body and mind alike; imbalance among the body's own four elements produces restlessness, swelling, breathlessness, or rigidity; the changes brought by time are simply aging itself, from which arises the old saying that samadhi does not come easily in old age; dying is the disordering of every faculty as the four elements finally dissolve at life's very end; and pervasive coarseness is nothing other than the suffering of formations itself, running through pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling alike, since no conditioned state, however momentarily comfortable, escapes its own constant, restless changeability. All eighteen, the treatise confirms, fall entirely within the two broader kinds of coarse-heaviness already explained in the previous fascicle, outflow-coarseness belonging to affliction itself, and outflow-possessing coarseness belonging to the ripened, habituated body that even a fully liberated arhat still, for a time, continues to carry.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this eighteenfold catalogue as a deliberate widening of the lens: where the earlier discussion in fascicle 58 spoke only of affliction's own coarseness, this new list ranges across the five heinous crimes at one extreme and the mundane friction of hunger, sleep, and aging at the other, showing that "coarse-heaviness" names not one narrow technical phenomenon but the entire spectrum of incapacity, moral, karmic, and simply physical, that keeps a being from sustained practice. He highlights the pervasive coarseness of the suffering of formations as the treatise's own reminder that even a moment of genuine pleasure is never actually exempt, since its own restless, ever-changing nature is present within it from the very start, whether or not that fact happens to be noticed in the moment.

8. Section: Seven Ways a Confused Mind Moves

The treatise briefly notes several further turnings of affliction, referring back to earlier discussions for their full detail: the turning of object and manifestation will be explained extensively in its own proper place further ahead; the turning of grade-differentiation, mild, moderate, and intense, should be understood exactly as already explained under the skillful understanding of the aggregates; and the turning of force or forcelessness should likewise be understood as already explained in the Basic Section, affliction arising with real force when driven by close association with unwholesome companions, hearing improper teaching, and attending incorrectly, but arising without force when it is merely a spontaneous lapse of mindfulness, more habit than deliberate act. The turning of cause and effect notes that affliction, karma, and rebirth alike all take ignorance as their root cause, and that the very result these three produce becomes, in its own turn, a further cause for renewed affliction, though the treatise is careful to specify that only unwholesome affliction within the desire realm actually ripens into such a result, other neutral or wholesome states never doing so. The treatise then turns to a seventh and final turning, the turning of confused conduct, already named in seven kinds in an earlier discussion: wrongly understanding, failing to understand, uncertainly understanding, wrongly understanding with clinging attachment, being rooted in a particular cause, arising from a particular fear, and arising spontaneously. Taking up the first three of these in turn, wrongly understanding conduct belongs to self-view, extreme-view, and wrong view together, each generating a mistaken grasp of what is actually known, confused specifically about the four noble truths; failing to understand conduct belongs to ignorance alone, the simple absence of any clear grasp at all; and uncertainly understanding conduct belongs to doubt, a mind caught wavering between two paths, unable to settle whether the four truths are genuinely so or not.

9. Section: Closing Confused Conduct, and Which Afflictions Reference a Real Object

The treatise completes its sevenfold account of confused conduct. Wrongly understanding conduct joined with clinging attachment belongs to view-attachment, precept-attachment, and the greed, hatred, and conceit that take some view as their own object, all rooted in self-view and arising through discrimination, and so severed at the path of seeing. That same wrongly understanding conduct, considered specifically as rooted in its own cause and basis, is delusion about the truths of suffering and origination, the very causal ground from which the whole cycle of birth and death proceeds; considered instead as arising from a particular fear, it is delusion about the truths of cessation and the path, the reluctance and dread that keeps a being from pursuing the very practice that would free them. And confused conduct arising spontaneously belongs to whatever affliction manifests innately, without discrimination, severed only gradually through cultivation. These seven, the treatise notes, are simply the same ten root afflictions already catalogued at length, self-view, extreme-view, wrong view, view-attachment, precept-attachment, greed, hatred, conceit, ignorance, and doubt, now viewed from a different angle entirely, sorted according to how they confuse a mind about reality rather than according to their own names. The treatise then turns to a subtler question still: of these afflictions, how many reference some genuinely real object, and how many do not? Views and conceit, it answers, reference no real object at all, since they operate by discriminating a self where, among the conditioned formations that actually make up a being, no such self has ever genuinely existed; greed and hatred, by contrast, do reference a real object, arising specifically with respect to conventionally real agreeable or disagreeable things in the world; while ignorance and doubt run through both categories at once, arising equally with respect to what is real and what is merely imagined.

10. Section: Which Afflictions Arise Spontaneously With Every Kind of Feeling

The treatise now asks how many afflictions correspond to the pleasant faculty of feeling, and so on through each of the five, pleasant, painful, joyful, distressed, and neutral. Answering first with respect to affliction arising spontaneously, innately, and without discrimination, the treatise explains that every such affliction can, in principle, manifest alongside any of the three basic kinds of feeling, since spontaneous affliction is not particular about the character of whatever object it happens to encounter, arising just as readily toward an agreeable circumstance, productive of ease in body and mind, as toward a disagreeable one, productive of distress, or toward a neutral one, productive of neither. More specifically, spontaneous affliction that operates through the five sense-consciousnesses together with the sixth corresponds to all five feelings, pleasant, painful, joyful, distressed, and neutral alike, while spontaneous affliction confined to the mental sphere alone, not shared with the five sense-consciousnesses, corresponds only to the three feelings proper to that sphere, joy, distress, and neutrality, since bodily pleasure and bodily pain specifically require the five physical senses to arise at all. The treatise then turns to affliction that is not spontaneous, but rather arises through active discrimination, promising to explain in turn, affliction by affliction, exactly which of the five feelings each one corresponds to, beginning with greed.

11. Section: Greed and Hatred Through the Five Feelings

The treatise now works through each root affliction's own correspondence with feeling in turn, beginning with greed. Greed corresponds to distress and pain when a being wishes to unite with pleasant feeling and never be parted from it, yet that very wish goes unmet, pleasant feeling remaining unattained or already lost, or when a being wishes never to unite with painful feeling, yet that very wish likewise goes unmet, painful feeling arising regardless; greed corresponds instead to joy and pleasure under the opposite circumstance, the wish for pleasant feeling actually being fulfilled, or the wish to avoid painful feeling actually succeeding; and greed corresponds to neutral feeling whenever craving attachment arises with respect to whatever aggregates are, at that moment, neither pleasant nor painful, as when one simply craves and clings to one's own body regardless of what it happens to be feeling. Hatred follows a related but distinct pattern. It corresponds to distress and pain when a being's own body and mind are directly pressed upon by suffering, giving rise to inward resentment toward that very suffering, or when hatred arises with respect to disagreeable beings or circumstances one dwells upon with aversion; it corresponds instead to joy and pleasure when a being harbors ill will toward an enemy, wishing that enemy to sink into misery unaided or to be denied happiness altogether, and that very wish is then actually fulfilled, the satisfaction of a cruel hope realized bringing its own perverse pleasure.

He notes that greed's own three-way split, distress when thwarted, joy when satisfied, and mere neutral clinging when the object itself is neither pleasant nor painful, maps with uncomfortable precision onto ordinary daily experience, the same underlying craving simply taking whichever emotional coloring its own momentary success or failure happens to produce.

12. Section: Self-View, Wrong View, and the Perverse Comfort of Denying Karma

The treatise turns to self-view and extreme-view. When the eternalist form of these views regards whatever aggregates accompany pleasant feeling as a self or as belonging to a self, or regards them as permanent, it corresponds to the joyful faculty; when it regards the aggregates accompanying painful feeling in the same way, it corresponds to the distressed faculty; and when it regards the aggregates accompanying neutral feeling in the same way, it corresponds to the neutral faculty. The annihilationist form of extreme-view runs entirely the opposite way, since fearing the loss of a self identified with present pleasure corresponds instead to distress, while welcoming the imagined cessation of a self identified with present pain corresponds instead to pleasure. View-attachment and precept-attachment, taking self-view or extreme-view as their own object, simply follow whichever correspondence belongs to the particular view they cling to. Wrong view follows a stranger pattern still: one who has, in the past, performed virtuous deeds, upon adopting the wrong view that denies any karmic fruit whatsoever, corresponds to the distressed faculty, since they now regret that virtue as entirely wasted effort; while one who has, in the past, performed unwholesome deeds, upon adopting that same wrong view denying karmic fruit, corresponds instead to the joyful faculty, relieved and even pleased to believe that no painful consequence will ever follow such deeds after all — a perverse comfort drawn directly from denying the very moral order that would otherwise hold one accountable.

13. Section: Relief in Doubting Bad News — Conceit, Doubt, and Ignorance

The treatise names two kinds of conceit in brief: elevated conceit and lowly conceit. Elevated conceit itself takes three forms, each corresponding to joy: comparative conceit, the mind lifted up upon genuinely surpassing others; discerning conceit, the mind lifted up by one's own quickness and clarity of understanding; and possessions conceit, the mind lifted up by one's own greater wealth compared to others. Lowly conceit runs the opposite way, corresponding instead to distress, since it belongs to one who, though genuinely lacking by comparison, insists the gap is not so great after all, or that being unclear is really no worse than being clear, or that being poor now is only temporary and hardly worth minding. Doubt corresponds to distress when someone, having previously trusted that virtuous conduct genuinely brings gain, honor, praise, and a pleasant rebirth, is then led by an unwise companion to waver over whether any of this is actually true, the once-comforting certainty dissolving into unease. Doubt corresponds instead to joy under the opposite circumstance, when someone who had trusted that unwholesome conduct genuinely brings loss, disrespect, mockery, and a painful rebirth is instead led to waver over whether any of this is actually true, the once-feared certainty dissolving into a strange relief. Ignorance alone, the treatise notes, corresponds indiscriminately to all five feelings at once, since ignorance of reality accompanies every state of mind a being not yet awakened can ever pass through. The correspondence of every remaining secondary affliction with the feeling-faculties is here left unexplained for brevity's sake. This entire fine-grained account, the treatise explains, deliberately goes well beyond the earlier, coarser account already given, which was suited only to beginning practitioners needing a simple, unconfused starting point; this more detailed treatment is offered instead for long-practicing students, equipping them to recognize the many differing ways affliction actually operates, both within their own minds and within the minds of others.

14. Section: Three Clusters of Affliction, Bound to Three Realms

Asked how many of these three clusters are unwholesome and how many are neutral, the treatise answers that only a portion of the first cluster, the one bound to the desire realm, is unwholesome, while the remaining two clusters, bound to the form and formless realms, are entirely neutral, specifically of the obscuring yet karmically indeterminate kind, defiled enough to cloud the mind and obstruct the noble path, yet too weak in their own force to actually ripen into any determinate karmic fruit. It is specifically the unwholesome portion, and only that portion, that carries a genuine ripening result, capable of drawing a being down into an evil destiny; every other portion, whether neutral or belonging to the higher two realms, produces no such ripening result at all.

15. Section: Eight Questions Every Practitioner Actually Wants Answered

The treatise closes its account of the desire realm's own numerous, forceful, externally-directed, evil-conduct-generating, much-suffering-producing, gravely culpable, slowly-abandoned affliction, set against the upper two realms' own scarce, mild, internally-directed, non-evil-conduct-generating, little-suffering-producing, lightly culpable, already-abandoned affliction, offering a summary verse at this point in the chapter to hold the whole comparison together: numerous, defiling, and external in its door; generating evil conduct and manifold suffering; culpable, and slow to leave desire; opposed to concentration, and scattered rather than single in what it generates. With this comparison closed, the treatise turns to a cluster of questions that any sincere practitioner genuinely wants answered: how, exactly, is affliction actually severed? At what point can one rightly say affliction has been severed? From which affliction can severing meaningfully be spoken of at all? Is severing sudden or gradual? In what sequence does it proceed? How many kinds of severing are there? What marks appear once affliction has genuinely been severed? And what benefit does severing affliction actually bring? Taking the first two questions together, the treatise answers first by way of five successive stages: affliction begins to be severable once the provisions of wholesome dharma have been fully accumulated at the resource stage; once access to the very threshold of realization has been gained at the stage of joined practice, heat, summit, forbearance, and supreme worldly dharma; once the ground of seeing has genuinely been attained; and once habituated practice has continued at the ground of cultivation — while affliction can rightly be said to have been entirely severed only once the ultimate ground itself has been reached, an arhat, a solitary awakened one, or a buddha having exhausted every affliction there is to exhaust.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these eight opening questions as the treatise's own remarkably direct acknowledgment of what every sincere practitioner is actually asking underneath all the technical vocabulary, not abstract curiosity about affliction's own nature, but practical questions about their own path: when will I know I have actually made progress, is this something that happens all at once or only gradually, and what will it even feel like when it does. He highlights the five-stage answer to the first two questions as a map any practitioner can locate themselves on, resource-gathering, threshold-approach, the breakthrough of seeing, the long labor of cultivation, and the ultimate ground, each stage marking a genuinely different relationship to affliction rather than a single undifferentiated progress bar.

16. Section: Four Kinds of Yoga — Faith, Aspiration, Diligence, and Skillful Means

The treatise offers a second way of answering the same two questions, this time by way of four kinds of yoga, already explained in full in the Stage of the Hearers. The yoga of faith requires two aspects, trusting and complying with what the Three Jewels and the four truths teach, and generating a genuinely pure confidence in their real, existing virtues, resting on two grounds in turn, observing for oneself that the very principle of defiled and pure cause and effect genuinely holds, and witnessing directly the spiritual power of an awakened teacher or noble disciple. The yoga of aspiration requires wishing for attainment of the noble path or unsurpassed awakening itself, wishing to diligently seek out and question genuinely accomplished teachers, wishing to earnestly gather the provisions such attainment requires, and wishing to follow wholeheartedly wherever the cultivation of calming and insight actually leads. The yoga of diligence requires sustained effort in hearing the teaching, in reflecting upon it, in actually cultivating it through repeated practice, and in clearing away, day and night, whatever obstacles, foremost among them the five hindrances, stand in the way of accomplishing calming and insight. And the yoga of skillful means requires the strengthening power of moral discipline, guarding one's own mindfulness carefully, thereby remaining free of heedlessness in body, speech, and mind alike, and settling the mind correctly inward in actual meditative cultivation. It is through diligently applying these four kinds of yoga together, the treatise explains, that affliction becomes severable at all, and it is only once these same four have been thoroughly and skillfully mastered that affliction can rightly be said to have been entirely severed.

17. Section: Five Steps of Comprehensive Knowing

The treatise offers a third angle on how affliction becomes severable and how it is finally severed entirely, framed this time around the very conditions leading to nirvana: continuity's own maturation, corresponding to the completeness of one's birth circumstances; receiving compliant teaching, corresponding to the completeness of hearing correct dharma; correct inward attention, corresponding to taking nirvana as one's own guiding aim and to the ripening of the very wisdom that leads to liberation; and the arising of the antidote path, corresponding to actually cultivating the antidote itself — four steps leading affliction to become severable, while reaching total purification, both mundane, the four meditative absorptions and four formless attainments, and transcendent, the noble path carried from its first fruit through to full arhatship or unsurpassed awakening, is what allows one to say every affliction has finally been severed. A fourth angle follows, framed around comprehensive knowing and permanent severing, unfolding in five steps: knowing affliction's own basis, the very ground, itself unclean, from which affliction actually arises; knowing affliction's own intrinsic nature, its shared quality of inner unrest together with each affliction's own particular character; knowing affliction's own faults, the harm it has caused, is causing, and will continue to cause across past, present, and future alike; no longer clinging firmly to affliction the moment it does arise, recognizing it immediately as mistaken and letting it go rather than dwelling in it; and finally taking up the antidote itself through correct attention, actually cultivating calming and insight. Only by passing genuinely through all five of these steps does affliction become severable at all, and it is the completed arising of this very antidote that allows one to say, at last, that every affliction has been severed.

18. Section: "Skillfully Cultivating Both Together, Only Then Are Both Released"

The treatise offers a fifth angle, framed around the skillful cultivation of calming and insight together: cultivating calming alone, or cultivating insight alone, is not enough; only cultivating both makes affliction severable, and only once the bondage of grasped characteristics has genuinely been released, together with the bondage of coarse-heaviness itself, can one say that every affliction has been severed. Mark-bondage is the mind's own habitual grasping at the characteristics of whatever object appears before it, taking cups, people, and every ordinary thing encountered as simply, solidly real; coarse-heaviness-bondage is the deeper bondage of the affliction-seed itself. As the Blessed One himself declared in a verse the treatise now quotes directly: mark-bondage binds sentient beings, and coarse-heaviness bondage binds them too; only by skillfully cultivating both calming and insight together are both bonds finally released. Insight cultivated alone easily degenerates into a dry, ungrounded cleverness; calming cultivated alone, lacking insight's own wisdom, likewise fails to sever anything at all — genuine release from both bonds requires both practices in full, sustained partnership. A sixth angle follows, framed around what the actual practice of yoga accomplishes: knowing thoroughly the very object one meditates upon, and delighting in that object once its own true nature is understood, are what make affliction severable in the first place; and once the very basis harboring affliction's seed has been destroyed, and that basis has genuinely been transformed from its defiled state into a purified one, no longer turning through birth and death, one can say that every affliction has finally, entirely been severed. With this, the treatise closes its answer to the first two of its eight opening questions, how affliction can be severed, and at what point severing can rightly be declared complete.

19. Section: Sudden Breakthrough, Gradual Labor — And Where Practice Actually Begins

Turning to its fourth question, whether severing is sudden or gradual, the treatise gives a clear answer: affliction severed at the path of seeing is severed suddenly, not gradually, since the very realization that directly and truly beholds the four noble truths is what severs it, and this realization corresponds to an attitude of genuine renunciation toward the very objects it examines, so that, in three swift mind-moments, inwardly negating the false imputation of a real being, inwardly negating the false imputation of real dharmas, and comprehensively negating both together, every one of the hundred and twelve afflictions confused about the four truths, ten in the desire realm's own case for each truth, nine in the form and formless realms' own case since hatred is absent there, are cut through all at once, entirely and without remainder. Affliction severed by the path of cultivation, by contrast, is severed only gradually, requiring repeated, sustained practice rather than a single breakthrough, since it is innate rather than discrimination-based and does not yield to a single moment of direct realization the way discrimination-arisen affliction does. Turning to its fifth question, the actual sequence in which affliction is severed, the treatise begins where practice itself actually begins: a householder must first sever unwholesome livelihood, such as killing, and the wrong views that deny cause and effect altogether; one who has gone forth from the household life must next sever whatever obstructs the very joy of renunciation itself, the discursive thoughts of desire, of ill will, and of harming others; one whose mind is not yet settled in concentration must next sever whatever obstructs the attainment of meditative absorption, the discursive thoughts of one's own family, one's own homeland, and the wish to postpone practice indefinitely under the comfortable illusion of not dying anytime soon; and one who has begun to settle the mind in meditation must next sever whatever obstructs the very attainment of stabilized mental application, the five hindrances together with the body's own accumulated fatigue and heaviness.

20. Section: Nine Ways to Name a Single Severing

The treatise completes its eight-stage sequence: next to be severed are the hundred and twelve afflictions belonging to the path of seeing itself; next, the sixteen afflictions belonging to the path of cultivation, self-view, extreme-view, greed, hatred, ignorance, and conceit in the desire realm, and the same minus hatred in the form and formless realms; next, whatever afflictions specifically obstruct meditative absorption and are tied to painful, distressed, pleasant, joyful, and neutral feeling respectively, the first meditative absorption freeing one from distress, the second from pain, the third from joy, the fourth from pleasure, and the signless absorption beyond even the four formless attainments freeing one from neutral feeling itself; and finally, for the bodhisattva alone, whatever belongs to the hindrance to what is to be known, beyond the hindrance of affliction itself. 21. Section: A Single Coin Refused — What Severed Affliction Actually Looks Like

Turning to its seventh question, what marks appear once affliction has genuinely been severed, the treatise names four. First, the three poisons simply do not arise: confronted with an agreeable object, whether inferior or superior, present or absent, and even when deliberately, forcefully bringing the most desirable object imaginable to mind and examining it closely, no attachment forms; and exactly as with the agreeable, so too with whatever might once have provoked anger or confusion, no aversion and no bewilderment arise either. Second, the mind abides in supreme equanimity, mindful and clearly comprehending: seeing a form with the eye, hearing a sound with the ear, and so through every one of the senses down to the mind's own cognition of mental objects, neither joy nor sorrow arises, only an unshaken, level abiding, fully aware and fully present. Third, such a person genuinely accomplishes the ascetic's own qualities: true, unfeigned fewness of desire, wanting others to know nothing of whatever virtues one actually possesses; contentment with whatever little one has; genuine seclusion from defilement; vigorous diligence in wholesome practice; sustained mindfulness of the Three Jewels; settled meditative stability; and a wisdom evident in body, speech, and mind alike. A story from the sutras illustrates fewness of desire at its most extreme: after the Buddha's final nirvana, King Aśoka, making offerings of treasure at stupa after stupa, heard of the venerable Bakkula's own legendary lack of desire, and offered him a single coin as a test, only to have the temple guardian spirit fling the coin straight back out, moving the king to exclaim in genuine admiration, truly, this is fewness of desire indeed, unwilling to accept even a single coin. Fourth, such a person is free of idle conceptual proliferation, naturally and effortlessly delighting in what is free of all such chatter, while needing to deliberately exert and rouse the mind merely to engage, when necessary, in ordinary worldly talk of kings, nations, thieves, or food. These four together, the treatise concludes, are the very marks of affliction already, genuinely severed.

22. Section: Eight Questions Closed — Security, Coolness, and a Task Complete

Turning at last to its eighth and final question, what benefit severing affliction actually brings, the treatise offers an extended answer. Through attainment of the four meditative absorptions, a practitioner transcends distress, pain, joy, and pleasure in turn; through attainment of the four formless absorptions, transcends the very perception of form and every perception bound to sense-based resistance; through attaining the fruit of a learner on the noble path, transcends the suffering of the evil destinies; and through attaining the fruit of one beyond further learning, an arhat, transcends every kind of suffering there is, the suffering of birth and all that follows it, entirely and for good. Beyond this transcending, five further benefits follow. A learner who has severed a portion of affliction attains security, freedom from the dread affliction itself always carries with it; an arhat who has severed every affliction entirely attains the very first, the foremost security there is. In just the same way, a learner attains coolness, relief from affliction's own burning heat, while an arhat attains the very first, foremost coolness, the basis of every beneficial thing that follows from it. A practitioner attains as well the very first, most excellent dwelling in present joy, freely entering, at will, whatever noble abiding, divine abiding, or sublime abiding they wish, emptiness, signlessness, wishlessness, or the cessation of feeling and perception among the noble abidings, the four absorptions or four formless states among the divine abidings, or loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity among the sublime abidings. Whatever such a person has thereby attained, moreover, in walking or in standing, never again regresses, unlike the meditative attainments of the merely worldly path, which can indeed be lost. And such a person's own genuine good is, at last, entirely and completely fulfilled, with nothing further left to accomplish, having already done everything there truly was to do. With this, the treatise has now answered every one of its eight opening questions concerning the severing of affliction in full.

24. Section: Inner and Outer, Present and Absent, One's Own Kind and Another's

26. Section: Twenty Ways Affliction Actually Shows Its Face

27. Section: The Moment of Conception, Seen From the Inside

The treatise completes its twenty conditions, matching each to the person and manifestation already named, pleasure, suffering, neutrality, desire, discursive thought, contact, latent tendency, past habituation, bad companionship, hearing improper teaching, improper attention, disbelief, laziness, lost mindfulness, distraction, poor discernment, heedlessness, affliction itself, not yet being free of desire, and simply being an unawakened ordinary being by nature, this last belonging to one beyond any current cure. It is according to these very conditions, the treatise concludes, that affliction actually manifests, closing its full account of the twenty persons, twenty manifestations, and twenty conditions together. The treatise now turns to a related question of striking psychological depth: when a being's own rebirth-continuity links them to a new body within some particular realm, does the entire body of affliction belonging to that realm link along with them, or only a portion? The answer is that it is the entire body of affliction, not a portion. This holds first because one not yet free of desire is reborn specifically in the very place their own desire binds them to, never in a place they have already become free of; and it holds further because even a noble one who has seen the path but not yet fully freed themselves of desire still carries that realm's own affliction-seed bound to their present body, a seed that becomes, in its own turn, the very cause of their next, different body, so that even a stream-enterer or once-returner, entering the womb with genuine awareness of who their parents actually are, quite unlike an ordinary being's own utterly confused entry, is nonetheless still pulled along by this same residual seed — though poignantly, some such noble ones are said to forget their own attainment entirely upon birth, its memory only reawakened later by some fortunate condition. And it holds finally at the very moment of conception itself, when self-love arises toward one's own coming body, and, depending on whether that body will be female or male, attraction arises toward one parent and aversion toward the other, together with a flickering doubt, wondering whether this man, this woman, is truly the one now joined with oneself, and self-view, self-belonging-view, and conceit all arise there as well — from which it should be understood that every affliction belonging to that realm, not craving alone, truly participates in linking a being to their own rebirth.

28. Section: Seven Ways a Being Is Linked to Their Own Rebirth

30. Section: Five Factors for a Complete Act of Killing — And a Bodhisattva's Compassionate Exception

Taking killing as its paradigm case, the treatise asks how the very own-mark of the unwholesome karma-path of killing and its companions is established, and answers with five factors together: a defiled mind arises; the wish and intention to act on it arises; one proceeds specifically against the intended object; the deed itself is actually carried out; and the deed reaches its own completion. Only when every one of these five is genuinely present does an act qualify as the fully complete karma-path of killing, or any comparable unwholesome deed. A defiled mind means specifically that greed, hatred, or delusion obscures the mind at the moment of acting, one governed by greed driven to steal, one governed by hatred driven to kill, one governed by delusion driven into wrong view through simple ignorance of cause and effect. The treatise's own commentary notes an important exception here for the bodhisattva: should a bodhisattva observe a bandit about to murder many people out of greed, or about to harm a great teacher, a solitary awakened one, or a fellow bodhisattva, or about to commit one of the deeds of immediate retribution, and, reflecting with genuine care, think to themselves, if I do not end this being's life, they will commit this terrible deed and suffer immensely for it in hell; better that I alone bear the consequence of ending their life than let them fall into that suffering, and then, moved by a mind of compassion rather than hatred, and accepting full responsibility with deep humility regarding their own future accountability, actually takes that life — such a bodhisattva, the treatise states plainly, commits no violation of the bodhisattva precepts at all, and in fact generates great merit through this very act, precisely because the defiled mind of hatred that would otherwise be required is genuinely absent, replaced instead by compassion alone.

31. Section: When a Killing Is Not Quite a Killing

The treatise completes its account of what fails to constitute a fully complete karma-path. A defiled mind and the wish to act may both be present, and the intended object correctly identified without any confusion, and yet the deed itself never actually manifests, and still the intended result comes about regardless, as when one intending to kill another never in fact lifts a hand, yet the intended victim, hearing of the threat, dies of sheer terror before the deed is ever carried out, or takes their own life first out of that same fear — even here, the karma-path remains incomplete, since the deed itself was never actually enacted. And a defiled mind, the wish to act, correct identification of the object, and the deed's own actual enactment may all be present, and yet the deed still fails to reach its own completion, as when one attacks an intended victim, believes them dead, and flees the scene, only for that victim to be fortunately rescued and survive after all — this too falls short of the fully complete karma-path. Only when every one of the five factors is genuinely present together, a defiled mind, the arisen wish, correct identification of the intended object, the deed's actual manifestation, and its own final completion, does an action qualify as the fully complete karma-path in question. By this brief account of the own-mark of an unwholesome karma-path, every one of the ten unwholesome karma-paths, not killing alone, should be understood and determined according to this same fivefold pattern, each requiring its own full complement of these five factors before it can properly be called complete.

32. Section: A More Detailed Five — The Object and the Four Kinds of Perception

The treatise now offers a more detailed, expanded account of the ten unwholesome karma-paths' own specific varieties, naming five aspects in this fuller version: the object, the perception, the wish, the affliction, and the combined means and completion. The object refers to whatever particular basis each individual karma-path specifically depends upon, whether a sentient being or something not sentient at all, each of the ten unwholesome karma-paths turning upon whatever object properly belongs to it. Perception is of four kinds: perceiving that which is not the intended object as though it were not the intended object, but being mistaken about the object's own identity; perceiving that which is not the intended object as though it were the intended object; perceiving that which genuinely is the intended object as though it genuinely were; and perceiving that which is not the intended object as though it correctly were not — taking killing as the illustrative case, since killing depends specifically on a sentient being as its object, failing to recognize a given being as the very being one intends to kill is a mistaken perception, and just as easily, mistaking some other being, or even something not sentient at all, for the intended victim is likewise a mistaken perception; both such distortions belong to mistaken perception, and every other karma-path should be understood according to this same pattern in turn, while correctly recognizing the intended object as itself, and correctly recognizing what is not the intended object as not itself, are both, by contrast, entirely free of any such distortion.

33. Section: Seven Combinations of Affliction, and Killing Worked Out in Full

The treatise completes its expanded five-aspect account. The wish to act may arise whether one's perception of the object was distorted or entirely accurate, the underlying motive present either way. The affliction driving the deed may be greed alone, hatred alone, or delusion alone, any two of these arising together or in succession, or all three arising together or in succession, seven possible combinations in total capable of driving a being toward any of the ten unwholesome deeds. And means and completion refers to actually setting the intended karma into motion and then reaching its own fulfillment, whether immediately, in that very moment, or only later, after some further time has passed. Applying these five aspects to killing specifically, its object is a sentient being; its perception is the killer's own correct, undistorted recognition of that being as the very one intended; its wish, resting on this correct recognition, is the resolve, I shall end this life; its affliction is whichever of the seven combinations of greed, hatred, and delusion happens to obscure the killer's mind at the time; and its means and completion is the actual carrying out of harm, whether by one's own hand or through another, the karma-path reaching its own completion the very moment the being dies without interruption, or, if death is delayed, only once that being's life actually ends at that later time — a distinction the tradition of the hearers holds to strictly, insisting the root karma-path is completed only at the moment of actual death, though this same strictness is not spelled out in quite the same terms within the Great Vehicle's own texts.

34. Section: Theft, Illicit Conduct, and False Speech Worked Out in Full

The treatise applies the same five-aspect pattern to theft: its object is property belonging to another, whether living or not; its perception is correctly recognizing that property without confusion; its wish is the desire to plunder or steal; its affliction is any of the seven combinations already named; and its means and completion is simply moving the object away from its original place, whether gathering it together within a village or a quiet place, or shifting it from where it properly belongs, even carrying off goods aboard a boat counting as theft the moment the boat itself moves. Applied next to sexual misconduct: its object is whatever partner or circumstance is not proper to engage, a woman one ought not approach at all, or, even with an otherwise proper partner, the wrong bodily part, the wrong place, the wrong time, or excess beyond what is fitting, and likewise any man or any person of indeterminate sex; its perception is correctly recognizing that partner without confusion; its wish is the desire to actually carry out the act; its affliction is again any of the seven combinations; and its means and completion is the union of the two bodies itself. Applied to false speech: its object is claiming to have seen, heard, sensed, or known what one has not, or denying having seen, heard, sensed, or known what one actually has; its perception is either accurately recognizing what was actually seen, heard, sensed, and known, or else inverting that very recognition; its wish is the desire to conceal the truth and speak falsely instead; and its affliction, again, is any of the seven combinations of greed, hatred, and delusion.

35. Section: Divisive Speech, Harsh Speech, and the Subtlety of Idle Chatter

False speech reaches its own completion once whoever is listening or engaged in the exchange understands what has been said, the karma-path complete the moment the falsehood actually lands. Divisive speech, sowing discord, takes as its object beings who are either presently in harmony or already estranged; its perception is correctly recognizing which of these two conditions actually holds; its wish is delighting in seeing them further estranged or kept from reconciling; its affliction is any combination of the three poisons; and its completion is reached once those very beings, whether once harmonious and now broken apart, or already estranged and now driven further apart still, actually come to understand what has been said and done. Harsh speech takes as its object whatever being one wishes to wound or offend; its perception, wish, and affliction follow the same pattern; and its completion, notably, requires nothing more than the scolding words themselves being spoken, the target's own understanding not required at all, unlike divisive speech, since harsh speech's own purpose is simply venting anger rather than accomplishing anything through the listener's own comprehension. Idle chatter takes as its object whatever talk leads to no genuine benefit; its perception, wish, and affliction follow the same pattern once more; and its completion is reached the very instant the words are spoken, whether as one of the three prior unwholesome speech-acts committed at the wrong time or without real purpose, or standing entirely on its own, as when reciting heretical texts to entertain another, or indulging privately in idle song, weeping, or lament even when no one else is present to hear it. Idle chatter, the tradition holds, is so pervasive and so subtle that only a fully awakened Buddha is able to sever its very root entirely, which is precisely why the Buddha alone, among all teachers, is honored with the title World-Honored One.

36. Section: Covetousness, Malice, and Wrong View — The Tenth Karma-Path Complete

Covetousness takes as its object property, people, or things belonging to another; its perception is correctly recognizing them; its wish is the very craving itself, wanting them; its affliction is the three poisons, complete or incomplete; and its completion is reached once one firmly resolves that this very person, thing, or property must, one way or another, become one's own. Malice shares the same object and perception as harsh speech, whatever being one wishes to harm; its wish is the desire to injure, whether wishing death, physical harm, the loss of another's wealth or family, or simply that they suffer and grieve in whatever way; its affliction, again, is the three poisons, complete or incomplete; and its completion is reached the moment the intention to cause harm becomes firmly, decisively fixed in the mind, needing no further outward action at all. Wrong view takes as its object what genuinely, truly exists, the Three Jewels, the four noble truths, and the like; its perception is the distorted view that treats what genuinely exists as though it did not exist at all; its wish is delighting in and clinging to this very distortion as though it were correct; its affliction, once more, is the three poisons, complete or incomplete; and its completion is reached once the denial of what genuinely exists becomes firmly and decisively fixed as settled conviction. With this, the treatise completes its detailed working-out of every one of the ten unwholesome karma-paths according to the same five-aspect pattern. The treatise then turns to a further refinement specifically for the karma-path of killing, distinguishing three further kinds: killing whose fault continues to increase after the deed itself, killing whose fault does not increase, and killing that carries no fault at all.

37. Section: An Accidental Killing Repaid Across Two Lifetimes

The treatise explains its threefold distinction for killing through three generating conditions: arising from affliction, generating suffering, and reaching the satisfaction of one's own wish. Killing whose fault increases possesses all three conditions together, driven by greed, hatred, or delusion, producing genuine suffering for oneself or another, and reaching the killer's own satisfied wish, a gladness at having done what was intended. Killing whose fault does not increase possesses only the first two conditions, arising from affliction and producing suffering, but lacks the satisfaction of a fulfilled wish, as when the deed is completed yet immediately regretted, or attempted yet never actually completed. And killing that carries no fault at all possesses only the second condition, generating suffering alone, without any affliction driving it and without any wish being fulfilled, as when a bodhisattva kills purely out of compassion, entirely free of greed, hatred, or the wish to kill, even though the being killed still suffers regardless, or when killing occurs through simple accident, tied to karma from an earlier life rather than present malice. The tradition recalls the story of the monk An Shigao, who, knowing that he had accidentally killed another person in a past life, foresaw that he himself would be accidentally killed in the present life at Kuaiji, and used the very occasion of his own foreseen death to guide a young man toward renunciation and monastic practice — the debt of an accidental killing repaid, across two lifetimes, life for life, entirely without malice on either side, and so entirely without fault, even while genuine suffering still occurred.

38. Section: "One Thought of Anger Arising Opens a Hundred Thousand Gates of Obstruction"

The treatise offers hatred's own five aspects, closely paralleling covetousness's own structure. A mind of hatred arises through discrimination based on past circumstance or present provocation, generating the wish to cause harm. A mind of intolerance is the inability to bear whatever is felt as unwelcome or disadvantageous. A mind of resentment dwells repeatedly, and not in accordance with reason, on ill will toward the disadvantage one has suffered, this affliction-tinged grudge persisting stubbornly in memory long after the original provocation has passed. A mind of scheming actively plots against the very being one resents, thinking of how to strike them, how to kill them, and further wishing upon them every manner of misery and grief. And a mind of obscuration, exactly as with covetousness, is so thoroughly covered over by hatred's own grip that one feels no shame at all, recognizing neither hatred's own genuine fault nor any need to escape it — as the old teaching warns, a single thought of anger arising opens a hundred thousand gates of obstruction all at once, and however much the other person may genuinely be at fault, it is the one who gives way to anger who is harmed first and worst of all, every accumulated merit consumed in that very fire, the practitioner's own basic understanding that anger has no place on the path having simply failed to take hold.

He treats the old saying about anger opening a hundred thousand gates of obstruction as this section's own most quotable and most urgently practical teaching, precisely because it locates the deepest cost of anger not in whatever conflict provoked it but in the burned merit and clouded mind of the one who gave way to it.

39. Section: Thunder in a Clear Cold Sky — Closing Self-Nature, Opening Method

Should even one of the five aspects be missing, hatred cannot be called complete; only when all five are genuinely present together does it qualify as such. The Sutra in Forty-Two Sections observes that anger in a householder still enjoying worldly pleasures is understandable enough, but that a renunciant who has genuinely left desire behind has no business harboring it at all, comparing such anger to a sudden bolt of thunder bursting out of an otherwise clear, cold sky, utterly out of place against the very stillness renunciation was meant to bring. Wrong view, too, has its own five aspects: a mind of delusion, failing to genuinely understand what is actually known; a mind of cruelty, delighting in every kind of evil deed; a mind transgressing the very stream of wholesome conduct, generated by unreasoned, incorrect speculation about the Three Jewels and the four truths; a mind of destruction, denying that giving, loving care, or reverent offering bring any genuine result at all, and slandering every virtuous practice as meaningless; and a mind of obscuration, so covered over by wrong view's own grip that one feels no shame, recognizing neither its fault nor any need to abandon it. As with covetousness and hatred before it, wrong view is complete only when every one of these five aspects is genuinely present together. With this, the treatise closes its account of the first of karma defilement's seven gates, self-nature, examined through the ten unwholesome karma-paths in full, and turns to its second gate, the actual means and methods by which such karma is carried out, beginning again with killing: by one's own hand, or with a stone, a club, a blade, by binding and confining, by starving, by torment, by punishment, by poisoned potions or curses, or even, the tradition records, through necromantic method, animating a corpse or a headless half-corpse by incantation to carry out the killing itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the thunder-in-a-clear-sky image as a genuinely sharp piece of spiritual accountability: the very same anger that might be forgivable, even expected, in someone still fully immersed in worldly life becomes something else entirely in one who claims to have set that life aside, the contrast between professed renunciation and actual conduct making the lapse far more jarring than it would otherwise be. He notes that the closing of self-nature and the opening of method mark a genuine shift in the treatise's own approach, moving from the inner psychological structure shared by every complete unwholesome act to the concrete, often startlingly specific external methods by which such acts have actually been carried out across recorded human history, including practices, like necromantic killing, that a modern reader might not expect to find catalogued in a philosophical treatise at all.

40. Section: For Profit, For Fear, For Sport — The Motives Behind Wrongdoing

The treatise names killing's own motives: for the sake of wealth and profit, out of fear of being harmed oneself, to eliminate a hated enemy, in the mistaken belief that killing is lawful or even virtuous, as certain outsiders hold that animals exist purely to be killed for human use, or simply for sport and amusement, as when insects, frogs, or fish are killed purely for entertainment — and whether carried out by one's own hand or through instigating another to do it, the offense of killing is equally incurred either way. Turning to theft, the treatise catalogues its own methods: open, undisguised robbery; stealthy theft; breaking through a wall or undoing a barrier, as in wartime plunder; ambush along a road; refusing to repay a debt one owes; refusing to return property entrusted to one's own keeping; obtaining goods through deception, flattery, or fraud, as when one falsely displays spiritual attainment one does not actually possess in order to secure offerings; feigning pitiable distress to elicit charity; using intimidation or a display of power to extort what belongs to another; and, once again, whether carried out personally or through instigating someone else. Its motives follow in turn: for oneself, for another, out of simple fear, to avoid being killed or imprisoned, to subdue an opponent, for one's own use, to support one's own family or teachers, or out of sheer envy at another's greater wealth — every one of these, the treatise concludes, constitutes the offense of taking what is not given.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this catalogue of motives as the treatise's own frank acknowledgment that wrongdoing is rarely simple or single-minded, encompassing everything from cold calculation to genuine terror to something as seemingly trivial as boredom or sport, and that each of these motives, however different they feel from the inside, still generates the very same karmic offense in the end. He highlights the false display of spiritual attainment for the sake of offerings as a particularly pointed inclusion within the catalogue of theft, since it names a form of deception the tradition takes with special seriousness, exploiting genuine religious devotion itself as the very instrument of the fraud.

41. Section: Wrong Part, Wrong Time, Wrong Place — Sexual Misconduct Catalogued

The treatise catalogues sexual misconduct's own forbidden circumstances in detail. Approaching one's own mother or any woman under another's protection, the sutras explain at length, is included among the gravest offenses, comparable in weight to the deeds of immediate retribution; an unmarried woman is understood to be protected in three ways, by her own respected family and kin, by royal or civil authority should she belong to a court or official household, and by whatever guardians keep watch over her, and approaching any woman so protected, or another man's own wife, is likewise forbidden; and, from the male practitioner's own perspective the text addresses, any man, and any person lacking full male faculties, is equally not to be approached, whether the acting party or the receiving one. Beyond the wrong partner, there is the wrong bodily part, anything other than the woman's own proper place being improper; the wrong time, during menstruation, during the final stage of pregnancy, while nursing an infant, while observing the eight precepts, or during illness where such activity would be harmful; the wrong place, any hall where the venerable gather, any shrine, any public setting, or any hard, uneven ground causing real discomfort; the wrong measure, more than five times in immediate succession being reckoned as excess; and simply failing to follow ordinary worldly custom altogether. Whether engaged in personally or arranged through another as go-between, and whether conducted openly or in secret, through deception, or by abusing a position of entrusted intimacy, every one of these circumstances constitutes the offense of sexual misconduct.

He highlights the treatise's insistence that both the principal actor and any facilitating go-between share equally in the offense as a consistent pattern running through every one of the ten unwholesome karma-paths so far catalogued, closing off the excuse that arranging wrongdoing for another somehow escapes the wrongdoing itself.

42. Section: How a Lie Travels, and the Many Faces of a Cutting Word

The treatise turns to the remaining verbal karma-paths. False speech arises for one's own sake, for another's sake, out of fear, or for material gain, whether claiming to have seen, heard, sensed, or known what one has not, or denying having seen, heard, sensed, or known what one actually has, and may be conveyed in writing, through a deceptive silence taken as tacit agreement, through a gesture or expression of the body, through false testimony offered as proof, spoken personally, or spoken through another induced to say it. Divisive speech may rest on a true fact deliberately deployed to drive two parties apart, or on a fabricated falsehood invented for the very same purpose, or may be delivered under cover of intimacy, friendship, or service rendered to one party in order to win the trust needed to divide them from another; its own motives include simple self-interest, the wish to harm another, being instigated by a third party, falsely denigrating someone's genuine virtue as nonexistent or incomplete, or feigning fear to drive two parties apart, whether spoken personally or through another. Harsh speech may be delivered to someone's face or behind their back, before a crowd or in seclusion, whether the fault alleged is genuine or invented, in writing, or through a mockingly assumed manner; it may arise from one's own restless agitation or simple lack of inner calm, and the tradition records that it has, at times, taken the form of insults grounded in another's low birth, in another's own bodily or sensory impairment, or in another's past violation of precepts or discipline — every one of these named here as instances of this very unwholesome karma-path, condemned rather than condoned, whether spoken personally or induced through another.

He highlights divisive speech's own two possible bases, a true fact weaponized and an outright fabrication, as the treatise's own reminder that dishonesty is not the only way to fracture a relationship, since even something genuinely true can become a tool of real harm the moment it is deployed with the specific intention of driving two people apart.

43. Section: Even Craving a Reputation for Few Desires Is Still Covetousness

The treatise closes its account of idle chatter's many forms: verses composed to accompany dance, or set to music, or both together, or lyrics carrying no genuine purpose composed to neither; loudly reciting and enthusiastically praising, and broadly expounding to others, whatever books and treatises outside the Buddha's own teaching lead to no genuine benefit; speech arising from disputes, lawsuits, and rivalry, or delighting in holding forth before a crowd about the affairs of kings, ministers, bandits, or nations; and even false speech, divisive speech, or harsh speech itself, whenever spoken carelessly, without any real thought or discernment, all fall under this same broad category. Idle chatter is further explained through seven grounds: talk arising from dispute and contention; the evil incantations certain outside brahmins are known to recite; words spoken thoughtlessly under the pressure of one's own suffering; playful, joking, entertaining talk; ordinary, unfocused socializing carrying no connection to the Dharma; the ravings of a deranged mind; and speech aimed at securing one's own material support through flattery and false pretense — all of these together constituting the full offense of idle chatter. Turning to covetousness, the treatise names eight distinct forms this desire actually takes. The first arises in one who serves a master, wishing, how might I become just like my master myself, commanding servants and having whatever I wish carried out at my own word. The second wishes that everything belonging to that same master, their parents, spouse, servants, and every one of the seven kinds of holdings and ten kinds of life's necessities, should belong to oneself instead. The third, and this applies with particular force to renunciants themselves, wishes that others come to know and praise oneself as one of few desires, contentment, seclusion, vigorous diligence, settled mindfulness, calm stability, and wisdom, one whose outflows are entirely exhausted, generous in giving, strict in discipline, and broadly learned — the very moment such a wish for one's own reputation arises, even concerning genuinely wholesome qualities, it has already become covetousness, joined with an unwholesome state of mind. The fourth wishes that kings, merchants, monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen alike come to revere, honor, serve, and make offerings to oneself.

He notes that this passage should give any diligent practitioner real pause, since wanting to be seen as virtuous is a subtler and more easily overlooked trap than wanting ordinary wealth, precisely because it wears the costume of virtue while still being, underneath, the very same grasping mind.

44. Section: Eight Forms of Covetousness Complete, and Malice Begins With Revenge

The treatise names covetousness's remaining forms. The fifth wishes for material provisions, profit, clothing, food and drink, bedding, and medicine for illness. The sixth wishes for rebirth in the heavens, there to enjoy the exquisite five heavenly pleasures at play. The seventh wishes for a superior rebirth still, in the outside teachers' own imagined realm of Rudra or of Viṣṇu, or as an especially wealthy and eminent human being, or even in the heaven of those who freely enjoy what others transform for them, delighting in the thought of enjoying whatever others have created purely for one's own pleasure. And the eighth wishes to obtain for oneself the very parents, spouse, servants, workers, friends, officials, relatives, siblings, or fellow renunciants that presently belong to someone else. All eight of these, the treatise concludes, belong entirely to the karma-path of covetousness. Turning to malice, the treatise begins its account through nine grounds of ill will, the first directed at oneself: should one think, he wishes me ill without cause, and so I shall wish him ill in return, this very thought is already malice — even ordinary worldly people, the tradition observes, are capable of repaying hatred with virtue rather than more hatred, so how much more should one walking the Buddhist path refuse to entertain such a thought at all.

45. Section: Nine Grounds of Malice Complete — All Ten Karma-Paths' Methods Now Closed

The treatise extends malice's opening thought, wishing revenge for harm already suffered, being presently suffered, or expected in the future, from this same pattern applied to oneself outward to loved ones and to enemies alike, for nine grounds of malice altogether. Malice further includes wishing to gain power over one's own enemies and ill-wishing companions, freely binding, persecuting, banishing, or beating them, scattering their wealth, or seizing their spouse, friends, family, and home; wishing that such harm instead befall them at someone else's hand rather than one's own; and wishing that misfortune simply arise on its own, without any agent at all, imagining one's enemy spontaneously engaging in improper conduct of body, speech, and mind, thereby losing wealth, friends, family, reputation, happiness, and life itself, forfeiting every wholesome quality, and finally falling, upon death, into the evil destinies. Every such mind of ill will, the treatise concludes, belongs entirely to the root karma-path of malice. Turning finally to wrong view, the treatise names its own defining examples: the settled conviction that giving bears no result at all is wrong view, and, more broadly, denying that any cause operates, denying that any function operates, denying that any result follows, and destroying what is genuinely real, denying one's own parents, denying beings born spontaneously, and denying that any true arhat, outflows entirely exhausted, exists in the world at all, are every one of them the root karma-path of wrong view. With this, the treatise brings its detailed working-out of the actual methods behind all ten unwholesome karma-paths to its full and complete close.

He highlights wishing harm to arise spontaneously, without any agent, as this catalogue's own subtlest form of malice, requiring no plan and no action whatsoever, only the quiet, persistent hope that misfortune will simply find one's enemy on its own — proof that malice can be entirely passive and still count, in full, as the very same unwholesome karma-path as active cruelty.

46. Section: Why This One Denial Is Singled Out as "Wrong View"

The treatise closes this entire stretch of the text with a final question: since every distorted view could in principle be called wrong view, why does the Blessed One, specifically within the context of the ten karma-paths, single out only this denying, slanderous view and call it wrong view? Two reasons are given. First, this particular wrong view is the most severe among every kind of wrong view there is, since relying on it, a certain ascetic or brahmin comes to cut off every one of their own wholesome roots entirely, no longer giving, no longer keeping precepts, no longer practicing anything wholesome at all, doing nothing but evil from that point onward. Second, this same wrong view most strongly facilitates unwholesome conduct, since one who holds it, having denied cause, function, and result altogether, proceeds to act however they wish among every evil deed without the slightest restraint, and it is for this reason that this particular view is specifically singled out and placed within the unwholesome karma-paths. It should be understood, the treatise adds in closing, that every other distorted view remains, in its own right, genuinely wrong view as well, simply not the one selected for special emphasis here, this one alone being uniquely capable of severing wholesome roots entirely and uniquely capable of unleashing unrestrained evil in their place.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: every distorted view is called wrong view — why does the Blessed One, within the karma-paths, speak only of this slandering view as wrong view? Answer: because this wrong view is the most excellent among all wrong views. Why so? Because, relying on this wrong view, a certain ascetic or brahmin severs every wholesome root. Also, this wrong view most closely accords with unwholesome karma. One who holds wrong view acts as they wish among every unwholesome dharma, and for this reason this view is specifically said to belong among the unwholesome karma-paths. It should be known that every other view is not thereby excluded from being genuinely wrong view in its own right.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing question and answer as a fitting capstone to the treatise's own entire detailed working-out of the ten unwholesome karma-paths, since it explains, with genuine precision, why the tradition reserves its single harshest technical label for exactly this one denial among the countless mistaken views a mind can hold: not because other errors are somehow innocent, but because this particular denial, cutting off cause, function, and result all at once, removes the very floor beneath every other safeguard against wrongdoing, leaving nothing left to restrain a mind that has embraced it. He notes that Fascicle 59's own source material comes to its natural close at exactly this point, having carried the reader from the extensive analysis completing affliction defilement, through karma defilement's own opening account of its five establishing aspects, its own self-nature examined through all ten unwholesome karma-paths in full, and now the actual methods and motives behind each of them worked out in complete and exhaustive detail — a fitting resting point before karma defilement's own remaining aspects continue in the fascicle that follows.
