Fascicle 76 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十六 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

The Secret Meaning of Mind, Intellect, and Consciousness

This is the fifth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the ultimate truth's five characteristics closed in the previous fascicle, the Chapter on Excellent Qualities' citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra turns to its second of seven topics: the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness. It opens with the bodhisattva Guanghui asking the Buddha what it takes to be called skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, and the Buddha's answer beginning to unfold the workings of the appropriating consciousness that underlies a being's turning through birth and death.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 76 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Guanghui Asks — What Makes a Bodhisattva Skillful in the Secret Meaning of Mind

This is the fifth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground works through fifteen chapters in all; the eleventh of these, on excellent qualities, is by far the longest, running from this fascicle back through the previous one and onward through most of several more still to come. Its direct, extended determination falls into two parts — working through essential meanings, already given in the previous fascicle, and citing the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra directly, which unfolds across seven topics of its own; the ultimate truth's characteristics, the first of these seven, closed at the very end of the previous fascicle, and this one takes up the second: the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness. Further, the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra itself describes: the bodhisattva Guanghui asked the Buddha, saying: As the World-Honored One has said, there are bodhisattvas skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. To what degree, exactly, is a bodhisattva called skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness? By what standard does the Tathāgata designate a bodhisattva as such?

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中菩薩地之五。復次，心、意、識相，當知如解深密經中，廣慧菩薩請問佛言：如世尊說：於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩。於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩者，齊何名為於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩？如來齊何施設彼為於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩？

Ru-Yi Translation

The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, part five. Further, the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra itself describes: the bodhisattva Guanghui asked the Buddha, saying: As the World-Honored One has said, there are bodhisattvas skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. To what degree, exactly, is a bodhisattva called skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness? By what standard does the Tathāgata designate a bodhisattva as such?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first places this fascicle within the treatise's larger plan: the Chapter on Excellent Qualities determines fifteen chapters' worth of the Bodhisattva Ground's own material, running all the way from this fascicle through most of the next several, divided first into a direct, extended determination — itself split between essential meanings, already covered, and this direct citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra across seven further topics — and only later into a briefer sorting of world-systems. Of the Saṃdhinirmocana's seven topics, the characteristic of the ultimate truth closed the previous fascicle; this one opens the second, on mind, intellect, and consciousness, since a being's turning through birth and death is driven by exactly these three, and only by understanding them clearly can that turning be reversed toward liberation — the ultimate truth showed what reality itself is, and this chapter now turns to show what the mind that must realize it actually is. He introduces the bodhisattva Guanghui, "Vast Wisdom," as named for a wisdom so vast, deep, and consummately skillful that it takes in countless teachings both of the world and beyond it, and explains that "mind," "intellect," and "consciousness" can be read either as three names for one and the same thing, as the āgamas often use them interchangeably, or as three distinct terms: mind (citta) as the store consciousness in which the seeds of every defiled and pure dharma gather and from which they arise; intellect (manas) as the seventh consciousness, manas, which forever clings to the store consciousness as an inner self; and consciousness (vijñāna) as the six consciousnesses' own function of discerning their objects through the six sense faculties. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this chapter's real weight falls on explaining the store consciousness, also called the appropriating consciousness, and that his own exposition leans chiefly on Master Woncheuk's eight-consciousness reading, supplemented by Venerable Yanpei's seven-consciousness account, which treats the store consciousness as purely a repository of seeds and the appropriating consciousness as simply another name for the seventh consciousness itself.

2. Section: The Buddha's Praise, and the Consciousness That Matures at Conception

Having said this, the Blessed One then said to the bodhisattva Guanghui: Excellent, excellent, Guanghui! You are now able to ask the Tathāgata a meaning this profound, wishing to benefit and bring ease to countless beings, having compassion on the world and on the gods, humans, asuras, and the rest, so that they may attain benefit and ease — it is for this that you have raised this question. Listen carefully, and I shall explain to you the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. Guanghui, know this: among the various beings turning through birth and death in the six destinies, born into one or another class of being, whether by egg, by womb, by moisture, or by transformation, arising as a particular kind of body — within this, it is the all-seed consciousness that first matures, coming together and growing, again and again, into fullness.

Chinese Source

說是語已，爾時世尊告廣慧菩薩曰：善哉！善哉！廣慧！汝今乃能請問如來如是深義，汝今為欲利益安樂無量眾生，哀愍世間及諸天人、阿素洛等，為令獲得義利安樂，故發斯問。汝應諦聽！吾當為汝說心、意、識祕密義。廣慧當知！於六趣生死彼彼有情，墮彼彼有情眾中，或在卵生、或在胎生、或在濕生、或在化生身分生起，於中最初一切種子心識成熟，展轉和合增長廣大。依二執受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having said this, the Blessed One then said to the bodhisattva Guanghui: Excellent, excellent, Guanghui! You are now able to ask the Tathāgata a meaning this profound, wishing to benefit and bring ease to countless beings, having compassion on the world and on the gods, humans, asuras, and the rest, so that they may attain benefit and ease — it is for this that you have raised this question. Listen carefully, and I shall explain to you the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. Guanghui, know this: among the various beings turning through birth and death in the six destinies, born into one or another class of being, whether by egg, by womb, by moisture, or by transformation, arising as a particular kind of body — within this, it is the all-seed consciousness that first matures, coming together and growing, again and again, into fullness. It rests on two kinds of appropriation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Buddha's praise is unusually warm here, since a question this profound, properly answered, benefits not only the questioner but every being still circling through the six destinies out of compassion for whom Guanghui asked at all. He then turns to the substance: whether a being is born from an egg, a womb, moisture, or spontaneously — the last typical of gods, ghosts, and hell-beings, arising all at once with no visible cause — what actually counts as the moment of birth, even for a womb-born being, is not emergence from the mother but the very first instant a stream of consciousness enters the womb; this is what the text calls the all-seed consciousness reaching maturity, the eighth consciousness under one of its several names, so called because it holds within it the seeds of every defiled and pure dharma a being has ever produced. From that first instant, this consciousness and the fertilized union of the parents' contribution — itself only an external condition, not the life itself — come together and gradually develop: consciousness conditions name-and-form, and name-and-form in turn grows into the six sense faculties over the following weeks, exactly as an earlier fascicle describes the gradual completion of a fetal being's support. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that this whole process of maturing, uniting, and growing to fullness has the all-seed consciousness as its center — it is, as a well-known verse puts it, the very consciousness that "comes first and leaves last" in a being's round of birth and death — though other supporting conditions are needed as well. This consciousness, he adds, rests on two kinds of appropriation, taking hold of and sustaining both the physical body and its own seeds — a topic the passage takes up next.

3. Section: Two Kinds of Appropriation — the Body, and the Traces of Language Itself

The first is the appropriation of the material sense faculties together with what they depend on; the second is the appropriation of the habitual traces of characteristics, names, discrimination, verbal expression, and conceptual proliferation. In the realm of form, both kinds of appropriation are present in full; in the formless realm, both are not present.

Chinese Source

一者、有色諸根及所依執受，二者、相、名、分別言說戲論習氣執受。有色界中具二執受，無色界中不具二種。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first is the appropriation of the material sense faculties together with what they depend on; the second is the appropriation of the habitual traces of characteristics, names, discrimination, verbal expression, and conceptual proliferation. In the realm of form, both kinds of appropriation are present in full; in the formless realm, both are not present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first appropriation as the all-seed consciousness taking hold of the five pure sense faculties together with the gross physical organs they depend on, sustaining them so they neither decay nor fall apart — a straightforwardly physical kind of appropriation, present for any being possessing a body of form. The second is subtler: "habitual traces" is simply another name for seeds, named this way from the angle of how they were laid down through habituation rather than the angle of their power to give rise to future events, and what gets habituated here is the entire apparatus of ordinary conceptual experience — the seven consciousnesses' own way of presenting an object, and the sixth consciousness's further layer of naming and verbal expression built on top of it — the two together depositing, moment by moment, the very seeds that will later ripen into another round of the world of beings and the receptacle world surrounding them, which in turn gets carved up by name and characteristic all over again, entrenching the cycle further. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that a being in the realm of form possesses both appropriations at once — a physical body sustained by the first, and the entire apparatus of conceptual habituation sustained by the second — whereas a being in the formless realm, having no physical body at all, is sustained by the second appropriation alone, since even the most rarefied meditative absorption is not free of subtle conceptual activity until liberation itself is reached.

4. Section: Two More Names — the Appropriating Consciousness, and the Store Consciousness

Further, Guanghui! This consciousness is also called the appropriating consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness follows after the body and holds it fast. It is also called the store consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness takes hold of the body and lies hidden within it, sharing the very same safety or danger the body itself undergoes.

Chinese Source

復次，廣慧！此識亦名阿陀那識。何以故？由此識於身隨逐執持故。亦名阿賴耶識。何以故？由此識於身攝受藏隱同安危義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, Guanghui! This consciousness is also called the appropriating consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness follows after the body and holds it fast. It is also called the store consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness takes hold of the body and lies hidden within it, sharing the very same safety or danger the body itself undergoes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "appropriating consciousness" through three functions the Consciousness-Only tradition attributes to it: holding the seeds of every dharma, taking hold of the sense faculties and what they depend on, and taking hold of the very continuity of rebirth itself — this eighth consciousness follows a being's body from the moment of conception until death without ever once letting go, which is exactly what earns it this name. "Store consciousness" he glosses through the sense of depending, dwelling, and clinging that its name most literally carries: this consciousness sustains the body, keeping it from decaying, while simultaneously hiding within that very body as its concealed abode, so that body and consciousness share one and the same fate — an earlier fascicle's own account of a being's first embryonic stage makes this vivid, describing how mind and body are mutually dependent from that very first moment, the fertilized union remaining intact only so long as mind sustains it, and mind losing its own hiding place the instant that union is damaged. Master Cháng Bǎi calls this consciousness's activity extremely subtle and hard to know directly — only the root wisdom free of discrimination can actually penetrate it — which is precisely why an ordinary being's coarse, scattered mind has no way of grasping, moment to moment, that this is the very consciousness carrying it from one life into the next.

5. Section: A Fourth Name — Mind, Accumulated and Nurtured by Its Own Objects

It is also called mind. Why is this so? Because this consciousness is accumulated and nurtured by form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the rest.

Chinese Source

亦名為心。何以故？由此識色聲香味觸等積集、滋長故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is also called mind. Why is this so? Because this consciousness is accumulated and nurtured by form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "mind" here renders the Sanskrit citta, carrying the sense of gathering and arising: this consciousness is not itself an accumulation, yet every experience of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch that the six active consciousnesses take in leaves behind a seed deposited within it, so that it grows and accumulates precisely by being nurtured through the objects the other consciousnesses encounter — and, resting on that accumulation, it goes on to give rise to further experience in turn, name and form each conditioning the other endlessly. With this fourth name given, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, the treatise has now covered every name the eighth consciousness carries in this opening account: called the all-seed consciousness from the standpoint of its maturing at conception, the appropriating consciousness from the standpoint of holding the body fast, the store consciousness from the standpoint of hiding within the body and sharing its fate, and mind from the standpoint of being built up through everything the senses gather in. He adds that a later treatise on Consciousness-Only teaching expands this same set to seven names in all, adding to these four the further titles of "that on which all is known to depend," since it grounds every wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral dharma; "seed consciousness," since it universally sustains the seeds of both mundane and supramundane dharmas; "ripened consciousness," since it carries a being through the ripening fruits of past karma across the three realms, a name that drops away once Buddhahood is reached; and "consciousness free of taint," reserved for a Buddha alone, once every trace of defiled seed has been left behind for good — and Master Cháng Bǎi notes one further point of scholarly disagreement worth flagging before the passage continues: some readers of the Saṃdhinirmocana take the all-seed consciousness as the passage's true center, with the other names merely its aliases, while others — pointing to how the text singles out the appropriating consciousness alone as what a later verse calls "profound and subtle, its seeds flowing like a torrent" — take the appropriating consciousness itself as the center, with the broader account of the all-seed consciousness serving only to lead the discussion toward it gradually.

6. Section: The Six Consciousnesses Turn — Resting on the Appropriating Consciousness

Further, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, and established by it, the body of six consciousnesses turns — namely, eye-consciousness, and ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness. Among these, there is the case where eye-consciousness arises conditioned by the eye and by form; and, arising together with and following alongside that eye-consciousness, at the same time and taking the same object, discriminating mind-consciousness turns. There is likewise the case where ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness arise conditioned by the ear, nose, tongue, and body together with sound, smell, taste, and touch; and, arising together with and following alongside these, at the same time and taking the same object, discriminating mind-consciousness turns. Guanghui! If, at some time, only a single eye-consciousness turns, then at that very time only a single discriminating mind-consciousness turns together with that eye-consciousness, taking the same object. And if, at some time, two, three, four, or all five bodies of consciousness turn, then at that very time only a single discriminating mind-consciousness turns together with those five bodies of consciousness, taking the same object.

Chinese Source

復次，廣慧！阿陀那識為依止、為建立故，六識身轉。謂眼識，耳、鼻、舌、身、意識。此中有識，眼及色為緣，生眼識；與眼識俱隨行，同時、同境有分別意識轉。有識，耳、鼻、舌、身及聲、香、味、觸為緣，生耳、鼻、舌、身識；與耳、鼻、舌、身識俱隨行，同時、同境有分別意識轉。廣慧！若於爾時一眼識轉，即於此時唯有一分別意識，與眼識同所行轉；若於爾時二、三、四、五諸識身轉，即於此時唯有一分別意識，與五識身同所行轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, and established by it, the body of six consciousnesses turns — namely, eye-consciousness, and ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness. Among these, there is the case where eye-consciousness arises conditioned by the eye and by form; and, arising together with and following alongside that eye-consciousness, at the same time and taking the same object, discriminating mind-consciousness turns. There is likewise the case where ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness arise conditioned by the ear, nose, tongue, and body together with sound, smell, taste, and touch; and, arising together with and following alongside these, at the same time and taking the same object, discriminating mind-consciousness turns. Guanghui! If, at some time, only a single eye-consciousness turns, then at that very time only a single discriminating mind-consciousness turns together with that eye-consciousness, taking the same object. And if, at some time, two, three, four, or all five bodies of consciousness turn, then at that very time only a single discriminating mind-consciousness turns together with those five bodies of consciousness, taking the same object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the six active consciousnesses can only ever function at all because the appropriating consciousness holds their seeds and sustains the sense faculties they depend on — were it not for the appropriating consciousness's continuous grip on the body, the six could never arise, exactly as an earlier fascicle puts it: the store consciousness serves the six consciousnesses as their seed and as their support, since it is the seed from which every wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral consciousness springs, and it is what allows the sense faculties themselves to keep functioning at all. He walks through the classic account of what happens the instant eye-consciousness arises: an initial, bare moment of direct awareness is immediately followed by mind-consciousness joining in — first searching, then settling on a judgment, and only then coloring the experience with attachment or aversion — so that mind-consciousness is never simply absent whenever any one of the five sense consciousnesses is active, always arising together with it and taking the very same object. And crucially, no matter how many of the five sense consciousnesses happen to be active at once — seeing, hearing, and touching all at the same moment, say — there is never more than a single mind-consciousness accompanying them, one unified discriminating awareness running alongside however many sense doors happen to be open at once, rather than five separate mental correlates each doing their own thing in isolation.

7. Section: Two Similes — a Rushing Torrent, and a Well-Polished Mirror

Guanghui! It is just as with a great, rushing torrent of water: if the conditions for a single wave to arise are present, only a single wave turns; and if the conditions for two or many waves to arise are present, many waves turn. Yet this very torrent, of its own continuous kind, flows on eternally, without break and without end. And it is just as with a well-polished mirror's surface: if the conditions for a single reflection to arise are present, only a single reflection arises; and if the conditions for two or many reflections to arise are present, many reflections arise. Yet it is not that this mirror's surface itself turns into the reflection, nor is there ever any exhaustion of its capacity to be used.

Chinese Source

廣慧！譬如大暴水流，若有一浪生緣現前，唯一浪轉；若二、若多浪生緣現前，有多浪轉。然此暴水自類恆流，無斷無盡。又如善淨鏡面，若有一影生緣現前，唯一影起；若二、若多影生緣現前，有多影起。非此鏡面轉變為影，亦無受用滅盡可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Guanghui! It is just as with a great, rushing torrent of water: if the conditions for a single wave to arise are present, only a single wave turns; and if the conditions for two or many waves to arise are present, many waves turn. Yet this very torrent, of its own continuous kind, flows on eternally, without break and without end. And it is just as with a well-polished mirror's surface: if the conditions for a single reflection to arise are present, only a single reflection arises; and if the conditions for two or many reflections to arise are present, many reflections arise. Yet it is not that this mirror's surface itself turns into the reflection, nor is there ever any exhaustion of its capacity to be used.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the torrent as illustrating exactly what makes the appropriating consciousness different in kind from the six active consciousnesses: a single gust of wind raises a single wave, a stronger gust raises many, yet the torrent itself, the water flowing beneath every wave, never stops flowing regardless — where the six consciousnesses switch on and off from moment to moment, tasting only when there is something to taste, hearing only when there is something to hear, even falling silent altogether in dreamless sleep, the appropriating consciousness carried forward by past karma never once pauses, flowing on through every waking and sleeping moment and on into the next life besides, ceasing only when the very causes and conditions sustaining rebirth are finally exhausted at final nirvāṇa. He quotes a related scripture's own image of the ocean struck by wind, giving rise to wave after wave without any gap between them, and applies it directly: sense objects strike the store consciousness the way wind strikes the sea, and seeds ripening into actual experience are the resulting waves — no wind, no waves, but the ocean itself is there regardless, exactly as the appropriating consciousness underlies the six consciousnesses' intermittent activity without ever itself being interrupted. The mirror simile makes a second, related point: one face reflected, or many, the mirror's own surface is never actually transformed into any of those reflected images, and using it to reflect one guest after another never wears the mirror down or exhausts what it can do — so too, the six active consciousnesses arise and vanish depending on conditions, but the appropriating consciousness holding their seeds is never itself used up by any of it, continuing unbroken from the very seeds of ordinary conceptual habituation that keep the whole cycle of birth and death going.

8. Section: The Similes Applied — and Why Conceptual Knowledge Alone Is Not Yet Full Skill

Just so, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, which is like this rushing torrent, and established by it, if, at some time, the conditions for a single eye-consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time a single eye-consciousness turns; and if, at some time, the conditions for all five bodies of consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time all five bodies of consciousness turn. Guanghui! Although such a bodhisattva, resting on the wisdom that knows how things stand, and established by it, has become skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, the Tathāgatas do not, on these grounds alone, designate that bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Chinese Source

如是廣慧！由似暴流阿陀那識為依止、為建立故，若於爾時有一眼識生緣現前，即於此時一眼識轉；若於爾時乃至有五識身生緣現前，即於此時五識身轉。廣慧！如是菩薩雖由法住智為依止、為建立故，於心、意、識祕密善巧；然諸如來不齊於此施設彼為於心、意、識一切祕密善巧菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just so, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, which is like this rushing torrent, and established by it, if, at some time, the conditions for a single eye-consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time a single eye-consciousness turns; and if, at some time, the conditions for all five bodies of consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time all five bodies of consciousness turn. Guanghui! Although such a bodhisattva, resting on the wisdom that knows how things stand, and established by it, has become skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, the Tathāgatas do not, on these grounds alone, designate that bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the sūtra's own text passes over the seventh consciousness, manas, without naming it directly here, though a related passage elsewhere spells out why it must still be implied: the store consciousness serves the six active consciousnesses as their seed and their support, and it is likewise the store consciousness's own presence that makes manas possible, and manas in turn that makes mind-consciousness itself able to function — much as the five sense faculties make the five sense consciousnesses possible. He notes this is exactly where later commentators divide: some read the whole passage through a seven-consciousness lens, treating the appropriating consciousness as simply another name for manas; others, following Master Woncheuk, read it through an eight-consciousness lens, taking the silence about manas here as simple economy of expression rather than any denial of its role. Turning to what merely conceptual understanding still lacks, Master Cháng Bǎi explains "the wisdom that knows how things stand" as the correct, non-inverted grasp of what the Buddha has taught — knowing, for instance, that suffering truly follows from its origin, without yet having directly realized this through meditation — a wisdom fully available even to an ordinary being who has simply studied and reflected well, whether tainted or untainted depending on whether the path has yet been reached. A bodhisattva who has mastered this conceptual understanding of mind, intellect, and consciousness has genuinely accomplished something, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, but the Buddha is about to draw a sharper line: real, complete skill in this secret meaning requires something this conceptual mastery alone does not yet provide.

9. Section: True Ultimate Skill — Seeing None of the Eighteen at All

Guanghui! If bodhisattvas, each individually within themselves, truly do not see the appropriating consciousness, do not see it as a distinct entity, do not see the store, do not see it as a distinct entity, do not see accumulation, do not see mind, do not see the eye and form and eye-consciousness, do not see the ear and sound and ear-consciousness, do not see the nose and smell and nose-consciousness, do not see the tongue and taste and tongue-consciousness, do not see the body and touch and body-consciousness, and do not see the mind and mental objects and mind-consciousness — such bodhisattvas are called skillful in the ultimate, and the Tathāgata designates them as skillful in the ultimate. Guanghui! It is precisely to this degree that a bodhisattva is called skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, and it is precisely to this degree that the Tathāgata designates a bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Chinese Source

廣慧！若諸菩薩，於內各別如實不見阿陀那，不見阿陀那識，不見阿賴耶，不見阿賴耶識，不見積集，不見心，不見眼色及眼識，不見耳聲及耳識，不見鼻香及鼻識，不見舌味及舌識，不見身觸及身識，不見意法及意識，是名勝義善巧菩薩，如來施設彼為勝義善巧菩薩。廣慧！齊此名為於心、意、識一切祕密善巧菩薩，如來齊此施設於心、意、識一切祕密善巧菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Guanghui! If bodhisattvas, each individually within themselves, truly do not see the appropriating consciousness, do not see it as a distinct entity, do not see the store, do not see it as a distinct entity, do not see accumulation, do not see mind, do not see the eye and form and eye-consciousness, do not see the ear and sound and ear-consciousness, do not see the nose and smell and nose-consciousness, do not see the tongue and taste and tongue-consciousness, do not see the body and touch and body-consciousness, and do not see the mind and mental objects and mind-consciousness — such bodhisattvas are called skillful in the ultimate, and the Tathāgata designates them as skillful in the ultimate. Guanghui! It is precisely to this degree that a bodhisattva is called skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, and it is precisely to this degree that the Tathāgata designates a bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that once a bodhisattva actually enters the root wisdom free of discrimination, both what is grasped and the mind grasping it become unattainable — the six sense faculties, their six objects, the six resulting consciousnesses, language itself, and the receptacle world all cease to appear — so that a mind resting in that direct realization no longer differentiates "this is the appropriating consciousness's function" from "this is the store consciousness's own nature," any more than it separates out the eye from form from eye-consciousness, or the mind from mental objects from mind-consciousness. This is not forgetting or ignorance, he stresses, but the exact opposite: a Chan teacher's image of space itself shattering and the ground beneath one's feet dropping away, the bottom of the lacquer bucket finally breaking through — every one of the eighteen elements, every trace of the tainted dependent nature built out of ordinary naming, simply fails to arise, because this consciousness no longer generates concepts through clinging to language the way an ordinary mind involuntarily does at the mere mention of a plum or a slice of cake. Only at this point, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, does the Buddha's designation of "skillful in the entire secret meaning" actually apply — the passage's closing line uses "to this degree" twice, once marking the bodhisattva's own direct attainment and once marking the Buddha's own act of naming it so in words for others to understand. With this, the sūtra's direct exposition of the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness comes to its full close, and what remains is for the Buddha to restate the whole teaching once more in verse.

10. Section: The Famous Verse — Why the Buddha Kept This Teaching Secret

At that time, the Blessed One, wishing to proclaim this meaning once more, spoke a verse: The appropriating consciousness is profound and subtle, all its seeds flowing like a torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate it and cling to it as a self.

Chinese Source

爾時世尊欲重宣此義而說頌曰：阿陀那識甚深細　一切種子如暴流　我於凡愚不開演　恐彼分別執為我

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Blessed One, wishing to proclaim this meaning once more, spoke a verse: The appropriating consciousness is profound and subtle, all its seeds flowing like a torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate it and cling to it as a self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this one of the most quoted verses in the whole Consciousness-Only tradition, cited by both an earlier fascicle of this very treatise and by the Cheng Weishi Lun to settle the question of why this teaching is not found spelled out in the earlier discourses. "Profound" he reads as directed at the merely clever: even a sharp, unschooled mind gets nowhere trying to reason its way to what the appropriating consciousness actually is, since it lies beneath the reach of ordinary reflection entirely. "Subtle" he reads as directed specifically at the śrāvaka: even a diligent disciple of the lesser vehicle, whose entire training is built around cutting off affliction through the sixth consciousness's own contemplation of no-self, has no particular need to trace the teaching all the way down to its subtlest root, and so may never come to understand the appropriating consciousness even after real progress on the path. The second line describes what it holds: countless seeds of affliction and of past deeds, and, in some beings, seeds of purity and of the noble path as well, all carried forward in unbroken succession the way a torrent's water never stops flowing, each seed ripening into experience and each experience depositing new seeds in turn, on and on without any beginning that can be found. And the final couplet gives the reason for the silence: the Buddha did teach on this very territory even in the earlier discourses, but centered there on the six active consciousnesses and their own impermanence and selflessness, since his audience then was chiefly ordinary beings and disciples aiming at quick liberation through the four noble truths and the contemplation of no-self — teaching them the profound and subtle appropriating consciousness risked nothing less than having them seize on it as a hidden, permanent self, the very clinging the whole teaching exists to dismantle. Only for bodhisattvas, who must eliminate not only affliction but the obstruction to all that can be known, and who must master every dharma without exception on the way to full awakening, does the Buddha finally teach this profound and subtle consciousness in the Great Vehicle's own scriptures. With this verse, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, the treatise's determination of the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness — the second of the Saṃdhinirmocana's seven topics under the Chapter on Excellent Qualities — closes completely, and what follows next is the third topic: the characteristics of all dharmas, the three natures.

11. Section: Deben Asks — and the Three Characteristics of All Dharmas Are Named

Further, the characteristics of all dharmas should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra itself describes: the bodhisattva Deben asked the Buddha, saying: World-Honored One! As the World-Honored One has said, there are bodhisattvas skillful in the characteristics of dharmas. To what degree, exactly, is a bodhisattva called skillful in the characteristics of dharmas? By what standard does the Tathāgata designate a bodhisattva as such? Having said this, the Blessed One then said to the bodhisattva Deben: Excellent, excellent, Deben! You are now able to ask the Tathāgata a meaning this profound, wishing to benefit and bring ease to countless beings, having compassion on the world and on the gods, humans, asuras, and the rest, so that they may attain benefit and ease — it is for this that you have raised this question. Listen carefully, and I shall explain to you the characteristics of dharmas. The characteristics of dharmas are, in brief, of three kinds. What are the three? The first is the imagined characteristic; the second is the dependent characteristic; and the third is the perfected characteristic.

Chinese Source

Listen carefully, and I shall explain to you the characteristics of dharmas. The characteristics of dharmas are, in brief, of three kinds. What are the three? The first is the imagined characteristic; the second is the dependent characteristic; and the third is the perfected characteristic.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces Deben, "Root of Virtue," as named either for having planted deep roots of merit and wisdom, or for a virtue so abundant it forms a whole forest others can shelter under, and notes his question mirrors Guanghui's exactly — asking both to what degree a bodhisattva has genuinely attained skill and by what standard the Buddha names it so. He locates this third topic within the treatise's larger scheme: where an earlier fascicle divides all dharmas into as many as six hundred and sixty categories, and a later treatise reduces the same territory to a hundred, the Saṃdhinirmocana condenses everything — every conditioned and unconditioned dharma alike — into just three characteristics. The imagined characteristic, he explains in brief, is the character ordinary beings project onto things through the sheer habit of naming, taking language's own constructions to be the real nature of what is named; the dependent characteristic is how things actually arise, each conditioned by causes it depends on rather than standing on its own; and the perfected characteristic is reached by stripping the imagined characteristic away from the dependent, revealing the true suchness that was there all along. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that these three are neither simply identical nor simply separate from one another — the dependent characteristic sits at the center of the whole scheme, becoming the imagined characteristic the moment an ordinary mind takes its own naming to be real, and becoming the perfected characteristic the moment wisdom instead sees through that naming to what is actually there — so that a practitioner's task is threefold: to recognize the imagined characteristic for what it is, to sever it, and to realize the perfected characteristic waiting underneath the very same dependent phenomena all along.

12. Section: The Imagined Characteristic — Mistaking a Name for a Real Nature

What is the imagined characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas' intrinsic nature and distinctions are provisionally established through names, on up to causing verbal expression to arise accordingly.

Chinese Source

云何諸法遍計所執相？謂一切法名假安立自性差別，乃至為令隨起言說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the imagined characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas' intrinsic nature and distinctions are provisionally established through names, on up to causing verbal expression to arise accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the imagined characteristic is what an ordinary mind produces the moment it takes the provisional names assigned to things — their supposed intrinsic nature and their distinctions from one another — to be the very things themselves, and then goes on speaking as though this were simply so. He cites the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own gloss: within consciousness alone, with no actual object present at all, something nonetheless appears resembling an object — and it is exactly this resemblance, mistaken for the real thing, that constitutes the imagined characteristic. He reaches for the classic image of someone walking into a dark room and mistaking a coiled rope for a snake: there is no snake there at all, only a rope and a startled mind's own projection, and yet the fear is entirely real while the mistake lasts. Ordinary conceptual life, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is saturated with exactly this kind of mistake — perceiving, reasoning, even studying the Dharma itself all still lean on names, and until the root wisdom free of discrimination is directly realized, a person has no way to step outside naming and see that what is named was never, in itself, some fixed thing existing apart from the mind's own dependent processes. Yet he is careful to add that this does not make ordinary language worthless: names, though provisional, still serve a genuine function, since reasoning carefully through what a name does and does not capture is itself the very method by which true wisdom eventually gets drawn out — and even a noble one who has already gone beyond naming altogether must still return to ordinary words to teach others and lead them toward the very liberation that language, used skillfully, can point beyond itself.

13. Section: The Dependent Characteristic — Because This Exists, That Exists

What is the dependent characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas possess a nature arisen from conditions — that because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises. That is: ignorance conditions volitional formations, and so on, up through the gathering together of this whole mass of pure suffering.

Chinese Source

云何諸法依他起相？謂一切法緣生自性，則此有故彼有，此生故彼生。謂無明緣行，乃至招集純大苦蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the dependent characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas possess a nature arisen from conditions — that because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises. That is: ignorance conditions volitional formations, and so on, up through the gathering together of this whole mass of pure suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "the other" a dependent thing depends on means, in full, four kinds of condition: the causal condition proper, the most direct and internal cause, as a seed is to its sprout; the condition of equal, uninterrupted succession, by which one moment of mind clears the way for the very next; the condition of the object taken, whatever a mind or mental factor actually cognizes; and the contributing condition, everything else that helps or at least does not obstruct an event from occurring — physical dharmas needing only the causal and contributing conditions, mental dharmas needing all four together. Nothing dependently arisen, he stresses, ever stands on its own; each thing is only ever "this" giving rise to "that," most concretely traced through the twelve links from ignorance conditioning volitional formations all the way to the whole mass of suffering these formations bring about. He cites the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own threefold account: the dependent characteristic's cause is the store consciousness, which supplies its seeds; its own nature is nothing but false discrimination itself; and its distinguishing feature is eleven kinds of consciousness this false discrimination manifests as — the body, the one who has a body, the experiencer, what is experienced, the one who experiences it, the sense of time, of number, of place, of language, of the difference between self and other, and of dying into a fortunate or unfortunate rebirth — all of them nothing more than consciousness, displaying no real object at all beneath what they seem to present. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this same principle of "because this exists, that exists" quietly demolishes four mistaken outsider views at once: it refutes the claim that conditioned things arise from no cause at all, since here every "that" plainly depends on a "this"; it refutes the claim that they arise from some single, eternal, all-powerful cause, since an eternal cause could never itself newly arise; it refutes the claim that ignorance and its effects are somehow separate, unrelated events merely occurring side by side, since nothing here stands apart from what conditions it; and it refutes the claim that a cause has no real power to produce its effect, since the very existence of "that" is precisely what "this" is shown to accomplish.
