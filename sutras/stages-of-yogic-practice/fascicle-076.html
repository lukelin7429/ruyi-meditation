Fascicle 76 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十六 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

The Secret Meaning of Mind, Intellect, and Consciousness

This is the fifth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the ultimate truth's five characteristics closed in the previous fascicle, the Chapter on Excellent Qualities' citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra turns to its second, third, and fourth of seven topics in turn: the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness — the ālaya and ādāna consciousness behind a being's turning through birth and death; the characteristic of all dharmas — the imagined, dependent, and perfected natures, illustrated by blurred vision and a crystal gem; and the characteristic of dharmas' lack of any nature of their own — the three kinds of lacking a nature, their causal and liberating chains, the five lineages, and the four ways different listeners receive the teaching that all dharmas are without nature. It closes with Paramārthasamudgata's own demonstration of the three natures across every category of dharma, his four similes for the teaching's reach, and the Buddha's own account of the three turnings of the wheel of the Dharma — the four-truths teaching at the Deer Park, the hidden-aspect teaching of emptiness across the Prajñā period, and this revealed, definitive teaching now being given to every vehicle at once — exactly where the source lecture-transcript comes to its own close.

1. Section: Guanghui Asks — What Makes a Bodhisattva Skillful in the Secret Meaning of Mind

This is the fifth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground works through fifteen chapters in all; the eleventh of these, on excellent qualities, is by far the longest, running from this fascicle back through the previous one and onward through most of several more still to come. Its direct, extended determination falls into two parts — working through essential meanings, already given in the previous fascicle, and citing the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra directly, which unfolds across seven topics of its own; the ultimate truth's characteristics, the first of these seven, closed at the very end of the previous fascicle, and this one takes up the second: the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness. Further, the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra itself describes: the bodhisattva Guanghui asked the Buddha, saying: As the World-Honored One has said, there are bodhisattvas skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. To what degree, exactly, is a bodhisattva called skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness? By what standard does the Tathāgata designate a bodhisattva as such?

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中菩薩地之五。復次，心、意、識相，當知如解深密經中，廣慧菩薩請問佛言：如世尊說：於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩。於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩者，齊何名為於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩？如來齊何施設彼為於心、意、識祕密善巧菩薩？

Ru-Yi Translation

The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, part five. Further, the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra itself describes: the bodhisattva Guanghui asked the Buddha, saying: As the World-Honored One has said, there are bodhisattvas skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. To what degree, exactly, is a bodhisattva called skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness? By what standard does the Tathāgata designate a bodhisattva as such?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first places this fascicle within the treatise's larger plan: the Chapter on Excellent Qualities determines fifteen chapters' worth of the Bodhisattva Ground's own material, running all the way from this fascicle through most of the next several, divided first into a direct, extended determination — itself split between essential meanings, already covered, and this direct citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra across seven further topics — and only later into a briefer sorting of world-systems. Of the Saṃdhinirmocana's seven topics, the characteristic of the ultimate truth closed the previous fascicle; this one opens the second, on mind, intellect, and consciousness, since a being's turning through birth and death is driven by exactly these three, and only by understanding them clearly can that turning be reversed toward liberation — the ultimate truth showed what reality itself is, and this chapter now turns to show what the mind that must realize it actually is. He introduces the bodhisattva Guanghui, "Vast Wisdom," as named for a wisdom so vast, deep, and consummately skillful that it takes in countless teachings both of the world and beyond it, and explains that "mind," "intellect," and "consciousness" can be read either as three names for one and the same thing, as the āgamas often use them interchangeably, or as three distinct terms: mind (citta) as the store consciousness in which the seeds of every defiled and pure dharma gather and from which they arise; intellect (manas) as the seventh consciousness, manas, which forever clings to the store consciousness as an inner self; and consciousness (vijñāna) as the six consciousnesses' own function of discerning their objects through the six sense faculties. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this chapter's real weight falls on explaining the store consciousness, also called the appropriating consciousness, and that his own exposition leans chiefly on Master Woncheuk's eight-consciousness reading, supplemented by Venerable Yanpei's seven-consciousness account, which treats the store consciousness as purely a repository of seeds and the appropriating consciousness as simply another name for the seventh consciousness itself.

2. Section: The Buddha's Praise, and the Consciousness That Matures at Conception

Having said this, the Blessed One then said to the bodhisattva Guanghui: Excellent, excellent, Guanghui! You are now able to ask the Tathāgata a meaning this profound, wishing to benefit and bring ease to countless beings, having compassion on the world and on the gods, humans, asuras, and the rest, so that they may attain benefit and ease — it is for this that you have raised this question. Listen carefully, and I shall explain to you the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. Guanghui, know this: among the various beings turning through birth and death in the six destinies, born into one or another class of being, whether by egg, by womb, by moisture, or by transformation, arising as a particular kind of body — within this, it is the all-seed consciousness that first matures, coming together and growing, again and again, into fullness.

Chinese Source

說是語已，爾時世尊告廣慧菩薩曰：善哉！善哉！廣慧！汝今乃能請問如來如是深義，汝今為欲利益安樂無量眾生，哀愍世間及諸天人、阿素洛等，為令獲得義利安樂，故發斯問。汝應諦聽！吾當為汝說心、意、識祕密義。廣慧當知！於六趣生死彼彼有情，墮彼彼有情眾中，或在卵生、或在胎生、或在濕生、或在化生身分生起，於中最初一切種子心識成熟，展轉和合增長廣大。依二執受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having said this, the Blessed One then said to the bodhisattva Guanghui: Excellent, excellent, Guanghui! You are now able to ask the Tathāgata a meaning this profound, wishing to benefit and bring ease to countless beings, having compassion on the world and on the gods, humans, asuras, and the rest, so that they may attain benefit and ease — it is for this that you have raised this question. Listen carefully, and I shall explain to you the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness. Guanghui, know this: among the various beings turning through birth and death in the six destinies, born into one or another class of being, whether by egg, by womb, by moisture, or by transformation, arising as a particular kind of body — within this, it is the all-seed consciousness that first matures, coming together and growing, again and again, into fullness. It rests on two kinds of appropriation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the Buddha's praise is unusually warm here, since a question this profound, properly answered, benefits not only the questioner but every being still circling through the six destinies out of compassion for whom Guanghui asked at all. He then turns to the substance: whether a being is born from an egg, a womb, moisture, or spontaneously — the last typical of gods, ghosts, and hell-beings, arising all at once with no visible cause — what actually counts as the moment of birth, even for a womb-born being, is not emergence from the mother but the very first instant a stream of consciousness enters the womb; this is what the text calls the all-seed consciousness reaching maturity, the eighth consciousness under one of its several names, so called because it holds within it the seeds of every defiled and pure dharma a being has ever produced. From that first instant, this consciousness and the fertilized union of the parents' contribution — itself only an external condition, not the life itself — come together and gradually develop: consciousness conditions name-and-form, and name-and-form in turn grows into the six sense faculties over the following weeks, exactly as an earlier fascicle describes the gradual completion of a fetal being's support. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that this whole process of maturing, uniting, and growing to fullness has the all-seed consciousness as its center — it is, as a well-known verse puts it, the very consciousness that "comes first and leaves last" in a being's round of birth and death — though other supporting conditions are needed as well. This consciousness, he adds, rests on two kinds of appropriation, taking hold of and sustaining both the physical body and its own seeds — a topic the passage takes up next.

3. Section: Two Kinds of Appropriation — the Body, and the Traces of Language Itself

The first is the appropriation of the material sense faculties together with what they depend on; the second is the appropriation of the habitual traces of characteristics, names, discrimination, verbal expression, and conceptual proliferation. In the realm of form, both kinds of appropriation are present in full; in the formless realm, both are not present.

Chinese Source

一者、有色諸根及所依執受，二者、相、名、分別言說戲論習氣執受。有色界中具二執受，無色界中不具二種。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first is the appropriation of the material sense faculties together with what they depend on; the second is the appropriation of the habitual traces of characteristics, names, discrimination, verbal expression, and conceptual proliferation. In the realm of form, both kinds of appropriation are present in full; in the formless realm, both are not present.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the first appropriation as the all-seed consciousness taking hold of the five pure sense faculties together with the gross physical organs they depend on, sustaining them so they neither decay nor fall apart — a straightforwardly physical kind of appropriation, present for any being possessing a body of form. The second is subtler: "habitual traces" is simply another name for seeds, named this way from the angle of how they were laid down through habituation rather than the angle of their power to give rise to future events, and what gets habituated here is the entire apparatus of ordinary conceptual experience — the seven consciousnesses' own way of presenting an object, and the sixth consciousness's further layer of naming and verbal expression built on top of it — the two together depositing, moment by moment, the very seeds that will later ripen into another round of the world of beings and the receptacle world surrounding them, which in turn gets carved up by name and characteristic all over again, entrenching the cycle further. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that a being in the realm of form possesses both appropriations at once — a physical body sustained by the first, and the entire apparatus of conceptual habituation sustained by the second — whereas a being in the formless realm, having no physical body at all, is sustained by the second appropriation alone, since even the most rarefied meditative absorption is not free of subtle conceptual activity until liberation itself is reached.

4. Section: Two More Names — the Appropriating Consciousness, and the Store Consciousness

Further, Guanghui! This consciousness is also called the appropriating consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness follows after the body and holds it fast. It is also called the store consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness takes hold of the body and lies hidden within it, sharing the very same safety or danger the body itself undergoes.

Chinese Source

復次，廣慧！此識亦名阿陀那識。何以故？由此識於身隨逐執持故。亦名阿賴耶識。何以故？由此識於身攝受藏隱同安危義故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, Guanghui! This consciousness is also called the appropriating consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness follows after the body and holds it fast. It is also called the store consciousness. Why is this so? Because this consciousness takes hold of the body and lies hidden within it, sharing the very same safety or danger the body itself undergoes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains "appropriating consciousness" through three functions the Consciousness-Only tradition attributes to it: holding the seeds of every dharma, taking hold of the sense faculties and what they depend on, and taking hold of the very continuity of rebirth itself — this eighth consciousness follows a being's body from the moment of conception until death without ever once letting go, which is exactly what earns it this name. "Store consciousness" he glosses through the sense of depending, dwelling, and clinging that its name most literally carries: this consciousness sustains the body, keeping it from decaying, while simultaneously hiding within that very body as its concealed abode, so that body and consciousness share one and the same fate — an earlier fascicle's own account of a being's first embryonic stage makes this vivid, describing how mind and body are mutually dependent from that very first moment, the fertilized union remaining intact only so long as mind sustains it, and mind losing its own hiding place the instant that union is damaged. Master Cháng Bǎi calls this consciousness's activity extremely subtle and hard to know directly — only the root wisdom free of discrimination can actually penetrate it — which is precisely why an ordinary being's coarse, scattered mind has no way of grasping, moment to moment, that this is the very consciousness carrying it from one life into the next.

5. Section: A Fourth Name — Mind, Accumulated and Nurtured by Its Own Objects

It is also called mind. Why is this so? Because this consciousness is accumulated and nurtured by form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the rest.

Chinese Source

亦名為心。何以故？由此識色聲香味觸等積集、滋長故。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is also called mind. Why is this so? Because this consciousness is accumulated and nurtured by form, sound, smell, taste, touch, and the rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "mind" here renders the Sanskrit citta, carrying the sense of gathering and arising: this consciousness is not itself an accumulation, yet every experience of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch that the six active consciousnesses take in leaves behind a seed deposited within it, so that it grows and accumulates precisely by being nurtured through the objects the other consciousnesses encounter — and, resting on that accumulation, it goes on to give rise to further experience in turn, name and form each conditioning the other endlessly. With this fourth name given, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, the treatise has now covered every name the eighth consciousness carries in this opening account: called the all-seed consciousness from the standpoint of its maturing at conception, the appropriating consciousness from the standpoint of holding the body fast, the store consciousness from the standpoint of hiding within the body and sharing its fate, and mind from the standpoint of being built up through everything the senses gather in. He adds that a later treatise on Consciousness-Only teaching expands this same set to seven names in all, adding to these four the further titles of "that on which all is known to depend," since it grounds every wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral dharma; "seed consciousness," since it universally sustains the seeds of both mundane and supramundane dharmas; "ripened consciousness," since it carries a being through the ripening fruits of past karma across the three realms, a name that drops away once Buddhahood is reached; and "consciousness free of taint," reserved for a Buddha alone, once every trace of defiled seed has been left behind for good — and Master Cháng Bǎi notes one further point of scholarly disagreement worth flagging before the passage continues: some readers of the Saṃdhinirmocana take the all-seed consciousness as the passage's true center, with the other names merely its aliases, while others — pointing to how the text singles out the appropriating consciousness alone as what a later verse calls "profound and subtle, its seeds flowing like a torrent" — take the appropriating consciousness itself as the center, with the broader account of the all-seed consciousness serving only to lead the discussion toward it gradually.

6. Section: The Six Consciousnesses Turn — Resting on the Appropriating Consciousness

Further, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, and established by it, the body of six consciousnesses turns — namely, eye-consciousness, and ear-, nose-, tongue-, body-, and mind-consciousness. Among these, there is the case where eye-consciousness arises conditioned by the eye and by form; and, arising together with and following alongside that eye-consciousness, at the same time and taking the same object, discriminating mind-consciousness turns. There is likewise the case where ear-, nose-, tongue-, and body-consciousness arise conditioned by the ear, nose, tongue, and body together with sound, smell, taste, and touch; and, arising together with and following alongside these, at the same time and taking the same object, discriminating mind-consciousness turns. Guanghui! If, at some time, only a single eye-consciousness turns, then at that very time only a single discriminating mind-consciousness turns together with that eye-consciousness, taking the same object. And if, at some time, two, three, four, or all five bodies of consciousness turn, then at that very time only a single discriminating mind-consciousness turns together with those five bodies of consciousness, taking the same object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the six active consciousnesses can only ever function at all because the appropriating consciousness holds their seeds and sustains the sense faculties they depend on — were it not for the appropriating consciousness's continuous grip on the body, the six could never arise, exactly as an earlier fascicle puts it: the store consciousness serves the six consciousnesses as their seed and as their support, since it is the seed from which every wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral consciousness springs, and it is what allows the sense faculties themselves to keep functioning at all. He walks through the classic account of what happens the instant eye-consciousness arises: an initial, bare moment of direct awareness is immediately followed by mind-consciousness joining in — first searching, then settling on a judgment, and only then coloring the experience with attachment or aversion — so that mind-consciousness is never simply absent whenever any one of the five sense consciousnesses is active, always arising together with it and taking the very same object. And crucially, no matter how many of the five sense consciousnesses happen to be active at once — seeing, hearing, and touching all at the same moment, say — there is never more than a single mind-consciousness accompanying them, one unified discriminating awareness running alongside however many sense doors happen to be open at once, rather than five separate mental correlates each doing their own thing in isolation.

7. Section: Two Similes — a Rushing Torrent, and a Well-Polished Mirror

Guanghui! It is just as with a great, rushing torrent of water: if the conditions for a single wave to arise are present, only a single wave turns; and if the conditions for two or many waves to arise are present, many waves turn. Yet this very torrent, of its own continuous kind, flows on eternally, without break and without end. And it is just as with a well-polished mirror's surface: if the conditions for a single reflection to arise are present, only a single reflection arises; and if the conditions for two or many reflections to arise are present, many reflections arise. Yet it is not that this mirror's surface itself turns into the reflection, nor is there ever any exhaustion of its capacity to be used.

Chinese Source

廣慧！譬如大暴水流，若有一浪生緣現前，唯一浪轉；若二、若多浪生緣現前，有多浪轉。然此暴水自類恆流，無斷無盡。又如善淨鏡面，若有一影生緣現前，唯一影起；若二、若多影生緣現前，有多影起。非此鏡面轉變為影，亦無受用滅盡可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Guanghui! It is just as with a great, rushing torrent of water: if the conditions for a single wave to arise are present, only a single wave turns; and if the conditions for two or many waves to arise are present, many waves turn. Yet this very torrent, of its own continuous kind, flows on eternally, without break and without end. And it is just as with a well-polished mirror's surface: if the conditions for a single reflection to arise are present, only a single reflection arises; and if the conditions for two or many reflections to arise are present, many reflections arise. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the torrent as illustrating exactly what makes the appropriating consciousness different in kind from the six active consciousnesses: a single gust of wind raises a single wave, a stronger gust raises many, yet the torrent itself, the water flowing beneath every wave, never stops flowing regardless — where the six consciousnesses switch on and off from moment to moment, tasting only when there is something to taste, hearing only when there is something to hear, even falling silent altogether in dreamless sleep, the appropriating consciousness carried forward by past karma never once pauses, flowing on through every waking and sleeping moment and on into the next life besides, ceasing only when the very causes and conditions sustaining rebirth are finally exhausted at final nirvāṇa. He quotes a related scripture's own image of the ocean struck by wind, giving rise to wave after wave without any gap between them, and applies it directly: sense objects strike the store consciousness the way wind strikes the sea, and seeds ripening into actual experience are the resulting waves — no wind, no waves, but the ocean itself is there regardless, exactly as the appropriating consciousness underlies the six consciousnesses' intermittent activity without ever itself being interrupted. The mirror simile makes a second, related point: one face reflected, or many, the mirror's own surface is never actually transformed into any of those reflected images, and using it to reflect one guest after another never wears the mirror down or exhausts what it can do — so too, the six active consciousnesses arise and vanish depending on conditions, but the appropriating consciousness holding their seeds is never itself used up by any of it, continuing unbroken from the very seeds of ordinary conceptual habituation that keep the whole cycle of birth and death going.

8. Section: The Similes Applied — and Why Conceptual Knowledge Alone Is Not Yet Full Skill

Just so, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, which is like this rushing torrent, and established by it, if, at some time, the conditions for a single eye-consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time a single eye-consciousness turns; and if, at some time, the conditions for all five bodies of consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time all five bodies of consciousness turn. Guanghui! Although such a bodhisattva, resting on the wisdom that knows how things stand, and established by it, has become skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, the Tathāgatas do not, on these grounds alone, designate that bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Chinese Source

如是廣慧！由似暴流阿陀那識為依止、為建立故，若於爾時有一眼識生緣現前，即於此時一眼識轉；若於爾時乃至有五識身生緣現前，即於此時五識身轉。廣慧！如是菩薩雖由法住智為依止、為建立故，於心、意、識祕密善巧；然諸如來不齊於此施設彼為於心、意、識一切祕密善巧菩薩。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just so, Guanghui! Resting on the appropriating consciousness, which is like this rushing torrent, and established by it, if, at some time, the conditions for a single eye-consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time a single eye-consciousness turns; and if, at some time, the conditions for all five bodies of consciousness to arise are present, then at that very time all five bodies of consciousness turn. Guanghui! Although such a bodhisattva, resting on the wisdom that knows how things stand, and established by it, has become skillful in the secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, the Tathāgatas do not, on these grounds alone, designate that bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the sūtra's own text passes over the seventh consciousness, manas, without naming it directly here, though a related passage elsewhere spells out why it must still be implied: the store consciousness serves the six active consciousnesses as their seed and their support, and it is likewise the store consciousness's own presence that makes manas possible, and manas in turn that makes mind-consciousness itself able to function — much as the five sense faculties make the five sense consciousnesses possible. He notes this is exactly where later commentators divide: some read the whole passage through a seven-consciousness lens, treating the appropriating consciousness as simply another name for manas; others, following Master Woncheuk, read it through an eight-consciousness lens, taking the silence about manas here as simple economy of expression rather than any denial of its role. Turning to what merely conceptual understanding still lacks, Master Cháng Bǎi explains "the wisdom that knows how things stand" as the correct, non-inverted grasp of what the Buddha has taught — knowing, for instance, that suffering truly follows from its origin, without yet having directly realized this through meditation — a wisdom fully available even to an ordinary being who has simply studied and reflected well, whether tainted or untainted depending on whether the path has yet been reached. A bodhisattva who has mastered this conceptual understanding of mind, intellect, and consciousness has genuinely accomplished something, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, but the Buddha is about to draw a sharper line: real, complete skill in this secret meaning requires something this conceptual mastery alone does not yet provide.

9. Section: True Ultimate Skill — Seeing None of the Eighteen at All

Guanghui! If bodhisattvas, each individually within themselves, truly do not see the appropriating consciousness, do not see it as a distinct entity, do not see the store, do not see it as a distinct entity, do not see accumulation, do not see mind, do not see the eye and form and eye-consciousness, do not see the ear and sound and ear-consciousness, do not see the nose and smell and nose-consciousness, do not see the tongue and taste and tongue-consciousness, do not see the body and touch and body-consciousness, and do not see the mind and mental objects and mind-consciousness — such bodhisattvas are called skillful in the ultimate, and the Tathāgata designates them as skillful in the ultimate. Guanghui! It is precisely to this degree that a bodhisattva is called skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness, and it is precisely to this degree that the Tathāgata designates a bodhisattva as skillful in the entire secret meaning of mind, intellect, and consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that once a bodhisattva actually enters the root wisdom free of discrimination, both what is grasped and the mind grasping it become unattainable — the six sense faculties, their six objects, the six resulting consciousnesses, language itself, and the receptacle world all cease to appear — so that a mind resting in that direct realization no longer differentiates "this is the appropriating consciousness's function" from "this is the store consciousness's own nature," any more than it separates out the eye from form from eye-consciousness, or the mind from mental objects from mind-consciousness. This is not forgetting or ignorance, he stresses, but the exact opposite: a Chan teacher's image of space itself shattering and the ground beneath one's feet dropping away, the bottom of the lacquer bucket finally breaking through — every one of the eighteen elements, every trace of the tainted dependent nature built out of ordinary naming, simply fails to arise, because this consciousness no longer generates concepts through clinging to language the way an ordinary mind involuntarily does at the mere mention of a plum or a slice of cake. Only at this point, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, does the Buddha's designation of "skillful in the entire secret meaning" actually apply — the passage's closing line uses "to this degree" twice, once marking the bodhisattva's own direct attainment and once marking the Buddha's own act of naming it so in words for others to understand. With this, the sūtra's direct exposition of the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness comes to its full close, and what remains is for the Buddha to restate the whole teaching once more in verse.

10. Section: The Famous Verse — Why the Buddha Kept This Teaching Secret

At that time, the Blessed One, wishing to proclaim this meaning once more, spoke a verse: The appropriating consciousness is profound and subtle, all its seeds flowing like a torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate it and cling to it as a self.

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the Blessed One, wishing to proclaim this meaning once more, spoke a verse: The appropriating consciousness is profound and subtle, all its seeds flowing like a torrent; I do not reveal it to the ordinary and the foolish, fearing they would discriminate it and cling to it as a self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi calls this one of the most quoted verses in the whole Consciousness-Only tradition, cited by both an earlier fascicle of this very treatise and by the Cheng Weishi Lun to settle the question of why this teaching is not found spelled out in the earlier discourses. "Profound" he reads as directed at the merely clever: even a sharp, unschooled mind gets nowhere trying to reason its way to what the appropriating consciousness actually is, since it lies beneath the reach of ordinary reflection entirely. "Subtle" he reads as directed specifically at the śrāvaka: even a diligent disciple of the lesser vehicle, whose entire training is built around cutting off affliction through the sixth consciousness's own contemplation of no-self, has no particular need to trace the teaching all the way down to its subtlest root, and so may never come to understand the appropriating consciousness even after real progress on the path. The second line describes what it holds: countless seeds of affliction and of past deeds, and, in some beings, seeds of purity and of the noble path as well, all carried forward in unbroken succession the way a torrent's water never stops flowing, each seed ripening into experience and each experience depositing new seeds in turn, on and on without any beginning that can be found. And the final couplet gives the reason for the silence: the Buddha did teach on this very territory even in the earlier discourses, but centered there on the six active consciousnesses and their own impermanence and selflessness, since his audience then was chiefly ordinary beings and disciples aiming at quick liberation through the four noble truths and the contemplation of no-self — teaching them the profound and subtle appropriating consciousness risked nothing less than having them seize on it as a hidden, permanent self, the very clinging the whole teaching exists to dismantle. Only for bodhisattvas, who must eliminate not only affliction but the obstruction to all that can be known, and who must master every dharma without exception on the way to full awakening, does the Buddha finally teach this profound and subtle consciousness in the Great Vehicle's own scriptures. With this verse, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, the treatise's determination of the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness — the second of the Saṃdhinirmocana's seven topics under the Chapter on Excellent Qualities — closes completely, and what follows next is the third topic: the characteristics of all dharmas, the three natures.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi introduces Deben, "Root of Virtue," as named either for having planted deep roots of merit and wisdom, or for a virtue so abundant it forms a whole forest others can shelter under, and notes his question mirrors Guanghui's exactly — asking both to what degree a bodhisattva has genuinely attained skill and by what standard the Buddha names it so. He locates this third topic within the treatise's larger scheme: where an earlier fascicle divides all dharmas into as many as six hundred and sixty categories, and a later treatise reduces the same territory to a hundred, the Saṃdhinirmocana condenses everything — every conditioned and unconditioned dharma alike — into just three characteristics. The imagined characteristic, he explains in brief, is the character ordinary beings project onto things through the sheer habit of naming, taking language's own constructions to be the real nature of what is named; the dependent characteristic is how things actually arise, each conditioned by causes it depends on rather than standing on its own; and the perfected characteristic is reached by stripping the imagined characteristic away from the dependent, revealing the true suchness that was there all along. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that these three are neither simply identical nor simply separate from one another — the dependent characteristic sits at the center of the whole scheme, becoming the imagined characteristic the moment an ordinary mind takes its own naming to be real, and becoming the perfected characteristic the moment wisdom instead sees through that naming to what is actually there — so that a practitioner's task is threefold: to recognize the imagined characteristic for what it is, to sever it, and to realize the perfected characteristic waiting underneath the very same dependent phenomena all along.

12. Section: The Imagined Characteristic — Mistaking a Name for a Real Nature

What is the imagined characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas' intrinsic nature and distinctions are provisionally established through names, on up to causing verbal expression to arise accordingly.

Chinese Source

云何諸法遍計所執相？謂一切法名假安立自性差別，乃至為令隨起言說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the imagined characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas' intrinsic nature and distinctions are provisionally established through names, on up to causing verbal expression to arise accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the imagined characteristic is what an ordinary mind produces the moment it takes the provisional names assigned to things — their supposed intrinsic nature and their distinctions from one another — to be the very things themselves, and then goes on speaking as though this were simply so. He cites the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own gloss: within consciousness alone, with no actual object present at all, something nonetheless appears resembling an object — and it is exactly this resemblance, mistaken for the real thing, that constitutes the imagined characteristic. He reaches for the classic image of someone walking into a dark room and mistaking a coiled rope for a snake: there is no snake there at all, only a rope and a startled mind's own projection, and yet the fear is entirely real while the mistake lasts. Ordinary conceptual life, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is saturated with exactly this kind of mistake — perceiving, reasoning, even studying the Dharma itself all still lean on names, and until the root wisdom free of discrimination is directly realized, a person has no way to step outside naming and see that what is named was never, in itself, some fixed thing existing apart from the mind's own dependent processes. Yet he is careful to add that this does not make ordinary language worthless: names, though provisional, still serve a genuine function, since reasoning carefully through what a name does and does not capture is itself the very method by which true wisdom eventually gets drawn out — and even a noble one who has already gone beyond naming altogether must still return to ordinary words to teach others and lead them toward the very liberation that language, used skillfully, can point beyond itself.

13. Section: The Dependent Characteristic — Because This Exists, That Exists

What is the dependent characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas possess a nature arisen from conditions — that because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises. That is: ignorance conditions volitional formations, and so on, up through the gathering together of this whole mass of pure suffering.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the dependent characteristic of dharmas? It is that all dharmas possess a nature arisen from conditions — that because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises. That is: ignorance conditions volitional formations, and so on, up through the gathering together of this whole mass of pure suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that "the other" a dependent thing depends on means, in full, four kinds of condition: the causal condition proper, the most direct and internal cause, as a seed is to its sprout; the condition of equal, uninterrupted succession, by which one moment of mind clears the way for the very next; the condition of the object taken, whatever a mind or mental factor actually cognizes; and the contributing condition, everything else that helps or at least does not obstruct an event from occurring — physical dharmas needing only the causal and contributing conditions, mental dharmas needing all four together. Nothing dependently arisen, he stresses, ever stands on its own; each thing is only ever "this" giving rise to "that," most concretely traced through the twelve links from ignorance conditioning volitional formations all the way to the whole mass of suffering these formations bring about. He cites the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own threefold account: the dependent characteristic's cause is the store consciousness, which supplies its seeds; its own nature is nothing but false discrimination itself; and its distinguishing feature is eleven kinds of consciousness this false discrimination manifests as — the body, the one who has a body, the experiencer, what is experienced, the one who experiences it, the sense of time, of number, of place, of language, of the difference between self and other, and of dying into a fortunate or unfortunate rebirth — all of them nothing more than consciousness, displaying no real object at all beneath what they seem to present. Master Cháng Bǎi notes this same principle of "because this exists, that exists" quietly demolishes four mistaken outsider views at once: it refutes the claim that conditioned things arise from no cause at all, since here every "that" plainly depends on a "this"; it refutes the claim that they arise from some single, eternal, all-powerful cause, since an eternal cause could never itself newly arise; it refutes the claim that ignorance and its effects are somehow separate, unrelated events merely occurring side by side, since nothing here stands apart from what conditions it; and it refutes the claim that a cause has no real power to produce its effect, since the very existence of "that" is precisely what "this" is shown to accomplish.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi parses "true suchness" itself: dharmas' shared, selfless nature is called "equal," since it shows no distinction from one instance to the next; "true" marks it off from anything tainted, and from the imagined characteristic's own empty pretense; "suchness" marks it off from the dependent characteristic's own arising and ceasing, since this nature never varies across past, present, or future. He notes the treatise elsewhere counts as many as seven kinds of true suchness — of flux, of characteristics, of mere cognition, of what is provisionally established, of wrongdoing, of purification, and of correct practice — or, by a later treatise's fuller count, as many as ten, one growing clearer at each of the ten grounds. He explains the perfected characteristic itself comes in two related senses: an intrinsic perfected nature, present in tainted dharmas as their own real nature and sometimes called the "stained" true suchness, since it is there all along even before defilement is cleared away; and a purified perfected nature, present once that defilement has actually been removed and called the "unstained" true suchness — the same underlying reality running through both defiled and pure dharmas alike, differing only in whether the obscuring imagined characteristic has yet been stripped from it. He adds a further, simpler division from a related treatise: an unconditioned perfected nature, comprising true suchness and nirvāṇa themselves, changeless by definition; and a conditioned perfected nature, comprising the entire noble path, called perfected because it never departs from what it takes as its object. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses what the passage itself makes explicit: this perfected nature is never simply handed over — a bodhisattva reaches it only through bold diligence and undistorted reflection sustained as genuine causes, penetrating it first at the very first ground and then, ground by ground, gradually perfecting that same realization all the way to unsurpassed, complete awakening, where alone it is finally brought to full completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through several older commentators' readings of why the affliction itself, rather than the phantom hairs and wheels it produces, stands for the imagined characteristic. One reading holds that the affliction is what deposits the habitual seed of clinging in the mind — the way a diseased eye leaves behind the very tendency to keep misperceiving — so that the affliction represents the imagined characteristic precisely as a cause, while the phantom shapes it produces are its dependent fruit; a second holds that the affliction is simply the settled conviction that the phantom hairs are truly real, that very conviction being the imagined characteristic, while the phantom hairs themselves, arisen from a genuine cause however diseased, are already the dependent characteristic. Whichever reading is followed, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, the underlying point survives: the imagined characteristic and the dependent characteristic are as inseparable as the disease and its symptoms — wherever clinging occurs, some dependent appearance is being misread, and wherever a dependent appearance draws clinging, the imagined characteristic is right there riding along with it, the two arising and ceasing together until wisdom finally intervenes. The clear-eyed person's simile, by contrast, needs no such untangling: once the affliction itself is gone, the eye simply takes in space as it actually is, with no phantom hairs superimposed on it at all — this unclouded seeing is exactly what the perfected characteristic is, not some third, separate object waiting elsewhere, but simply the same field of experience once the imagined characteristic has been stripped away from what was dependently arising all along.

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that all four colors repeat the identical structure precisely so that no single case could be mistaken for a special exception — the crystal gem's own colorless purity never changes throughout, only the mistaken names an onlooker keeps hanging on it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the mapping out point by point. The crystal gem's own nature, clear and colorless beneath whatever borrowed hue happens to be showing, is the dependent characteristic itself — genuinely there, genuinely arisen from real conditions, yet never, in itself, blue or red or golden at all. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this parable makes vivid what the earlier blurred-vision simile stated more abstractly: the dependent characteristic is never itself false, only ever misread; the imagined characteristic is the misreading; and the perfected characteristic is nothing more mysterious than the dependent characteristic correctly seen, exactly as clear crystal was there the entire time beneath every color a confused eye mistook it for.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the apparent collision this question puts its finger on: one whole body of teaching describes dharmas as genuinely arising, genuinely ceasing, each with its own real characteristic to be known and cultivated; another body of teaching, spoken at another time, declares all dharmas without any nature whatsoever, unborn, unceasing, still and empty from the very start. Master Cháng Bǎi adds a further reason rooted in how śrāvakas and bodhisattvas each realize emptiness differently: a śrāvaka realizes only the emptiness of the person while still treating dharmas themselves as ultimately real, and so remains afraid of birth and death and eager to escape into nirvāṇa; a bodhisattva realizes the emptiness of both person and dharmas together, sees birth and death and nirvāṇa as, at bottom, not two separate things, and so neither fears the one nor clings to the other, remaining able to labor for countless ages on behalf of beings without ever needing to withdraw. It is precisely this deeper, twofold emptiness — and the real danger of mistaking it for nihilism — that makes Paramārthasamudgata's question so urgent, and it is this question the Buddha now turns to answer in full.

Master Cháng Bǎi announces this as the turning point of the whole chapter: the Buddha's sweeping statement that "all dharmas are without any nature of their own" was never a blanket denial of everything, but a precise, threefold teaching mapped directly onto the three natures already established — the imagined characteristic answering to the lack of nature belonging to characteristics, the dependent characteristic to the lack of nature belonging to arising, and the perfected characteristic to the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate. He explains the first with the Compendium of the Great Vehicle's own account of how the imagined characteristic comes about: it is the sixth consciousness — on one classical reading, the sixth and seventh together, or, on another, all eight — that does the actual imagining, projecting a supposedly real nature onto the dependent characteristic through the sheer force of habituated naming, so that the dependent characteristic becomes "what is imagined" and this projected nature becomes "the imagined characteristic" proper. Since this imagined nature is established only by a name assigned to it, never by any real characteristic of its own — the way calling five aggregates "a self" never actually produces a self anywhere among, outside, or between those aggregates, however thoroughly one searches — it simply has no nature to speak of at all, and this, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses, is exactly and only what the Buddha meant by teaching that dharmas "lack a nature of their own": not that the dependent and perfected characteristics are equally empty of reality, but that the imagined characteristic specifically was never real to begin with.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi flags this as the point where the whole teaching could most easily be misread, and where a related treatise is unusually careful to head off exactly that misreading: what is denied here is not the dependent characteristic's own existence — every dependently arisen dharma genuinely is established through its own intrinsic characteristic, arising from real causes and conditions — what is denied is only a very specific kind of arising, the "arising of its own accord" that would make something spring up with no cause at all, or from some single eternal cause, the way certain outsiders imagined an uncaused universe or an all-powerful creator. Since nothing in the dependent world ever actually arises that way — everything without exception depends on some other condition to come about at all — this particular nature, self-generated arising, is precisely what dharmas lack, and this lack is exactly what "arising without a nature of its own" means. Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that this is a world of difference from saying dependent things do not exist at all: to deny that arising has this kind of self-sufficient nature is not to deny that things arise, any more than denying a rope is a snake denies that the rope is there — this careful, narrow target is what keeps the teaching of emptiness from collapsing into a nihilism that would deny cause and effect altogether, exactly the danger Paramārthasamudgata's opening question was raised to guard against.

It is that, because dharmas lack a nature of their own in the sense of arising, they are said to lack a nature of their own; and, in just this sense, dependently arisen dharmas are also called the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate. Why is this so? Among dharmas, whatever is a pure object I designate as the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate. The dependent characteristic is not a pure object; this is why it, too, is called the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi flags this as a genuinely subtle move: the dependent characteristic has already been assigned its own lack of nature, the lack of nature belonging to arising, yet here it is folded a second time into the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate — not as a contradiction, but because the dependent characteristic itself, on closer inspection, carries two aspects. Considered simply as something arisen from seeds through habituation, it is the lack of nature belonging to arising already explained; but considered as an object — asking whether it is the kind of thing pure, undefiled wisdom actually takes as its object — it falls short, since the dependent characteristic, still entangled with the imagined characteristic's own projections whenever an ordinary mind engages it, is never itself the pure object that only the perfected characteristic can be. By this second, more exacting standard, even the dependent characteristic must be said to lack the nature that would make it "ultimate" in the strict sense — not because it does not exist, but because existing and being a pure object are two different questions, and the dependent characteristic answers only the first. Master Cháng Bǎi cites a related treatise's own account of how one and the same dependent nature can be described three ways depending on the angle taken — as dependent, when considered simply as arisen from habituated seeds; as imagined, when it becomes what an ordinary mind's projection actually fastens onto; and as perfected, when its own imagined overlay is finally seen through — and notes this is exactly why a bodhisattva who has reached the sixth ground can, for the first time, hold the dependent nature's illusion-like appearing and its underlying emptiness in view together, rather than having to alternate between seeing one and losing sight of the other.

27. Section: The Third Named, Part Two — the Perfected Nature Itself

The selflessness of dharmas in all dharmas is called the ultimate, and can also be called the lack of nature of one's own — for this is dharmas' own ultimate truth, and it is what the lack of nature of one's own reveals. For this reason, it is called the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the perfected characteristic earns the title "lack of nature belonging to the ultimate" in the fullest, most proper sense — unlike the dependent characteristic's borrowed share of the same title, the perfected characteristic simply is the selflessness of dharmas, dharmas' own ultimate truth, and precisely what the lack of nature of one's own reveals once it is directly realized. He observes a subtlety a careful commentator once raised: why does the sūtra say "ultimate" and then add "lack of nature of one's own," when either term alone would seem to suffice? The answer is that the two mark cause and fruit together — "ultimate" names what the perfected nature is, while "lack of nature of one's own" names why it deserves that name, namely because it is exactly what remains once the lack of nature belonging to characteristics and to arising have both been seen through. With all three lacks of nature now fully named — the imagined characteristic as empty of any nature through mere naming, the dependent characteristic as empty of any nature through self-generated arising, and both the tainted dependent characteristic and the perfected characteristic as, in different ways, the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate — Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that none of this denies the three natures established earlier; it simply names the very same three natures from their negative side, exactly as positively describing a nature and negating what that nature is not are two views of one and the same reality. What remains, he says, is for the Buddha to explain, through three similes, precisely how these three are to be pictured.

28. Section: Three Similes — Flowers in the Sky, an Illusion, and Space Itself

Good son! It is just as with flowers seen in the sky — the lack of nature belonging to characteristics should be known in just the same way. It is just as with a conjured, illusory image — the lack of nature belonging to arising should be known in just the same way, and the partial lack of nature belonging to the ultimate should be known in just the same way. And it is just as with space itself, which is revealed only through the absence of any nature belonging to the many forms within it, pervading every place — the partial lack of nature belonging to the ultimate should be known in just the same way, since it is what the selflessness of dharmas reveals, and since it pervades everywhere.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through all three similes in turn. Flowers seen floating in empty sky are exactly what the imagined characteristic is: there is no flower there at all, only a projection with no substance whatsoever, matching how the lack of nature belonging to characteristics has no real thing behind it in the first place. A conjured, illusory image — a phantom elephant or horse produced by a skilled illusionist — genuinely does appear, arisen from real causes and conditions even though what it appears to be is not really there, matching the dependent characteristic exactly: it lacks a nature of its own arising independently, yet it is not simply nothing, and this same illusion-like appearing, once its dependence on the imagined overlay is recognized, is also why the tainted dependent characteristic earns a partial share of the title "lack of nature belonging to the ultimate." And space itself — never a thing you could point to directly, known only through the absence of any material form obstructing it, yet present absolutely everywhere without exception — is exactly how the perfected characteristic's own share of that same title should be pictured: not a further object added on top of everything else, but the very absence of self and dharmas revealed once every obstruction has been cleared away, pervading every dharma equally the way space pervades every place equally. Master Cháng Bǎi notes these three similes were chosen with real precision — a flower that never existed, an illusion that arises but is not what it seems, and a space that is nowhere localized yet found everywhere — each fitted exactly to the specific kind of "lacking a nature" it illustrates, so that none of the three could be swapped for another without losing what makes it apt.

29. Section: The Three Restated — and Why "Unborn, Unceasing" Rests on the First Alone

Good son! I rest on these three kinds of lack of nature of one's own, and, with this hidden intent, say that all dharmas are entirely without any nature of their own. Paramārthasamudgata! Know that I rest on the lack of nature belonging to characteristics, and, with this hidden intent, say that all dharmas neither arise nor cease, are originally still, and are themselves nirvāṇa by nature. Why is this so? If a dharma's own characteristic is utterly without anything at all, then there is no arising; if there is no arising, then there is no ceasing; if there is neither arising nor ceasing, then it is originally still; and if it is originally still, then it is itself nirvāṇa by nature — for within it there is not the slightest thing that could still be made to enter nirvāṇa. For this reason, I rest on the lack of nature belonging to characteristics, and, with this hidden intent, say that all dharmas neither arise nor cease, are originally still, and are themselves nirvāṇa by nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing restatement — "I rest on these three kinds of lack of nature of one's own" — as formally sealing the whole first stage of the answer: the imagined characteristic maps to the lack of nature belonging to characteristics, the dependent characteristic to the lack of nature belonging to arising, and both the tainted dependent characteristic and the perfected characteristic together to the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate. But this alone does not yet explain the sūtra's original, stronger phrasing — not just "without a nature," but "unborn, unceasing, originally still, nirvāṇa by nature" — and it is exactly this the Buddha now isolates with great precision: this stronger language rests on the lack of nature belonging to characteristics alone, not on the lack of nature belonging to arising. Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the logic holds together so tightly: the imagined characteristic was never a real thing to begin with, so it has no characteristic of its own whatsoever — and what was never a real thing in the first place cannot be said to have arisen, and what never arose cannot be said to cease, and what neither arises nor ceases has, in the fullest sense, always already been still, needing nothing further done to it, since there was never any real defilement there in the first place requiring a nirvāṇa to remove it. He stresses this is a substance-empty emptiness, distinct from the dependent characteristic's own kind of emptiness — the dependent characteristic genuinely does arise and genuinely does cease, moment by moment, and so cannot be described this same way — which is exactly why the sūtra is careful to specify that this strongest language of "unborn and unceasing" answers only to the imagined characteristic's total unreality, not to the dependent characteristic's own real, if conditioned, arising and passing.

30. Section: Unborn, Unceasing, by a Second Route — Resting on the Perfected Nature

Good son! I likewise rest on the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate revealed by the selflessness of dharmas, and, with this hidden intent, say that all dharmas neither arise nor cease, are originally still, and are themselves nirvāṇa by nature. Why is this so? The lack of nature belonging to the ultimate revealed by the selflessness of dharmas — always, at every time, the nature of dharmas abides unconditioned, since it is never joined with any defilement whatsoever; and, always, at every time, because the nature of dharmas abides in this way, it is unconditioned. Because it is unconditioned, there is neither arising nor ceasing; and because it is never joined with any defilement whatsoever, it is originally still, and is itself nirvāṇa by nature. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi shows this second route arriving at the very same destination by a different path than the one just given for the imagined characteristic. There, the reasoning ran through total unreality — what was never a thing at all cannot arise, cannot cease, and so is originally still. Here, the reasoning runs instead through the perfected nature's own unconditioned constancy: dharmas' true nature simply abides, always and at every time, untouched by any defilement whatsoever, and it is exactly this permanent, unconditioned abiding that makes it correct to call this nature, too, unborn, unceasing, originally still, and nirvāṇa by nature — not because it was never real, as with the imagined characteristic, but because what is genuinely real about it never enters the impermanent cycle of arising and ceasing to begin with. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the treatise takes care to give both explanations rather than resting on just one, since a related compendium elsewhere works through all three natures together to reach the same conclusion, while this text specifically isolates the imagined and perfected natures as the two routes that genuinely support this strongest language, leaving the dependent nature's own real, conditioned arising untouched by either explanation. What follows next, he says, is the Buddha's account of why these three teachings of no-nature were established at all — not because any being actually observes three separate natures out in the world, but because of a very specific mistake beings make in relating to just two of them.

31. Section: Why Three Non-Natures at All — Not Observed Separately, but Superimposed

Further, Paramārthasamudgata! Rather, it is because beings superimpose the imagined nature onto the dependent nature and the perfected nature that I establish these three kinds of lack of nature of one's own.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as clarifying what the whole three-natures teaching was ever actually responding to: ordinary beings do not walk around consciously distinguishing an imagined nature from a dependent nature from a perfected nature and mistakenly clinging to each in turn — no being ever "separately observes" the imagined nature as something real, since it was never a real object of observation to begin with. What actually happens is narrower and more specific: beings take the dependent nature — real, arisen from conditions — and superimpose onto it a fixed, independently real nature that was never there, and they do the same to the perfected nature, either by denying it outright or by treating even it as some further graspable thing. It is this single act of superimposition, committed against both the dependent and the perfected natures, that the three teachings of no-nature exist to correct. Master Cháng Bǎi connects this directly to two opposite failures he has already flagged as bracketing the whole discussion: one kind of being, still caught in the earlier teaching of existence, superimposes a fixed self-nature onto the dependent world and so remains bound by fear of birth and death and craving for nirvāṇa; another kind of being, hearing the later teaching of emptiness without fully digesting it, superimposes nonexistence onto the perfected nature itself, denying that any of the three natures are real at all and falling into the very nihilism the earlier chapters on wrongly-grasped emptiness were written to refute. The teaching that follows, he says, is aimed precisely at healing both errors together, and this is exactly the ground the definitive, third stage of the Buddha's teaching stands on.

32. Section: From Naming to Clinging to Rebirth — the Chain That Keeps Saṃsāra Turning

Just as this clinging thus occurs, so too, upon the dependent nature and the perfected nature, they come to cling to the imagined nature. By this causal chain, the dependent nature of a future life is generated. By this same causal chain, they become stained by the defilement of the afflictions, or stained by the defilement of karmic action, or stained by the defilement of rebirth, and for a long time race on and turn through birth and death without any rest — whether in the hells, among animals, among hungry ghosts, among the gods, among the asuras, or among human beings — undergoing every kind of suffering and affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces this as the treatise's own account of exactly how superimposition turns into a full round of rebirth, in four tightening stages. First, clinging to the imagined characteristic makes a being start talking as though the dependent and perfected natures were fixed things — "this is real," "that is empty" — the mere act of speaking already carrying the imagined nature's own assumption inside it. Second, this speech, repeated, deposits habitual seeds in the mind three ways: for those who understand language well, the clinging arises consciously the moment words are used; for those who do not — Master Cháng Bǎi's example is an infant or an animal, unable to speak yet still capable of grasping at things as real — the very same clinging operates as a latent tendency, needing no words at all to take hold. Third, this habituated clinging becomes settled conviction, no longer a passing thought but a standing way of relating to both the dependent and the perfected natures. And fourth, this settled conviction becomes the seed of an actual future rebirth: stained by the afflictions that clinging stirs up, by the karma those afflictions drive a being to create, and by the very rebirth that karma then ripens into, a being races on through the six destinies without rest — high or low, painful or pleasurable, every realm from hell to the god-realms alike still caught in the same wheel, since even the god-realms, free of gross physical pain, remain marked by the coarser suffering of impermanence and the eventual fall back into lower rebirth. Master Cháng Bǎi calls this the "dark," unwholesome side of how the three natures actually operate in an unawakened being's life — cause and effect laid out in full, from a single act of naming all the way to another lifetime's suffering — and notes that the very next passage turns to the opposite, "bright" side: how the very same three teachings, rightly understood, reverse this entire chain instead of feeding it.

Master Cháng Bǎi traces this as the mirror image of the earlier chain into saṃsāra, now running in reverse toward release, in four steps matching the four investigations and four kinds of true wisdom already introduced in an earlier fascicle. First, right belief and understanding: hearing that the imagined nature is the lack of nature belonging to characteristics and that the dependent and perfected natures together compose the lack of nature belonging to the ultimate, a practitioner at the stages of heat and summit carefully discerns name, thing, essential nature, and distinction as nothing but provisional constructs. Second, freedom from clinging: engaging the dependent nature directly without any longer taking the imagined characteristic laid over it as real. Third, the actual elimination of the dependent characteristic's tainted appearing: three wisdoms working together — verbal habit no longer depositing new seeds, no longer being consciously seized on the instant it arises, and no longer lying latent even beneath the surface of awareness — so that whether or not words are actually spoken, whatever appears is recognized as unreal. And fourth, full liberation: the very power of this insight, sustained in present experience, permanently cuts off the cause of any future rebirth, bringing genuine disenchantment, genuine freedom from craving, and complete release from the three defilements of affliction, karmic action, and rebirth alike — the destination a śrāvaka reaches in the unconditioned remainder of nirvāṇa. With this, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, the treatise closes its full account of the "bright," purifying side of how the three natures actually function in practice, completing its explanation of the three lacks of nature in both their causal and their liberating aspects; what follows next turns to how this same single path is shared by all three vehicles, and to a distinction of spiritual capacities among beings that qualifies just how universally it applies.

It is on this basis that I, with hidden intent, say that there is only a single vehicle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "this path, this course" as the very three lacks of nature just explained — every one of the three vehicles, whatever their differing scope of compassion or length of training, must equally see through the imagined nature laid over the dependent, and equally realize the perfected nature underneath it; a śrāvaka clears away clinging to a self, a pratyekabuddha clears away the same clinging through deeper contemplation of dependent origination, and a bodhisattva clears away clinging to both self and dharmas together — but all three walk the identical route of stripping the imagined from the dependent to reach the perfected, differing only in how far and how thoroughly they walk it. This shared route, he explains, is exactly what licenses the Buddha's otherwise startling claim that there is only a "single vehicle" — a teaching Master Cháng Bǎi traces through eight distinct senses drawn from a related compendium: the equality of the reality all three vehicles ultimately realize; the equality of selflessness they all equally attain; the equality of the liberation from affliction they all equally reach; the shared indeterminate lineage that lets even a confirmed śrāvaka eventually turn toward the greater path; the mutual identification of self and other, and of dharma and dharma, that erases any fixed boundary between "small" and "great" vehicles; the Buddha's own past lives as a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha, presented as manifestations of a single underlying reality; and the simple fact that the Buddha's own vehicle is the one truly final destination, beyond which no further vehicle remains. Master Cháng Bǎi is careful to flag, though, that "one vehicle" is itself spoken with hidden intent, not a flat denial that the three vehicles are really distinct — a qualification the very next passage exists to make explicit.

35. Section: Five Vehicles, Not One — the Śrāvaka Who Can Never Sit Beneath the Bodhi Tree

It is not that, throughout the entire realm of beings, there are no various kinds of lineage among beings — no distinction of dull faculties, middling faculties, or sharp faculties. Good sons! Why is this? Because, from the outset, they possess only an inferior lineage; because their compassion is, without exception, meager; and because they are, without exception, terrified of every kind of suffering. Because their compassion is meager without exception, they turn their backs, without exception, on the work of benefiting other beings; because they are terrified of suffering without exception, they turn their backs, without exception, on undertaking the practices that must be carried out. Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as the qualification the "single vehicle" teaching of the previous section was always waiting for: that teaching was spoken with hidden intent, not as a denial that real differences of lineage exist among beings. The treatise's own considered position, he notes, actually recognizes five vehicles — the human and heavenly vehicles besides the three that lead out of saṃsāra, plus the icchantika who attains none of them — and within that fuller picture, three vehicles are the final, literal teaching while "one vehicle" is only the skillful, provisional one. He cross-references two earlier passages on the varieties of śrāvaka: an earlier fascicle's threefold division into the transformation-born śrāvaka (a manifestation of a buddha or bodhisattva), the vow-bound śrāvaka (of indeterminate lineage, who has simply vowed to seek nirvāṇa with remainder but can still turn toward the greater path), and the inherent-nature śrāvaka (whose compassion has always been meager and who dreads suffering by nature) — and a later fascicle's fourfold division into transformation-born, overreaching-conceit (one who mistakes attainments in meditation for the fruits of the path, or mistakes arhatship for the final word and refuses to believe in buddhahood), turned-toward-bodhi, and fixed-on-quiescence. The "fixed on quiescence alone" śrāvaka of this passage, he explains, is exactly the inherent-nature śrāvaka of the earlier list: definite in lineage, with no capacity to redirect, in contrast to the indeterminate lineage that can still turn small into great. He brings in the Sūtra on the Unsurpassed Basis's four obstructions to buddhahood — the icchantika's obstruction of turning one's back on the Mahāyāna, the outside path's obstruction of clinging to views of a self, the śrāvaka's obstruction of dread and exhaustion with birth and death, and the pratyekabuddha's obstruction of turning one's back on benefiting others — and notes Master Woncheuk's observation that both two-vehicle sages actually share the latter two obstructions in full: it is precisely this dread of suffering and this indifference to others' welfare, present without exception and incapable of being dislodged, that makes the definite-lineage śrāvaka impossible to lead to buddhahood no matter how skillful the Buddha's methods — and it is exactly this impossibility that grounds the doctrine that three vehicles are ultimate and one vehicle only a convenient way of speaking.

36. Section: The Śrāvaka Who Turns Toward Bodhi — Closing the Account of the Three Non-Natures

Because, having already become liberated from the obstruction of the afflictions, if they are met with the awakening brought about by the buddhas and others, their minds, too, can then become liberated from the obstruction to what is to be known. It is because, at the very outset, they undertook practice for their own benefit alone, and became liberated from the obstruction of the afflictions, that the Tathāgata designates them as being of the śrāvaka lineage.

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as the counterpart case to the previous section, and the one that keeps "single vehicle" from being an empty phrase: the indeterminate-lineage śrāvaka, having already cleared away the obstruction of the afflictions — the coarser obstruction that blocks liberation from birth and death — retains an unexhausted capacity, once he meets a buddha or bodhisattva who opens the greater teaching to him and stirs a taste for its vast merits, to turn back and clear away the far subtler obstruction to what is to be known as well, the one that blocks omniscience rather than mere liberation. Precisely because that capacity remains, the Buddha says he may, by "another way of speaking" (an approach distinct from the literal one), already call such a person a bodhisattva even while he still goes by the name of śrāvaka — a name he keeps only because his very first undertaking of practice was for his own liberation alone, not because his lineage is fixed the way the previous section's śrāvaka's is. With this second case in hand, Master Cháng Bǎi closes the account: there remains, in the end, a true difference of three lineages; calling it "one vehicle" was only ever a skillful way of speaking, valid precisely for the indeterminate-lineage śrāvaka who can turn small into great, and this closes out the entire discussion of the three vehicles and the lineages that qualify them, and with it the treatise's full account — causal and liberating alike — of what the three lacks of nature of one's own actually mean.

37. Section: One Voice, Many Understandings — Why the Prajñā Teaching Was Spoken in Hidden Aspect

Good sons! The Tathāgata, relying only on these three kinds of lacking a nature of one's own, and by way of a profound, hidden intent, has, in the sūtras of provisional meaning that he has proclaimed, spoken the essentials of all dharmas in a hidden aspect — saying that all dharmas are without any nature of their own, unborn and unceasing, originally quiescent, and by their own nature in nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens the treatise's account of why one teaching lands so differently in different minds by citing the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra's famous line: "the Buddha proclaims the Dharma with a single voice, and beings, each according to their kind, each attain their own understanding of it — every one of them supposes the World-Honored One speaks their own language, and speaks these many meanings for themselves alone." Every listener feels personally addressed, he notes — the same private conviction that arises between any teacher and any student — and this, more than any defect in the teaching itself, is why the four very different receptions about to be described can all arise from one and the same sermon. He grounds "well spoken" (善說) in an earlier fascicle's account of the Buddha's speech as perfect in its beginning, middle, and end — delightful to hear, free of difficulty to practice, and leading all the way to final liberation — and "well-established discipline" (善制毗奈耶) as the rules the Buddha laid down precisely as the need for them arose, capable of subduing and extinguishing every affliction; only a bodhisattva who has already realized the root wisdom of the first ground, he adds, possesses the "resolve of utmost purity" needed to teach without a trace of the searching, deliberating thought an ordinary teacher's speech still carries. He then identifies exactly what is being called "hidden aspect, provisional-meaning" teaching here: the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras' own declaration that all dharmas lack any nature, are unborn and unceasing, originally quiescent, and by nature in nirvāṇa. Read on its own, that declaration says nothing about the three natures at all — it simply says "empty." Read through the three-non-natures framework this fascicle has just spent so long establishing, it turns out to conceal, rather than deny, the very structure of imagined, dependent, and perfected natures underneath it: the Prajñā sūtras hide the "existence" of dependent and perfected natures beneath a language of pure negation, and it takes exactly the vocabulary just unpacked to recover what was hidden. This is the hinge on which everything that follows turns — because different listeners bring radically different capacities to that act of recovery, the very same "everything is empty" can either open onto the deepest of teachings or collapse into the crudest kind of nihilism, and the four sections that follow trace out both outcomes in turn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this first, best-case listener by five completed conditions working together: superior roots of goodness free of greed, hatred, and delusion; obstructions already cleared through cultivating calm and insight; the faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom already matured into fit vessels for the teaching; resolute understanding of the Mahāyāna already deeply cultivated; and abundant stores of both merit and wisdom already gathered. For a listener with all five in place, he notes, there is no such thing as "secret" or "provisional" at all — hearing the bare declaration that all dharmas lack any nature is enough, since such a listener grasps the three-natures structure hidden within it on the spot, without needing it spelled out. The swiftness that follows tracks the listener's vehicle: a śrāvaka of this caliber quickly attains arhatship, a pratyekabuddha quickly attains his own fruit, and a bodhisattva quickly attains unsurpassed bodhi — and in every case, the deep, unshakeable faith produced is faith not merely in the teaching but in the Buddha himself as one whose own awakening is genuinely all-encompassing, since only such faith, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, keeps the practitioner from ever falling away once the initial breakthrough has been made. For a listener with all five conditions complete, he adds, there is, strictly speaking, no need even to study Yogācāra specifically in order to reach the noble path — because such a listener already penetrates true reality without ever departing from dependent origination, and so can never fall into the wrongly-grasped emptiness the next several sections exist to diagnose.

39. Section: Sincere Faith Without Full Realization — Four of Five, and a Humility That Still Bears Fruit

40. Section: Clinging to the Words — the Teacher Who Falls into Wrongly-Grasped Emptiness

If there are beings who — in extended terms, who have not yet planted superior roots of goodness, not yet purified their obstructions, not yet brought their mental continuum to maturity, and not yet accumulated superior stores of merit and wisdom — whose nature is not sincere and upright, not belonging to the sincere and upright class, who do possess the power to discern and settle what should be affirmed and what set aside, yet who remain fixed within their own view, holding it as final: if such beings, upon hearing this teaching, lack the power to understand my profound, hidden words exactly as they are, and though they give rise to understanding regarding this teaching, cling to its meaning merely by following the words, holding that all dharmas are, without qualification, utterly without any nature, without qualification unborn and unceasing, without qualification originally quiescent, and without qualification by nature in nirvāṇa — then, by this cause and condition, they acquire, with regard to all dharmas, a view of nonexistence and a view of no characteristic at all, and, having acquired this view of nonexistence and this view of no characteristic, they deny that any characteristic whatsoever exists, thereby denying the imagined characteristic, the dependent characteristic, and the perfected characteristic of all dharmas alike. Why is this? Because it is only on account of there being a dependent characteristic and a perfected characteristic that the imagined characteristic can be provisionally established at all; if one takes the dependent characteristic and the perfected characteristic themselves to be without any characteristic, one has, by that very act, also denied the imagined characteristic — and it is for this reason that such a person is said to deny all three characteristics. Though they give rise, with regard to my teaching, to the notion that it is indeed a teaching, they give rise, with regard to what is not its true meaning, to the notion that this is its true meaning; and because of this double error, though they take what I have taught to be my teaching, they take what is not its meaning to be its meaning. Though it is by giving rise to faith and understanding regarding the teaching that their merit increases, it is by their very clinging to what is not its meaning that their wisdom is lost — and once wisdom is lost, vast and boundless wholesome dharmas are lost along with it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this third listener as intelligent enough to discern and decide for himself, unlike the sincere-but-unrealized listener before him — and it is exactly that intelligence, misapplied, that becomes the danger: rather than pressing further into what is truly definitive, he settles for his own reading and treats it as final, falling into what the tradition calls wrongly-grasped emptiness (惡取空). Hearing that all dharmas lack any nature, are unborn and unceasing, are originally quiescent, and are by nature in nirvāṇa, he takes each phrase at face value and without qualification — not realizing that the negation is aimed at the imagined nature superimposed on things, not at the dependently arisen things themselves or at the true nature underlying them — and so denies all three natures at once: no dependent characteristic, hence no imagined characteristic could ever have been superimposed on anything to begin with, and no perfected characteristic either. The treatise's rebuttal, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, is precise rather than merely polemical: a merely imputed thing can only ever be spoken of as existing at all because it has some real basis to be imputed onto — exactly as the Establishment of the Teaching Treatise puts it, an imputed dharma without any real thing behind it destroys not one but both, the real and the imputed together, the way there is no shadow of a tree without the tree itself. He traces the doctrinal genealogy back to the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra's three kinds of wrong view — denying effects but not causes, denying both effects and causes but not all dharmas, and denying everything without exception — placing this listener's error squarely in the third and most severe class, and closes by noting the real cost is not merely intellectual: sincere faith in the sūtra as the Buddha's own word still yields merit, but the very same clinging to a nihilistic misreading of its meaning erodes wisdom, and once wisdom is lost, so too is the capacity to advance any further in wholesome practice at all.

41. Section: The Student Who Follows — and the Student Who Slanders It as Māra's Word

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the teacher of the previous section and these two kinds of students as one connected case study in how a single misreading propagates and branches. The second student is more independent-minded but fares far worse: hearing the same bare declaration and, without any teacher's framing at all, feeling an instinctive terror at what sounds like the denial of all cause and effect, this student leaps to the conclusion that no buddha could have taught anything so alarming — it must be Māra's word — and proceeds to denounce the sūtra itself as false. Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the weight of this particular error with the account, preserved in the biography of Xuanzang, of a scholar-monk of Kashmir who vowed to compose treatises refuting the Mahāyāna and erasing Vasubandhu's reputation for good; no sooner had he made this vow than his mind grew deranged, his tongue split fivefold, and blood poured from his whole body — recognizing, before he died, that this agony was the fruit of his own wrong view, he burned his own manuscripts, begged his fellow students never to slander the Mahāyāna, and the ground where he died sank into a pit. 42. Section: The Listener Who Denies It Outright — and a Summary of All Four Kinds

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the fourth and final listener as lacking every one of the five conditions from the start, and as differing from the third listener's students in one crucial respect: this is not a case of following a teacher's error, but of a prior karmic obstruction — carried over from denouncing the true teaching in some previous life — resurfacing the instant the right conditions meet it, so that the mere hearing of "all dharmas lack any nature" triggers outright denial with no teacher's misreading needed as an intermediate step. Drawing all four listeners together, Master Cháng Bǎi summarizes the pattern the treatise has now laid out in full: the listener with all five conditions swiftly attains the ultimate; the listener missing only the stores of merit and wisdom keeps ripening toward maturity through sincere faith alone; the listener who clings to the words and falls into wrongly-grasped emptiness, together with the student who follows him, loses wisdom and wholesome dharmas while the more independent-minded student who instead denounces the teaching as Māra's word incurs grave karmic obstruction; and the listener who denies everything from the outset, driven by karmic obstruction already carried over from before, compounds that obstruction further still. He notes, with evident concern, that such figures are not merely a matter of ancient history — that teachers claiming attainments they do not have, dismissing the genuine teachers around them, and promising realization without the corresponding practice can still be found today, and that a mistaken reading of "emptiness," however sincerely delivered, remains exactly the kind of teaching this passage warns must not be allowed to spread unchallenged.

43. Section: Five Verses — the Three Non-Natures, the One Vehicle, and Two Names for the Uncontaminated Realm

At that time, the World-Honored One, wishing to proclaim this meaning once more, spoke these verses: All dharmas alike are without any nature of their own, unborn and unceasing, originally still; the very nature of dharmas is forever in nirvāṇa — what wise person would say this holds no hidden intent? Lacking a nature of characteristic, lacking a nature of arising, and lacking a nature of the ultimate — all this I have already made manifest; if one does not know the Buddha's hidden intent in speaking so, the true path is ruined and cannot be reached. Those who become pure by resting on the paths that purify rely on this one path alone, and on no second; it is on this basis that, within it, I establish a single vehicle — not that there is no difference among the natures of beings. Within the realm of sentient beings there are births beyond measure: some deliver only their own single self, bound for quiescence alone; others, with great compassion and valor, realize nirvāṇa without abandoning other beings — and this is exceedingly hard to attain. Subtle and hard to conceive is the uncontaminated realm; within it, liberation is everywhere without difference. All aims accomplished, free of affliction and suffering — these are spoken of, in two different terms, as permanence and as bliss.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the five verses as a compressed reprise of everything the chapter has established. The first restates that the imagined nature's unborn, unceasing, originally quiescent character, and the perfected nature's own eternal repose in nirvāṇa, both carry a hidden intent no wise listener should take at face value — a line aimed squarely at the third and fourth listeners of the previous sections, who took the words alone as the whole of the meaning. The second names the three lacks of nature directly — of characteristic, of arising, and of the ultimate — as exactly what has already been shown in full, and warns that missing the hidden intent behind them wrecks the true path outright, exactly as the teacher and students of the previous sections discovered to their cost. The third grounds the one-vehicle teaching in the single purifying path all three vehicles share, while insisting in the same breath that this does not erase real differences among beings' natures — the "one vehicle, three vehicles" tension the treatise has been holding together since the discussion of lineages several sections earlier. The fourth contrasts the definite-lineage śrāvaka, who delivers only himself into quiescence, with the bodhisattva or indeterminate-lineage practitioner whose great compassion refuses to abandon other beings even while facing the same dread of birth and death — calling this refusal, rightly, exceedingly hard to attain. The fifth turns to the uncontaminated realm reached at the end of the path: subtle beyond ordinary conception because it lies beyond the reach of language-bound thought altogether, and equal without difference across the three vehicles' liberation from the afflictions, even though only a buddha's own realization carries the further, unshared qualities of full awakening. Master Cháng Bǎi glosses the closing pair, "permanence" and "bliss," carefully against a possible misreading: nirvāṇa is not called permanent and blissful because some fixed, positive thing called permanence or bliss is found there, but only because affliction, being impermanent and unquiet, is at last extinguished — its extinction alone is what "permanence" names — and because no future suffering of rebirth will ever arise again — that non-arising alone is what "bliss" names. The Discourse Text warns explicitly against clinging to these words as though they pointed to some substantial thing; they are, the treatise insists, only another way of speaking about the same liberation from affliction and suffering already described, not a second attainment beyond it. With this, the whole of the section on Paramārthasamudgata's questions and the Buddha's teaching on the hidden aspect of the "all dharmas without nature" doctrine is complete, and Paramārthasamudgata is about to demonstrate, in his own words, exactly how well he has understood it.

44. Section: Paramārthasamudgata's Own Demonstration — the Three Natures Applied to Form

At that time, Paramārthasamudgata Bodhisattva again said to the Buddha: World-Honored One! As I now understand the meaning of what the World-Honored One has taught: if, within this very activity that the imagined characteristic, as it occurs in the field of discrimination, depends upon, because the imagined characteristic does not truly exist, this very own-nature — the lack of any nature of its own, the true suchness that is the pure object of the wisdom realizing the selflessness of dharmas — is what is called the perfected characteristic. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this as Paramārthasamudgata proving, in his own words, that he has genuinely grasped what the Buddha has taught — not by repeating it back abstractly, but by running the entire three-natures rubric through a single concrete case: the aggregate of form. The imagined characteristic, he explains, is simply every name-based overlay laid onto the actual process of experience — "form aggregate" itself as a label, together with the further labels "arising," "ceasing," "permanently cut off," and "fully comprehended" that get hung on it in turn; none of this naming activity has any reality of its own; it is provisional designation through and through. And the perfected characteristic is reached the moment this dependent process is seen clearly enough that the imagined characteristic laid over it is recognized as never having been real at all — what remains, once that overlay drops away, is simply the dependent process's own true nature, its own lack of any nature of its own, which is nothing other than thusness, the selflessness of dharmas, the pure object a sage's wisdom actually cognizes. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Here begins the third and final division of this chapter's citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra: establishing, through the Buddha's own account of the three turnings of the wheel of the Dharma, that the definitive teaching now being explained — the teaching that all dharmas lack any nature of their own — is truly final, with no higher teaching yet to come. Master Cháng Bǎi lingers over the setting of this first turning: Ṛṣipatana, "where the sages fell," named for five hundred flying sages who plummeted from the sky on hearing a woman's song drift up from below, and the Deer Park itself, famed for three virtues — abundant groves sheltering from rain, heat, and wind alike; water prized for its coolness, softness, and fragrance; and ground so richly blessed that birds and beasts gather there without fear, said to conceal a treasure that only rises to the surface when a wheel-turning king appears in the world. Because of these three virtues, he explains, the site had long drawn three kinds of seekers even before the Buddha's own arrival — ascetic sages practicing austerities in ages without a buddha, pratyekabuddhas pursuing their own solitary realization, and, now, the Buddha himself together with the five companions who had once practiced austerities beside him. It was for these five alone — those "setting out toward the śrāvaka vehicle" — that the Buddha first turned the four noble truths through three cycles of twelve aspects, laid out fully in the Sūtra of Turning the Dharma Wheel: an initial cycle simply naming each truth — this is suffering, this is its origin, this is its cessation, this is the path — bringing the first listeners to the path of seeing; a second cycle prescribing what to do with each — suffering is to be known, its origin abandoned, its cessation realized, the path cultivated — carrying them through the path of cultivation from the first fruit onward; and a third cycle spoken from the vantage of accomplishment — I have known suffering, abandoned its origin, realized its cessation, cultivated the path — marking the path beyond training, the attainment of arhatship. No one in the entire world, Master Cháng Bǎi stresses — not Māra, not the first-dhyāna Brahmā, not the most learned renunciant or brāhmaṇa — had ever been able to turn such a wheel before. And yet, precisely because this first turning taught existence while concealing emptiness beneath it — teaching selflessness of persons while leaving the deeper selflessness of dharmas unstated — it remained a teaching with something higher still above it, not yet fully explicit, and so continued to be, in the centuries after, a ground on which real doctrinal disputes — most notably within the Abhidharma schools over just how real the dharmas taught here actually are — could still take root.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi places this second turning across the traditional span of twenty-two years the Buddha is said to have devoted to expounding the Prajñāpāramitā teachings, spoken now not for śrāvakas alone but for those setting out on the Mahāyāna, declaring that all dharmas are without any nature of their own, unborn and unceasing, originally quiescent, by nature in nirvāṇa. He reads the two turnings so far as deliberate mirror images of each other: the first turning hid emptiness while teaching existence, so that beings could first take hold of the reality of suffering, its cause, its cessation, and the path without being overwhelmed; the second turning now hides existence while teaching emptiness, so that the same beings, having taken hold, could be drawn beyond clinging to what they had grasped. The hidden aspect here, he stresses, was never a denial of cause and effect — the Buddha never spoke so as to erase karma and its fruit, and it is exactly because emptiness itself is possible that all dharmas can be established at all — but rather a concealment aimed at those not yet equipped to hold emptiness and dependent origination together: teach such beings emptiness without qualification, and not a few will forget the very causality the teaching was never meant to deny, exactly as the earlier account of wrongly-grasped emptiness in this same fascicle already showed happening in practice. More wondrous than the first turning though it was, it remained, in the same way, a teaching with something still higher above it, its own deepest structure still concealed rather than declared outright — and so it, too, continued to be ground on which real dispute could take hold, a ground the third and final turning now exists to close.

World-Honored One, now, at this third time, you turn the wheel of the true teaching universally for those setting out toward every vehicle alike, relying on the teaching that all dharmas are without any nature of their own, unborn and unceasing, originally quiescent, by nature in nirvāṇa, lacking any nature at all — turning it in a revealed aspect. This is foremost in wonder, utmost in rarity; the wheel of the teaching that the World-Honored One turns now has nothing above it, no further room to be surpassed; it is the true, definitive meaning, and is not a resting-place for any dispute at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies exactly what distinguishes this third turning from the two before it: not a different topic, but a different manner of speaking about the very same "all dharmas lack a nature of their own" — no longer hidden, but revealed. Where the second turning left the three natures concealed beneath a language of blanket negation, this Yogācāra teaching now states outright what has real being and what does not: the imagined nature has no reality at all, while the dependent and perfected natures do, each in its own qualified sense, so that the treatise's audience can finally know as existent what exists and know as nonexistent what does not, without the risk of nihilism the second turning's silence made possible. Its audience, too, is now universal — not śrāvakas alone, not bodhisattvas alone, but all three vehicles, and, on the treatise's own fuller reckoning, all five — which is part of why the sūtra calls it foremost in wonder: nothing remains above it, nothing further needs to be received, and it closes the very disputes the first two turnings left open. Master Cháng Bǎi is candid, though, about the limits of that closure: however finally this sūtra speaks of itself, disputes did not in fact stop — later students of the teaching argued over it just as fiercely as before, and the very claim that a Yogācāra "third turning" supersedes a Madhyamaka "second turning" became, in East Asian Buddhism, one of the most consequential and contested doxographical judgments in the tradition's history, met by schools that ranked the Prajñā teaching of emptiness as final in its own right and organized the very same three sermons into a different order of depth. Read within the treatise's own three-vehicle framework rather than as a verdict against any rival school, he suggests, the point is narrower and more defensible: the first turning was spoken distinctly for śrāvakas, the second distinctly for bodhisattvas as a teaching apart, and only the third for every vehicle without exception — because the Buddha, endowed with the wisdom of dependent origination, always taught exactly the dharma fitted to the conditions and capacities of those before him, so that each, in its own time and to its own audience, could actually be led onto the path.

51. Section: A Fingernail of Dirt Against the Whole Earth — the Merit of This Teaching

World-Honored One! If there are good sons or good daughters who, hearing this profound, definitive teaching that the Tathāgata has spoken — that all dharmas are without any nature of their own, unborn and unceasing, originally quiescent, by nature in nirvāṇa — come to faith and understanding regarding it, copy it out and safeguard it, make offerings to it and circulate it, receive and recite it and review it, reflect on it as it truly is, and, through the diligent effort of actual practice, give rise to the corresponding cultivation — how much merit do they generate? Having said this, the World-Honored One then said to Paramārthasamudgata Bodhisattva: Paramārthasamudgata! The merit generated by such good sons or good daughters is immeasurable and countless, difficult to convey even through simile — I shall now explain a small portion of it for you, in brief. It is like the dirt beneath a fingernail compared to the dirt of the whole earth: not a hundredth part, not a thousandth part, not even a part in a hundred thousand — by any reckoning, by any comparison, not even the smallest conceivable fraction of it. Or it is like the water caught in the print of an ox's hoof compared to the water of the four great oceans: not a hundredth part, and so on in full detail, not even the smallest conceivable fraction of it. In just the same way, the merit gained by hearing the sūtras of provisional meaning, coming to faith and understanding regarding them, and, in full detail, giving rise, through actual practice, to the corresponding cultivation, compared to the merit gathered by hearing this teaching of definitive meaning, coming to faith and understanding regarding it, and, in full detail, giving rise, through actual practice, to the corresponding cultivation — is not a hundredth part, and so on in full detail, not even the smallest conceivable fraction of it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads Paramārthasamudgata's question as invoking, once again, the ten dharma-practices already unpacked earlier in the fascicle — hearing, faith and understanding, copying, safeguarding, offering, circulating, receiving, reciting, reviewing, reflecting, and cultivating — now asked specifically of this third-turning, definitive teaching. The Buddha's answer reaches for two similes of near-total disproportion: dirt clinging to a fingernail against the dirt of the entire earth, and the water pooled in a single ox's hoofprint against the water of all four great oceans, each pushed through a full ladder of ancient Indian orders of magnitude — hundred, thousand, hundred-thousand, on up through the calculated and comparative reckonings, to the finest conceivable fraction, the uparniṣad portion, sometimes likened to what a modern reader might call one part in a trillion — with even that finest fraction still found wanting. He grounds the underlying logic in Vasubandhu's treatise on the Prajñā teachings, which names four distinct ways one thing can surpass another: superiority in number, as here, where the sheer scale of merit dwarfs its counterpart; superiority in power, as an uncontaminated wholesome dharma outweighs a contaminated one regardless of quantity; superiority beyond comparison, where nothing at all resembles the thing being measured, so that no fraction, however fine, can really capture the gap; and superiority in causal efficacy, since a supreme wholesome cause yields unsurpassed awakening while an ordinary contaminated wholesome deed yields only a fruit still confined within the three realms. Taken together, Master Cháng Bǎi says, these are less a boast than an explanation: it is precisely because this is the teaching that finally reveals what the first two turnings left concealed that receiving it, understanding it, and actually practicing it accomplishes something the earlier teachings, however wondrous in their own time, could never by themselves complete.

52. Section: Its Name — the Teaching of Ultimate, Definitive Meaning — and the Fruits It Bore

Having said this, Paramārthasamudgata Bodhisattva again said to the Buddha: World-Honored One! Within this Dharma-gate of explaining what is profoundly hidden, what should this teaching be called? How should I uphold and retain it? The Buddha said to Paramārthasamudgata Bodhisattva: Good sons! This is called the Teaching of Ultimate, Definitive Meaning. You should uphold and retain it as the Teaching of Ultimate, Definitive Meaning. When this Teaching of Ultimate, Definitive Meaning was spoken, within the great assembly, six hundred thousand beings gave rise to the mind of unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening; three hundred thousand śrāvakas became free of dust and free of defilement, and attained the pure Dharma eye with regard to all dharmas; one hundred and fifty thousand śrāvakas permanently exhausted all contaminants, and their minds attained liberation; and seventy-five thousand bodhisattvas attained the forbearance of the non-arising of dharmas.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the sūtra names itself here — the Teaching of Ultimate, Definitive Meaning — with an authority the Buddha extends to no earlier portion of this same discourse, and that Paramārthasamudgata, whose very name means "one born from the ultimate," is the fitting figure to have drawn this final naming out of the Buddha. He glosses "dust" and "defilement" through the Compendium of Synonyms: dust as wisdom already arisen but not yet consummate, still permitting the intermittent or uninterrupted activity of self-conceit at the moment of direct realization; defilement as the latent seeds underlying that same conceit, together with the afflictions cut at the path of seeing — both now permanently absent, hence "far from dust, free of defilement." He reads the numbers themselves as tracking, almost one for one, the very typology of listeners the chapter has just spent so long describing: six hundred thousand beings newly resolving on unsurpassed awakening as those whose faith has finally ripened into aspiration; three hundred thousand śrāvakas attaining the pure Dharma eye as reaching stream-entry, the direct realization of selflessness of persons; one hundred and fifty thousand attaining full liberation as reaching arhatship outright; and seventy-five thousand bodhisattvas attaining the forbearance of the non-arising of dharmas as advancing to a still deeper realization — which he ties back to an earlier fascicle's threefold division of this very forbearance into the forbearance of the non-arising of the essential nature, gained by penetrating the imagined nature's utter unreality; the forbearance of the non-arising by nature, gained by penetrating the dependent nature's freedom from any nature that arises of itself; and the forbearance of the non-arising of the defilement of affliction and suffering, gained by penetrating the perfected nature's own emptiness of every trace of affliction. With these fruits reported, the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra's citation that has run since early in this fascicle — covering, in turn, the characteristics of mind, intellect, and consciousness; the characteristic of the three natures; and the characteristic of dharmas' lack of any nature of their own, together with its resolution through the three turnings of the Dharma wheel — comes to its close, and with it, the content available from this fascicle's source text is complete.
