Fascicle 65 · 瑜伽師地論卷第六十五 · The Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection Opens

Four Objects of Reflection, and the Reflecting Mind's Own Twenty-Four Aspects

With the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing now closed, Maitreya opens the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection, the wisdom born of carefully considering what has been heard until impermanence, suffering, and non-self become genuinely settled convictions, beginning with the four objects a practitioner must reflect upon and the twenty-four aspects, in twelve contrasting pairs, of the very mind that does the reflecting.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 65 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Closing Hearing, Opening Reflection — Four Objects to Be Reflected Upon

Having already explained the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Hearing, Maitreya now turns to explain the decisive discernment of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection, the wisdom gained not simply by having heard the Dharma but by carefully turning over its own nature and distinctions until impermanence in all conditioned things, suffering pervading all conditioned existence, the absence of self in all dharmas, and the peace of nirvana become settled and genuine convictions rather than mere information received. This same wisdom always leads onward to the wisdom attained through cultivation, which unites with concentration itself and, through gradually deepening insight, carries a practitioner from a still-defiled cultivated wisdom all the way to the undefiled wisdom that directly realizes the twin emptiness of self and dharmas — so that a genuine disciple, at every moment, should be moving from hearing to reflection, from reflection to cultivation, and from cultivation to direct realization of suchness itself. This ground's own nature first unfolds into what is to be reflected upon, briefly of four kinds: reflection on phenomena themselves, reflection on what exists and what does not exist, reflection on cause and result, and reflection on the vehicles. Reflection on phenomena means reflecting on the six skillful matters already explained at length elsewhere — skill regarding the aggregates, the elements, the sense-spheres, dependent origination, what is and is not a genuine occasion for a given result, and the faculties. Reflection on what exists and does not exist means reflecting on the five kinds of existent dharma already given in the Basic Section — dharmas existing through their own particular characteristic, through a shared characteristic, through a merely provisional characteristic, through a causal characteristic, and through a resultant characteristic — together with the five kinds of non-existent dharma: what has not yet arisen, what has already ceased, what is simply absent relative to something else, what does not exist from the ultimate standpoint though provisionally spoken of, and what is utterly non-existent under any description whatsoever, such as the child of a barren woman. Reflection on cause and result means reflecting on the ten causes, four conditions, and five results already explained in the Basic Section's own Ground of Reflection and Investigation, together with the further account of defiled and pure causation given across several fascicles of the Compendium of Decisive Discernment. Reflection on the vehicles means reflecting on the teachings, practices, and fruits distinguished among the three vehicles of śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva, together with the further distinctions of precepts, concentration, and wisdom belonging to each.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fascicle's own opening lines as a small but complete map of the entire Buddhist path of study, hearing leading to reflection, reflection leading to cultivation, and cultivation leading finally to direct realization, each stage strictly necessary and none skippable no matter how eager a practitioner might be to reach the end. He notes Elder Miao Jing's own remark, recorded here, wondering aloud what one's own realization would look like after reading this single treatise eighty times over, as a vivid reminder that a text of this scope is never meant to be read once and set aside, but returned to again and again as understanding itself continues to deepen.

2. Section: The Reflecting Mind Itself — Bad Reflection and Good Reflection

Having explained the four objects to be reflected upon, a further topic opens: the mind that actually does the reflecting. What is reflected upon is simply whatever object is taken as a condition; what does the reflecting is this very thought itself, a single moment of mind that may be wholesome or unwholesome, and different in kind depending on which it is. Study of the Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection accordingly proceeds from the Basic Section all the way through the Compendium of Decisive Discernment, advancing step by step into ever deeper reflection, and is of great benefit to a practitioner cultivating the noble path of any of the three vehicles. The mind capable of this reflection is shown, in what follows, to bear twenty-four aspects altogether, comprising twelve contrasting pairs — wholesome thought against unwholesome, thought conjoined with the Dharma against thought not so conjoined, and so on up to thought conjoined with the noble path against thought not so conjoined — each pair together called the capacity for reflection. Briefly, there are two basic kinds of reflecting thought: unwholesome reflective thought and wholesome reflective thought. A mind conjoined with greed, hatred, and delusion is called unwholesome reflective thought, exactly as already explained in the Basic Section; a mind conjoined with faith, freedom from greed, freedom from hatred, and freedom from delusion is called wholesome reflective thought.

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, briefly, there are two kinds of reflecting thought: unwholesome reflective thought and wholesome reflective thought. Unwholesome reflective thought is as already explained in the Basic Section.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the clean distinction drawn here between the object of reflection and the mind that reflects upon it, a distinction easy to state but genuinely important to keep in view, since a practitioner can easily assume that simply reflecting on the right object guarantees a wholesome result, when in truth the very same object can be turned over by a mind conjoined with greed, hatred, and delusion just as readily as by one conjoined with faith and their absence. He treats the promise of twenty-four aspects in twelve contrasting pairs as this fascicle's own signal that the quality of reflection itself, not merely its content, will be examined here in considerable and useful detail.

3. Section: Twelve Contrasting Pairs of Reflective Thought

Unwholesome reflective thought is explained by pointing back to the sixteen non-Buddhist theories already catalogued in the Basic Section, each rooted in improper mental engagement and each giving rise to greed, hatred, and delusion in turn, along with the graver fault of reflecting on six matters that should never be reflected upon at all — a self, sentient beings generally, worldly affairs, the particular karmic results of beings, the meditative states of one absorbed in concentration, and the inconceivable states of the buddhas — each of these six barred from reflection because a self and sentient beings possess no genuine characteristic of their own to reflect upon in the first place, worldly affairs cannot possibly accord with the noble path, karmic results lie beyond an ordinary being's own power of understanding, and the states of meditators and buddhas alike are simply too profound to be fruitfully turned over by ordinary thought. Wholesome reflective thought is understood, by direct contrast, as its own precise mirror image. What follows then extends this same good/bad structure across eleven further contrasting pairs of reflection, each following exactly the same pattern: reflection led by what is not the Dharma, versus reflection led by the Dharma itself, such as the six perfections of giving, precepts, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom; reflection led by what is contrary to monastic discipline, versus reflection led by the discipline's own precepts against killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and the rest; reflection on what is not noble, versus reflection on the noble ones and their own teaching, such as the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment; reflection on the unwholesome, versus reflection on the wholesome; reflection on what should not be cultivated, versus reflection on what should be cultivated; reflection on the not-good, versus reflection on the good; reflection on the dark, versus reflection on the bright; reflection that leads to what is meaningless, versus reflection that leads to what is meaningful; reflection on the inferior realms of desire, versus reflection on the sublime realms of form, formlessness, or the transcendent path; reflection on what is blameworthy, versus reflection on what is blameless; and reflection on what should be avoided, such as craving, hostility, and harmfulness, versus reflection on what should not be avoided, such as having few desires, contentment, and diligence. Whatever leads a being toward continued bondage in birth and death should never be reflected upon; only whatever accords with the noble path and with buddhahood itself deserves a practitioner's genuinely careful reflection. This closes the account of the reflecting mind's own twenty-four aspects and the entire topic of this ground's own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this long list of eleven contrasting pairs as the treatise's own repeated insistence, from as many angles as possible, on a single underlying rule: a practitioner's own attention is a limited and precious resource, and every moment spent turning over what cannot possibly help — a self that has no real characteristic, karmic results beyond one's own power to fathom, worldly gossip and distraction — is a moment not spent on what actually leads toward the noble path. He notes that six specific objects are singled out as altogether unfit for reflection, a self, sentient beings in general, worldly affairs, others' karmic results, meditators' inner states, and the buddhas' own inconceivable domain, precisely because each looks, at first glance, like a perfectly natural thing to wonder about, making this warning all the more necessary for an ordinary practitioner to keep in mind.

4. Section: What Exists and What Does Not — The Buddha's Own Question

A further topic opens, what is to be known, taking as its starting point the World-Honored One's own words: noble disciples know what exists as existing and what does not exist as not existing — but what, precisely, counts as existing, and what as not existing? Briefly, by two marks, one should know that something exists: first, if it is presently arising, has already arisen, or belongs to the present moment, referring to conditioned phenomena bearing the marks of arising, abiding, changing, and ceasing, and in particular the very first instant of arising together with what has already arisen but not yet ceased; second, if it counts as a real thing, as an event, as a meaning, or as something perfectly accomplished, referring in turn to whatever is established as its own characteristic without depending on anything else, to the aggregates and other basic categories that dependently arise, to whatever a name serves to convey as its designated referent, and to the true nature of all dharmas alike. These two marks correspond closely to the fivefold classification of existent dharmas already given in the Basic Section's own Ground of Wisdom Attained through Reflection — existing through its own particular characteristic, through a shared characteristic, through a merely provisional characteristic, through a causal characteristic, and through a resultant characteristic — and to that same source's fivefold classification of the nature of existence itself as perfected, dependently arisen, imagined, particular, or inexpressible, since nature and characteristic are never really two separate things, a nature always needing some characteristic through which to appear and a characteristic always resting on some underlying nature. Three separate commentarial traditions are recorded as reading the finer details of this correspondence somewhat differently, yet all agree that the first mark points chiefly to dependently arisen phenomena as they presently exist, while the second mark's four terms together point to the very same three natures — imagined, dependent, and perfected — approached now from the standpoint of existence itself rather than from the standpoint of delusion and awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own brief question — what counts as existing, and what does not — as deceptively simple, since answering it properly draws on nearly the entire architecture of the three natures already built up across this treatise's earlier fascicles. He notes that even the treatise's own commentators, cited here in three separate traditions, do not fully agree on how precisely to map this passage's own fourfold terms onto that architecture, treating this as a healthy reminder that even a text this authoritative leaves real room for careful, ongoing scholarly reflection rather than settling every last question by fiat.

5. Section: Real Existence and Provisional Existence — Why "Self" Is Only a Name

Having posed the question of existence and non-existence in brief, a further topic opens: how, in short, should real existence and provisional existence themselves be understood? Whatever dharma does not depend on or rest upon anything else in order for its own particular characteristic to be established should, briefly, be understood as really existing — as when form, sound, and the other objects, together with the primary elements of earth, water, fire, and wind, arise directly from their own causes and conditions without needing to lean on some other dharma in order to be designated form or sound at all. Whatever dharma, by contrast, does depend on and rest upon something else in order for its own characteristic to be established should be understood as only provisionally existing, not existing as a real thing in its own right — as when a vase or a basin depends upon, and is designated only in dependence upon, color, shape, and the other qualities that actually compose it, the name "vase" itself arising only because a perceiving mind, encountering those qualities together, forms the further discriminating thought that reaches for that name. In just this way, taking the aggregates of form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness themselves as what is depended upon, a self and sentient beings and the rest come to be provisionally designated, extending even to concepts such as the attainment of non-perception and the attainment of cessation, which are likewise only provisionally designated in dependence upon the four mental aggregates of feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness simply not currently operating. Here, the aggregates of form and the rest, arising from actual causes and conditions, are what really exist; but a self, a sentient being, a life-holder, one who is born, and one who again and again takes up a new destiny — every name by which an enduring, unchanging, sovereign self might be called — are not real existents at all, existing only provisionally, resting entirely on the aggregates from which they are merely and conventionally derived.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the vase-and-color simile as one of the clearest illustrations anywhere in the treatise of the difference between real and provisional existence, a vase never being some further thing floating alongside its own color and shape, but simply a convenient name the mind reaches for once it has already perceived those qualities together. He highlights the direct application of this same logic to the self as the passage's own real target, the aggregates of form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness being genuinely, causally real, while "self," "sentient being," and every kindred term are shown to be exactly as provisional as the word "vase," resting entirely on those five aggregates and possessing no further reality of their own beyond the naming.

6. Section: Twenty-Four Provisional Dharmas Not Associated with Mind, and Two Provisional Attainments

Just as a self and the rest are provisionally designated in dependence on the aggregates, so too are form, feeling, perception, mental formation, and consciousness themselves provisionally designated in dependence on those same aggregates viewed as objects of perception, form naming the physical body composed of the four primary elements, feeling naming what receives an object, perception naming what apprehends its mark, formation naming what actively constructs, and consciousness naming what discerns its object — each a real dharma as arisen from its own causes and conditions, yet named only provisionally through the further act of conceptual designation. In just the same way, taking form, smell, taste, and touch as what is depended upon, food and drink, carriages, vases, clothing, every kind of ornament, houses, armies, and forests are all provisionally designated, none of these existing as anything beyond the very form, smell, taste, and touch that compose them, everyday consciousness merely gathering these qualities together and reaching for a corresponding conventional name. On conditioned dharmas more broadly, twenty-four further provisional designations are given in the very same way, none of them real dharmas in their own right, but each merely marking some further phase or relation among dharmas that are: arising, aging, abiding, impermanence, the seed itself, manifest and unmanifest karmic expression, attainment, the life faculty, shared class-membership among beings of a kind, the name-body, phrase-body, and letter-body that make up language, the ordinary unenlightened being's own status, combination and non-combination of causes and conditions, continuity across rebirth, the fixed correspondence between a specific cause and its specific result, correspondence itself, swiftness, sequence, time, spatial location, and number — every one of these twenty-four provisionally established rather than really existing in its own right. Unconditioned dharmas receive the same treatment: space and space's own unconditioned status are provisionally designated simply in dependence on the sheer absence of form arising in a given place, and likewise the attainment of non-perception and the attainment of cessation are provisionally designated simply in dependence on the six operating consciousnesses' feeling, perception, and other mental factors not currently functioning at all during the period of that very absorption. This closes the account of real and provisional existence.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the extension of provisional existence all the way down to the five aggregates' own names, and outward to the twenty-four dharmas not associated with mind and even to the unconditioned space and the loftiest meditative attainments, as this passage's own way of insisting that provisional designation is not a rare exception but the ordinary condition of nearly every name a being relies on, real existence in the strictest sense belonging only to the bare causal dharmas themselves. He highlights the closing pair, the attainment of non-perception and the attainment of cessation, as a pointed reminder that even the most exalted states a meditator can reach are still, strictly speaking, only provisionally named, lest a practitioner mistake naming something highly refined for having thereby found something ultimately real.

7. Section: Why the Self Cannot Be Apprehended and Cannot Be Seen

A further topic opens, elaborating this same distinction in full detail, beginning with why the self specifically is not really existent. A question is first raised: by the very logic of demonstrative reasoning itself, verified through direct perception, inference, and reliable testimony together, on what basis can one decisively know that a self is not really existent at all? The answer given is simply this: because it cannot be apprehended, and because it cannot be seen. Why can it not be apprehended? Because searched for within the six inner faculties, within the six outer objects, within the six consciousnesses arising in between them, or entirely apart from the aggregates altogether, no self is ever actually found in any of these locations, whether one supposes the self simply is the aggregates or supposes instead that the self is something separate from the aggregates residing somehow within them. And why can it not be seen? Because just as the eye and the other faculties, being real existents among the twelve sense-fields, each display their own distinct and observable function — the eye seeing form, the ear hearing sound, and so on for the rest — the self that non-Buddhist thinkers imagine is never found to display any distinct function of its own that can actually be observed at all. Since the self's own characteristic can nowhere be apprehended, and since its own distinct function can nowhere be seen, one should know that the self imagined by ordinary beings is not really existent at all. A further question then presses the point: if the self truly cannot be apprehended anywhere and cannot be seen at all, then why do so many non-Buddhist renunciants insist they can both apprehend and see it, and take such evident delight in displaying and establishing it as real? The answer is direct: they do not, in fact, apprehend or see it either; rather, resting on the view of a self and the conceit of a self as their own support, they give rise to a distorted discrimination and a distorted reckoning, and it is this distorted reckoning alone, contrary to genuine reasoning, that they take such delight in displaying and establishing as real.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twofold argument, an exhaustive search finding no self anywhere and no distinct function belonging to any self ever being observed, as one of the treatise's own cleanest formal demonstrations of non-self, structured so that a non-Buddhist opponent's own reliance on direct perception and inference is turned back against their own conclusion. He finds the closing exchange especially sharp, the treatise refusing to simply dismiss the non-Buddhist renunciants who claim to perceive a self, but instead diagnosing exactly what has actually happened in their own minds: not a genuine finding of the self at all, but a wrong reckoning propped up from the outset by the very view of self and conceit of self it was supposedly meant to discover.

8. Section: Four Possible Theories of Self, and Why the First Fails

Pressed further on how one can know that the self is neither really existent nor even presently existent, and is thus neither apprehensible nor visible, the treatise answers by first cataloguing every theoretical position a non-Buddhist thinker might take, reasoned through to its very furthest limit: no such theory extends beyond four possibilities. The first holds that the self simply is the aggregates themselves. The second holds that the self is other than the aggregates, yet abides within them. The third holds that the self is neither simply the aggregates nor abides within them, but instead abides apart from the aggregates in some further, unconditioned dharma distinct from them. The fourth holds that the self is neither the aggregates, nor abides within them, nor abides in any unconditioned dharma apart from them, but rather has no aggregates whatsoever, being entirely unassociated with any dharma belonging to the aggregates at all. Whatever theory of self any thinker's own discriminating view arrives at is necessarily contained within one of these four positions; nothing lies beyond or beside them. And every one of these four positions asserting the self's real existence proves, on examination, entirely unreasonable. Why so? Because if the self simply is the aggregates, and is not other than them, then this very self is only provisionally established in dependence on the aggregates, and this fault alone already follows of its own accord — but there is a further difficulty besides: the aggregates are impermanent, and each is conjoined with its own distinct characteristic, form marked by obstruction, feeling by reception, perception by grasping a mark, formation by construction, and consciousness by discernment, five quite different characteristics; if the self simply were these aggregates, it would likewise have to be impermanent rather than constant, and would have to be five things rather than one, and so could not possibly be a single, real, enduring self at all. For this reason, the theory that the self simply is the aggregates does not accord with reason.

Ru-Yi Translation

Every theory holding the self really existent, reasoned to its furthest limit, does not exceed four kinds: first, that the self simply is the aggregates; second, that the self is other than the aggregates, yet abides within them; third, that the self is neither the aggregates nor abides within them, but abides apart from them in some further dharma; fourth, that the self is neither the aggregates, nor abides within them, nor abides apart from them, having no aggregates at all and being unassociated with any of them. All such theories of the self's real existence are unreasonable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold catalogue as an exhaustive net cast wide enough to catch every conceivable theory of self a philosopher could propose, self as identical to the aggregates, self as distinct yet housed within them, self as distinct and housed in some further unconditioned reality, or self as utterly unconnected to the aggregates altogether, so that refuting all four leaves genuinely no remaining place for a real self to hide. He notes that the first position falls almost immediately, since claiming the self simply is the aggregates already concedes, by that very claim, that the self is nothing more than a provisional name laid over five impermanent and mutually distinct phenomena, which is exactly the conclusion the non-Buddhist thinker was trying to avoid.

9. Section: If the Self Is Other Than the Aggregates, Is It Impermanent or Permanent?

Turning to the second position, that the self is other than the aggregates yet abides within them, a double question is posed at once: is this self, so conceived, impermanent, or is it permanent? If impermanent, then this very self would have to arise and cease moment by moment exactly as the aggregates do, so that when the body dies in one place, the self residing within it need not die at that very same moment, and might instead arise anew somewhere else entirely, one being's own aggregates performing an action while an entirely different self, unconnected to that action, receives its ripened result — an absurd consequence following automatically from the theory itself; and further, if one searches for this further, separate, arising-and-ceasing self within the six inner faculties, the six outer objects, or the six consciousnesses arising between them, it is nowhere to be found at all, exactly as before. If instead this self is held to be permanent, unchanging being the very mark of permanence, then this same self could undergo no variation whatsoever, whether looking toward the future or examining the present, which itself proves unreasonable, since living beings plainly do undergo variation in both. Looking toward the future, an unchanging self would have to mean no birth, no aging, no sickness, no death, and no harm ever befalling it at all, and it could never become, at one time a god, at another a human being, an animal, a hungry ghost, or a hell-being — yet birth, aging, sickness, and death, and passage among these very destinies, are plainly and constantly observed. Examining the present, an unchanging self would have to mean no pleasure and no pain arising at all in response to desirable and undesirable objects encountered through the six senses, no craving and no hostility, and no delusion either, remaining entirely unmoved by any object presented to it and never swept along by any root affliction such as greed, hatred, or delusion, nor by any secondary affliction arising from them — yet a being's own moods, attachments, and aversions plainly do shift constantly from moment to moment depending on what is encountered, which flatly contradicts the claim that the self is unchanging in the present as well. The refutation continues by drawing out still further absurd consequences that would follow if such an unchanging self were truly incapable of undertaking either wholesome or unwholesome conduct at all.

If the self is permanent, being without variation is its very mark, and if this self has no variation, then variation through either of the two conditions of the future or the present is unreasonable, whether looking to what is to come or to the present dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this dilemma, impermanent or permanent, as the classic double bind facing anyone who wants a self distinct from the aggregates yet still recognizably personal, since impermanence collapses the self into exactly the same causal flux as the aggregates it was meant to transcend, while permanence strips it of every feature, birth, death, feeling, and moral responsibility, that actually makes it recognizable as a self at all. He highlights the argument's own appeal to plain, everyday observation, a being's moods and attachments visibly shifting from moment to moment, as its real force, since no amount of abstract theorizing about an unchanging self can withstand the simple fact that the person doing the theorizing is, from one moment to the next, observably not the same.

10. Section: An Unchanging Self Would Already Be Liberated, and Sky-Flowers on a Barren Woman's Child

The refutation of the second position continues: since this unchanging self, by hypothesis, undergoes no variation in either the future or the present, it could never be benefited by pleasure, nor tainted by whatever affliction arises bound up with pleasure, nor could it ever perform wholesome or unwholesome conduct on that basis; and likewise it could never be harmed by pain, nor tainted by affliction bound up with pain, nor could it perform wholesome or unwholesome conduct on that basis either — from which it follows that, being entirely unassociated with either wholesome or unwholesome conduct in the present life, it would have no cause at all for obtaining a desirable or undesirable body in a future life, and so ought never even to be reborn at all. By this same reasoning, one should not suppose any such unchanging self exists, since it is precisely the variation of one's own inner body and mind that gives rise, in each life to come, to birth, aging, death, and every kind of change — a fact directly contradicting the theory's own implications, and thus contradicting the non-Buddhist's very own teaching. Worse, since such a self could never experience its own body's birth, aging, sickness, or death, could never experience pleasure or pain or any defiled state at all, it would already, right now, have to be utterly liberated and pure — yet this plainly contradicts what is directly perceived, since ordinary beings are observably filled with defilement and must labor by every possible means to purify body and mind in hope of a future favorable rebirth. For this reason, the theory that the self is other than the aggregates yet abides within them does not accord with reason, and this second position is thereby fully refuted. The third position is refuted still more swiftly: if the self is held to abide, apart from the aggregates altogether, within some further unconditioned dharma distinct from them, then this alleged dharma apart from the aggregates cannot even be shown to possess its own characteristic in the first place, let alone serve as a place for any self to abide in — precisely as if someone claimed to know for certain that the child of a barren woman wears a garland of sky-flowers upon its head, when a barren woman's child does not exist at all, so that nothing can truly be known about a garland upon its head either. Just so, this third theory of self is refuted, and does not accord with reason.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

It is as if someone said: I know for certain that the child of a barren woman wears a garland of sky-flowers upon its head. One should know that this theory of self is exactly the same.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the barren woman's child as one of the most memorable images in the entire Buddhist logical repertoire, precisely because it exposes a mistake easy to overlook: once a thinker has posited some further, unconditioned "place apart from the aggregates," it feels natural to go on describing what resides there, when the honest first question, whether any such place exists at all, was never actually answered. He notes the second position's own closing blow, that an unchanging self would already have to be perfectly liberated right now, as the argument's most personally pointed moment, since it invites every reader to check the claim directly against their own present experience of defilement rather than against abstract theory alone.

11. Section: All Four Theories Refuted — Only the Aggregates Make Liberation Coherent

The fourth and final position is refuted in turn: if one holds that the self is unassociated with any dharma belonging to the aggregates at all, having no aggregates whatsoever, then this self, lacking form, would not be associated with a body at all; lacking feeling, would not be associated with pleasure, pain, or any sensation at all; lacking perception, would not be associated with any of the many distinct concepts a mind forms at all; lacking mental formation, would not be associated with wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral intention at all; and lacking consciousness, would not be associated with discerning any object encountered through the senses at all. Such a self would have no basis to depend on, no feeling, no perception, no deliberation, and no discrimination whatsoever — from which it follows that this self, requiring no effort or practice of any kind, would already have to be utterly and finally liberated, entirely free of defilement from the very outset — yet this is simply not the fact of the matter, since no being is found to be liberated without practice in this way. For this reason, this fourth theory too does not accord with reason, and with its refutation, every one of the four positions by which a self could possibly be held really existent has now been shown untenable. One should therefore know that "self" and its kindred terms are, with respect to the aggregates, only provisionally established, never really existing as a thing in their own right; and it is precisely because the self does not exist, and only the aggregates truly exist, that the entire rational structure of both affliction and purification can actually be established and made coherent. Every stage of this reasoning through dependent origination — ignorance conditioning volitional formations, formations conditioning consciousness, and so on in sequence all the way to birth conditioning aging and death, and its own reversal once ignorance itself is extinguished, all the way to the final cessation of aging and death — unfolds entirely within the five aggregates alone; the Buddha taught no sixth thing beyond form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness, and the entire passage of an ordinary being into a noble one, and finally into buddhahood itself, is accomplished wholly among these five, with no further self required anywhere to make it happen.

Chinese Source

復次，由彼一切依我分別妄所計我不成就故，當知我等於諸蘊中，但假建立，非實有物。由我非有，唯有蘊故，一切雜染、清淨道理皆得成就。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's own closing turn as the true payoff of the entire preceding argument: refuting a self is never pursued here merely as a piece of abstract logic-chopping, but precisely because a real, unchanging self would make the whole Buddhist account of bondage and liberation utterly incoherent, since nothing that cannot change could ever become defiled in the first place or ever become free. He highlights the plain statement that the Buddha taught no sixth thing beyond the five aggregates as this passage's own quietly radical claim, the entire drama of saṃsāra and nirvana, ordinary being and buddha, unfolding without remainder within exactly five impermanent, interdependent processes and nothing more.

12. Section: How Affliction and Purification Are Each Accomplished, Entirely without a Self

A further topic opens, distinguishing in detail exactly how affliction and purification are each accomplished without recourse to any self. Affliction's own accomplishment is explained first: because the six inner and six outer sense-fields arise in a being's present life, ignorance-associated contact arises through them, and from this there comes to be, with respect to the body, a feeling of being either benefited or diminished; conditioned by this same feeling, craving for union arises toward whatever is beneficial and craving for separation arises toward whatever is harmful, and, resting on these very cravings, every root affliction and secondary affliction proceeds to unfold in turn; and it is for this reason that pure and impure karma, whichever a being is driven to enact, comes to arise at all. Given that afflictions and karmic action have thus arisen, a future life inevitably brings with it birth, aging, death, and every form of suffering built upon them — so that, within these impermanent aggregates alone, with no real self present anywhere, the entire rational structure of affliction is fully accomplished. Purification's own accomplishment is explained by direct contrast: through hearing the teaching of another, such as the Buddha or a noble disciple, together with one's own proper inner mental engagement in response, ignorance is gradually set aside and clarity arises in its place; through this very absence of delusion, one comes to directly understand that every feeling whatsoever, whether pleasant, painful, or neutral, is itself suffering, and through this understanding one becomes able to cut off the craving that would otherwise arise within any feeling, together with every root and secondary affliction resting upon it; and because of this, the pure and impure karma that would otherwise generate a further life to come simply ceases to arise at all. Given that both karmic action and affliction have thus been cut off, every future existence, and the birth and suffering built upon it, will never arise again — this great mass of suffering, having depended on nothing but the five aggregates from the very beginning, being thereby forever brought to rest, again with no real self present anywhere in the entire process from start to finish.

Chinese Source

謂有內外諸處生故，於現法中起無明觸，由此於身便有饒益、損減受生。由此為緣，發起和合、乖離等愛，及有依此一切煩惱、隨煩惱轉。為此義故，淨不淨業生起可得。又由他音、內正作意為因緣故，遠離無明，發起於明。由無癡故，了達諸受皆悉是苦；由此能斷於諸受中所有貪愛，及斷依此一切煩惱、若隨煩惱；由此因緣，能感後有淨不淨業不復生起。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the inner and outer sense-fields arise, ignorance-associated contact arises in the present life, and from this, with respect to the body, feelings of benefit and diminishment arise. Conditioned by this, craving for union and for separation arise, and, resting on these, all root and secondary afflictions unfold. For this reason, pure and impure karma comes to arise. And through the sound of another's teaching and one's own proper mental engagement as cause and condition, ignorance is set aside and clarity arises. Through freedom from delusion, one understands that all feelings are suffering; through this, one can cut off the craving within all feelings, and all afflictions resting upon it; by this cause and condition, the pure and impure karma that generates future existence no longer arises.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this final pairing, affliction's own accomplishment mirrored precisely by purification's, as the entire fascicle's most practically important passage so far, since it shows that removing the mistaken view of a self is not an end in itself but exactly what clears the way for ignorance-associated contact to be replaced by its own clarified counterpart, feeling by feeling, craving by its absence, all the way down the same twelve links of dependent origination that drive ordinary bondage. He highlights the passage's own insistence that both bondage and freedom happen entirely within the five aggregates, with nothing left over for a self to do, as the treatise's own answer to a natural worry many practitioners raise: if there is no self, who is it that becomes liberated — and the answer given here is that the very same five aggregates that are presently bound are precisely what become purified, no further self required to receive that freedom.

13. Section: Nine Kinds of Sentient Being, and Why Trees Do Not Truly Have Life

A concluding summary closes this whole topic: since there is no self, and only the aggregates truly exist, the entire rational structure of both affliction and purification is thereby accomplished — a being's own life is never anything more than form, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness working together, sometimes defiled and sometimes purified, and it is this alone that allows both the turning of birth and death and the final return to nirvana to actually take place. A further topic then opens: sentient beings themselves are likewise not real in the strict sense, but only provisionally designated upon these same aggregates, extending across nine kinds distinguished throughout the three realms — those without feet, such as serpents; those with two feet, such as humans and birds; those with four feet, such as cats and dogs; those with many feet, such as centipedes; those possessing form, belonging to the desire and form realms; those without form, belonging to the formless realm; those possessing perception, comprising nearly every being below the peak of existence; those without perception, namely the beings born into the fourth absorption's own heaven of non-perception; and those neither perceiving nor non-perceiving, the beings abiding at the very summit of existence in the formless realm's highest heaven. These nine kinds are established, briefly, through three causes alone: through differences in bodily movement, distinguishing the footless from the two-footed, four-footed, and many-footed; through differences in the body itself, distinguishing those with form from those without; and through differences of mind, distinguishing those with perception, without perception, or neither perceiving nor non-perceiving. Certain non-Buddhist renunciants of the naked, unbound tradition claim, by contrast, that trees and plants all possess life, reasoning that trees are observed growing exactly as beings possessing consciousness do. This claim is refuted through careful questioning: are the non-Buddhists willing to say that a tree's own growth is caused by life alone, or that some further cause of growth exists besides? If life alone is the cause, then it is unreasonable that a tree, not yet having lost that supposed life, should nonetheless sometimes fail to grow at all, as when drought withers branches while the trunk and roots remain alive yet do not increase; but if some further cause of growth exists besides mere life, such as warmth, sunlight, and rain, then a tree lacking life altogether could still grow perfectly well through that further cause on its own, making the appeal to life as its cause entirely unnecessary either way.

Chinese Source

復次，此中假立一切有情，所謂無足、二足、四足、多足、有色、無色、有想、無想、非想非非想處有情。當知如是九種有情，略由三種因緣建立，總攝一切有情之類。復有離繫出家外道，作如是說：一切樹等皆悉有命，見彼與內有命數法同增長故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, here all sentient beings are provisionally designated: those without feet, with two feet, four feet, many feet, possessing form, formless, possessing perception, without perception, and neither perceiving nor non-perceiving. One should know that these nine kinds of sentient being are established, briefly, through three causes, comprehensively including every kind of sentient being. Further, certain naked non-Buddhist renunciants say: all trees and the like possess life, since they are seen growing in the same way as the inner dharmas possessing life.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this ninefold classification of sentient beings, reduced to just three underlying causes of bodily movement, bodily form, and mental capacity, offers a genuinely economical map of the entire range of rebirth across the three realms, from a legless serpent up to the most rarefied being at the very summit of existence. He treats the coming debate with the naked non-Buddhist renunciants over whether trees possess life as a valuable historical window into how seriously ancient Indian philosophical schools argued over the boundary between sentient and insentient existence, a question with real ethical stakes for any tradition, like the Jain lineage referenced here, that grounds its precepts against harm in claims about which things actually possess life.

14. Section: Trees Neither Resemble Nor Fail to Resemble Living Beings — And Why Names Are Always Provisional

The refutation continues with two further challenges. First: do the non-Buddhists have an inferential reason to support the claim that lifeless things do not grow, or do they not? If they claim to have such a reason, no such supporting reason or example can actually be found anywhere, making the claim unreasonable; and if they admit to having no such reason at all, then asserting without any reason that it simply must be so is equally unreasonable — the Compendium of the Mahāyāna's own teaching that the ālaya-consciousness alone is the true seed of every dharma, the external world itself arising through this same internal condition, being sufficient to explain a tree's own growth without any need to posit life in the tree itself. Second: do trees and other plants resemble living, conscious beings entirely, or not entirely? If claimed to resemble them entirely, then the many respects in which trees plainly do not resemble living beings become impossible to explain — a tree's roots sink downward into the earth while only its upper portion grows, it cannot move its own body at all without the help of wind, it never once answers back no matter how long one speaks to it, it is never observed performing any wholesome or unwholesome deed, and a single severed branch can simply be grafted elsewhere to grow as an entirely new tree, none of which holds true of any living, conscious being. But if the non-Buddhists instead admit that trees do not resemble living beings entirely, then this very admission undermines their own claim, since if resemblance is what would justify attributing life to a tree, its many points of non-resemblance should equally justify denying that it has life at all. By these three challenges together, whether growth is attributed to life or some other cause, whether the claim rests on any findable reason or none, and whether trees are said to resemble living beings entirely or only partly, the theory that all trees and plants possess life fails to hold up under any of its own possible defenses. A further topic then opens: why is it known that food and drink, carriages, vases, clothing, and ornaments, each variously established upon color, smell, taste, and touch, are all themselves merely provisionally existing rather than real in their own right?

Thus, whether growth has some further cause or not, whether there is or is not a stated reason for lifeless things not growing, and whether trees resemble living beings entirely or not entirely, this theory does not accord with reason. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the tree-grafting example, a single branch cut off and grown anew elsewhere as an entirely separate tree, as an especially sharp piece of evidence against the naked non-Buddhists' claim, since no being genuinely possessing an individual life could be so straightforwardly divided and continued in two separate places at once, a point any gardener could confirm directly through their own experience. He notes the passage's own brief but significant citation of the Compendium of the Mahāyāna's ālaya-consciousness teaching, offered here almost in passing as the true explanation for why the external, seemingly living world of plants and growth arises at all, without any of it requiring the postulation of life in the way sentient beings themselves possess it.

15. Section: Why Naming Never Pervades Uniformly, Closing the Ground's Own Nature

The answer given is this: whatever is thought of as a distinct object either arises within a given gathering of color, smell, taste, and touch, or does not arise within that same gathering — food-thought arising where a gathering of color, smell, taste, and touch composes noodles, but not where that same kind of gathering composes a beverage instead; carriage-thought arising where a gathering composes a vehicle, but not where it composes a garment instead — so that, throughout every such case without exception, this naming never pervades uniformly across every possible gathering of color, smell, taste, and touch, unlike color, smell, taste, and touch themselves, which do pervade uniformly across every one of these named objects without any gap whatsoever, food, carriages, vases, clothing, and ornaments alike each depending, without exception, on some particular composition of color, smell, taste, and touch. For this reason, one should know that food, carriages, vases, clothing, and ornaments are all merely provisionally existing, while color, smell, taste, and touch themselves are really existing things. A further distinction then opens: whatever provisional object is designated upon such existing dharmas depends, briefly, on one of two kinds of underlying mass — either a single unified mass, as when an individual portion of food, a single vehicle, a garment, or an ornament is named simply in dependence on one whole aggregated mass of matter; or a mass that is not single, as when a painting, a sculpture, a house, a palace, or an army and forest is named only in dependence on many quite distinct aggregated masses of matter brought together at once, a house requiring doors, windows, kitchen, and bedroom each built of different materials, an army requiring elephants, horses, and soldiers each of an entirely different nature, all combined under one single conventional name. This closes the entire account of this ground's own nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the simple observation that a teacup's own name can never be transferred to a vase, however similar their underlying color, smell, taste, and touch might superficially seem, as this passage's own elegant proof that names never pervade their material basis the way the four sensory qualities themselves do, a small but rigorous piece of reasoning underlying the entire distinction between real and provisional existence developed across this ground. He notes the closing distinction between a single mass and a combination of many distinct masses, illustrated by a single garment against an entire palace or army, as this section's own quiet reminder that provisional naming operates at every possible scale, from the smallest everyday object up to the largest and most elaborately composite one.

16. Section: Opening the Dharmas Subsumed — Form and the Formless, Five Characteristics of What Has Form

With this ground's own nature now fully accounted for, a major new topic opens, the dharmas subsumed under it, unfolding across twenty-seven distinct establishments in turn, the first being the distinction between dharmas that have form and dharmas that are formless. What are the dharmas that have form? Briefly, there are the ten form-processing sense-fields — the eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body faculties together with form, sound, smell, taste, and touch as their objects — together with the form included within the mental object's own sphere, the sixth consciousness's own domain. These are called dharmas having form precisely because each genuinely possesses form's own particular characteristic, form itself being described through form. This having-form dharma is established, in turn, through five distinct characteristics: through its own basis in event, through its own particular characteristic, through its shared characteristic, through its realm, and through its function. Regarding its basis in event: every form whatsoever, without exception, is either one of the four primary elements themselves, earth, water, fire, and wind, or is derived from those four primary elements, this alone, briefly, comprehensively including every event belonging to form, with nothing whatsoever left over or added beyond it. Regarding its own particular characteristic, form itself further divides into three: pure form, namely the five subtle sense faculties of eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body; the object taken by pure form, namely the five corresponding objects of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch taken by those same five faculties; and the form taken by mind alone, namely the form included within the mental object's own sphere as grasped by the sixth consciousness rather than by any of the five physical faculties.

This having-form dharma is established through five distinct characteristics. What are the five? Through event, through its own characteristic, through its shared characteristic, through realm, and through function.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the opening of this new section, the dharmas subsumed under this ground, unfolding across twenty-seven distinct establishments, as a signal that the fascicle now shifts from the largely argumentative register of refuting a self toward a more purely classificatory register, carefully sorting every dharma into its proper place beginning with the most basic distinction of all, form against the formless. He highlights the threefold division of form into pure form, its object, and the form taken by mind alone as a genuinely useful map connecting the physical sense faculties, their physical objects, and the additional, more subtle form-dharmas cognized only by mental consciousness, three categories a practitioner will find echoed throughout the rest of this classificatory section.

17. Section: Three Shared Characteristics of Form, and Form's Own Two Realms

Pure form itself refers to the five faculties of eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body, each made of the four primary elements and each serving as the basis on which its corresponding consciousness depends. The object taken by pure form refers to the objects of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, themselves belonging to the same class as the sense faculty currently engaging them, called of the same class when actually arising together with a given consciousness in the act of perception, and called of a similar class when, even in the absence of that consciousness's own present activity, the pure sense faculty itself continues arising in an unbroken series of its own kind regardless. The form taken by mind alone refers to whatever image arises as the very object contemplated within meditative concentration, as when a meditator practicing the contemplation of impurity directly perceives, within concentration itself, an image of bleached bones through mental consciousness alone. Form's own shared characteristic then unfolds into three further kinds: first, whatever can be described according to its own spatial location — such as long, short, square, round, coarse, fine, upright, crooked, high, or low, each occupying its own distinctive shape in space — together with whatever can further be described according to its very appearance within that location, such as blue, yellow, red, or white; second, the very fact that pure form and the objects taken by pure form alike undergo increase and decrease; and third, the very fact that all such form, once actually touched by some contacting condition, whether by hand or foot, by a thrown stone or a wielded weapon, by heat, cold, hunger, thirst, the bite of a mosquito, the buffeting of wind and sun, or the sting of a snake or scorpion, is thereby immediately damaged and destroyed. Form's own realm is then distinguished into two: form generated by karmic action bound up with craving for the meditative realm itself is called form belonging to the conduct of form, and form generated by karmic action bound up with craving still tied to the desire realm's own instability is called form belonging to the conduct of desire, gods of the form realm alone possessing a fully adorned, luminous body arising from their own delight in concentration, while beings of the desire realm's own coarser body arises instead from ordinary defiled craving and the karmic action it drives.

Made of the four primary elements, the five pure forms on which the five consciousnesses depend, belonging to the eye and other sense-fields, are called pure form. The objects of form and the rest, of the same class as pure form's own domain, are called the object taken by pure form. The image that is the object of meditative concentration is called the form taken by mind. Form's shared characteristic is likewise of three kinds. Form generated by karmic action bound up with craving for the meditative realm is called form belonging to the conduct of form; form generated by karmic action bound up with craving for the unsettled realm is called form belonging to the conduct of desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the distinction between "of the same class," when a sense faculty and its consciousness actually operate together, and "of a similar class," when the faculty continues its own series even without present cognitive activity, as a genuinely subtle piece of Abhidharma analysis worth sitting with, since it shows that even an unused sense faculty is never simply inert but continues its own causal continuity moment to moment. He notes the contrast between form-realm devas' luminous, concentration-born bodies and desire-realm beings' own coarser, craving-driven bodies as a vivid illustration of how directly a being's own karmic history shapes even something as basic as the kind of physical form they come to inhabit.

18. Section: Closing Having-Form Dharmas, Opening the Formless by Direct Contrast

The formless realm, having entirely abandoned any craving for form, possesses no such form belonging to either realm of conduct at all, so that form's own establishment through realm is, briefly, of only these two kinds. A further distinction refines the third shared characteristic, destruction through contact: this same vulnerability to destruction does not pervade every realm equally, being absent among the desire realm's own gods, present throughout the rest of the desire realm's beings across the human, hell, hungry-ghost, and animal destinies alike; even among the desire realm's gods, form is damaged by hand, foot, stone, or weapon, as when Trāyastriṃśa gods contend in battle against the asuras, yet is never damaged by heat, cold, hunger, or thirst, since these gods' every requisite of food and enjoyment arises instantly and spontaneously the moment it is wished for, so that even hunger or thirst, when felt at all, brings no actual harm; and the form realm's own gods, free of hostility altogether and nourished by the very joy of meditative absorption itself, experience no such destruction through contact whatsoever, whether by hand and weapon or by any other condition at all. Form's final characteristic, its function, is then explained: whatever wholesome, unwholesome, or morally neutral bodily or verbal action is performed is itself called karmic form, and this completes form's own establishment through function. With all five characteristics of having-form dharmas now fully explained, formless dharmas are understood by simple and direct contrast: since they occupy no location in space, they undergo no increase or decrease and no destruction through contact of any kind, and since they are present without distinction across all three realms alike rather than confined to only two of them, they are called not karmic form but karmic mind instead — each of these same five characteristics applying to the formless in a manner precisely reversed from how it applies to form.

Chinese Source

如是諸色，由界差別，略有二種。無色界中，無如是色。若善、不善、無記身業語業，是名業色。當知是名色業差別。無色諸法，亦由五相差別建立；與此相違，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus form, distinguished by realm, is briefly of two kinds; the formless realm has no such form. Whatever wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral bodily or verbal action there is, is called karmic form; one should know this is form's own distinction through function. Formless dharmas are likewise established through five distinct characteristics; one should know their marks by direct contrast to what has just been explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing method here, explaining the formless dharmas simply by reversing each of the five characteristics already given for form, as a model of economy running throughout this entire classificatory section, trusting the reader to actively perform each reversal rather than repeating the whole analysis a second time from scratch. He highlights the detail that even desire-realm gods remain vulnerable to injury by weapon though immune to hunger and cold, while form-realm gods are free entirely from every such vulnerability, as a finely graded picture of how thoroughly a being's own realm of rebirth shapes not just their body's origin but its very susceptibility to harm.

19. Section: Two Kinds of Material Aggregate, and Three Ways Elements Combine without Separation

A further topic elaborates the material aggregate itself, briefly of two kinds: an aggregate not shared among the primary elements, meaning any aggregate in which only a single kind of primary element can actually be found, as when rock, gemstones, and pearls consist purely of the earth element, ponds, streams, and rivers purely of the water element, flame and lamplight purely of the fire element, and the winds of the four directions purely of the wind element; and an aggregate not exclusive to a single element, meaning any aggregate in which two, three, or all four kinds of primary element together can be found, as when snow, moist trees, leaves, flowers, and fruit combine earth and water, a heated gem combines fire and earth, a swaying tree combines earth, water, and wind, and a living body combines all four at once, its solidity the earth element, its fluids the water element, its warmth the fire element, and its capacity for movement and speech the wind element. Careful distinctions are then drawn among three different senses in which elements may be said to combine without separation. Elements are mixed without separation only once more than a single indivisible particle of each combines together, this describing how, even in an aggregate said to consist of only one primary element, such as stone or a gem, that single element still exists together, at the level of its subtlest particles, with the very form derived from it, the eye faculty, for instance, still requiring not just earth but also form, smell, taste, and touch to be genuinely present as its own basis. Elements occupy the same place without separation when different kinds of primary elements interpenetrate one another at a single location without displacing one another at all, exactly as red, yellow, blue, and white luminous gems placed together in one basket each continue shining without their light ever separating out from the others', two particles of the very same kind of element, by contrast, never able to occupy this same single place together at once. And two or more distinct primary elements are said to combine without separation in this fuller sense precisely when their combination arises together from a single generative cause with genuine unified function, exactly as many different powdered substances, ground fine and mixed together with water, form one single indistinguishable mass entirely unlike sesame seeds or beans simply poured into the same container, each grain still fully distinct.

Chinese Source

復次，略由二種色聚建立諸聚。一、不共大種聚，二、非不共大種聚。不共大種聚者，謂於此中，唯有一類大種可得。非不共大種聚者，謂於此中，有二大種、或多大種種類可得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, briefly, material aggregates are established through two kinds: the aggregate not shared among the primary elements, and the aggregate not exclusive to a single element. The aggregate not shared among the primary elements is one in which only a single kind of primary element can be found. The aggregate not exclusive to a single element is one in which two or more kinds of primary element can be found.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing distinction between combining "without separation" and merely being glued or bound together, as with sesame seeds held in honey or sand held in syrup, still visibly many separate grains rather than one truly unified substance, as a genuinely useful check against mistaking mere proximity for real material combination. He finds the example of a living body's own four elements — solidity, fluid, warmth, and movement — an especially direct way of bringing this otherwise quite abstract Abhidharma analysis of matter back to the reader's own immediate bodily experience.

20. Section: Five Marks of Visible Form, Opening What Has Resistance

A further establishment opens: visible dharmas are distinguished through five marks — manifest color, referring to blue, yellow, red, white, light, shadow, brightness, darkness, smoke, cloud, dust, mist, and even open space itself, each clearly and directly seen; shape-color, referring to long, short, square, round, coarse, fine, high, low, upright, or crooked form, each a visible spatial shape; expressive color, referring to walking, standing, sitting, lying down, grasping, releasing, bending, and stretching, bodily conduct that, though simply activity being carried out, is nonetheless clearly observable in its outward display; the eye faculty's own domain, referring to whatever object among manifest color, shape-color, and expressive color the eye faculty itself is capable of taking as its object; and the eye consciousness's own object of cognition, referring to that same domain as actually discerned by eye consciousness. Invisible dharmas are likewise established through five marks, understood simply through the direct reversal of each of these same five — neither manifest color, nor shape-color, nor expressive color, nor an object within the eye faculty's own domain, nor an object discerned by eye consciousness, describing instead such dharmas as sound, smell, taste, touch, and the form included within the mental object's own sphere. A further establishment then opens in turn: dharmas possessing resistance are likewise distinguished through five marks.

Chinese Source

復次，由五種相，建立有見諸法差別。何等為五？謂顯色故、形色故、表色故、眼境界故、眼識所緣故。亦由五相，建立無見諸法差別；與上相違，應知其相。復次，由五種相，建立有對諸法差別。何等為五？

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, by five marks, the distinctions among visible dharmas are established. What are the five? Manifest color, shape-color, expressive color, the eye faculty's own domain, and the eye consciousness's own object. By five marks likewise, the distinctions among invisible dharmas are established, understood by direct contrast to the above. Furthermore, by five marks, the distinctions among dharmas possessing resistance are established. What are the five?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights expressive color, the sheer visibility of ordinary bodily conduct such as walking or bending an arm, as a subtle but important addition to manifest color and shape-color, since it reminds the reader that even action itself, not merely static appearance, belongs squarely within the domain of what the eye can see and eye consciousness can discern. He notes that the treatise's own habit of establishing the negative case, here invisible dharmas, simply by reversing the positive case already given continues to serve as this entire classificatory section's chief economy, letting five carefully chosen marks do the work that might otherwise require twice as much explanation.

21. Section: Five Marks of Resistance, Opening the Strange Form Born Only of Deep Concentration

Dharmas possessing resistance are distinguished, in turn, through five marks: each occupies its own distinct place, thereby standing apart from every other; each obstructs any other material aggregate from occupying or passing through that same place, unlike space, which accommodates every dharma without obstruction whatsoever; each, once actually touched by hand, foot, stone, or weapon, is thereby damaged and destroyed; each is, without exception, an object taken by one of the sense faculties' own pure form; and each is, without exception, an object discerned by whichever consciousness arises in dependence on that same pure form — obstruction unfolding, more precisely, into two kinds, obstructing another aggregate's occupation of the very same place, and obstructing another aggregate's passage through it. Dharmas lacking resistance are established through the same five marks understood by direct reversal. A further, quite unusual topic then opens in detail: dharmas both invisible and free of resistance are established, likewise, through five marks — through their own causal condition, arising only from the conviction generated within an especially majestic root absorption, and from no lesser meditative state at all; through occupying a location, since once such form has arisen it does indeed have some place where it can be found, exactly as, when a meditator visualizes Amitābha Buddha standing directly before them, that buddha's presence within concentration genuinely occupies a location even though unseen by ordinary eyes; through its own manner of appearing, resembling, though not actually identical to, the ten ordinary form-processing sense-fields in its own self-nature; through undergoing no change whatsoever, being entirely unharmed by any touch of hand, foot, stone, or weapon; and through being the object of one particular kind of mind alone, namely this same especially majestic root absorption, and no other state of mind whatsoever.

Chinese Source

一、各據別處而安住故；二、於餘色聚容受、往來等業為障礙故；三、為手足、塊刀杖等所觸，便變壞故；四、一切皆為諸清淨色之所取故；五、一切皆為依清淨色識所緣故。復次，即由五相，應知建立無見無對諸法差別。一、因緣故；二、據處所故；三、顯現故；四、無變異故；五、所緣故。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, each occupies its own distinct place and thereby stands apart; second, each obstructs other aggregates' accommodation and passage; third, each is damaged when touched by hand, foot, stone, or weapon; fourth, each is taken by the pure sense faculties; fifth, each is discerned by consciousness depending on pure form. Furthermore, by five marks likewise, one should know that invisible, non-resistant dharmas are established: through causal condition, through occupying a location, through their manner of appearing, through undergoing no change, and through being an object of cognition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi finds this unusual category of form, invisible and free of resistance, occupying its own place yet obstructing nothing, indestructible by any ordinary contact, and visible only to an especially majestic meditative mind, one of the treatise's own most evocative pieces of Abhidharma analysis, precisely because it gives a technical name and a genuine ontological status to exactly the kind of experience a devoted practitioner reports when a visualized buddha appears vividly present before them in concentration. He highlights the visualization of Amitābha Buddha as the passage's own chosen example, a detail any reader familiar with Pure Land practice will recognize immediately, and treats it as reassurance that such visualized presence, however unlike ordinary matter, is not therefore dismissed by this treatise as unreal or merely imaginary, but is instead carefully placed within its own precise category of genuine, if highly unusual, form.

22. Section: Like Magical Transformation, Seen Only by a Majestic Mind — Opening What Has Outflow

This unusual form, invisible and free of resistance, places no obstruction whatsoever on any other aggregate's own accommodation or passage, nor is it ever an object taken by any of the ordinary pure sense faculties or discerned by consciousness depending on them, nor is it ever an object of cognition for any mind at all beyond this one especially majestic root absorption — for these reasons it is called both invisible and free of resistance. Just as not every mind whatsoever is capable of producing magical transformation, only a mind of truly great majestic power being able to accomplish this and no lesser mind besides, so too, only a mind of extremely still concentration, possessed of this same great majestic power, can serve as the condition giving rise to such invisible, non-resistant form; and just as a magically transformed object is the object of neither an ordinary undeveloped mind nor a merely concentrated but lesser mind, but belongs solely to the majestic mind capable of producing it, so too this special form belongs solely to the very mind whose own majestic concentration first gave rise to it. This closes the account of invisible, non-resistant dharmas, established through marks precisely the reverse of those already given for dharmas possessing resistance. A further major establishment then opens: dharmas having outflow and dharmas free of outflow, outflow itself being a further name for affliction, so called because affliction continuously leaks out from a being's own six sense-gates, causing that being to circle endlessly through birth and death; scholars of differing schools, however, read the term's own scope with considerable disagreement, some holding that only the fifteen sense-fields directly bound up with the five physical faculties and their five objects, together with the five corresponding forms of consciousness, count strictly as having outflow, while mind, mental objects, and mental consciousness may be either defiled or undefiled depending on the case, and others holding that even wholesome and morally neutral states of mind themselves still count as having outflow, since latent afflictive tendencies remain bound up with the entire mental continuum regardless of any single moment's own particular moral quality.

Chinese Source

是名與上五相相違，當知建立無見無對諸法差別。復次，由五相故，建立有漏諸法差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

This is called the reversal of the five marks given above, and one should know it establishes the distinctions among invisible, non-resistant dharmas. Furthermore, by five marks, the distinctions among dharmas having outflow are established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the magical-transformation simile as an especially apt closing image for this unusual category of form, since it captures exactly the point that such form is neither an ordinary illusion nor an ordinary physical object, but a genuine third kind of thing produced only by a correspondingly extraordinary cause. He notes the treatise's own candor in reporting real scholarly disagreement over the very scope of "having outflow," some traditions restricting it strictly to defiled sense-experience while others extending it to wholesome and neutral mental states too, as a healthy reminder that even so basic and frequently used a term as "outflow" carries genuine interpretive history behind it rather than one single settled meaning.

23. Section: Five Marks of What Has Outflow, Beginning with Its Own Basis in Event

Dharmas having outflow are established through five marks: through their own basis in event, through latent tendency, through association, through being an object, and through arising. Their basis in event refers to the pure inner sense faculties themselves, together with whatever external objects are associated or not associated with those faculties, and mind and its mental factors of whichever moral quality, defiled, wholesome, or neutral — none of these, considered simply as bare events, is itself outflow, yet each becomes something having outflow through one or another of the remaining four marks: a pure faculty or a moment of mind having outflow whenever its own afflictive seed remains unsevered, this being called latent tendency, and also called coarse residue; a defiled mind and its mental factors having outflow simply through their own direct association with affliction; the objects actually taken by the five sense-consciousnesses having outflow whenever those very consciousnesses, in the moment of direct perception, are themselves conjoined with affliction, this being called having outflow through being an object; and the entire cluster of mind and mental factors belonging to the six consciousnesses having outflow through arising, whenever an afflictive seed not yet permanently severed, together with an object apt to provoke affliction, together with an improper mental engagement occurring in response to that very object, together give rise, whether presently arising, already arisen, or yet to arise, to affliction itself, and likewise whenever an outer circumstance arises through the ripened result, or the further influence of that ripened result, of any unwholesome affliction previously committed.

Chinese Source

謂由事故、隨眠故、相應故、所緣故、生起故。云何有漏法事？謂清淨內色，及彼相依不相依外色，若諸染汙心心所，若善、無記心心所等。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through event, through latent tendency, through association, through being an object, and through arising. What is the event of a dharma having outflow? The pure inner forms, and the external forms associated or not associated with them, defiled mind and mental factors, and wholesome or neutral mind and mental factors.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fivefold analysis of "having outflow" as a deliberately careful piece of technical precision, distinguishing sharply between a dharma's own bare event, which is morally neutral in itself, and the further four conditions, an unsevered latent tendency, direct association with affliction, serving as affliction's own object, or arising through affliction's own causal power, any one of which is enough to make that same bare event count as having outflow. He notes that this same careful distinction matters practically as well as academically, since it clarifies that even a wholesome or neutral state of mind is not automatically free of affliction merely by virtue of its own momentary moral quality, so long as some deeper, unsevered afflictive seed still lies latent beneath it.

24. Section: Latent Tendency, Association, and Being an Object — Three Ways a Dharma Has Outflow

Regarding the remaining four marks in turn: whenever, within any pure form or within any of the mind and mental factors already described, an afflictive seed remains unharmed and unsevered, this is called latent tendency, and also coarse residue, and for as long as it remains not entirely severed without remainder, everything associated with it should be understood as having outflow through this very mark of latent tendency. Whenever mind and its mental factors are actually defiled, directly conjoined with affliction itself, this is called having outflow through association. Regarding whatever counts as an event, is an object of direct present perception, and arises through the further influence of what itself has outflow, all such objects taken by consciousness in this way are called having outflow through being an object — meaning specifically that when the five sense-consciousnesses, in the very moment of direct present perception, take the five objects as their object while conjoined with affliction, it is precisely this that is called having outflow through being an object, since anything already past or yet to come cannot itself serve as such a present object of affliction, and anything merely conceptually elaborated by mental consciousness, however vividly, is likewise not this same present, direct kind of object; only the present, direct sensory object actually seized upon by an afflicted sense-consciousness counts as having outflow in this particular sense.

Chinese Source

若於清淨諸色，及於如前所說一切心心所中，煩惱種子未害未斷，說名隨眠，亦名麤重。若彼乃至未無餘斷，當知一切由隨眠故，說名有漏。若諸染汙心心所，由相應故，說名有漏。若諸有事，若現量所行，若有漏所生增上所起，如是一切漏所緣故，名為有漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, within pure forms and within all the mind and mental factors already described, an afflictive seed remains unharmed and unsevered, this is called latent tendency, also called coarse residue. As long as it is not entirely severed without remainder, one should know that all of this has outflow through latent tendency. If mind and its mental factors are defiled, they have outflow through association. Whatever is an event, an object of direct present perception, and arises through the influence of what has outflow — all such objects taken through this affliction have outflow through being an object.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the careful restriction placed on the mark of "being an object," limited strictly to what an afflicted sense-consciousness actually perceives directly in the present moment, as an important safeguard against a much broader and less precise claim, since it would otherwise be tempting to say that anything ever thought about with affliction, including memories of the past or anticipations of the future, thereby "has outflow" in exactly the same sense. He treats this precision as characteristic of the entire fivefold analysis, each mark narrowly defined so that the overall account of what has outflow remains rigorous rather than a single vague catch-all label applied loosely to anything touched by affliction in any way whatsoever.

25. Section: Closing What Has Outflow, Opening What Is Free of It

The fifth and final mark, arising, is explained across three further aspects. Generally, whatever dharma arises, whether presently arising, already arisen, or yet to arise, does so through the coming together of three conditions: an afflictive seed not yet permanently severed, an object apt to provoke affliction actually presenting itself, and an improper mental engagement occurring in response to that very object — and every dharma so arising has outflow through this mark of arising. More specifically, within the desire realm, every ripened result belonging to the aggregates, together with whatever surrounding world arises through the further influence of that same ripened result, likewise has outflow through arising, since it proceeds entirely from unwholesome affliction. And even within the form and formless realms, wherever a morally neutral result continues to arise through affliction bound to craving for those higher realms, this too has outflow through arising, its own afflictive seed not yet severed even though its immediate moral quality is neutral rather than unwholesome. Thus, through these five marks together, event, latent tendency, association, being an object, and arising, the distinctions among dharmas having outflow are fully established. A further establishment then opens directly by contrast: dharmas free of outflow, likewise distinguished through five marks. First, certain dharmas are free of outflow simply through being free of affliction's actual manifestation in the present moment — the basis and object of any wholesome or morally neutral mind and its mental factors, so long as affliction is not currently active at all. Second, certain dharmas are free of outflow through the severing of latent tendency itself — referring to an arhat who has permanently severed every affliction to be abandoned by the path of seeing and the path of cultivation alike, all of whose remaining wholesome deeds, together with a portion of neutral bodily and verbal conduct such as ordinary comportment and skillful craft, are free of outflow precisely because no afflictive seed remains beneath them any longer.

Chinese Source

由生起故成有漏者，謂諸隨眠未永斷故，順煩惱境現在前故，於彼現起不如理作意故。由此因緣，諸所有法正生、已生、或復當生，如是一切，由生起故，說名有漏。復次，由五相故，建立無漏諸法差別。一、有諸法離諸纏故說名無漏。二、有諸法隨眠斷故，說名無漏。

Ru-Yi Translation

What becomes having outflow through arising means that, since latent tendencies are not yet permanently severed, since an object apt to provoke affliction presents itself, and since improper mental engagement occurs in response to it, all dharmas that thereby arise, whether presently, already, or yet to arise, have outflow through arising. Furthermore, by five marks, dharmas free of outflow are established. First, certain dharmas are free of outflow through being free of affliction's manifestation. Second, certain dharmas are free of outflow through the severing of latent tendency.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that even a morally neutral result arising within the form or formless realm is still classified as having outflow, since its own underlying afflictive seed, craving for those higher realms, has not actually been severed, a useful reminder that a state's outward moral neutrality in any given moment says nothing at all about whether its deeper root has genuinely been uprooted. He treats the arhat's own remaining wholesome and neutral conduct, now entirely free of outflow because every afflictive seed beneath it has been permanently severed, as this section's own hopeful counterpoint, showing that the very same kinds of ordinary activity, wholesome deeds and simple bodily comportment alike, can become genuinely outflow-free once their root affliction is gone.

26. Section: Nirvana, the Path of Seeing, and the Path of Cultivation — Closing What Is Free of Outflow

Third, certain dharmas are free of outflow through the utter cutting off of defilement itself: an arhat's every defiled mind and mental factor no longer operating at all, and it is precisely this very non-operation that reveals nirvana, this same nirvana itself being called free of outflow. Fourth, certain dharmas are free of outflow through serving as the very antidote that cuts off what is to be abandoned by the path of seeing, and through their own intrinsic nature of liberation — referring to the entire path of seeing itself, the moment a practitioner first enters the noble path and directly realizes the four truths, discernment free of all conceptual construction arising then and there as liberation in its very own nature. Fifth, certain dharmas are free of outflow through serving as the very antidote that cuts off what is to be abandoned by the path of cultivation, and through their own continuous, sustained liberation — referring to the entire transcendent path of cultivation together with the path of no further learning, the path of seeing's own liberation called simply pure, and the path of cultivation's own further, continuous liberation called radiantly bright, since it alone carries that same liberation through to its final, complete perfection. Through these five marks together, then, the distinctions among dharmas free of outflow are fully established, closing this entire topic: in short, wherever affliction fails to manifest, or wherever its own seed has been permanently severed, a dharma is thereby called free of outflow. A further major establishment then opens: dharmas bound up with contention and dharmas free of contention, contention itself referring to whatever gives rise, through anger's own unchecked power, to fighting, litigation, dispute, and rivalry among beings, and its own reversal, contention's genuine absence.

Chinese Source

三、有諸法由斷滅故，說名無漏。謂一切染汙心心所，彼不轉故，說名無漏。由彼不轉，顯了涅槃，即此涅槃說名無漏。四、有諸法是見所斷斷對治故，自性解脫故，說名無漏。謂一切見道。五、有諸法是修所斷斷對治故，自性相續解脫故，說名無漏。謂出世間一切修道及無學道。復次，由五相故，建立有諍諸法差別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, certain dharmas are free of outflow through the cutting off of defilement — all defiled mind and mental factors no longer operating, and it is this non-operation that reveals nirvana, itself called free of outflow. Fourth, certain dharmas are free of outflow through serving as the antidote cutting off what the path of seeing abandons, and through their own nature of liberation — the entire path of seeing. Fifth, certain dharmas are free of outflow through serving as the antidote cutting off what the path of cultivation abandons, and through their own continuous liberation — the transcendent path of cultivation and the path of no further learning. Furthermore, by five marks, dharmas bound up with contention are established.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction drawn here between the path of seeing's own liberation, called simply pure, and the path of cultivation's own further liberation, called radiantly bright, as a genuinely evocative pair of images for two real stages of the same journey, an initial breakthrough clarity giving way, only through sustained further practice, to a liberation carried all the way through to its own complete perfection. He notes the closing establishment of contention and its absence as a welcome shift back toward more immediately recognizable territory after this long, technical stretch on outflow, anger's own power to provoke fighting and rivalry being something every reader can recognize directly in ordinary experience rather than only through careful Abhidharma analysis.

27. Section: Five Dark Categories Born of Grasping the Aggregates, and Their Reversal

Dharmas bound up with contention are established through five marks: their basis in event, namely the five aggregates as objects of grasping; their causal condition, namely relishing attachment and relishing indulgence toward those very aggregates; their own nature, namely the aggregates' own impermanence, suffering, and liability to decay; their companion, namely the ignorance that remains oblivious to this same impermanence, suffering, and decay; and their co-arising, the very afflicted conduct of body, speech, and mind that these first four conditions together give rise to, unfolding into five dark categories, each its own source of unrest — fighting, litigation, and strife arising from craving for desired objects, from wrong views, or from evil conduct, leaving the mind never at peace through its own resentment and anger; shamelessness that dwells persistently amid whatever afflictions happen to be present, leaving the mind never at peace as affliction burns continuously within; the mistaken ascetic practices of certain renunciants and brāhmaṇas who, turning against the true path, pursue self-starvation, casting themselves into fire, or leaping from high cliffs in the false conviction that ending this life's suffering through such means brings liberation, leaving the mind never at peace through wasted effort spent on a way that leads nowhere; every kind of manifest evil conduct of body, speech, and mind, leaving the mind never at peace as it heads toward an unfortunate rebirth; and delight in further existence to come, leaving the mind never at peace since any future existence necessarily brings with it birth, aging, death, and every other form of suffering built upon them. Because these five conditions, event, causal condition, own nature, companion, and co-arising, are thus all mutually dependent upon one another, such dharmas are called bound up with contention. Dharmas free of contention are established through the very same five marks understood by direct reversal: the five aggregates entirely free of grasping, in which contention's own nature, causal condition, companion, and co-arising can nowhere be found at all — these very ungrasped aggregates comprising precepts, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation, the five qualities that distinguish a buddha or an arhat, also called the incomparable aggregates, or the aggregates free of outflow.

Ignorance regarding this very contention is called contention's companion. Through this, the five dark categories unfold, called co-arising. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the five dark categories as an unusually direct catalogue of exactly how attachment to the five aggregates plays out in an ordinary life, from open fighting and shameless indulgence in affliction, through mistaken and self-destructive ascetic practice, all the way to simple delight in continued existence, each one named here as its own distinct source of unrest rather than lumped together as one generic affliction. He highlights the mistaken self-mortifying ascetics, seeking release through starvation, fire, or a fatal fall, as this passage's own pointed warning that sincere effort and even real willingness to suffer are, on their own, no guarantee of a correct method, since only uprooting affliction itself, not merely ending this one body's present suffering, actually leads toward liberation.

28. Section: Opening Defilement — Pleasant Feeling's Own Craving

A further establishment then opens: dharmas bound up with defilement and dharmas free of defilement, distinguished through the same five marks already explained in detail for contention, applied now somewhat differently. Their basis in event remains, once again, the five aggregates as objects of grasping. Their causal condition, however, refers instead to the pleasant and joyful feelings that arise through contact at the six sense-fields. Their own nature refers to the craving that these same pleasant and joyful feelings themselves give rise to. Their companion refers to the greed and craving directly associated with those very feelings. And their co-arising refers, exactly as already explained above, to the same five dark categories, fighting and strife, shamelessness amid affliction, mistaken ascetic practice turning against the true path, every kind of manifest evil conduct, and delight in future existence to come.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, by five marks, dharmas bound up with defilement are established. What are the five? Event, causal condition, and the rest, as already explained at length above through five distinct marks.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this establishment's close parallel to the one immediately preceding it, sharing the very same five-mark framework and even the very same five dark categories of co-arising, as the treatise's own way of showing that contention and defilement are, at root, two closely related descriptions of the same underlying bondage to the five aggregates, contention emphasizing the resulting strife and unrest, defilement emphasizing instead the pleasant feeling and craving that set the whole process in motion in the first place. He notes that tracing defilement's own causal condition specifically to pleasant and joyful feeling, rather than to painful feeling, is a genuinely important point often overlooked, since it is precisely the sweetness of a pleasant sensation, not merely the sting of an unpleasant one, that most readily gives rise to the craving driving a being's own bondage.

29. Section: Closing Defilement, Opening Indulgence in the Five Desirable Objects

Explained one mark at a time: the event of what is bound up with defilement is, once again, the five aggregates as objects of grasping; its causal condition is the very pleasant and joyful feeling arising through contact at the six sense-fields; its own nature is the craving that this same contact, taken as condition, gives rise to; its companion is whatever greed and attachment accompanies that craving; and its co-arising is, exactly as already explained, the five dark categories. Because the aggregates as objects of grasping are thus mutually bound up with defilement's own nature, its causal condition, its companion, and its co-arising, such dharmas are called bound up with defilement, and dharmas free of defilement are established through this same fivefold structure understood by direct reversal, exactly as already explained for what is free of contention. A further establishment then opens: dharmas relying on indulgence and dharmas relying on renunciation, distinguished again through the same five marks. Their basis in event refers specifically to the five aggregates as objects of grasping bound to the desire realm alone; their causal condition refers to the five desirable sense objects of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch that accord with and provoke desirous craving; their own nature refers to the desirous craving itself that arises when a being possessed of such indulgence takes these five desirable objects as its very condition and object; their companion refers to the wrong aspiration and discriminative thought toward these same desired objects, arising together with improper mental engagement; and their co-arising refers, once more, to the five dark categories already explained at length. Because the desire realm's own aggregates as objects of grasping are thus held together by indulgence's causal condition, companion, and co-arising, such dharmas are called relying on indulgence.

Ru-Yi Translation

By the same five marks, dharmas free of defilement are established, understood by direct reversal, exactly as already explained for what is free of contention. Furthermore, by five marks, one should know that dharmas relying on indulgence are established. What are the five? Event, causal condition, and the rest, as already explained at length.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the treatise itself now begins to move more quickly through this long series of parallel five-mark establishments, at times simply pointing back to an already-explained pattern rather than spelling every mark out afresh, since contention, defilement, and now indulgence all trace back to essentially the same underlying bondage to the aggregates, examined each time from a slightly different angle. He highlights the restriction of "indulgence" specifically to the desire realm's own five aggregates as a useful reminder that this whole analysis concerns ordinary craving for form, sound, smell, taste, and touch in particular, setting up a natural contrast with whatever practice or attainment might instead lead a being to renounce exactly this kind of craving.

30. Section: Renunciation's Reversal, and Opening the Worldly and the Transcendent

Dharmas relying on renunciation are established through this same fivefold structure understood by direct reversal: their event is instead the form realm's own five aggregates together with the formless realm's own four aggregates, entirely removed from the desire realm's five desirable objects; their causal condition is this very removal from those desirable objects; their own nature is the removal of desirous craving itself; their companion is a mental engagement conjoined with the very intention to leave desire behind; and their co-arising is the removal of the five dark categories altogether — the Compendium of the Basis's own teaching that attachment clinging outwardly to external sense objects is called relying on indulgence, its own reversal called relying on renunciation, extending in its fullest sense all the way to an arhat who, having permanently severed every afflictive seed in this very life, has left behind the unwholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind together with all craving for any further existence across the three realms, this being renunciation's own final and complete accomplishment. A further major establishment then opens: worldly dharmas and transcendent dharmas, beginning first with the worldly.

Chinese Source

又由五相，當知建立依止出離諸法差別；與上相違，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

By the same five marks likewise, one should know that dharmas relying on renunciation are established, understood by direct reversal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the arhat's own complete renunciation, severing every afflictive seed and every trace of craving for further existence across all three realms, as the fullest and final sense in which this establishment's own reversal can be understood, well beyond the more modest renunciation of a being merely reborn in the form or formless realm while some subtler craving still remains. He notes the opening of worldly and transcendent dharmas as a fitting next step in this long classificatory sequence, since indulgence and renunciation have already drawn the reader's attention squarely toward the difference between an ordinary being's own bondage and a noble one's own freedom, a distinction this next establishment now examines directly in its own terms.

31. Section: Five Marks of the Worldly, Five Marks of the Transcendent

Worldly dharmas are established through five marks: every pure form together with the objects taken by pure form; every defiled mind and its mental factors; every morally neutral mind and its mental factors, weak enough in their own force that they cannot produce any ripened result at all; every wholesome mind and its mental factors, whether still to be severed, as with an ordinary being or a learner still bound by affliction, or already severed, as with an arhat whose remaining wholesome dharmas, still arising through conceptual discrimination rather than through non-conceptual wisdom, remain worldly nonetheless; and the invisible, non-resistant form that serves as the object of properly worldly meditative absorption alone. The Compendium of the Basis explains this plainly: whatever dharma has outflow, is bound up with contention, carries the flavor of craving, or relies on indulgence is called worldly, and even the conventional wisdom that counters these very things, arising as it still does through conceptual discrimination based on conventional truth, together with whatever it gives rise to, is likewise still called worldly; only what is contrary to all of this is called transcendent. Transcendent dharmas are then established through their own five marks: serving as the very antidote cutting off what the path of seeing abandons; serving as the very antidote cutting off what the path of cultivation abandons; liberation through freedom from all conceptual marks, referring to the śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and bodhisattvas who have already entered the wisdom of suchness utterly free of discursive elaboration, together with their own associated mind and mental factors; the invisible, non-resistant form that serves as the object of properly transcendent meditative absorption instead, as with the pure forms of earth, water, fire, wind, blue, yellow, red, and white taken as objects within the eight bases of mastery; and liberation, whether only partial, as with a learner whose affliction has been only partly overcome, or complete, as with an arhat whose affliction has been entirely overcome without remainder.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the striking detail that even an arhat's own remaining wholesome dharmas are still classified as worldly, since they continue to arise through conceptual discrimination rather than through wisdom entirely free of it, a genuinely important reminder that "worldly" and "transcendent" here track a much finer distinction than simply defiled versus pure conduct. He treats the parallel invisible, non-resistant forms serving as objects of worldly versus transcendent meditative absorption, ordinary jhāna-images on one side and the eight bases of mastery's own pure elemental colors on the other, as this passage's own vivid demonstration that even meditative experience itself is not automatically transcendent merely by being deep or refined, but only once it is genuinely conjoined with non-conceptual wisdom.

32. Section: Five Worlds a Being Falls Into, and the Seeds Behind What Is Conditioned

A further establishment then opens, dependent on this same fivefold worldly distinction: dharmas belonging to any of five worlds, the world of sentient beings, the container world they inhabit, the world of desire, the world of form, and the world of formlessness, are together called dharmas that fall — falling, that is, into continued entanglement within the three realms — while transcendent dharmas, falling into none of these five worlds at all, are called dharmas that do not fall. A further major establishment then opens: conditioned dharmas and unconditioned dharmas, conditioned dharmas established through five marks corresponding closely to the six defining features of a seed already explained elsewhere in this treatise. First, not yet having arisen in the future, corresponding to a seed's own need to await the coming together of many conditions before it can actually give rise to anything at all. Second, having already ceased in the past, corresponding to a seed's own momentary ceasing, arising and perishing constantly rather than persisting unchanged. Third, abiding in its own particular characteristic in the present, corresponding both to a seed's own fixed nature, always yielding a result of the very same moral quality as itself, and to its drawing forth specifically its own kind of result and no other. Fourth, causal continuity, referring to a seed's own uninterrupted continuation, each moment's seed giving rise to the next in an unbroken series. And fifth, resultant continuity, referring to a seed's simultaneous existence together with the very result, the actual current manifestation, that it gives rise to even as it does so — this same present manifestation, arising moment by moment from the ālaya-consciousness together with its own accompanying seeds, being what conditioned dharmas, taken all together, actually amount to.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the mapping of these five marks of conditioned existence onto the six defining features of a seed, already established earlier in this treatise's own account of the ālaya-consciousness, as a satisfying moment of synthesis, showing that the same underlying theory of momentary, causally continuous seeds giving rise to present manifestation explains both an individual dharma's own three-time structure and the entire conditioned world's continuous unfolding at once. He highlights the fivefold world established just before this, sentient beings, their container world, and the three realms of desire, form, and formlessness, as together comprising the full scope of what a being can possibly "fall into," making transcendence, by contrast, not the mere avoidance of one or two of these but a complete stepping outside all five at once.

33. Section: Neither Different Nor the Same — Why Every Question About Nirvana Is Idle Speculation

Unconditioned dharmas are established through this same fivefold structure understood by direct reversal, referring simply to nirvana, attained the moment conditioned dharmas themselves are extinguished and suchness itself is directly realized — various traditions within this treatise elsewhere counting the unconditioned as eight kinds, or six, or four, space, cessation through discernment, cessation not through discernment, stillness, the cessation of feeling and perception, and true suchness among them, yet all such counting differs only in which aspect of one single, undifferentiated reality is being named, never in the reality itself. A pointed clarification then follows: if someone asks whether nirvana is different from conditioned dharmas, one should know this is already an improper question, and any answer or reflection on it improper in turn; and precisely the same holds for asking whether it is not different, whether it is both different and not different, or whether it is neither different nor not different — every one of these four positions is nothing but idle conceptual proliferation. Why so? Because nirvana is revealed only once conditioned dharmas themselves have ceased, and yet its own mark plainly differs from theirs; to insist it is simply different, then, is already to treat as a fit subject for question, answer, and reflection something that no such treatment can actually reach, since this is a matter to be directly realized within, never adequately captured by any assertion of "different" spoken in words at all.

Ru-Yi Translation

The extinguishing of conditioned dharmas is called the attainment of nirvana. If one asks whether nirvana is different from conditioned dharmas, this is an improper question, answer, and reflection alike. So too if one asks whether it is not different, both different and not different, or neither different nor not different — all such questions, answers, and reflections are equally improper.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold rejection, refusing to say nirvana is different from conditioned dharmas, not different, both, or neither, as one of the treatise's clearest examples of the classical four-cornered logic used throughout Buddhist philosophy to point beyond conceptual thought altogether rather than settle on any one of its four possible positions. He highlights the underlying reason given here, that nirvana is revealed only by conditioned dharmas' own cessation yet cannot itself be captured by any verbal assertion of sameness or difference, as this passage's own quiet insistence that some truths are only ever known by direct realization, language itself falling short no matter how carefully the four alternatives are phrased.

34. Section: Nirvana's Own Meaning and Mark, Closing the Conditioned and Unconditioned

The remaining three positions of the fourfold question are refuted in turn by the very same reasoning: to insist nirvana is simply not different from conditioned dharmas is equally idle conceptual proliferation, since this too treats as sayable what cannot be said; to insist it is both different and not different commits both errors already given at once, and so is equally improper; and to insist, since conditioned dharmas have ceased, that nirvana must therefore be neither different nor not different, as though nothing at all remained to speak of, is likewise improper, since nirvana is a positive attainment reached precisely through that very cessation, not a bare negation or an utter absence of anything whatsoever. Nirvana's own meaning is that it is revealed by every wholesome, purifying dharma actually brought to completion through practice; and nirvana's own mark is the mark of peaceful cessation, entirely free of conceptual elaboration, known, one should understand, only through direct realization within, never through any description reachable from outside. This closes the account of conditioned and unconditioned dharmas together. A further major establishment then opens: the known, the cognized, and the object dharmas, distinguished through five marks: through their own basis in event, referring to the event of conditioned dharmas together with the event of unconditioned dharmas; through differences of category and function; through differences in the basis of wisdom, whether oriented toward benefiting oneself or benefiting others; through differences among the kinds of wisdom itself, extending from ordinary conventional wisdom up through the wisdom of the ten powers; and through differences in whatever further wisdom this same wisdom includes within it, extending to supernatural faculties, unhindered eloquence, and the like.

He notes the opening of this new establishment, the known, the cognized, and the object dharmas, as a natural transition back toward more ordinary epistemological territory, its own five marks moving from the sheer being of things toward how a wise mind actually comes to know and make use of them.

35. Section: Conventional Wisdom Knows What Is Provisionally Designated

Regarding the known dharma's own basis in event: briefly, all conditioned and unconditioned dharmas together are called the event of what is known. Regarding differences of category and function: this same event divides into five categories of the known, each with its own corresponding function or wisdom that cognizes it. Some things are known simply because they are provisionally designated; others are known because they belong to ultimate truth; others because they mark the very completion of practice; others because they concern whether another being's mind is pure or impure; and others because they concern the full differentiation of every possible kind. Regarding the first of these: conventional wisdom is what knows whatever is provisionally designated, since it is precisely this wisdom that recognizes an object as provisionally established through language in the first place, thereby faithfully knowing conventional principles such as wholesome and unwholesome conduct, blameworthy and blameless conduct, and dependent origination taught at length through the twelve links, discerning correctly which part of this should be abandoned and which cultivated, and further understanding conventional speech itself well enough to travel and act freely throughout the world according to whatever causes and conditions actually present themselves, this same conventional truth itself unfolding into four kinds: the conventional truth of the world, whereby a house, a vase, an army, or a forest is named though possessing no single unified substance of its own; the conventional truth of principle, whereby the Buddha establishes categories such as the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields based on many actual facts and their underlying rationale; the conventional truth of attainment, whereby the Buddha establishes the Four Noble Truths as the very basis for such attainments as stream-entry; and the conventional truth of the ultimate, whereby the twin emptiness of self and dharmas, though strictly beyond any such designation at all, is nonetheless provisionally named in this way simply to help give rise to the wisdom that directly realizes it. An arhat who has fully attained this conventional wisdom, having transcended the four marks that bind an ordinary being, abiding in any of the four aggregates as a home for consciousness, entanglement in discursive elaboration, the wall built of craving for desire, and the moat dug by ignorance, is thereby able to travel freely through the world in a manner that neither gods nor humans can properly recognize or grasp.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the image of an arhat traveling freely through the world unrecognized by gods or humans alike as one of the treatise's more quietly striking pictures of liberation, precisely because it is not withdrawal from the world at all but an entirely ordinary-looking presence within it, free only of the four invisible bonds, mental abiding, discursive elaboration, craving, and ignorance, that keep an ordinary being trapped without their even noticing. He highlights the fourfold classification of conventional truth, worldly, rational, attainment-based, and ultimate-as-conventional, as a genuinely useful lens for the entire treatise's own frequent use of conventionally designated terms, reminding the reader that even a phrase such as "twin emptiness of self and dharmas" is itself only a provisional pointer toward something ultimately beyond any naming at all.

36. Section: Six Knowledges of the Path of Seeing, and the Arhat's Own Certainty

Turning to the second category of the known, ultimate truth: dharma-knowledge, analogical knowledge, and the four further knowledges of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path leading to that cessation together know whatever belongs to ultimate truth — dharma-knowledge cutting off the desire realm's own view-generated affliction through undefiled insight into the Four Noble Truths there, analogical knowledge doing the same for the form and formless realms by direct comparison, and the remaining four knowledges directly realizing, in turn, the compelling nature of suffering, the way affliction gathers to produce it, its own cessation once that affliction is uprooted, and the path of precepts, concentration, and wisdom that actually leads there; and it is by genuinely knowing ultimate truth in this way that a practitioner becomes able to actually sever whatever afflictions are to be abandoned by the path of seeing and by the path of cultivation alike. Turning to the third category, the very completion of practice: the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of no further arising, both belonging to an arhat who has directly and fully realized this completion, the former knowing that every affliction throughout the three realms has been exhausted without remainder, the latter knowing that no further rebirth will ever again be received, together allow such a practitioner to know with settled certainty, entirely free of doubt, that the path once cultivated has now reached its own final and complete perfection, and free as well of any trace of the excessive pride that might otherwise mistake an incomplete attainment for one already fully realized.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the pairing of the knowledge of exhaustion with the knowledge of no further arising as a genuinely reassuring conclusion to the entire path, the first confirming that every affliction has actually been used up, the second confirming that no further rebirth driven by those same afflictions remains to come, together removing not only doubt about the path's own completion but also the subtler danger of excessive pride, mistaking a partial attainment for the real thing. He notes that this same twofold knowledge belongs, in this treatise's own account, specifically to an arhat's own direct self-verification, a built-in check ensuring that liberation is not merely believed in but actually and confidently known from the inside.

37. Section: Self-Benefiting and Other-Benefiting Wisdom, Opening the Kinds of Wisdom Itself

A bodhisattva's own eightfold way of instructing others rounds out this same category of the known: carefully investigating a disciple's own mind, then their faculties, their inclinations, and their latent tendencies in turn, showing them accordingly whichever gate of practice, the contemplation of impurity, of loving-kindness, of dependent origination, of the elements, or of the breath, best suits their own particular need, and then teaching them the conduct that properly counters the two extremes of belief in permanence and belief in annihilation, so that they may finally set aside every trace of excessive pride, mistaking what is not yet done for done, not yet attained for attained. This closes the account of category and function. Regarding the basis of wisdom, distinguished into practice benefiting oneself and practice benefiting others: whatever conventional wisdom accords with severing affliction, whatever ultimate wisdom directly severs it, and whatever wisdom knows the very completion of that severing, all these belong to the basis of benefiting oneself; while the wisdom that knows another's own inclinations and latent tendencies, together with the wisdom of the ten powers that knows every possible kind of difference among all things, both belong to the basis of benefiting others. Turning now to differences among kinds of wisdom itself: conventional wisdom, whether wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral, and whether accompanied by outflow or entirely free of it, remains, in every one of these cases, purely worldly, since it always proceeds through conceptual discrimination regardless of its own particular moral quality; even its outflow-free form is found only within the continuum of one who has already severed every affliction without remainder, and both this form and every other kind of conventional wisdom besides are together called conventional wisdom, and both remain, without exception, worldly. Dharma-knowledge, analogical knowledge, and the rest, by contrast, are understood to be entirely transcendent, and thus entirely free of outflow as well.

38. Section: A Never-Before-Attained Wisdom, and Why Even the Pure Can Still Be Called Worldly

The knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of no further arising are understood to arise only once every outflow has been exhausted, yet these same two knowledges themselves admit of a further distinction. Taken without any further conceptual elaboration, simply as the direct realization that every outflow has been permanently exhausted and that no future suffering will ever again arise, they are purely free of outflow and purely transcendent. But once a further reflective thought arises upon this same realization, thinking in so many words, "I have exhausted every outflow, my future suffering will never again arise," this same knowledge, though itself still entirely free of outflow, now proceeds through conceptual discrimination, and is for this reason called worldly as well as transcendent at once, a conventional wisdom belonging to both — a wisdom never before attained by any ordinary being or any learner still on the path, arising only within the very continuum of an arhat who has newly attained this fruit. The wisdom that knows another's mind is, by contrast, purely worldly without exception, since discerning whether another being's mind is defiled or undefiled always requires conceptual discrimination; found within the continuum of an ordinary being or a learner still bound by affliction, it has outflow, while found within the continuum of one beyond all further learning, it is entirely free of outflow. A question then naturally arises: for what reason is worldly wisdom, arising within a mind already entirely pure, nonetheless still called free of outflow at all?

39. Section: Why an Arhat's Ordinary Wisdom Is Free of Outflow, Opening What Further Wisdoms Are Subsumed

The answer is threefold: because the afflictive seed latent within an arhat's own continuum has already been permanently severed; because this same wisdom takes as its object only an undefiled mind, never a defiled one, unlike an ordinary being's or a learner's version of this same wisdom; and because its own nature is simply never associated with any affliction whatsoever — for these three reasons together, by severed latent tendency, by its object, and by its own association, this wisdom is entirely free of outflow even while remaining, in its conceptual form, thoroughly worldly. The wisdom of the ten powers, likewise, never having been attained at all before buddhahood itself is reached, belongs purely to worldly wisdom conjoined with conceptual elaboration, since even a buddha's own complete understanding of every possible kind of difference among beings and dharmas alike still proceeds through some measure of conceptually articulated knowledge. A further mark of this same establishment then opens: what further wisdom is subsumed within the five categories and their functions already explained. Supernatural knowledge, the wisdom of the gates of liberation, unhindered understanding, the wisdom free of contention, the wisdom of fulfilled aspiration, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the three foundations of mindfulness, the wisdom of every possible kind, and the buddha's own unique qualities not shared even with the two lesser vehicles — every one of these, according to its own nature, is subsumed within the very same wisdoms already explained. Of the six kinds of supernatural knowledge, the first five all belong to conventional wisdom, with outflow when found within an ordinary being or a learner and without outflow when found within one beyond further learning; the sixth, the supernatural knowledge of the exhaustion of outflow, is subsumed within the very knowledge of exhaustion and of no further arising already explained, itself purely transcendent when free of conceptual elaboration and belonging to both worldly and transcendent conventional wisdom together once conceptually elaborated, exactly as already distinguished above.

40. Section: The Three Gates of Liberation, Each Its Own Cluster of Knowledges

The three gates of liberation are each mapped, in turn, onto their own particular cluster of knowledges already explained. The gate of emptiness, contemplating that the aggregates arise merely through the coming together of causes and conditions and are themselves, in their own nature, empty of any self or anything belonging to a self, is subsumed within all eight knowledges together, dharma-knowledge, analogical knowledge, the four knowledges of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path, together with the transcendent knowledge of exhaustion and of no further arising, since a śrāvaka's own contemplation of the emptiness of self and a bodhisattva's own further contemplation of the emptiness of both self and dharmas alike may each be reached and verified through any of these eight. The gate of wishlessness, contemplating that the impermanent and suffering-laden result of rebirth within any of the three realms is nothing to be wished for at all, is subsumed instead within only six of these same knowledges, dharma-knowledge, analogical knowledge, the knowledges of suffering and of its origin, and the knowledges of exhaustion and of no further arising, excluding specifically the knowledge of cessation and the knowledge of the path, since those two instead take nirvana's own signless peace as their object rather than the three realms' own impermanence and suffering that gives rise to this particular absence of wishing.

41. Section: Passing Through Walls, Walking on Water, and Touching the Sun

The gate of signlessness, contemplating the very cessation of these same aggregates as a peaceful stillness beyond every possible mark, is subsumed within five knowledges, dharma-knowledge, analogical knowledge, the knowledge of cessation, and the knowledges of exhaustion and of no further arising, this completing the account of how each of the three gates of liberation draws on its own distinct cluster among the same underlying knowledges. Unhindered understanding, the wisdom free of contention, the wisdom of fulfilled aspiration, the ten powers, and every one of the unique buddha-dharmas not shared even with the lesser vehicles are, in turn, all subsumed within conventional wisdom, and all of them, found within an arhat's or a Tathāgata's own continuum, are entirely free of outflow. The four kinds of unhindered understanding belonging to a bodhisattva are explained in turn: unhindered understanding of dharma, an unimpeded and unretreating wisdom, gained through cultivation, regarding every dharma's own exhaustive particulars and its shared, unchanging nature alike; unhindered understanding of meaning, this same unimpeded wisdom applied instead to every dharma's own distinct aspects; unhindered understanding of terminology, applied to every dharma's own interpretive expression; and unhindered understanding of eloquence, applied to every distinct category of dharma altogether. 42. Section: Naming the Domain, the Wisdom, and Its Attainment

This domain is called supernatural precisely because the wisdom capable of producing it is able to fully receive and freely display it. The wisdom itself, called the supernatural wisdom, is that which possesses truly great and majestic power, accomplished through cultivation, itself the very fruit of a root absorption brought to full perfection, pure and radiant, free of every taint and every secondary affliction, and capable of transforming its object entirely at will — a wisdom belonging to genuine cultivated attainment, never to be confused with whatever lesser supernatural capacity a being might simply be born already possessing. It is called the supernatural wisdom precisely because it is capable of receiving and displaying this same domain. Its attainment refers to the very seed of this wisdom, once taken up and nourished by whatever conditions give rise to it, growing ever stronger in its own power and continuing on, moment after moment, without interruption — this being called the attainment of supernatural wisdom, brought about through the meditator's own contemplation of twelve mental images, including images of lightness, of pliancy, of the very nature of open space, and of body and mind acting together in full accord, cultivated precisely in order to draw forth this very power. All of these together, the domain itself, the wisdom that produces it, and the attainment through which it is achieved, gathered as one single whole, are called the penetrating realization of supernatural wisdom, "penetration" here simply naming this same unobstructed capacity to act.

43. Section: The Divine Ear, and Reading Another's Mind through the Body's Own Signs

The divine ear itself refers to the pure form belonging to the ear, attained as the fruit of cultivated concentration; the wisdom conjoined with the ear-consciousness that arises in dependence on this same divine ear, capable of hearing the words of suffering, joy, sorrow, and delight spoken by beings across all six destinies together with every other sound in the world, is called divine-ear wisdom; and its own attainment, along with the combined penetration uniting domain, wisdom, and attainment as one, follows exactly the same pattern already explained for the supernatural domain. Regarding the knowledge that reads another's mind: another being's own mind, shifting according to whichever affliction such as craving currently colors it, is called the difference of mind, itself something a practitioner learns to discern by carefully studying the bodily signs that betray each affliction in turn, cultivated through contemplating exactly how affliction transforms the body's own appearance — a being possessed by desire showing restless, fidgeting faculties and a face given to near-constant smiling; a being possessed by anger showing a habitually furrowed brow, halting and harsh speech, and a complexion that shifts and darkens; and a being possessed by delusion showing mumbled, unclear speech, confusion about even simple matters, and a manner of talking that is coarse and hard to follow — such careful observation, cultivated and repeatedly cultivated, giving rise to the very wisdom that discerns another person's mind, thoughts, and consciousness exactly as they truly are. 45. Section: Penetration and Illumination — Why Only Three of the Six Powers Are True Wisdom

A meditator who has genuinely mastered these supernatural knowledges, meeting whatever occasion actually arises, is able to display them freely, dwell peacefully within them, act for the benefit of others through them, and skillfully answer whatever question others bring, and it is precisely someone possessing all of this who is called one who has mastered supernatural knowledge. A further distinction then opens regarding their own respective scope: the first three supernatural knowledges, the supernatural domain, the divine ear, and the reading of another's mind, are penetrations alone, not illuminations, since none of them actually counters ignorance itself; the last three, recollection of past lives, the divine eye, and the exhaustion of outflow, are both penetrations and illuminations at once, since each directly counters a different kind of ignorance concerning the three times — recollection of past lives countering the mistaken belief in an eternal, unchanging self carried over from the past, the divine eye countering the mistaken belief that death is simply annihilation with nothing at all to follow, and the exhaustion of outflow countering the delusion that some presently attained state, such as deep meditative bliss, already amounts to nirvana itself. The Treatise on the Great Perfection of Wisdom draws this same distinction sharply: simply knowing that a past life occurred is mere penetration, while knowing the actual causes and karmic conditions that shaped it is illumination; simply knowing that death here leads to birth there is penetration, while knowing exactly how cause and condition connect the two without ever failing is illumination; simply exhausting the fetters without knowing whether further rebirth will or will not occur is penetration, while knowing with full assurance that no further rebirth will ever occur is illumination — these three illuminations being attained by great arhats and great pratyekabuddhas alike, yet only imperfectly, since even the greatest among them can recollect only some finite number of past lives or eons before their knowledge simply runs out, whereas a buddha alone perceives every single moment of arising, abiding, and ceasing across the whole of the past, present, and future without any limit whatsoever, and it is for this reason that a buddha alone is called perfect in both wisdom and conduct.

Furthermore, the first three supernatural knowledges are penetration alone, not illumination; the last three are both penetration and illumination, since they counter ignorance concerning the three times.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between mere penetration and genuine illumination as this section's own crucial safeguard against being dazzled by supernatural power for its own sake, since walking through walls or reading another's mind, however impressive, counters no ignorance whatsoever unless paired with the deeper insight that actually dismantles a mistaken view of self, of death, or of liberation itself. He highlights the comparison between even a great arhat's own capped, finite recollection of past lives and a buddha's own utterly unlimited perception of every arising and ceasing across all three times as this passage's own vivid way of showing what "perfect in wisdom and conduct" actually means, a completeness of realization that no lesser attainment, however extraordinary its outward display, can ever quite equal.

46. Section: The Ugly Monk Who Humbled a Proud Woman with a Miracle

Each of the six supernatural knowledges carries its own particular usefulness. The first, the supernatural domain, is able to win over beings of every different disposition, earning their genuine respect and reverence — as when a certain Vajra-woman, making an offering to a pratyekabuddha, saw an unusually unattractive monk arrive begging for alms and scornfully mocked his appearance, only to be so utterly humbled by the very same monk's later display of supernatural power that she prostrated herself in complete reverence and repented on the spot; and yet, precisely because such power so readily wins admiration, a practitioner who displays it before fully exhausting affliction courts real danger, since crowds eager to witness a miracle will come calling endlessly, leaving no time left at all for genuine practice of the path. The second, the divine ear, allows one to properly discern another's defiled or wholesome speech and skillfully rebuke whatever fault it contains in a way that brings the listener genuine joy rather than resentment, moving them to abandon evil and turn toward good. The third, the reading of another's mind, allows one to discern precisely whether a being's mind is pure or defiled and thereby to offer exactly the instruction and admonition that being actually needs. The last three together free beings from the extreme of belief in permanence and the extreme of belief in annihilation, dispel every trace of excessive pride regarding what has and has not actually been realized, and, resting on the exhaustion of outflow itself, teach the middle way that avoids both extremes, guiding beings to cultivate precepts, concentration, and wisdom toward a genuine and complete realization of nirvana rather than the mistaken conviction that meditative absorption alone is already liberation. A further topic then opens: the wisdom free of contention and the wisdom of fulfilled aspiration, taking up first how these are established among śrāvakas. Considering the force of three distinct kinds of benefit, an arhat monk of both kinds of liberation, sharp in faculties, may come to dwell continuously within the absorption free of contention, exactly as the venerable Subhūti was known to do.

Chinese Source

又初神通，能迴異類，令他於己發生尊敬。第二神通，知他所行染淨語業，能善訶責，令其歡喜。第三神通，善能知他若淨不淨心行差別，能正教授及與教誡。後三神通，能令遠離常邊、斷邊，能無顛倒離增上慢，依於漏盡宣說中道，即於此中能善教授。復次，觀察三種義勢力故，俱分解脫利根阿羅漢苾芻住無諍定。

Ru-Yi Translation

The first supernatural knowledge wins over beings of differing kinds, earning their respect. The second allows one to discern another's defiled or wholesome speech, skillfully rebuking it so as to bring joy. The third allows one to discern purity or defilement in another's mental conduct, offering fitting instruction and admonition. The last three free beings from the extremes of permanence and annihilation, free them of excessive pride, and, resting on the exhaustion of outflow, teach the middle way. Furthermore, considering the force of three kinds of benefit, an arhat monk of both kinds of liberation, sharp in faculties, dwells in the absorption free of contention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the Vajra-woman humbled by the unattractive monk's own hidden power as a genuinely memorable illustration of how supernatural display can accomplish, in a single instant, what extensive argument might never manage, dissolving her scorn into reverence and repentance far faster than any lecture could have. He treats the treatise's own candid warning, that displaying power before affliction is fully exhausted invites a stream of admirers with no time left for practice, as a valuable piece of practical caution any teacher might take to heart, extraordinary ability being no guarantee at all against becoming distracted by its own popularity. He also welcomes the arrival of Subhūti by name, a recognizable figure from the Perfection of Wisdom literature, as the treatise's own way of grounding this new topic, the absorption free of contention, in a concrete and beloved example rather than pure abstraction.

47. Section: Three Reasons an Arhat Enters the Absorption Free of Contention

Three distinct causes lead such an arhat to dwell within this same absorption free of contention. The first is a past vow: a practitioner who, while still cultivating the path, once heard of the innumerable distinct benefits belonging to this absorption free of contention, felt a genuine joy arise on hearing of them, and on the strength of that joy formed a resolute aspiration to attain it, thereafter taking that very aspiration itself as an object and cultivating it again and again with fervent resolve, so that once arhatship is finally attained, this same past vow now serves as both cause and condition drawing the mind naturally to delight in entering and dwelling within precisely this absorption. The second is remorse: a practitioner who, back when still an ordinary being driven by affliction, caused innumerable beings to fall into contention, inflicting upon them all manner of harm, injury, and hostility, and who, having now attained the fruit of arhatship, feels deep remorse and shame at this same foolish conduct of the past, thereupon diligently cultivates the absorption free of contention precisely so as never again to be the occasion of such harm. And the third is the protection of others' minds: a practitioner who, having already attained arhatship, wishes for countless beings to perform deeds that ripen into a desirable result within this very life, and wishes for them to actually receive that desirable result within this same life, and who therefore skillfully takes up dwelling in the absorption free of contention as the very means of accomplishing this wish. Through this cause and condition, the arhat cultivates the fourth meditative absorption, the furthest and most consummate of all, as the very basis for giving rise to a state of mind he thinks of as non-contention, one that protects the minds of others, so that whatever conduct he himself displays will never become, for any other being, an occasion for affliction or dispute — and it is precisely for this reason that this state is called freedom from contention.

Chinese Source

謂或有一，昔曾於彼無諍等持，聞有無量差別勝利，心生喜樂，發起勝願。由此因緣，緣彼為境，猛利意樂數數熏修。彼既證得阿羅漢已，由彼為因、由彼為緣，即於是中心樂趣入，是故今者住無諍定。又復有一，昔異生時，令諸有情起無量諍，於彼發起種種惱害瞋恨等事，今既證得阿羅漢果，於昔所行愚夫之行，生大悔愧，是故今者住無諍定。又復有一，自既證得阿羅漢果，欲令無量眾生造作順現法受可愛果業，又欲令彼於現法中受可愛果，是故方便住無諍定。由此因緣，熏修邊際第四靜慮以為依止，發生無諍想三摩地防護他心。於自所起一切威儀，終不令他起煩惱諍，是故說此名為無諍。

Ru-Yi Translation

One who, in the past, heard of the innumerable distinct benefits of the absorption free of contention, felt joy arise, and formed a resolute vow, cultivating it repeatedly with fervent resolve, now delights in dwelling in it once arhatship is attained, this vow serving as both cause and condition. One who, as an ordinary being, caused innumerable beings to fall into contention through harm and hostility, and who now, as an arhat, feels great remorse and shame at this past foolish conduct, likewise dwells in this absorption. And one who, having attained arhatship, wishes countless beings to perform deeds ripening into a desirable result in this very life and to actually receive it, skillfully dwells in this absorption as the means to that end. Through this, one cultivates the fourth absorption as the basis for a mind of non-contention protecting others' minds, so that one's own conduct never becomes an occasion for another's affliction — hence it is called freedom from contention.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three causes, an old vow, genuine remorse, and active protection of others, as together painting an unusually intimate portrait of an arhat's own inner life, showing that even complete liberation from affliction does not erase a being's own history, an old aspiration once made or an old harm once caused can still shape how a fully liberated mind chooses to direct its own practice going forward. He highlights the second cause, remorse for harm done before liberation was ever attained, as especially moving, since it suggests that even an arhat's own past conduct as an ordinary being remains something to be gently reckoned with rather than simply erased once the goal is reached.

48. Section: Hiding from Some, Revealing Himself to Others — An Arhat's Compassionate Protocol

Wishing in this way to protect others' minds, such an arhat, wherever he happens to be dwelling, whether in a village, a town, or any settled place, carefully and comprehensively surveys, one by one, the minds of every being living there, examining in turn every street, every household, and each individual being's own future state of mind. Having observed in this way, if he comes to know that a particular village, town, street, household, or being would, upon merely catching sight of him even for a moment, certainly give rise to affliction and contention, he simply withdraws and conceals himself, ensuring that no such being ever sees him at all. But if he comes to know instead that it is not the seeing but precisely the not seeing that would give rise to affliction and contention in some other being, he skillfully arranges circumstances so that this same being does come to see him. And if he comes to know that complying with some particular request would itself prevent contention from arising at all, he then goes on to consider carefully whether the matter itself, the very thing being asked of him, is wholesome or unwholesome, allowing himself to be seen and complying with the request only once he has confirmed that what is asked is itself genuinely wholesome.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Wishing to protect others' minds in this way, wherever he dwells, in a village, town, or settled place, he comprehensively surveys the minds of all beings there, examining every street, household, and individual being's future state of mind. Having observed this, if he knows that some village, town, street, household, or being would certainly give rise to affliction and contention upon merely seeing him, he withdraws and conceals himself, so that they never see him. If he knows it is the not-seeing that gives rise to affliction, he arranges for them to see him. If he knows that compliance would prevent contention, he considers whether the matter is wholesome or unwholesome, and if it is pure, he meets them and complies.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this careful, almost procedural survey, street by street and household by household, as one of the most quietly extraordinary pictures of compassion in the entire treatise, an arhat's own supernatural power turned not toward spectacle but toward the humble, patient work of simply not becoming, even by accident, a source of someone else's affliction. He highlights the final safeguard, checking that a requested matter is itself genuinely wholesome before agreeing to comply, as this passage's own reminder that even an arhat's deep wish to avoid provoking contention in others is never allowed to override discernment of right and wrong, compassion here remaining carefully yoked to wisdom rather than collapsing into mere accommodation.

49. Section: Closing Non-Contention, Opening a Śrāvaka's Wish-Fulfilling Wisdom

If the matter itself is impure, or if he observes that complying would only cause contention to arise and continue in another's own mind, he chooses, having so observed, not to meet or be seen by that being at all. He further examines with equal care whether any particular word spoken, posture assumed, manner of teaching offered, style of robe worn, or way of instructing and guiding others might itself give rise to continued affliction and contention in another's mind, and, wherever this is so, simply avoids that particular word, posture, teaching, robe, or instruction entirely. It is precisely because such an arhat dwells, for the greater part of his life, abiding and acting in accordance with this same careful discipline that he is called one who dwells free of contention. A further topic then opens: wish-fulfilling wisdom, first as it belongs to śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas. An arhat of both kinds of liberation, sharp in faculties, cultivating the fourth meditative absorption to its own furthest perfection as a basis, forms, whether within the domain of a śrāvaka's own wisdom concerning the Four Noble Truths or a pratyekabuddha's own wisdom concerning dependent origination, an aspiration of this kind: may I come to know such and such an object of knowledge. Entering the fourth absorption on the strength of this same aspiration, once absorption is actually attained, everything earlier wished to be known becomes fully and directly known. Such an arhat wishes to know things for three reasons: to benefit his own disciples, observing whether a given disciple is likely to enter the certainty of the noble path within his own remaining lifetime, or whether, should he himself pass away first, some other teacher remains able to guide that same disciple onward; to sustain the Buddhadharma itself, observing whether some ongoing project of stupas, monasteries, or the monastic community will be completed within his own lifetime, or, if not, whether another will be able to carry it forward, and likewise discerning which rulers, ministers, or influential patrons might be won over to the Dharma rather than allowed to harm it; and to know whether the world itself is presently at peace or in danger, observing whether a given land is soon to face abundance or famine, safety or fear, health or plague.

Chinese Source

彼由多分住如是住，行如是行，是故說名住無諍者。云何願智？謂俱分解脫利根阿羅漢苾芻，熏修邊際第四靜慮為依止故，若聲聞乘隨聲聞智所行境界，若獨覺乘隨獨覺智所行境界，起如是願：願我當知如是如是所知境界。從此趣入熏修邊際第四靜慮，既入定已，隨先所願一切了知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dwelling and acting in this way for the greater part of his life, such an arhat is called one who dwells free of contention. What is wish-fulfilling wisdom? An arhat of both kinds of liberation, sharp in faculties, cultivating the fourth absorption to its own perfection as a basis, forms, whether within a śrāvaka's or a pratyekabuddha's own domain of wisdom, an aspiration to know a given object; entering the fourth absorption on this strength, once absorbed, everything wished to be known becomes fully known.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats wish-fulfilling wisdom as a genuinely practical counterpart to the more dramatic supernatural knowledges already discussed, an ordinary and recognizably pastoral concern, whether a student will reach the path in time, whether a building project will be finished, whether a ruler can still be won over, driving an arhat to actually cultivate and apply this particular power. He highlights the three reasons given, benefiting disciples, sustaining the teaching, and gauging the world's own welfare, as together painting arhatship not as remote detachment from ordinary affairs but as a still deeply engaged, still deeply responsible form of care for both the Dharma's own continuation and the wellbeing of the wider world.

50. Section: The Buddha's Wish-Wisdom Knows without Limit, and Why He Does Not Shun Contention

Turning now to how these same two wisdoms belong to the Tathāgata: every buddha's own wish-fulfilling wisdom extends, without any obstruction whatsoever, across the entirety of every knowable object there is, a scope no śrāvaka's or pratyekabuddha's own wish-fulfilling wisdom, confined only to whatever was specifically wished for and nothing besides, can ever equal. A verse captures this difference precisely: effortless, unattached, unobstructed, ever abiding in stillness, able to resolve every question asked. A buddha's wish-wisdom surpasses that of the lesser vehicles through five such marks: it requires no effort at all to bring forth, functioning instead as spontaneously and effortlessly as a wish-fulfilling gem responding to any need; it clings to no knowable object whatsoever, moving through all of them entirely unimpeded; it is free of obstruction, having severed both affliction's own obstruction and its habitual residue completely; it abides always in stillness, the very obstruction of concentration itself having been entirely cut off, exactly as a verse describes: the great one walks in stillness, the great one stands in stillness, the great one sits in stillness, the great one lies down in stillness; and it is able, at any time whatsoever, to skillfully resolve every question that anyone might ask. A further topic then opens regarding the wisdom free of contention: buddhas, unlike arhats, do not repeatedly enter this same absorption free of contention at all. Why is this so? Because there exist certain beings whose own supreme benefit, such as attaining one of the four fruits of the path, can only be accomplished together with, and not apart from, the very arising of affliction in their own minds — and rather than forsake such a being's own supreme benefit merely to avoid provoking that affliction, the Buddha simply does not withdraw from it at all.

Chinese Source

若諸如來，遍於一切所知境界，智無障礙。復次，諸佛如來於無諍定而不數入。所以者何？有諸眾生勝利益事，由起煩惱俱時成辦，如來於此勝利益事不能棄捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Every Tathāgata's wisdom extends, without obstruction, across the entirety of every knowable object. Furthermore, buddhas do not repeatedly enter the absorption free of contention. Why? Because there are beings whose supreme benefit can only be accomplished together with the arising of affliction, and the Tathāgata cannot forsake that supreme benefit for their sake.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own refusal to shun contention, choosing a being's ultimate liberation over the avoidance of momentarily provoking their affliction, as a striking counterpoint to the careful contention-avoiding protocol described earlier for arhats, showing that even so basic a value as sparing others from affliction must yield when a far greater good, their own eventual entry into the noble path, genuinely depends on that affliction actually arising first. He highlights the five marks distinguishing a buddha's own wish-wisdom, especially its complete freedom from effort, as together describing a kind of knowing so thoroughly integrated into awakening itself that it no longer requires any deliberate application at all, unlike even an arhat's own wish-wisdom, which still depends on first forming and holding an aspiration.
