Fascicle 92 · 瑜伽師地論卷第九十二 · Complete

Superior Craving and Many Abidings — Complete

Fascicle 92 closes the sense-bases section (處擇攝) with the last two particular verses of its second overall verse. The first, superior-grade craving and the rest (上品貪等攝), has twelve topics: superior craving, exhortation, sorrowful abiding, observation, drawing forth, unworthiness of offerings, knowledge and liberation, cultivation, the treatise on non-self, concentration, the view of dharmas, and suffering. The second, many abidings and the rest (多住等攝), also has twelve: single abiding, distance from nirvāṇa, the brief teaching, what is inwardly realized, discussing omniscience, marks, abandoning what is trained in, action and the rest, emptiness, following along, constant abiding, and the teacher-and-disciple's dual perfection. Fascicle 93 opens by stating that the sense-bases section has been explained in full.

3. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — Superior Craving's Causes

By three causes and conditions, a person gives rise to superior-grade craving toward an object condition. What are the three? First, robustness, not frailty; second, comeliness, not homeliness; third, habituation to craving, not its abandonment.

Chinese Source

三因緣故，補特伽羅於所緣境上品貪行。何等為三？一者、康彊，非羸劣；二者、端嚴，非醜陋；三者、習貪，非捨貪。

Ru-Yi Translation

By three causes and conditions, a person gives rise to superior-grade craving toward an object condition. What are the three? First, robustness, not frailty; second, comeliness, not homeliness; third, habituation to craving, not its abandonment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the long-form explanation (長行釋), whose twelve sections correspond to the summary verse's twelve topics, beginning with the first topic, superior craving (上貪), and within it bringing forth its causes and conditions (出因緣). Per the 披尋記: robustness and frailty describe the person, comeliness and homeliness the object condition, and habituation-versus-abandonment the craving-conduct itself — together the three causes of superior craving. A healthy, vigorous body inclines the senses toward craving; a comely object, whether one's own body or another's, invites it further; and a mind long habituated to indulging craving cannot let go of it once an object appears.

4. Section: The Remedies Begin — Briefly Marking Them Individually

Further, by three kinds of remedial embrace, even a person harboring such superior-grade craving can be made to diligently cultivate the pure life within the well-taught Dharma-Vinaya, subduing the mind and bringing it to stillness — how much more so one whose craving is merely middling or slight. What are the three? First, secretly guarding the sense-doors as the basis, so as to be far removed from every extreme of sensual pleasure; second, moderation in food, and reduced sleep in the early and later watches of the night, as the basis, so as to be far removed from every extreme of self-affliction; third, foremost mindfulness and clear comprehension as the basis, so as to practice the path of renunciation along the middle way.

Chinese Source

復由三種對治攝受，尚令如是懷上品貪補特伽羅，於善說法毗奈耶中勤修梵行，調伏其心，令得寂靜，何況但懷中軟品貪微薄塵者。何等為三？一者、密護根門為所依止，遠離一切欲樂邊故；二者、於食知量、初夜後夜減省睡眠為所依止，遠離一切自苦邊故；三者、最勝正念正知為所依止，行於中道出離行故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by three kinds of remedial embrace, even a person harboring such superior-grade craving can be made to diligently cultivate the pure life within the well-taught Dharma-Vinaya, subduing the mind and bringing it to stillness — how much more so one whose craving is merely middling or slight. What are the three? First, secretly guarding the sense-doors as the basis, so as to be far removed from every extreme of sensual pleasure; second, moderation in food, and reduced sleep in the early and later watches of the night, as the basis, so as to be far removed from every extreme of self-affliction; third, foremost mindfulness and clear comprehension as the basis, so as to practice the path of renunciation along the middle way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens clarifying the remedies (明對治), beginning with briefly marking them individually (略標別) — three practices that together hold the middle way between indulgence and self-affliction: guarding the six sense-doors from grasping at signs, eating and sleeping in moderation so practice supports rather than punishes the body, and foremost mindfulness and clear comprehension carried at all times.

5. Section: Explaining the Harder Points Begins — Distinguishing Marks: the Noble Ones' First Kind, Knowing Feeling and Perception While Walking

One should know that here, one well established in mind through the four foundations of mindfulness — or, when engaged in walking and objects appear before one, if one has not grasped at signs or their finer particulars, truly knows feeling's arising, abiding, and ceasing; if one has grasped at signs and their finer particulars, truly knows perception's arising, abiding, and ceasing.

Chinese Source

當知此中，於四念住善住心者，

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that here, one well established in mind through the four foundations of mindfulness —

Chinese Source

或於行時境界現前，若不取相及與隨好，如實了知受生住滅；若取其相及與隨好，如實了知想生住滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

or, when engaged in walking and objects appear before one, if one has not grasped at signs or their finer particulars, truly knows feeling's arising, abiding, and ceasing; if one has grasped at signs and their finer particulars, truly knows perception's arising, abiding, and ceasing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens explaining the harder points (隨難釋), beginning with distinguishing marks (別辨相) — first citing the noble ones (舉賢聖) and, within them, the first kind of most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension (第一種類), starting with knowing feeling and perception and the rest while walking (於行時). The 披尋記 clarifies that "well established in mind through the four foundations of mindfulness" spans every practitioner from the attainment of the access-concentration mental application, free of heedlessness, through the once-returner. Walking through town or temple, whenever an object of the six senses appears, before grasping its composite sign one truly knows only feeling arising and ceasing; once its finer particulars are also grasped, one truly knows perception arising and ceasing.

6. Section: While Abiding

Or, while abiding, one truly knows the arising and ceasing of discursive thought caused by that grasping.

Chinese Source

或於住時，如實了知彼因尋思生住與滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or, while abiding, one truly knows the arising and ceasing of discursive thought caused by that grasping.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

The 披尋記 lists the eight kinds of discursive thought: of desire, of ill will, of harming, of kinfolk, of one's homeland, of not dying, of contempt toward others, and of family status — each arising from improper attention to a grasped sign or particular. Seated in stillness, when any of these eight arises from having grasped an object's sign, one truly observes its arising, abiding, and ceasing without being carried away by it.

7. Section: Concluding Its Name as Most Excellent, Closing "The First Kind" Entirely

By such marks of mindfulness and clear comprehension, at all times and toward every kind of object condition, one guards one's own mind exactly according to the proper standard — this is called most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension.

Chinese Source

由如是相正念正知，於一切時，於一切種所緣境界，能如正軌守護其心，是名最勝正念正知。

Ru-Yi Translation

By such marks of mindfulness and clear comprehension, at all times and toward every kind of object condition, one guards one's own mind exactly according to the proper standard — this is called most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Whether walking or abiding, toward every kind of object at every time, guarding this one thought-moment of mind according to what the Buddha's sūtras and discipline teach — this is the most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension. With this, concluding its name as most excellent (結名最勝) closes entirely, closing the first kind (第一種類2: 知受想等/結名最勝) entirely.

8. Section: The Second Kind Begins — Marking That Class

There is further a most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension belonging to one who has already attained cessation-attainment, or already attained signless concentration, or already attained the concentration free of application and sustained thought.

Chinese Source

復有最勝正念正知，謂已獲得滅盡定者，或已獲得無相定者，或已獲得無尋伺者。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is further a most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension belonging to one who has already attained cessation-attainment, or already attained signless concentration, or already attained the concentration free of application and sustained thought.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the second kind of most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension, belonging to third-fruit and fourth-fruit noble ones — those who have attained cessation-attainment or signless concentration, or attained concentrations from the second dhyāna upward, free of application and sustained thought.

9. Section: Showing It Has No Superior

One should know that, resting upon the noble abodes and the divine abodes, apart from this most excellent mindfulness-abiding-in-knowledge, there is nothing further that can surpass it.

Chinese Source

當知依止聖住天住，除此最勝正念知住，更無有餘能過上者。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, resting upon the noble abodes and the divine abodes, apart from this most excellent mindfulness-abiding-in-knowledge, there is nothing further that can surpass it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

Resting in cessation-attainment or signless concentration is called the noble abode; resting in the dhyānas and formless attainments is called the divine abode, per the Śrāvaka Stage. Among these, apart from the first dhyāna (which still has application and sustained thought), every concentration from the second dhyāna upward is said to have attained freedom from application and sustained thought.

10. Section: Distinguishing Its Marks Begins — Citing Reliance on Cessation-Attainment to Truly Know Feeling

Whether abiding just after rising from cessation-attainment, or abiding as a skillful means just before entering it, one truly knows feeling's arising, abiding, and ceasing — this is called most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension.

Chinese Source

或從滅定起已而住，或將入定方便而住，如實了知受生住滅，是名最勝正念正知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether abiding just after rising from cessation-attainment, or abiding as a skillful means just before entering it, one truly knows feeling's arising, abiding, and ceasing — this is called most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens distinguishing its marks (辨彼相), beginning with citing reliance on cessation-attainment (舉依滅定) — a third-fruit or higher noble one, whether just risen from or just about to enter cessation-attainment, truly observes only the dharma of feeling arising and ceasing, with no self found in it whatsoever.

11. Section: Citing by Example Reliance on Signless Concentration, Closing "Distinguishing Its Marks" Entirely

Just as one truly knows feeling by reliance on cessation-attainment, so too one truly knows perception by reliance on signless concentration, and truly knows whatever application and sustained thought there are by reliance on the concentration free of application and sustained thought — one should know these are likewise so.

如依滅定如實知受，依無相定如實知想、無尋伺定如實了知所有尋伺，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Just as one truly knows feeling by reliance on cessation-attainment, so too one truly knows perception by reliance on signless concentration, and truly knows whatever application and sustained thought there are by reliance on the concentration free of application and sustained thought — one should know these are likewise so.

12. Section: Clarifying That It Is Most Excellent, Closing "The Second Kind" and "Citing the Noble Ones" Entirely

Because this most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension takes up only dharmas, one does not, toward such feeling, perception, application, or sustained thought, give rise to any false discrimination of self or what belongs to self.

Chinese Source

由此最勝正念正知唯取法故，不於如是受、想、尋伺，起我、我所虛妄分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because this most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension takes up only dharmas, one does not, toward such feeling, perception, application, or sustained thought, give rise to any false discrimination of self or what belongs to self.

With this, clarifying that it is most excellent (明最勝) closes entirely, closing the second kind (第二種類4: 標彼類/顯無上/辨彼相/明最勝) entirely, which closes citing the noble ones (舉賢聖2: 第一種類/第二種類) entirely.

13. Section: Distinguishing the Foolish

If, when the various distinctions of feeling, perception, application, and sustained thought arise in the foolish, they are unable to give rise, toward feeling and the other dharmas, to the perception of mere dharma, but instead only think: "I am able to feel" — and so on, at length. ... By this cause and condition, such people lack even mindfulness and clear comprehension, how much more the most excellent kind.

Chinese Source

若諸愚夫受、想、尋伺差別生時，於受等法不能發起唯有法想，但作是念：我能領受，乃至廣說。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, when the various distinctions of feeling, perception, application, and sustained thought arise in the foolish, they are unable to give rise, toward feeling and the other dharmas, to the perception of mere dharma, but instead only think: "I am able to feel" — and so on, at length.

Chinese Source

由是因緣，彼尚無有正念正知，何況最勝。

Ru-Yi Translation

By this cause and condition, such people lack even mindfulness and clear comprehension, how much more the most excellent kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens distinguishing the foolish (簡愚夫), the second of two matters under distinguishing marks (別辨相), contrasting the noble ones just described. The 披尋記 glosses "and so on, at length" as: "I am able to discern, I am able to observe" — every experience gets bound to a self, whether exalted or debased. Unable even to perceive that these are merely arising dharmas, the unlearned ordinary being cannot notice affliction arising at all, let alone attain the most excellent mindfulness and clear comprehension that sees no self, no self's-possession, anywhere in feeling, perception, application, or sustained thought.

14. Section: Briefly Gathering the Kinds, Closing "Distinguishing Marks" Entirely

Here, the mindfulness and clear comprehension spoken of later refers to the non-returner or the arhat; one should know that the mindfulness and clear comprehension spoken of earlier spans from the attainment of the access mental application, free of heedlessness, through every ordinary being's stage, up to the once-returner.

Chinese Source

此中後說正念正知，或不還果、或阿羅漢；當知前說正念正知，從得作意無有放逸諸異生位至一來果。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, the mindfulness and clear comprehension spoken of later refers to the non-returner or the arhat; one should know that the mindfulness and clear comprehension spoken of earlier spans from the attainment of the access mental application, free of heedlessness, through every ordinary being's stage, up to the once-returner.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and a companion sūtra

With this, briefly gathering the kinds (略攝類) closes entirely, closing distinguishing marks (別辨相2: 舉賢聖/簡愚夫) entirely, which closes explaining the harder points (隨難釋2: 別辨相/略攝類) entirely, which closes clarifying the remedies (明對治2: 略標別/隨難釋) entirely, which closes superior craving (上貪), the first of the third particular verse's twelve topics, entirely.

A companion sūtra recounts the Buddha teaching at Śrāvastī: whatever does not belong to oneself should be relinquished, just as one feels no distress over leaves and twigs in the monastery grove being carried off by others, since they were never "mine." The six sense-bases likewise are neither self nor self's-possession; relinquishing attachment to them brings lasting ease. Another sūtra praises the venerable Nanda, who was famously robust, comely, and given to strong craving — exactly the three causes named in the root text — yet who overcame them through the three remedies: closing the sense-doors, moderate eating with diligent practice in the early and late watches, and accomplished mindfulness and clear comprehension while walking and abiding, guarding all directions so that worldly craving, sorrow, and unwholesome states found no outlet. The Buddha held Nanda up to the assembly as the model disciple to emulate in exactly these three practices.

15. Section: Exhortation Begins — Requesting Others to Teach, by Its Causes and Conditions

Next, by two causes and conditions, the Tathāgata himself declares that his years are advanced, his bodily strength wearied, and exhorts his śrāvaka disciples to request others to teach in his place. First, so as to make those who, relying on their youth, act only with arrogance and dwell in heedlessness, become fearful and disenchanted with themselves; second, so that in the age to come, those bhikṣus whose years have grown old and who lack the strength to remain free of doubt and regret might exhort the younger bhikṣus to proclaim the true Dharma, while those bhikṣus in the prime of their years, possessed of full strength and free of doubt and regret, teach others fearlessly.

Chinese Source

復次，由二因緣，如來自言其年衰暮，身力疲怠，勸諸聲聞請他說法。一者、為令恃其少年，專行憍傲住放逸者，自怖厭故。二者、為令於當來世，諸有苾芻其年衰老，無有勢力遠離疑悔，勸請少年諸苾芻等宣說正法；諸有苾芻其年盛美，具足勢力遠離疑悔，無所恐懼為他說法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, by two causes and conditions, the Tathāgata himself declares that his years are advanced, his bodily strength wearied, and exhorts his śrāvaka disciples to request others to teach in his place. First, so as to make those who, relying on their youth, act only with arrogance and dwell in heedlessness, become fearful and disenchanted with themselves; second, so that in the age to come, those bhikṣus whose years have grown old and who lack the strength to remain free of doubt and regret might exhort the younger bhikṣus to proclaim the true Dharma, while those bhikṣus in the prime of their years, possessed of full strength and free of doubt and regret, teach others fearlessly.

This opens the second topic, exhortation (教授), beginning with requesting others to teach by way of its causes and conditions (勸請因緣), briefly marking and listing (略標列) the two reasons the Buddha delegates teaching: to humble the young and arrogant, and to prepare a smooth succession once the elders can no longer bear the labor themselves.

19. Section: Marks of Rightly Following Instruction Begins — Marking and Listing the Four Marks

Further, by four marks, one is called able to rightly follow teaching and exhortation. First, able to analyze the distinctions among the bases, attaining the wisdom of non-self within the formations — this is purity of view. Second, regarding feelings together with the cessation of their basis, free of overweening pride, most utterly still — this is purity of view. Third, able to transcend all future suffering — this is purity of view. Fourth, able to transcend all present suffering — this is purity of view.

Chinese Source

又由四相，名能隨順教授教誡。一、能分析諸處差別，於諸行中得無我智，見清淨故。二、於諸受并所依滅，離增上慢，最極寂靜，見清淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, by four marks, one is called able to rightly follow teaching and exhortation. First, able to analyze the distinctions among the bases, attaining the wisdom of non-self within the formations — this is purity of view. Second, regarding feelings together with the cessation of their basis, free of overweening pride, most utterly still — this is purity of view.

Chinese Source

三、能超越未來諸苦，見清淨故。四、能超越現在諸苦，見清淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, able to transcend all future suffering — this is purity of view. Fourth, able to transcend all present suffering — this is purity of view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the second dimension of exhortation, the marks of one able to rightly follow instruction (能隨順相), beginning with marking and listing (標列) the four marks of purified view: understanding non-self among the bases, understanding the cessation of feeling's basis as utter stillness, and transcending suffering both to come and already present.

20. Section: Explaining Each in Turn Begins — Purity of View Through Attaining Wisdom of Non-Self

Here, analyzing the internal and external bases, the consciousnesses, contacts, feelings, perceptions, volitions, and cravings and their many distinctions, one displays non-self; by relying on the skillful principle of dependent origination, this draws forth the first purity of view.

Chinese Source

此中分析內外諸處、識、觸、受、想、思、愛眾別，顯示無我，由依緣起方便道理，能引最初正見清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, analyzing the internal and external bases, the consciousnesses, contacts, feelings, perceptions, volitions, and cravings and their many distinctions, one displays non-self; by relying on the skillful principle of dependent origination, this draws forth the first purity of view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

This opens explaining each in turn (隨釋), beginning with purity of view through attaining wisdom of non-self (得無我智見清淨). The 披尋記 catalogs the six internal bases (eye and the rest), six external bases (form and the rest), six consciousness-bodies, six contact-bodies, six feeling-bodies, six perception-bodies, six volition-bodies, and six craving-bodies — analyzing this multiplicity shows only dharmas can be found, never a self. Relying on dependent origination's skillful principle means observing all three times through mere action, mere dharma, mere fact, mere cause, and mere effect, each falling under the four principles of reliance, function, proof, and natural law — only dharmas give rise to dharmas, with no agent and no experiencer anywhere to be found.

21. Section: Purity of View Through Utmost Stillness

As brightness relies upon the lamp, as a shadow relies upon the tree — since the lamp and tree are not, the brightness and shadow are not either. This displays that feelings, caused by the distinctions among the internal and external bases, likewise follow those bases into utter cessation once the bases cease without remainder. Free of overweening pride, one truly knows nirvana as the most excellent stillness — this draws forth the second purity of view.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra

Progressing from ordinary being through the path of seeing into the path of cultivation and finally the path beyond training, this fourfold purity of view — non-self among the bases, feeling's utter stillness, and transcendence of future and present suffering alike — is what it means to rightly follow the Buddha's teaching and exhortation. With this, purity of view through transcending present suffering closes entirely, closing explaining each in turn (隨釋4: 得無我智/最極寂靜/超越當苦/超越現苦) entirely, which closes marks of rightly following instruction (能隨順相2: 標列/隨釋) entirely, and with it exhortation (教授) as a whole.

A long companion sūtra follows, recounting how the Buddha, grown too old to teach the bhikkhunīs himself, instructed the saṅgha to take turns doing so; when the rotation fell to the reluctant Venerable Nanda, the Buddha personally directed him to go. Nanda then questioned the bhikkhunī Mahāprajāpatī and her five hundred companions by decisive questioning (決擇說) — is the eye self? Is what lies beyond the eye self? Is self within the eye, or the eye within self? — and so across all six internal and external bases, the six consciousness-, contact-, feeling-, perception-, volition-, and craving-bodies, each time confirmed as neither self nor self's-possession. He then taught, through the lamp-and-oil and tree-and-shadow similes, that any pleasure arising from these impermanent bases is equally impermanent — precisely the two purities of view on non-self and utter stillness just explained. The sūtra continues at length classifying six kinds of joy, sorrow, and equanimity as either attachment-based or freedom-based (thirty-six modes in all), teaches that the Buddha's Dharma is inexhaustible even after a lifetime of study, describes the Buddha's three foundations of equanimity toward disciples who honor or ignore his teaching, and closes by likening the Buddha to an unsurpassed tamer who alone can guide beings through all eight directions of liberation — the eight progressive attainments from form-meditation through the cessation of perception and feeling. Nanda's teaching left the bhikkhunīs, and Nanda himself in his own practice, transformed. This is called the second discourse on differentiating the six sense-bases; the lecture relates the first meaning of the dual perfection of teacher and disciple (師弟二圓滿), near the end of this fascicle, to the Madhyama-āgama's discourse on differentiating the six sense-bases.

A companion sūtra follows, in which the Buddha at Śrāvastī teaches what accords with restraint and what does not. Master Cháng Bǎi matches this to the two sufferings of the treatise: the first thing to cultivate is restraint of the faculties, through which precepts can be kept, calm abiding comes easily, and insight in concentration severs affliction.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains this as the practitioner who is truly pure and free of desire, cultivating the supramundane conduct of the ultimate truth; such a one is a true field of merit, to whom offerings should be made.

A companion sūtra follows, in which the Buddha, journeying through Kośala, instructs the brāhmaṇas and householders of a town how to answer if asked whom they should not honor. Śramaṇas and brāhmaṇas who, on seeing forms, are not free of craving, desire, thirst, and longing, whose minds are not still, and whose conduct is contrary to the Dharma, are no different from worldly people, and should not be honored. If asked how such freedom can be known, they should answer that these practitioners dwell in forests and under trees, on the simplest bedding, cultivate calm abiding and insight, and keep far from what stirs delight in the senses. Master Cháng Bǎi observes that the Buddha here teaches beings how to discern true and false fields of merit, and that this is also a caution for practitioners.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that liberation of mind, conjoined with non-greed, relies on concentration and is freed from the obstructions to concentration; liberation of wisdom, conjoined with non-delusion, relies on wisdom and is freed from the obstruction of affliction. Master Cháng Bǎi sums up: this is the order of the noble path, beginning with cultivation of the faculties and going on through the precepts, mind, and wisdom, then conscience and shame, the foundations of mindfulness, calm abiding and insight, to the three knowledges and ultimate liberation.

A companion sūtra follows, set in the Bamboo Grove at Rājagṛha. A non-Buddhist renunciant named Maudgalyāyana, his hair coiled on his head (縈髮目揵連), tells the Buddha that he goes among other teachers to hear them debate for victory and for refuting others. The Buddha answers that such teachers have long debated to win or to avoid being questioned, matching the treatise's outside teachers. The Buddha's own teaching, he says, is given for the fruit and benefit of the three knowledges and of the liberation of mind and wisdom.

Questioned step by step, the Buddha explains the sequence: the seven factors of awakening complete knowledge and liberation; the four foundations of mindfulness complete the seven factors; the three excellent conducts complete the four foundations; and restraint of the six bases of contact, with conscience and shame and pure precepts, completes the three excellent conducts. He then teaches each step: staying unattached toward pleasing and displeasing objects, seeing that misconduct brings evil results now and later, contemplating the body and the rest with mindfulness so that each factor of awakening is completed in turn, and cultivating each factor relying on seclusion, freedom from desire, and cessation. Master Cháng Bǎi matches these passages to the treatise line by line. At the end of the sūtra Maudgalyāyana attains the Dharma eye, realizes the first fruit, asks to go forth, and, cultivating diligently, accomplishes arhatship.

A companion sūtra follows. The young Uttara, a disciple of the outside teacher Pārāśarya (波羅奢那), tells the Buddha that his teacher defines cultivating the faculties as the eye not seeing forms and the ear not hearing sounds. The Buddha replies that the blind would then be those who cultivate the faculties well, and Ānanda adds the same of the deaf. Asked by Ānanda, the Buddha then teaches the noble unsurpassed cultivation: on seeing a pleasing form one cultivates the Tathāgata's mindful and clearly comprehending aversion; on seeing a displeasing form, non-aversion; and for the mixed cases, aversion and non-aversion in turn, or letting go of both and abiding in right mindfulness and right knowledge. These are five phrases, and the same holds for all six faculties. Master Cháng Bǎi explains that the five classes of disciples in the treatise are matched to these five phrases.

The Buddha then teaches two further things. A noble disciple's own cultivation of the faculties knows that the pleasing, displeasing, or mixed state has arisen, stills it, and abides in supreme equanimity, as swiftly as a strong man snaps his fingers; the sūtra gives a simile for each faculty. A trainee's path, in turn, feels shame and aversion toward these states and cultivates the perception of danger, and this is right practice. The Buddha closes by saying that what a Teacher should do for his disciples has been done, and that they should now do what they ought.

Two companion sūtras follow, which the lecture numbers 230 and 231. A bhikṣu, reflecting alone, asks the Buddha how one must know and see in order to be called one who sees the Dharma. The Buddha answers by way of the two dharmas, the eye and form and the rest. From these three things meet in contact, with feeling, perception, and volition; these four formless aggregates with the form of the faculty are called a person. Upon these one sets up the names being, person, and the like; says "I see," "I hear"; and speaks of a venerable one's name, birth, clan, food, and lifespan. This, Master Cháng Bǎi explains, answers to conventional truth.

All of it is impermanent, conditioned, and suffering, and this answers to ultimate truth. The remainderless ending of that suffering, the quiet of nirvāṇa, answers to the view of unconditioned dharmas. One who so knows and sees is said to see the Dharma. In the second sūtra the Buddha sets out the same in verse: others who speak of something else only add doubt, and only the Buddha's teaching ends craving and ignorance without remainder.

He cites the Grounds with Application and Sustained Thought, fascicle 10, which asks why, if ignorance has improper attention as its cause, the teaching of dependent origination does not state it first. Ignorance is defiled in its own nature; improper attention is not, and so cannot defile ignorance, but is itself defiled by the power of ignorance. Hence the text here says that improper attention has contact of ignorance as its cause.

241. Section: Explaining the Principle Begins — Only Arising from Conditions

He cites the determination of the Grounds of the Five Consciousnesses and of Mind, fascicle 56, on what is called a dharma arisen from conditions: it has no ruler, no agent, and no experiencer, has no activity of its own, arises from causes, proceeds on many conditions, was formerly absent and now exists, and having existed scatters and ceases.

242. Section: Having No Real Activity, Closing "Emptiness" Entirely

Chinese Source

又一切法都無作用，無有少法能生少法。是故說言：此有故彼有，此生故彼生。但唯於彼因果法中，依世俗諦，假立作用，宣說此法能生彼法。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, having no real activity (辰二無實用) closes entirely, closing explaining the principle (卯三釋道理2: 唯緣生/無實用) entirely, closing emptiness in ultimate truth (寅二勝義諦空3: 略標義/廣七種/釋道理) entirely, closing emptiness (丑九空2: 世俗諦空/勝義諦空) entirely. Master Cháng Bǎi explains that every dharma depends on conditions and none is accomplished alone. He cites the Ground of Wisdom Born of Reflection, fascicle 16: formations are momentary, and since they do not so much as abide, how much less do they have activity; speaking of their arising is what is called activity and agent.

The companion is the Discourse on Emptiness in the Ultimate Meaning (第一義空經), taught at Kammāsadhamma among the Kurus. When the eye arises it comes from nowhere, and when it ceases it goes nowhere; unreal, it arises, and having arisen it perishes. There is action and result but no agent; when these aggregates cease, other aggregates continue, apart from the conventional dharma. So too with the other faculties. Master Cháng Bǎi finds all seven emptinesses here. The conventional dharma is the statement of dependent origination in words: this exists, so that exists, and ignorance conditions formations on through the whole mass of suffering, with the same in reverse for cessation. Through this one enters the emptiness of the ultimate meaning.

The difference is this: in going along with object conditions conducive to sorrow, one has deep-hearted aversion and gives rise to ill will; in going along with object conditions conducive to equanimity, one has deep-hearted dullness and gives rise to delusion.

Equanimity based on indulgence clings to form and does not pass beyond it, and equanimity based on emergence is cultivated upon discerning impermanence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, the second mark (午二第二相) closes entirely, closing the answer (巳二釋2: 第一相/第二相) entirely, closing the disciples' virtue's completeness (辰二弟子德圓滿2: 徵/釋) entirely, closing explaining individually (卯二別釋2: 師德圓滿/弟子德圓滿) entirely, closing the first meaning (寅一初義圓滿2: 總標/別釋) entirely. Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a small attainment in practice should not be rested in; one must go on to the most complete state the noble ones accomplish.

The first meaning corresponds to the Madhyama-āgama's Discourse on Distinguishing the Six Sense-Bases (分別六處經), taught at Śrāvastī, which Master Cháng Bǎi follows section by section. It teaches the six internal bases, the six contacts (更樂), and the eighteen activities of mind (十八意行): joy, sorrow, and equanimity at each of the six objects. It then teaches the thirty-six "cuts" (三十六刀), the same as the thirty-six phrases of the Teacher: joy, sorrow, and equanimity, each in six kinds based on attachment and six based on freedom from desire, which he reads against the first mark of the Teacher's virtue. The sūtra teaches relying on those based on freedom from desire to abandon those based on attachment, and equanimity with a single object in the formless attainments over equanimity with many objects in the desire realm. The four kinds of disciples, foremost in conduct, mind, mindfulness, and wisdom, may hear the Buddha for a hundred years and his teaching will not be exhausted. Like four archers who shoot at once, they quickly penetrate it, and the phrases and their meanings remain measureless. This shows the first mark of the Teacher's virtue. The three mindfulnesses (三意止) follow, the second mark: when disciples do not revere or follow the teaching, the World-Honored One is not sorrowful; when they follow it, he is not elated; when they do so at times and not at others, he is moved by neither, abiding in equanimity with constant mindfulness and knowledge. Last, a worldly trainer drives elephant, horse, or ox in only one direction, but the Buddha, the unsurpassed trainer, guides beings to all eight directions, the eight liberations, from form observing forms through the cessation of perception and feeling.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Discourse on Distinguishing Non-Contention

With this, the general conclusion (總結) closes entirely, closing the later meaning (後義圓滿3: 總標/別釋/總結) entirely, which closes the teacher-and-disciple's dual perfection (師弟二圓滿2: 初義圓滿/後義圓滿) entirely, and with it many abidings and the rest (多住等攝) and the sense-bases section (處擇攝).

A long companion sūtra follows — the Madhyama-āgama's Discourse on Distinguishing Non-Contention (分別無諍經), taught at Kammāsadhamma among the Kurus. The Buddha rejects both the pursuit of sensual pleasure and the pursuit of self-affliction as two extremes, teaching the middle way of the noble eightfold path. He likewise rejects both praise and blame as extremes in speech: one should neither pass on untrue or unbeneficial talk, nor flatter to another's face, but speak with measured pace and fitting limit; and one should follow local custom in speech without clinging to any single dialect as uniquely correct, since conventional truth is itself only a provisional designation. The sūtra then works systematically through contentious (有諍) versus non-contentious (無諍) dharmas across every dimension already covered — the pursuit of pleasure versus the middle way, praise versus even-handed teaching, unresolved versus severed affliction, outward-seeking versus inward stillness, unmeasured versus measured speech, and clinging to versus not clinging to regional dialect — concluding that both sides of merely conventional dispute remain provisional, never the true non-contention of ultimate truth. Hearing this, Subhūti and the assembly rejoiced, and Subhūti spoke a verse on true emptiness as the real practice that abandons all contentious dharmas and abides in stillness. The lecture notes this closing citation corresponds to the disciples' second mark, on swiftly severing suffering-dharmas and swiftly realizing peace.

Fascicle 93's opening root text states plainly, "thus the sense-bases section (處擇攝) has already been explained," and turns to an entirely new division: dependent origination, food, truths, and the elements (緣起食諦界擇攝).
