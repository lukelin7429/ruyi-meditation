The Whole of the Holy Life

Fascicle 25 closes out the fourteen provisions for the path with the six that remain: good companionship, hearing the true teaching, reflecting on the true teaching, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the ornaments of a genuine renunciant — bringing the entire "provisions for detachment" section of the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation to completion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 25 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Good Companionship Opens: Eight Marks of a True Spiritual Friend

Fascicle 25 opens directly onto the ninth of the fourteen path-provisions, good companionship — naming eight qualities that together define a genuinely qualified spiritual friend, the kind of teacher and companion a practitioner should actually seek out.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之四……云何名善友性？謂八因緣故，應知一切種圓滿善友性。何等為八？謂如有一，安住禁戒、具足多聞、能有所證、性多哀愍、心無厭倦、善能堪忍、無有怖畏、語具圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, the fourth part of the third chapter … What is meant by the nature of a good friend? By eight causal factors, one should understand a good friend's nature as complete in every respect. What are the eight? It is like one who abides in restraint, is possessed of extensive learning, has genuine attainment, is deeply compassionate by nature, is tireless in mind, is well able to endure, is free of fear, and is complete in speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating fascicle 25 precisely: the last of four installments covering the "provisions for detachment" within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation — fascicle 22 covered detachment and moral discipline, 23 covered restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating, 24 covered wakeful practice and clear awareness in conduct, and this fascicle closes out the remaining six provisions, beginning with good companionship.

He grounds the topic's importance in a well-known exchange from the Āgamas: Ānanda once suggested to the Buddha that good spiritual friendship is "half of the holy life." The Buddha corrected him firmly — it is not half, but the entirety of the holy life, since a practitioner in genuine spiritual company can be counted on to progress diligently, to complete the roots of faith and the other wholesome qualities, and ultimately to escape birth, old age, sickness, and death. The Buddha adds that his own path to buddhahood depended on good companionship — without the guidance he himself received, he says, he would never have been the Dīpaṃkara Buddha's prophesied heir. Cháng Bǎi also cites a companion teaching, in which "good friend" is defined by an absence of comparison-driven pride: a genuine spiritual friend never thinks "I come from a nobler family than this other monk," or "I keep the precepts better," or "my concentration is deeper," or "I am wiser," or "I receive more support" — genuine equality of regard, whatever the actual differences in attainment, is itself part of what makes someone a good friend rather than merely an accomplished one.

The eight qualities named here — moral discipline, broad learning, genuine attainment, deep compassion, tireless energy, forbearance, fearlessness, and skillful speech — will each be unpacked individually in what follows. Cháng Bǎi notes parallel lists exist elsewhere in the treatise (four marks in the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's chapter on arousing the aspiration, eight in its chapter on serving with offerings), all converging on the same basic picture: a good spiritual friend is not simply someone knowledgeable, but someone whose entire character supports another person's genuine progress on the path.

2. The First Mark: Abiding in Restraint

Chinese Source

云何名為安住禁戒？謂安住具戒，善能守護別解律儀，如前廣說樂沙門性、樂婆羅門性，為自調伏、為自寂靜、為自涅槃，修行正行。如是名為安住禁戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by abiding in restraint? It means abiding fully in discipline, skillfully guarding the vows of individual liberation, as explained at length before — delighting in the nature of a true renunciant, delighting in the nature of one who has driven out unwholesome states, practicing correctly for the sake of taming oneself, for the sake of one's own peace, and for the sake of one's own nirvana. This is called abiding in restraint.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this first quality as a direct recap of moral discipline's own six-part structure, given in full in fascicle 22: fully abiding in the precepts received, skillfully guarding the vows of individual liberation across all seven categories of ordained and lay practitioners, complete deportment that never oversteps worldly or monastic convention, complete conduct that avoids the specific households and situations already named as unsuitable, genuine dread of even minor faults, and continual willingness to keep learning the training rules — undertaken for five underlying reasons: delighting in the noble eightfold path itself, delighting in having driven out unwholesome states, and taming, calming, and ultimately extinguishing one's own afflictions entirely. A good spiritual friend, in other words, must first and foremost actually live the discipline they would guide someone else toward — the remaining seven qualities build on this foundation rather than substituting for it.

3. Milestone: The Second Mark — Extensive Learning and Its Nine Qualities

The treatise's second mark of a genuine spiritual friend is extensive learning — not mere accumulation of facts, but a specific, ninefold quality of teaching mastered through hearing, reflection, and direct cultivation.

Chinese Source

云何名為具足多聞？謂若有法宣說開示，初中後善，文義巧妙，獨一、圓滿、清白、梵行。於如是類眾多妙法，能善受持，言善通利、意善尋思、見善通達。如是名為具足多聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by extensive learning? It means that, regarding a teaching proclaimed and explained — good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, its meaning subtle and its wording skillful, unique, complete, pure, and holy — one is able to receive and retain many such excellent teachings well: fluent in their language, skillfully investigating their meaning, and thoroughly penetrating what is seen. This is called extensive learning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the nine qualities named here as the treatise's own standard description of the Dharma itself. Good in the beginning means a teaching that brings genuine joy on first hearing; good in the middle means it can actually be practiced without undue hardship, following a middle way between indulgence and self-denial; good in the end means it leads all the way to the complete removal of defilement — arhatship for a hearer-disciple, buddhahood for a bodhisattva. Its meaning is subtle because it genuinely benefits and brings ease; its wording is skillful because it is well-composed and complete; it is unique because it shares nothing with non-Buddhist teachings; it is complete because it is boundless, pointing toward what ultimately exceeds language altogether; and it is pure and undefiled because the liberation it describes is free of affliction, both in itself and in its lasting continuity — all of it summed up as the noble eightfold path, called here "holy conduct."

Beyond simply being exposed to teaching of this quality, a genuine spiritual friend must actually master it through all three stages of wisdom: fluency in the language itself (built through hearing, called the wisdom of hearing), skillful investigation of its meaning through sustained reflection (the wisdom of reflection, grasping how a teaching's parts connect into a coherent whole), and direct, unmediated insight into the nature of things themselves (the wisdom of cultivation). Cháng Bǎi stresses that only someone who has genuinely completed all three — not merely memorized or intellectually understood a teaching — is qualified to guide someone else through it; anyone short of that level is still, in this respect, a student rather than a teacher, however much accumulated knowledge they may have.

4. Milestone: The Third Mark — Genuine Attainment's Twenty Perceptions

The treatise's third mark, genuine attainment, opens with twenty specific perceptions a qualified spiritual friend must have actually realized — not merely understood conceptually — spanning impermanence, suffering, non-self, and the direct contemplation of death and decay.

Chinese Source

云何名為能有所證？謂能證得勝無常想、無常苦想、苦無我想、厭逆食想、一切世間不可樂想、有過患想、斷想、離想、滅想、死想、不淨想、青瘀想、膿爛想、破壞想、膨脹想、噉食想、血塗想、離散想、骨鎖想、觀察空想。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by genuine attainment? It means being able to realize: the perception of surpassing impermanence, the perception of impermanence as suffering, the perception of suffering as non-self, the perception of revulsion toward food, the perception of the entire world as unlovable, the perception of its inherent flaws, the perception of cutting-off, the perception of detachment, the perception of cessation, the perception of death, the perception of impurity, and the perceptions of a corpse turning livid, festering, decaying, bloating, being devoured, smeared with blood, scattered apart, and reduced to bare bone — and, finally, the perception of examined emptiness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi groups these twenty into a clear progression. The first three — surpassing impermanence, impermanence as suffering, suffering as non-self — directly counter the three fundamental distortions of ordinary perception: mistaking what is impermanent for permanent, what is suffering for pleasurable, and what is without self for a genuine self, and together open onto all three marks of existence, including the unstated fourth, nirvana's peace. Revulsion toward food and the perception of the world as universally unlovable extend the same clarity to eating and to worldly experience generally, recognizing suffering's presence even in ordinary pleasure. The perception of flaws applies specifically to meditative attainment, recognizing that any lower absorption, however pleasant, still falls short of what lies beyond it. Cutting-off, detachment, and cessation name the three stages of liberation itself — the path of seeing that removes conceptually-based afflictions, the path of cultivation that removes the deeper, innate afflictions remaining after that, and the state of nirvana this progression aims at, whether the qualified "nirvana with residue" of an arhat still living, or the final "nirvana without residue" at life's end. Death-perception is simply sober awareness that death could come at any moment, life's remaining span never actually guaranteed. And the impurity contemplation — direct meditation on a corpse through its stages of livid discoloration, festering, decomposition, bloating, being devoured by animals, being smeared with blood, falling apart, and reduced finally to bare bone — is the same nine-stage practice already introduced in fascicle 24 as an antidote to desire, now named as a qualification a spiritual friend must have genuinely realized, not merely read about. The twentieth, examined emptiness, completes the sequence: direct seeing that no permanent, unified, controlling self can be found anywhere among the five aggregates — no one born, no one aging, no one dying, no one acting, and no one receiving the results of action, only causally arising processes empty of any such core.

5. Absorptions, Immeasurables, Fruits, Powers — Genuine Attainment Continues

Chinese Source

復能證得最初靜慮、第二靜慮、第三靜慮、第四靜慮、空無邊處、識無邊處、無所有處、最後非想非非想處。又能證得慈悲喜捨，或預流果、或一來果、或不還果、或神境通、或宿住通、或天耳通、或死生通、或心差別通、或阿羅漢。具八解脫靜慮等定，有大堪能，具大勢力，能善為他現三神變教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, one can realize the first, second, third, and fourth absorptions, the sphere of infinite space, the sphere of infinite consciousness, the sphere of nothingness, and finally the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. One can also realize loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; or the fruit of stream-entry, of once-returning, or of non-returning; or the supernormal power of miraculous ability, of recollecting past lives, of the divine ear, of seeing beings' death and rebirth, or of knowing others' minds; or arhatship itself. Possessing the eight liberations and such meditative attainments, one has great capability and great strength, and is able to skillfully instruct and guide others by manifesting three kinds of miraculous display.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a broad sweep across attainments the treatise has already detailed at length elsewhere: the four form-realm absorptions and four formless attainments (already described in the Ground of Meditative Equipoise); the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity (already unpacked in fascicle 24's antidote to ill-will); the four fruits of the path from stream-entry through arhatship; and five of the six supernormal powers — miraculous ability, recollection of past lives, the divine ear, seeing beings' death and rebirth according to their karma, and knowing others' minds (the sixth, the ending of defilements, is arhatship itself, already named). He highlights the eight liberations specifically as reserved for those with genuine meditative attainment beyond the third fruit, and closes with the three miraculous displays that make a spiritual friend's attainment actually useful to others: the display of miraculous power, which inspires genuine respect and thereby genuine attentiveness in a student; the display of mind-reading, which lets a teacher accurately diagnose a student's specific mental state; and the display of instruction, which lets a teacher give exactly the guidance a specific student's capacity and temperament actually call for. Together, Cháng Bǎi notes, these three displays are what makes "genuine attainment" more than a private accomplishment — they are what allows a spiritual friend's own realization to actually reach and benefit someone else.

6. Milestone: The Fourth Mark — Deep Compassion, Mapped to the Four Immeasurables

The treatise's fourth mark, deep compassion, unpacks into five specific things a genuine spiritual friend delights in giving — each traced to one of the four immeasurable states already introduced in earlier fascicles.

Chinese Source

云何名為為性哀愍？謂於他所常起悲憐，樂與其義、樂與其利、樂與其樂、樂與猗觸、樂與安隱。如是名為為性哀愍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by deep compassion by nature? It means constantly feeling compassion for others — delighting in giving them benefit, delighting in giving them advantage, delighting in giving them ease, delighting in giving them joyful contact, and delighting in giving them peace. This is called deep compassion by nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps each of the five delights to one of the four immeasurable states. Giving benefit — freeing someone from suffering — and giving advantage — guiding them toward wholesome conduct that brings lasting good — both flow from compassion (悲), the immeasurable specifically aimed at removing others' pain. Giving ease — conduct free of any wish to harm — flows from loving-kindness (慈), the immeasurable aimed at bringing others happiness. Giving joyful contact — genuine delight at another's success or virtue, however small — flows from sympathetic joy (喜). And giving peace — freeing someone from the agitation of craving and aversion — flows from equanimity (捨). Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own Bodhisattva-bhūmi simply defines "compassion" as the complete cultivation of all four immeasurables together: someone who has genuinely perfected loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity toward all beings is, by that very fact, what this passage means by "deeply compassionate by nature" — not sentimentality, but the settled fruit of real meditative cultivation.

7. The Fifth Mark: A Tireless Mind for Teaching

Chinese Source

云何名為心無厭倦？謂善能示現、善能教導、善能讚勵、善能慶慰，處於四眾宣說正法，不辭勞倦，翹勤無惰，起發圓滿，為性好樂，發勤精進。如是名為心無厭倦。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by a tireless mind? It means being skilled at demonstrating, skilled at instructing, skilled at encouraging, and skilled at rejoicing with others — teaching the true Dharma before the fourfold assembly without begrudging the effort, diligent and never lazy, arousing complete energy, delighting in it by nature, and generating sustained diligence. This is called a tireless mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the four verbs as four distinct teaching skills. Demonstrating means revealing the four principles of reasoning already introduced earlier in the treatise — dependence, function, proof, and natural law — so a student actually grasps how cause and effect, purity and defilement, genuinely work. Instructing means, once a student has developed real confidence in the teaching, formally establishing them in the training itself, so understanding turns into actual practice. Encouraging means recognizing when a student's resolve is flagging — discouraged by their own slow progress in knowledge, conduct, or attainment — and specifically bolstering their confidence so they can keep going. And rejoicing means genuinely celebrating a student's real progress when it happens, letting them know clearly that what they did was right. Cháng Bǎi notes the first two skills concern teaching the Dharma itself, while the latter two concern tending a student's morale — both equally necessary, and both requiring a teacher willing to keep showing up for the fourfold assembly of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen without fatigue ever becoming an excuse to stop.

8. Milestone: The Sixth Mark — Forbearance, Toward Both Hostility and Hardship

The treatise's sixth mark, forbearance, covers two distinct kinds of endurance: absorbing other people's hostility without retaliation, and enduring genuine physical hardship without complaint.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能堪忍？謂罵不報罵、瞋不報瞋、打不報打、弄不報弄，堪耐椎杵。……自推己過，以業異熟為所依趣，終不於他發生憤恚，亦不懷恨隨眠不捨。……又能堪忍寒熱、饑渴、蚊蝱、風日、蛇蝎惡觸，他所干犯、磣毒語言，身內所生猛利、堅勁、辛楚、切心、奪命苦受，為性堪忍，能有容納。如是名為善能堪忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by genuine forbearance? It means not repaying abuse with abuse, not repaying anger with anger, not repaying a blow with a blow, not repaying mockery with mockery, enduring even a beating. … Attributing the fault to oneself, taking it as the ripening of one's own past actions, one never gives rise to resentment toward another, nor harbors a lasting grudge. … One is also able to endure cold and heat, hunger and thirst, mosquitoes and gnats, wind and sun, the bite of snakes and scorpions, others' unjust intrusions, cruel and poisonous speech, and even severe, sharp, agonizing, life-threatening pain arising within the body itself — enduring all of it by nature, with genuine capacity to bear it. This is called genuine forbearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first form of forbearance — absorbing hostility — as resting on a specific reflection: whatever mistreatment comes, a spiritual friend attributes it to the ripening of their own past actions rather than to the other person's malice, treating the offender merely as the occasion through which an old karmic debt comes due. This reframing, he notes, is what actually makes "difficult to endure, yet endured" possible — not gritted-teeth suppression, but a genuine shift in how the harm is understood. He cites the Ratnakūṭa Sūtra's own extended list of specific reflections a bodhisattva uses to sustain this forbearance — that harsh words are as insubstantial as an echo, that the body struck is as insubstantial as a reflection, that the anger directed at oneself is itself illusory, that praise and blame alike leave one's true qualities unchanged, that worldly gain and loss cannot disturb a mind already settled, and more in the same vein — each pointing toward the same underlying clarity about the emptiness of both the harm and the one supposedly harmed.

The second form of forbearance — enduring genuine hardship — extends the same steadiness to physical circumstance: extreme weather, insect bites, hunger, hostile speech, and even severe bodily pain, all met without the mind buckling under them. Cháng Bǎi cites the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own reasoning for why this is possible: a practitioner who reflects honestly on how much suffering was once endured pointlessly, in pursuit of nothing more than fleeting sensual pleasure, realizes that enduring far less suffering now, in pursuit of the highest good, is a trade obviously worth making. Both forms of forbearance together, Cháng Bǎi notes, come not from raw willpower but from this kind of correct reflection — seeing clearly enough that endurance stops requiring effort at all.

9. The Seventh Mark: Fearlessness Before an Audience

Chinese Source

云何名為無有怖畏？謂處大眾說正法時，心無怯劣、聲無戰掉、辯無誤失，終不由彼怯懼因緣，為諸怖畏之所逼切，腋不流汗、身毛不豎。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by fearlessness? It means that, when teaching the true Dharma before a large assembly, the mind is not timid or weak, the voice does not tremble, and one's reasoning is without error — never oppressed by fear born of timidity, the armpits do not sweat, and the body's hair does not stand on end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this mark as the natural fruit of the marks already covered rather than a separate achievement: genuine command of the teaching (extensive learning), real inner realization (genuine attainment), and a mind purified of self-clinging together produce a composure that doesn't have to be performed — no trembling voice, no sweating, no visible signs of the nervous system's fight-or-flight response, because there's fundamentally nothing at stake for a self that isn't being clung to. A spiritual friend teaching from this place, he notes, isn't fearless through bravado, but simply has nothing left to protect.

10. Milestone: The Eighth Mark — Complete Speech, and All Eight Marks Close

The treatise closes the eight marks of a genuine spiritual friend with complete speech — eight further qualities describing exactly how such a person actually communicates — bringing the full eightfold definition of good companionship to its close.

Chinese Source

云何名為語具圓滿？謂彼成就最上首語、極美妙語、甚顯了語、易悟解語、樂欲聞語、無違逆語、無所依語、無邊際語。如是名為語具圓滿，言詞巧妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by complete speech? It means being accomplished in speech that leads toward nirvana above all else, speech of the utmost beauty, speech that is thoroughly clear, speech that is easily understood, speech people delight in hearing, speech free of anything objectionable, speech free of any hidden agenda, and speech of boundless scope. This is called complete speech, skillful in every word.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the eight: speech that leads toward nirvana above all else always keeps liberation as its ultimate destination, never mere entertainment or cleverness; speech of utmost beauty has a genuinely pleasing quality, likened to the sweet-voiced kalaviṅka bird; thoroughly clear speech chooses its words and phrasing with real precision; easily understood speech explains difficult things simply, moving skillfully from the accessible to the profound; speech people delight in hearing draws out genuine meaning that keeps listeners actually engaged; speech free of anything objectionable is offered without any personal wish to be believed or admired, purely for the listener's benefit; speech free of hidden agenda is measured correctly to the occasion — neither too much nor too little, since even good teaching, given at excessive length, can leave a listener overwhelmed rather than nourished; and speech of boundless scope draws on genuinely wide learning to open a teaching's full implications rather than a narrow slice of it. Cháng Bǎi illustrates the risk of over-measure with a familiar story: a preacher, delighted that even a single farmer showed up on an otherwise empty Sunday, poured out every teaching he knew at once — only for the farmer to gently point out afterward that even one chicken can't eat a feast meant for many. With this eighth mark named, all eight qualities of a genuine spiritual friend — moral discipline, extensive learning, genuine attainment, deep compassion, a tireless mind, forbearance, fearlessness, and complete speech — are now fully unpacked.

11. Milestone: Five Things a Qualified Spiritual Friend Can Do — Beginning With Skillful Admonishment

Having established all eight qualifying marks, the treatise names five specific capabilities a genuine spiritual friend brings to actually guiding another person, opening with the delicate art of pointing out someone else's fault well.

Chinese Source

成就如是八種因緣，善能諫舉、善作憶念、善能教授、善能教誡、善說正法。云何名為善能諫舉？謂若有餘，於增上戒毀犯尸羅，於增上軌毀犯軌則，由見聞疑能正諫舉，真實不以虛妄，應時不以非時，饒益不以衰損，柔軟不以麤獷，善友不以憎嫉。如是名為善能諫舉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having accomplished these eight causal factors, one is skilled at admonishing, skilled at reminding, skilled at instructing, skilled at guiding, and skilled at teaching the true Dharma. What is meant by skillful admonishment? It means that, when another has violated the precepts or broken the code of conduct, having seen it, heard of it, or come to suspect it, one admonishes them correctly: truthfully, not falsely; at the proper time, not an improper time; for their genuine benefit, not their harm; gently, not harshly; as a true friend, not out of resentment or envy. This is called skillful admonishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five capabilities as what the eight qualifying marks actually earn a practitioner the standing to do. Skillful admonishment specifically governs how a fault should be pointed out, whether personally witnessed, reported, or merely suspected — grounded in five deliberate choices: confirmed truth rather than rumor or assumption, private and well-timed delivery rather than public embarrassment or a poorly chosen moment, a genuine wish to help rather than to diminish, gentle rather than harsh language, and motivation rooted in real friendship rather than any trace of resentment or envy. The treatise elsewhere names the exact opposite of each as the mark of a botched, harmful confrontation: speaking at the wrong time, speaking falsely, speaking without any real benefit in view, speaking harshly, and speaking with anger already present — five ways an admonishment can do more damage than the original fault it addresses. Cháng Bǎi's point is that even correcting someone else is itself a skill requiring real discernment, not simply a license to criticize freely once a fault is noticed.

12. Skillful Reminding: Recalling a Forgotten Fault

Chinese Source

云何名為善作憶念？謂令憶念先所犯罪，或法、或義。云何名為令其憶念先所犯罪？謂若有餘，先起毀犯而不能憶，善作方便，令彼憶念。告言：長老！曾於某處、某事、某時，毀犯如是如是色類。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful reminding? It means causing someone to recall a fault previously committed, or a teaching, or its meaning. What is meant by causing someone to recall a fault previously committed? It means that, when another has committed a violation but cannot recall it, one skillfully finds a means to help them remember, saying: Venerable one, at such a place, on such an occasion, at such a time, you committed a fault of such and such a kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes skillful reminding from skillful admonishment by its specific purpose: not pointing out a fault someone is currently unaware of, but helping someone recall a fault they've genuinely forgotten — supplying the concrete details (the place, the occasion, the time, the specific nature of what was done) needed to jog a lapsed memory, addressed respectfully rather than accusingly. This same skill, he notes, extends to reminding someone of a teaching or its meaning they've simply let slip from memory — a second, equally necessary application of the same basic capacity: helping someone recover what they already once knew, rather than teaching them something wholly new.

13. Skillful Reminding Completes: Forgotten Teachings and Forgotten Good Deeds

Chinese Source

云何名為令憶念法？謂若有餘，於先所聞、所受正法，獨處思念。……彼若不憶，令其憶念，或復稱述、授與令憶，或興請問、詰難令憶。……云何名為令憶念義？謂若有餘，於先所聞、所受正義，有所忘失，為作憶念，宣說開示，令新令顯。又若有善能引義利、能引梵行，久時所作、久時所說，彼若忘失，亦令憶念。如是名為善作憶念。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by causing someone to recall a teaching? It means that, when another, reflecting alone on a teaching previously heard and received, cannot recall it, one skillfully helps them remember — repeating it back, supplying it directly, or drawing it out through questions and probing inquiry. What is meant by causing someone to recall its meaning? It means that, when another has forgotten the meaning of a teaching previously heard and received, one helps them recall it, explaining and clarifying it so that it appears fresh and clear once more. And if there is some wholesome deed or word, capable of bringing real benefit and supporting pure conduct, done or spoken long ago, which they have forgotten, one likewise helps them recall it. This is called skillful reminding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi completes the full scope of skillful reminding across three objects: a forgotten fault (covered previously), a forgotten teaching or its meaning, and — perhaps the most quietly encouraging of the three — a forgotten good deed or wholesome word from one's own past, one genuinely capable of bringing real benefit if only it were remembered and built upon again. He cites the Lotus Sūtra's own image of this role: the bodhisattva Samantabhadra promising to protect anyone who forgets even a single verse of the sūtra by reciting it back to them until it's fluent again — the same basic gesture the treatise describes here in more general terms. A genuine spiritual friend, in other words, isn't only correcting what's gone wrong; a significant part of the role is simply helping someone hold onto what they already, at some point, got right.

14. Milestone: Skillful Instruction — Thirteen Kinds of Discourse for Calming and Insight

The treatise's third capability, skillful instruction, concerns specifically guiding a student into seclusion, stillness, and the meditative attention that supports calming and insight — unpacked through thirteen distinct kinds of discourse a qualified spiritual friend draws on.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能教授？謂於遠離、寂靜瑜伽作意止觀，時時隨順教授而轉，時時宣說與彼相應無倒言論。所謂能趣心離障蓋甚可愛樂尸羅言論、等持言論、聖慧言論、解脫言論、解脫智見言論、少欲言論、喜足言論、永斷言論、離欲言論、寂滅言論、損減言論、無雜言論、隨順緣性緣起言論。如是名為善能教授。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful instruction? It means constantly guiding a student, in keeping with seclusion, stillness, and meditative attention to calming and insight, and constantly setting forth discourse that accords with these, free of error — discourse capable of leading the mind toward freedom from the obstructions and hindrances, and deeply delightful in itself: discourse on discipline, on concentration, on noble wisdom, on liberation, on the knowledge and vision of liberation, on having few desires, on contentment, on permanent abandonment, on freedom from craving, on cessation, on diminishment, on freedom from admixture, and on conformity with conditionality and dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes that genuine seclusion — the foundation this entire teaching rests on — itself has three components already detailed in fascicle 30: a suitable place (quiet, dignified, safe, well-provisioned, with access to a genuine teacher and fellow practitioners), proper deportment (correctly timed walking and sitting meditation, posture, and resolve), and actual withdrawal, both physical (not living entangled with others) and mental (setting aside every defiled or neutral state of mind in favor of wholesome, meditation-supporting attention).

He then groups the thirteen kinds of discourse into three broader categories, following the Compendium of Determinations. The first five — on discipline, concentration, noble wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation — together form the "noble teaching proper," describing the five-part body of qualities (discipline, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and liberation's own self-knowledge) that mark a fully accomplished practitioner. The next two — on having few desires and on contentment — form the "teaching of renunciation," addressing desire for what's not yet obtained and satisfaction with what already has been. The remaining six form the "teaching that frees the mind from hindrances," itself divided three ways: discourse on permanent abandonment, freedom from craving, and cessation addresses the afflictions generally, tracing the path from the initial removal of conceptually-based afflictions through the deeper removal of innate ones to nirvana itself; discourse on diminishment and freedom from admixture addresses the five hindrances specifically; and discourse on conformity with dependent origination addresses the subtlest obstruction of all — fundamental ignorance — by directly explaining causality's fourfold conditions and the twelve links, so a student sees clearly that phenomena arise lawfully from causes, without either a controlling self or pure chance behind them. Cháng Bǎi's summary point: skillful instruction is not generic encouragement, but a precisely calibrated body of teaching, aimed exactly at whatever is standing between a specific student and the calming and insight they're working to develop.

15. Milestone: Skillful Guidance — Correcting, Reintegrating, and Directing a Community

The treatise's fourth capability, skillful guidance, covers a teacher's full disciplinary role: correcting a student who has genuinely erred, welcoming them back once corrected, and steadily directing a whole community toward what should be cultivated and away from what should not.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能教誡？謂於大師所說聖教，能以正法、以毗奈耶平等教誨。……如實知彼隨於一處違越毀犯，便於時時如法訶責、治罰、驅擯，令其調伏。既調伏已，如法平等受諸利養，和同、曉悟、收斂、攝受。於所應作及不應作，為令現行不現行故。於其積習及不積習，教導教誨。如是名為善能教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful guidance? It means teaching with even-handed instruction, according to the true teaching and the discipline the great Teacher himself set forth. … Knowing truthfully that someone has, in some respect, transgressed or violated the training, one rightly censures, disciplines, or, if necessary, expels them, so as to bring about their correction. Once corrected, one allows them to share equally in the community's support once more, working in harmony with them, helping them understand clearly, restraining what should be restrained, and welcoming them back. Regarding what should be done and what should not, one ensures the one occurs and the other does not; regarding habits already formed and habits not yet formed, one instructs and guides accordingly. This is called skillful guidance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes skillful guidance from skillful admonishment by scope: admonishment addresses one instance of wrongdoing directly, while guidance covers a teacher's full disciplinary authority — the various roles of preceptor, instructor, and respected senior within a monastic community, correcting genuine violations through censure, formal discipline, or, in serious cases, expulsion, always guided by the same five qualities already named for admonishment (truthful, well-timed, beneficial, gentle, and rooted in genuine goodwill, never punitive anger). Correction is never the end point, though — once genuinely reformed, the corrected student is deliberately welcomed back into full, equal participation in communal life and support, helped to understand clearly what was and wasn't actually a violation, and guided to restrain whatever in their own conduct still risks harming themselves or others. Beyond individual correction, this same guidance extends to shaping an entire community's habits over time — steadily encouraging what should be cultivated (discipline, concentration, wisdom) and discouraging what has become habitual through countless past lives of unwholesome conditioning (the deeply worn grooves of greed, hatred, and delusion) — the long, patient work of turning ingrained bad habits into good ones.

16. Milestone: Skillful Teaching of the True Dharma — From Worldly Good to the Four Noble Truths

The treatise's fifth and final capability, skillful teaching, moves through two levels: worldly teaching suited to newcomers, and the supramundane teaching of the Four Noble Truths that surpasses anything found outside the Buddha's own tradition.

Chinese Source

云何名為善說正法？謂於時時能善宣說初時所作無倒言論。所謂施論、戒論、生天之論。於諸欲中，能廣開示過患出離清淨品法。又於時時宣說超勝四種聖諦相應言論。所謂苦論、集論、滅論、道論。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful teaching of the true Dharma? It means being able, from time to time, to teach correctly what a newcomer should first take up — namely, discourse on giving, discourse on discipline, and discourse on rebirth in the heavens — and, regarding sensual desires, broadly revealing their dangers, and the pure qualities found in renouncing them. And it further means, from time to time, teaching discourse connected with the four noble truths, which surpasses all else — namely, discourse on suffering, on its origin, on its cessation, and on the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as the treatise's classic two-tier teaching sequence. For someone new to the path, teaching begins with what's immediately actionable and encouraging: the value of generosity, the value of moral discipline, and the prospect of a favorable rebirth through both — before turning, gently but honestly, to the real dangers hidden inside sensual pleasure, which is impermanent, insubstantial, and ultimately deceptive, "like a magician's illusion beguiling the unlearned," and to the genuine purity found in letting go of it. Only once this groundwork is laid does teaching turn to what "surpasses all else" — the Four Noble Truths, unique to the Buddha's own realization: suffering, its origin in craving, its actual cessation, and the eightfold path that leads there.

Cháng Bǎi illustrates the Four Noble Truths' power with a story from King Aśoka's biography. The king had arranged for a curtain to separate the women of his court from a visiting Dharma teacher, who accordingly taught them only the gentle, preliminary topics of giving, discipline, and heavenly rebirth. One woman, having broken the rule against approaching him directly, pushed the curtain aside and asked what the Buddha himself had actually realized beneath the Bodhi tree — was it only giving and discipline, or something more? The teacher answered plainly: the Buddha realized that all conditioned, defiled phenomena are suffering, like molten iron; that this suffering arises from craving, like a poisonous tree; and that the eightfold path extinguishes both the suffering and its cause. Hearing this, the woman attained stream-entry on the spot, then presented herself to the king with a knife at her own throat, confessing she had broken his rule out of genuine thirst for the Dharma, "the way a parched ox will not avoid death to reach water." The king, moved rather than angered on learning she had gained the Dharma-eye through this exchange, abolished the curtain rule entirely, decreeing that any woman who wished to hear the teaching directly could do so from then on. Cháng Bǎi's point in retelling it: the Four Noble Truths, taught plainly and directly, can accomplish in a single hearing what gentler preliminary teaching alone cannot.

17. Milestone: Skillful Teaching Completes, and All Eight Marks Define a "Good Friend"

The treatise finishes describing what makes teaching genuinely skillful — well-composed speech, teaching given in accordance with the listener, and teaching offered with a pure heart — then closes the entire account of good companionship with a single formula naming all eight marks and five capabilities together.

Chinese Source

又此言論應時而發、殷重、漸次、相續、俱有、令其欣慶、令其愛樂、令其歡喜、令其勇悍、無所訶擯、相應、助伴、無亂、如法、稱順眾會。有慈憐心，有利益心，有哀愍心，不依利養恭敬讚頌，不自高舉、不陵懱他。如是名為善說正法。由彼成就如是八支，於時時間善能諫舉、善作憶念、善能教授、善能教誡、善說正法，是故說彼名為善友。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, this discourse is offered at the proper time, with earnest seriousness, in proper sequence, without interruption, complete in both structure and content, bringing about delight, fondness, joy, and courageous resolve in the listener, never leaving the deserving unpraised or the correctable uncorrected, in accordance with reason, mutually supportive, free of confusion, in keeping with the true teaching, and suited to the assembly at hand. It is offered with a heart of loving-kindness, a heart seeking the listener's benefit, and a heart of compassion, never for the sake of gain, honor, or praise, never with arrogance, and never belittling another. This is called skillful teaching of the true Dharma. Having accomplished these eight qualities of speech, one is, from time to time, skilled at admonishing, skilled at reminding, skilled at instructing, skilled at guiding, and skilled at teaching the true Dharma — and it is for this reason that such a one is called a good friend.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifteen qualities of "teaching in accordance" and the five qualities of "pure-minded teaching" as two final refinements layered onto the eight qualities of well-composed speech already given. Teaching in accordance means matching the delivery itself to the listener and occasion — properly timed and dignified in bearing, treating the teaching with genuine seriousness, delivered in clear sequence without interruption, complete in both outline and detail, and calibrated to produce real delight, fondness, joy, and courage in the listener rather than discouragement; it also means never leaving virtuous effort unacknowledged or a correctable fault unaddressed, reasoning drawn from direct perception, inference, and scriptural authority together, mutually reinforcing rather than scattered, unambiguous rather than needlessly obscure, faithful to the Four Noble Truths and the rest of genuine doctrine, and pitched to whatever audience is actually present — encouraging the already-faithful, persuading the uncertain, and gently opening even a hostile listener toward faith. Pure-minded teaching, meanwhile, governs the teacher's inner motivation regardless of audience: loving-kindness even toward someone who bears them ill will, a genuine wish for benefit even toward someone of poor conduct, and compassion for anyone caught in pleasure, pain, heedlessness, or degraded circumstance — all of it offered without any hidden wish for material support, praise, or reputation, and without a trace of arrogance or contempt for the listener.

With this, the treatise closes its entire account of good companionship (provision nine of the fourteen path-provisions) with a single unifying formula: someone who has genuinely accomplished all eight qualifying marks — discipline, learning, attainment, compassion, tirelessness, forbearance, fearlessness, and skillful speech — and who can, on that foundation, actually admonish, remind, instruct, guide, and teach as just described, is what the treatise means, in full, by a "good friend." Cháng Bǎi stresses that eloquence alone, without the underlying eight qualities, would be empty performance; it is only the combination of genuine inner accomplishment and genuinely skillful expression that makes someone qualified to guide another person's practice.

18. The Brief Summary of Good Companionship Opens

Chinese Source

如是廣辯善友性已。復云何知此中略義？

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus has good companionship been explained in detail. How, then, should the brief summary of this teaching be understood?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise now follows its familiar three-part pattern — brief outline, detailed exposition, brief summary — already used for wakeful practice and clear awareness in conduct in the previous fascicle. Having just completed the extraordinarily detailed exposition of good companionship's eight marks and five capabilities, the treatise now turns to condense everything covered into a compact, memorable summary, the same closing move that brought each earlier provision to completion.

19. Milestone: Good Companionship Closes — Four Factors, and the Whole Teaching Complete

The treatise condenses everything covered about good companionship into four essential factors, then closes the entire ninth provision with its standard formula uniting detailed exposition and brief summary.

Chinese Source

謂若善友心善稠密，為性哀愍，最初於彼樂為利益、樂為安樂。又即於此利益安樂，如實了知，無有顛倒，離顛倒見。又即於此利益安樂，有大勢力、方便善巧，能令積集、能令引發。又即於此利益安樂，翹勤無惰，起發圓滿，為性好樂，發勤精進。當知由此四因緣故，攝一切種總、略圓滿善知識性。如是名為此善友性所有略義。若前所說廣分別義，若此所說所有略義，一切總說為善友性。

Ru-Yi Translation

A good friend's mind is thoroughly settled and compassionate by nature, from the very outset delighting in bringing others benefit and ease. Regarding that very benefit and ease, one understands it truly, free of distortion, free of any mistaken view. Regarding that very benefit and ease, one has great capability and skillful means, able to help others accumulate it and bring it forth. And regarding that very benefit and ease, one is diligent and never lazy, arousing complete energy, delighting in it by nature, generating sustained effort. Know that by these four causal factors, the nature of a good friend is entirely and completely encompassed, in full and in brief alike. This is the brief summary of good companionship. Both what was explained in detail before, and this brief summary given now, are together called good companionship.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps these four factors directly onto the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own parallel list, noting they correspond to its first four (of five) marks of a good spiritual friend: genuinely wishing to benefit another from the start (drawing on compassion), correctly understanding what actually constitutes their benefit (drawing on extensive learning), having the real capability to bring that benefit about (drawing on genuine attainment), and never tiring in the effort (drawing directly on the fifth of the eight original marks). Though stated as only four factors here, Cháng Bǎi notes each still depends on the fuller eightfold structure already detailed — compassion without learning could easily misfire, learning without attainment stays merely theoretical, and neither means anything without the tireless follow-through to actually see it through.

With this brief summary, good companionship — the ninth of the fourteen path-provisions — closes in full: its brief outline, its extraordinarily detailed exposition across eight marks and five capabilities, and now its four-factor summary, together completing the teaching. Cháng Bǎi closes by naming the four classical steps of genuine practice this entire provision serves: first, drawing near a good spiritual friend; second, actually hearing the true teaching from them; third, reflecting on it correctly; and fourth, practicing in accordance with what has been understood — the exact sequence the treatise turns to next, as provisions ten and eleven, hearing and reflecting on the true teaching, open immediately.

20. Milestone: Hearing and Reflecting on the True Teaching Opens

Fascicle 25 continues directly into the tenth and eleventh path-provisions, treated together: hearing the true teaching and reflecting on it. The treatise opens by defining exactly what counts as "the true teaching" and who is qualified to proclaim it, then works through the classical twelvefold division of scripture in unusually rich technical detail, beginning with the discourses themselves.

Chinese Source

云何名為聞思正法？謂正法者，若佛世尊、若佛弟子、正士、正至、正善丈夫，宣說開顯分別照了。此復云何？所謂契經、應頌、記別，廣說如前十二分教。是名正法。……云何契經？謂薄伽梵，於彼彼方所，為彼彼所化有情，依彼彼所化諸行差別，宣說無量蘊相應語、處相應語、緣起相應語、食相應語、諦相應語、界相應語、聲聞乘相應語、獨覺乘相應語、如來乘相應語、念住正斷神足根力覺支道支等相應語、不淨息念諸學證淨等相應語。結集如來正法藏者，攝聚如是種種聖語，為令聖教久住世故，以諸美妙名句文身，如其所應次第安布、次第結集。謂能貫穿縫綴種種能引義利、能引梵行真善妙義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by hearing and reflecting on the true teaching? The true teaching is what is proclaimed, revealed, analyzed, and clarified by the Blessed One, by the Buddha's disciples, by upright persons, by those who have reached correctness, and by genuinely good persons. What, further, is this teaching? Namely, discourses, verse restatements, prophecies, and so on through the twelve divisions of scripture explained before. This is called the true teaching. … What are discourses? It means that the Blessed One, in various places, for various beings to be taught, according to the various differences in their conduct, proclaimed countless forms of teaching connected with the aggregates, connected with the sense-fields, connected with dependent origination, connected with nutriment, connected with the truths, connected with the elements, connected with the hearer-disciple vehicle, connected with the solitary-realizer vehicle, connected with the vehicle of the Tathāgata, connected with the foundations of mindfulness, right exertions, bases of miraculous power, faculties, powers, factors of awakening, and path-factors, and connected with the training in impurity, mindfulness of breathing, and the confirmed faiths. Those who compiled the treasury of the Tathāgata's true teaching gathered together these various holy sayings and, so that the sacred teaching might long abide in the world, arranged them in proper sequence with excellent words, phrases, and letters, compiling them in due order — that is, stringing and stitching together their various meanings, capable of bringing benefit, capable of supporting pure conduct, genuinely good and subtle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first clarifies who is qualified to proclaim "the true teaching": the Buddha himself; his ordained and lay disciples; "upright persons" who have simply set aside wrong conduct for correct conduct; "those who have reached correctness," meaning stream-enterers through arhats who have each cut off their own share of affliction; and "genuinely good persons" — precisely the spiritual friends just described in full, possessing all eight marks and five capabilities. The teaching itself is then organized into the traditional twelve divisions, and the treatise gives the discourses (the first and broadest division) an unusually detailed account of their actual subject matter: teachings connected with the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, dependent origination, the four kinds of nutriment, the Four Noble Truths, the elements, and each of the three vehicles (hearer-disciple, solitary-realizer, and bodhisattva) in turn, as well as teachings connected with the entire thirty-seven factors of awakening (the four foundations of mindfulness, four right exertions, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of awakening, and eightfold path) and with specific meditative trainings such as the contemplation of impurity, mindfulness of breathing, and the "four confirmed faiths" in the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and the precepts.

He then explains why these teachings are called a "sutra" (契經) at all: the Chinese term literally means "stringing together," evoking a thread running through beads — after the Buddha's passing, his disciples convened to recite everything they had heard, cross-checked it collectively to confirm its authenticity, and arranged it in careful sequence using memorable summary verses, precisely so the teaching could survive intact rather than scatter and be lost. "Sutra" in the fuller sense carries two meanings: compilation, the actual editorial work of gathering and ordering the material, and confirmation, the collective verification that what was preserved was genuinely the Buddha's own word. Cháng Bǎi frames the term "contact" (the other classical gloss on 契) as pointing in two directions at once: upward, in perfect accord with the Buddha's own realization, and downward, perfectly suited to the capacities of the beings receiving it — a teaching genuinely worth preserving this carefully precisely because it manages both at once.

21. Three More Divisions: Verse Restatements, Prophecies, and Standalone Verses

Chinese Source

云何應頌？謂於中間、或於最後宣說伽陀，或復宣說未了義經。是名應頌。云何記別？謂於是中，記別弟子命過已後當生等事，或復宣說已了義經。是名記別。云何諷頌？謂非直說，是結句說。或作二句、或作三句、或作四句、或作五句、或作六句等。是名諷頌。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are verse restatements? They are verses spoken in the middle or at the end of a teaching, or verses that further express a teaching not yet fully drawn out. This is called a verse restatement. What are prophecies? They are declarations of where a disciple will be reborn after death, or the concise restatement of a teaching already fully explained. This is called a prophecy. What are standalone verses? They are not spoken as prose, but composed in verse — in couplets, triplets, quatrains, or stanzas of five or six lines and more. This is called a standalone verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes these three closely related verse forms carefully. Verse restatement (應頌, "corresponding verse") is poetry that echoes or completes a prose passage already given — sometimes simply restating it more memorably, sometimes drawing out a meaning the prose alone left incomplete. Prophecy (記別) carries two quite different senses in the treatise: the more famous sense, a prediction of a disciple's future rebirth or eventual buddhahood, but also a second, more technical sense — a teaching stated briefly in outline being restated with its meaning fully drawn out, or a fully complete teaching being condensed back into brief statement. Standalone verse (諷頌, sometimes called "rhinoceros verse" after a famous early example about solitary practice) differs from verse restatement in having no prose counterpart at all — verses like those found in the Dhammapada or the Verses of the Elder Monks and Nuns, complete units of teaching in their own right, not echoing or condensing anything spoken in prose nearby.

22. Unprompted Teachings and the Opening of Circumstance-Based Teachings

Chinese Source

云何自說？謂於是中，不顯能請補特伽羅名字、種性，為令當來正法久住、聖教久住，不請而說。是名自說。云何因緣？謂於是中，顯示能請補特伽羅名字、種性，因請而說；及諸所有毗奈耶相應，有因有緣別解脫經。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are unprompted teachings? They are teachings in which the name and lineage of the one who requested them is not given, spoken without being requested at all, so that the true teaching and the sacred doctrine might long endure. This is called an unprompted teaching. What are circumstance-based teachings? They are teachings in which the name and lineage of the one who requested them is given, spoken in response to that request; and they also include all the discipline-related teachings, the rules of individual liberation, each arising from some specific cause and circumstance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Sūtra of Amitābha as the classic example of an unprompted teaching: the Buddha simply began speaking without any disciple having asked, moved to share it purely so the teaching would endure for the benefit of future generations — the treatise notes he did the same on seeing a wild elephant roaming freely (composing a verse on solitary contentment) and on seeing an old couple in poverty (composing a verse on the cost of a wasted youth), moments of spontaneous teaching arising from what he simply observed. Circumstance-based teachings are the mirror image: teachings that name exactly who asked the question that prompted them — sutras named for the bodhisattva or disciple whose inquiry occasioned them — and, more broadly, the entire body of monastic discipline, since every single training rule in the Vinaya was established only after some specific incident actually occurred, never speculatively in advance. Cháng Bǎi notes this is why the rules of individual liberation are always transmitted together with the story of their origin — the "cause and circumstance" is treated as inseparable from the rule itself.

23. Three Narrative Divisions: Parables, Past Lives of Disciples, and the Buddha's Own Past Lives

Chinese Source

云何譬喻？謂於是中，有譬喻說。由譬喻故，本義明淨。是名譬喻。云何本事？謂諸所有宿世相應事義言教。是名本事。云何本生？謂於是中，宣說世尊在過去世彼彼方分，若死、若生，行菩薩行、行難行行。是名本生。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are parables? They are teachings that include a comparison, through which the original meaning becomes clear and vivid. This is called a parable. What are accounts of past lives? They are teachings concerning matters connected with the past lives of the Buddha's disciples. This is called an account of past lives. What are birth stories? They are teachings that tell of the Blessed One, in various places in past lives, dying and being reborn, practicing the bodhisattva path, undertaking difficult practices. This is called a birth story.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates parables with the well-known salt-water simile: a spoonful of salt stirred into a small cup makes the water undrinkably bitter, but the same spoonful stirred into a great river vanishes without a trace — just as a small unwholesome act can ripen into a heavy result for someone who has cultivated little else, while the same act, set against a lifetime of sustained wholesome practice, barely registers at all. The comparison itself, he notes, is what makes an otherwise abstract point about karma vivid and immediately graspable.

He distinguishes the next two narrative divisions by whose past is being told. Accounts of past lives concern the Buddha's disciples specifically — stories of what they did or experienced in earlier lifetimes, excluding the Buddha's own past — while birth stories are reserved specifically for the Buddha's own past existences as a bodhisattva, undertaking the bodhisattva path across countless prior lives, sometimes as a king or merchant, sometimes as an animal such as a nine-colored deer, an elephant king, or a monkey king, each life illustrating some perfection carried to an extraordinary degree. Cháng Bǎi notes the distinction matters practically: birth stories model the entire arc of the bodhisattva path across a single extended narrative, while accounts of past lives are usually more targeted, illuminating why a particular disciple's present circumstances turned out the way they did.

24. Extended Teachings and Marvels

Chinese Source

云何方廣？謂於是中，宣說一切諸菩薩道，為令修證阿耨多羅三藐三菩提、十力、無畏、無障智等一切功德。是名方廣。云何希法？謂於是中，宣說諸佛、諸佛弟子……若共不共，勝於其餘、勝諸世間，同意所許，甚奇希有最勝功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are extended teachings? They are teachings that set forth the entire bodhisattva path, so that one may cultivate and realize unsurpassed complete awakening, the ten powers, the fearlessnesses, unobstructed knowledge, and every other such quality. This is called an extended teaching. What are marvels? They are teachings that set forth the Buddhas, and the Buddha's disciples — monks, nuns, female probationers, novices of both genders, and laymen and laywomen — describing qualities, whether shared with or unique among the world, surpassing all others, surpassing the world entirely, universally acknowledged as truly extraordinary, rare, and supreme.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies extended teachings with the great Mahāyāna sūtras — works like the Avataṃsaka, whose full title literally means "vast and expansive" — vast in content, immense in scope, and requiring, as the treatise itself notes, three incalculable eons of the most vigorous effort to bring to completion; nothing less than the entire bodhisattva path, aimed at unsurpassed complete awakening and the full range of a Buddha's unique qualities (the ten powers, the four kinds of fearlessness, and unobstructed omniscient knowledge), could be called "extended" in this full sense.

Marvels, by contrast, gather up the genuinely extraordinary — qualities of the Buddhas and their followers across all seven categories of ordained and lay disciples, whether these qualities are shared with worldly achievement in some diminished form or utterly without any worldly parallel, universally acknowledged by all who witness them as rare and supreme. Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own examples: Śāriputra's praise of the Buddha's unsurpassed qualities, and Ānanda's own testimony to the Buddha's truly marvelous nature — precisely the kind of firsthand, wondering acknowledgment this category of teaching is meant to preserve.

25. Milestone: The Twelfth Division Closes the Whole Framework — Analytical Treatises and the Three Baskets

The treatise names the twelfth and final division of scripture, analytical treatises, then closes the entire twelvefold framework by mapping it onto the three traditional baskets — sūtra, vinaya, and abhidharma — completing its full definition of "the true teaching."

Chinese Source

云何論議？所謂一切摩怛理迦、阿毗達磨，研究甚深素怛纜義，宣暢一切契經宗要。是名論議。……當知此中，若說契經、應頌、記別、諷頌、自說、譬喻、本事、本生、方廣、希法，是名素怛纜藏。若說因緣，是名毗奈耶藏。若說論議，是名阿毗達磨藏。是故如是十二分教，三藏所攝。如是一切，正士、正至、正善丈夫共所宣說，故名正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are analytical treatises? They are all the foundational analyses and further teachings that investigate the profound meaning of the discourses, fully expounding the essential themes of all scripture. This is called analytical treatise. … Know that, among these, discourses, verse restatements, prophecies, standalone verses, unprompted teachings, parables, accounts of past lives, birth stories, extended teachings, and marvels are comprised in the Basket of Discourses. Circumstance-based teachings are comprised in the Basket of Discipline. Analytical treatises are comprised in the Basket of Further Teaching. Thus these twelve divisions are comprised within the three baskets. All of this, proclaimed together by upright persons, those who have reached correctness, and genuinely good persons, is called the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the term "foundational analysis" (mātṛkā, literally "matrix" or "mother") as a name for exactly this genre: just as a mother gives birth, a mātṛkā-style teaching is what genuinely gives rise to wisdom, tracing a doctrine back to its root sources. Two kinds of scripture qualify: passages where the Buddha himself thoroughly analyzes the nature of phenomena, and passages where noble disciples who have already directly realized the truth analyze it correctly from their own attainment, without distortion. Both are also called abhidharma, "toward the Dharma" — oriented, that is, toward nirvana. Cháng Bǎi notes two broad kinds of treatise fall under this heading: commentaries that directly explain a specific sūtra (such as the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra's commentary on the Perfection of Wisdom), and treatises that instead gather and systematize themes across many scriptures through question-and-answer analysis — the very treatise being translated here, the Yogācārabhūmi, is of this second kind, composed by Maitreya, a bodhisattva of the highest, "final-link" stage, whose own realization qualifies the work as genuinely authoritative rather than merely erudite.

With analytical treatises defined, the entire twelvefold framework closes by sorting into the three traditional baskets: the first ten divisions (discourses through marvels) belong to the Basket of Discourses, since they concern real doctrinal content directly; circumstance-based teachings belong to the Basket of Discipline, since every training rule arose from some specific incident; and analytical treatises belong to the Basket of Further Teaching. Cháng Bǎi notes the three baskets map neatly onto the three trainings — the discourses supporting concentration, discipline supporting ethical conduct, and further teaching supporting wisdom — so that a monastic genuinely versed in all three is called a "Tripiṭaka master," accomplished in all three trainings at once. With this, "the true teaching" itself — everything proclaimed by upright persons, noble ones, and genuine spiritual friends together — is now fully defined, and the treatise turns to what it actually means to hear it.

26. Milestone: Hearing the True Teaching — Seven Kinds of Scholarship, Text and Meaning

Having defined the true teaching in full, the treatise turns to the tenth provision itself, hearing the true teaching — first distinguishing seven possible combinations of which basket a practitioner has actually mastered, then distinguishing hearing the words themselves from hearing their meaning.

Chinese Source

聽聞此故，名聞正法。此復云何？謂如有一，或受持素怛纜，或受持毗奈耶，或受持阿毗達磨，或受持素怛纜及毗奈耶，或受持素怛纜及阿毗達磨，或受持毗奈耶及阿毗達磨，或具受持素怛纜、毗奈耶、阿毗達磨。如是一切，名聞正法。此聞正法復有二種。一、聞其文，二、聞其義。

Ru-Yi Translation

Hearing this is called hearing the true teaching. What, further, does this mean? It means that a person may uphold the discourses alone, or the discipline alone, or the further teaching alone; or the discourses together with the discipline; or the discourses together with the further teaching; or the discipline together with the further teaching; or all three — discourses, discipline, and further teaching — together. All of this is called hearing the true teaching. This hearing of the true teaching is, moreover, of two kinds: hearing its words, and hearing its meaning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the seven possible combinations plainly: a specialist in discourses alone, a specialist in discipline alone, a specialist in further teaching alone, three possible pairings of any two baskets, and finally a master of all three together — the genuine Tripiṭaka master. He notes each basket serves a distinct function: the discourses help a practitioner grasp the Buddha's actual intent; the discipline helps root out faults, much as secular law adjudicates offenses, restraining unwholesome conduct and training the senses; and further teaching helps analyze and resolve subtle points of doctrine, arriving at genuine insight into the Four Noble Truths. Hearing all three, whatever the combination actually mastered, counts as "hearing the true teaching" in the sense meant here.

Cháng Bǎi then flags the distinction the treatise draws next as genuinely important: hearing merely the words of scripture — fluent recitation, correct memorization — is a real but limited accomplishment, quite different from actually hearing and grasping the meaning those words are meant to convey. Many people can recite a passage perfectly without having understood a word of what it points toward; the treatise is about to make that difference explicit.

27. Milestone: Hearing Closes, and Reflecting on the True Teaching Opens

Hearing the true teaching closes with the distinction between hearing mere words and hearing genuine meaning. The treatise then opens the eleventh provision, reflecting on the true teaching, first by naming six speculative traps to avoid, then by turning reflection toward the specific and general characteristics of all phenomena.

Chinese Source

云何思正法？謂如有一，即如所聞、所信正法，獨處空閑，遠離六種不應思處。謂思議我、思議有情、思議世間、思議有情業、思議果異熟、思議靜慮者靜慮境界、思議諸佛諸佛境界。但正思惟所有諸法自相、共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is reflecting on the true teaching? It means that one, dwelling alone in solitude, reflects on the true teaching just as it was heard and believed, while avoiding six things that should not be speculated upon: speculating about the self, about sentient beings, about the world, about the actions of sentient beings, about the ripening of their results, about the meditative states of accomplished meditators, and about the states of the Buddhas. Instead, one correctly reflects only on the specific and general characteristics of all phenomena.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first closes hearing the true teaching by noting that hearing mere words without grasping their meaning risks clinging to the letter at the expense of the point, while grasping meaning without genuine command of the words risks losing precision — genuine hearing requires both together, the reason traditional Dharma teaching was hedged with such careful protocol (incense offered, the teaching unpacked phrase by phrase, illustrated with apt comparison, neither over- nor under-explained).

He then identifies the six things not to be speculated upon as a shorter version of the nine already given in fascicle 24 — the self, sentient beings, and the world are dismissed for the same reasons as before (no fixed self can be found; "sentient being" is a concept dependent on that same fiction; the world's basic facts are simply as they are, not worth idle debate); karma and its ripening are added here because the specific mechanics connecting a given action to a given result are governed by conditions too complex and individually variable for ordinary reasoning to trace reliably — someone who lived a mostly unwholesome life might still die peacefully through some ripening seed of old merit, while a mostly wholesome life might end in confusion through some other cause, and speculating about the exact causal thread only produces confusion rather than insight; and the states of accomplished meditators and of the Buddhas remain beyond ordinary conceptual reach, direct experience being the only real path to them. In place of this idle speculation, the treatise redirects reflection toward something genuinely productive: the specific characteristics that distinguish each phenomenon (such as solidity as earth's specific mark, or moisture as water's) and the characteristics phenomena hold in common — corresponding, Cháng Bǎi notes, to the treatise's own five kinds of skillfulness in the aggregates, elements, sense-fields, dependent origination, and the possible and impossible, which collapse into skillfulness regarding specific characteristics (via the aggregates) and skillfulness regarding shared characteristics (via the remaining four).

28. Two Methods of Reflection: Numerical Enumeration and the Five Aggregates

Chinese Source

如是思惟，復有二種。一者、以算數行相，善巧方便算計諸法；二者、以稱量行相，依正道理觀察諸法功德過失。……謂言色者，即十色處，及墮法處所攝眾色，是名色蘊。所言受者，即三種受，是名受蘊。所言想者，即六想身，是名想蘊。所言行者，即六思身等，是名行蘊。所言識者，即六識身等，是名識蘊。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such reflection is, moreover, of two kinds: first, reflection through the mode of enumeration, skillfully calculating phenomena by number; second, reflection through the mode of evaluation, examining the merits and faults of phenomena according to correct reasoning. … As for form: it is the ten form-fields, together with the various forms comprised within the mental field — this is called the aggregate of form. As for feeling: it is the three kinds of feeling — this is called the aggregate of feeling. As for perception: it is the six groups of perception — this is called the aggregate of perception. As for formations: it is the six groups of volition, and so on — this is called the aggregate of formations. As for consciousness: it is the six groups of consciousness, and so on — this is called the aggregate of consciousness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two methods simply: enumeration organizes a teaching by number — the five aggregates, Four Noble Truths, four foundations of mindfulness, and so on — making complex material tractable by breaking it into countable units; evaluation instead applies the treatise's four principles of reasoning (dependence, function, proof, and natural law) to weigh a phenomenon's actual benefits and drawbacks. Both methods, he notes, apply equally to every category of teaching — the aggregates, sense-fields, elements, dependent origination, and so on — and the treatise now illustrates enumeration first, using the five aggregates as its example.

Form, counted numerically, comprises eleven items: the ten form-bases perceived by the five physical senses (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and their five corresponding objects), plus a further category of forms cognized only by the mind — restraint-forms (the invisible, ongoing moral commitment generated at ordination, which continues restraining unwholesome conduct even when not actively exercised), non-restraint forms (habitual unwholesome conduct lacking that same protective commitment), and forms a highly accomplished meditator can generate at will through the power of concentration. Feeling divides simply into three: painful, pleasant, and neutral. Perception divides into six, one for each sense faculty's contact with its object — eye-contact perception, ear-contact perception, and so on through mind-contact perception. Formations, broadest of the five, likewise divides into six by the same sixfold contact, though the category extends further still to cover every mental factor besides feeling and perception, and beyond that the various forces not directly associated with consciousness at all. And consciousness divides into the same six groups — though Cháng Bǎi notes the term properly spans all eight consciousnesses: the storehouse consciousness (called "mind"), the afflicted mental consciousness (called "intellect"), and the six sense-consciousnesses proper (called "consciousness" in the narrower sense) — with "six" here standing in for the complete eightfold structure.

29. Milestone: Evaluative Reflection Opens — The Principle of Dependence

The treatise turns to its second method of reflection, evaluation through the four principles of reasoning, opening with the first and most fundamental: the principle of dependence, which holds that nothing — not even the five aggregates themselves — exists except in dependence on something else.

Chinese Source

云何以稱量行相，依正道理思惟諸蘊相應言教？謂依四道理無倒觀察。何等為四？一、觀待道理，二、作用道理，三、證成道理，四、法爾道理。云何名為觀待道理？謂略說有二種觀待。一、生起觀待，二、施設觀待。生起觀待者，謂由諸因、諸緣勢力生起諸蘊，此蘊生起要當觀待諸因、諸緣。施設觀待者，謂由名身、句身、文身施設諸蘊，此蘊施設要當觀待名句文身。是名於蘊生起觀待、施設觀待。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is it to reflect through the mode of evaluation, examining teaching connected with the aggregates according to correct reasoning? It means observing without distortion according to four principles. What are the four? The principle of dependence, the principle of function, the principle of proof, and the principle of natural law. What is the principle of dependence? Briefly, there are two kinds of dependence: dependence in arising, and dependence in designation. Dependence in arising means that the aggregates arise through the power of various causes and conditions — their arising necessarily depends on those causes and conditions. Dependence in designation means that the aggregates are designated through bodies of names, phrases, and letters — their designation necessarily depends on those names, phrases, and letters. This is called dependence in the arising and designation of the aggregates.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats dependence in arising as the direct application of dependent origination to the five aggregates themselves: nothing arises from itself, from something else alone, from both together, or from no cause at all — the aggregates arise strictly through the convergence of the treatise's own ten causes and four conditions (causal condition, condition of immediate succession, object condition, and dominant condition), never as some self-standing, independently real thing. Dependence in designation applies the same logic to language itself: the very words "form," "feeling," "perception," "formations," and "consciousness" are not names attached to some already-existing, independently findable entity — the aggregates are only ever picked out as "the five aggregates" because names, phrases, and written characters were conventionally assigned to organize experience that way in the first place. The two dependencies run in both directions: causes and conditions are themselves only ever specified using names and concepts, and yet those same names and concepts only have any purchase because they track real causal regularities.

Cháng Bǎi draws out the practical payoff of holding both dependencies in mind together: recognizing that the aggregates arise only through causes and conditions removes the temptation to treat any dependently-arisen phenomenon as possessing some fixed, unchanging essence of its own; and recognizing that the aggregates are only ever picked out through conventional naming removes the further temptation to treat the concepts themselves — "self," "person," "thing" — as corresponding to something genuinely, independently real behind the label. Reflecting on any teaching this way, Cháng Bǎi notes, is itself a direct method (瑜伽方便) for bringing the mind into accord with the true nature of things, which is precisely why the treatise calls it a principle of reasoning rather than a merely abstract observation.

30. The Principle of Function: Each Phenomenon Does Its Own Distinct Work

Chinese Source

云何名為作用道理？謂諸蘊生已，由自緣故，有自作用，各各差別。謂眼能見色，耳能聞聲，鼻能齅香，舌能嘗味，身能覺觸，意能了法。色為眼境，為眼所行；乃至法為意境，為意所行。或復所餘如是等類，於彼彼法別別作用，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by the principle of function? It means that, once the aggregates have arisen, each has its own distinct function, arising from its own specific conditions, each function differing from the others. The eye is able to see form; the ear is able to hear sound; the nose is able to smell odor; the tongue is able to taste flavor; the body is able to feel touch; the mind is able to cognize phenomena. Form is the eye's object, the eye's domain of activity; and so on, up through phenomena being the mind's object, the mind's domain of activity. And for all other such categories of phenomena, each has its own distinct function in just the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the principle of function through the twelve sense-fields: the eye arises from its own seed together with the four great elements and the other supporting conditions, and its specific function — once arisen — is nothing other than seeing form; the ear's function is hearing sound; and so through the nose, tongue, and body to the mind itself, which, arising from its own seed together with the immediately preceding moment of consciousness, attention, and its mental object, performs the distinct function of cognizing phenomena generally. Each sense faculty's object is correspondingly its own exclusive domain of activity — form is strictly the eye's territory, sound strictly the ear's, and so on — no faculty performing another's function. Cháng Bǎi notes this same principle extends to every other category of phenomena without exception: each thing, the moment it arises, simply does its own specific work and no other, and recognizing this — rather than expecting one thing to substitute for or explain another's proper function — is itself the "principle of function" the treatise names here.

31. Milestone: The Principle of Proof — Three Ways of Knowing Something Is True

The treatise's third principle, proof, asks how a claim as fundamental as "all the aggregates are impermanent, dependently arisen, suffering, empty, and without self" can actually be verified — and answers with three distinct, complementary means of valid knowledge.

Chinese Source

云何名為證成道理？謂一切蘊皆是無常，眾緣所生，苦、空、無我。由三量故，如實觀察。謂由至教量故，由現量故，由比量故。由此三量證驗道理。諸有智者，心正執受、安置、成立。謂一切蘊皆無常性、眾緣生性、苦性、空性，及無我性。如是等名證成道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by the principle of proof? It means that all the aggregates are impermanent, arisen from various conditions, suffering, empty, and without self — and this is observed truly by means of three measures: authoritative scripture, direct perception, and inference. By these three measures, the principle is verified. Those who are wise correctly grasp, establish, and confirm it in their minds — namely, that all the aggregates have the nature of impermanence, the nature of being dependently arisen, the nature of suffering, the nature of emptiness, and the nature of non-self. This is called the principle of proof.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the three measures in turn. Authoritative scripture is the word of the Buddha and of noble disciples who have themselves directly realized the nature of things — trustworthy specifically because it never contradicts itself, genuinely counters affliction, and never misrepresents the actual characteristics of conditioned and unconditioned phenomena. Direct perception is unmediated, non-conceptual contact with an object as it actually is, in the present moment — Cháng Bǎi distinguishes ordinary sense perception, mental perception of an immediately present object, worldly perception generally, and, at the deepest level, the pure perception of a noble one's own direct realization, unmixed with any conceptual overlay at all. Inference is reasoning from what is already known to what is not yet directly observed — seeing smoke on a distant hillside and inferring fire, or reasoning from partial observation to a lawful regularity not yet directly witnessed.

Cháng Bǎi anticipates an obvious question: if scripture already carries the Buddha's authority, why require direct perception and inference as well? He points to the Buddha's own teaching to the Kālāmas, which counsels against accepting anything merely because it is widely repeated, traditional, popularly believed, found in scripture, logically plausible, commonsensical, based on a superficial analogy, congenial to one's own views, statistically likely, or spoken by a respected teacher — advising instead that a claim be tested and known for oneself before being embraced. Buddhism, he stresses, is built on seeing and understanding rather than on faith alone; even the Buddha himself invited his own followers to examine him critically rather than accept his teaching purely on reputation. Genuine confidence in a teaching, in other words, comes only from all three measures working together: scriptural trust generates genuine faith, direct perception generates real insight and plants the seeds of further wholesome practice, and inference settles the matter with reasoned certainty — together, and only together, constituting the principle of proof.

32. The Principle of Natural Law: Facts That Simply Are What They Are

Chinese Source

云何名為法爾道理？謂何因緣故，即彼諸蘊如是種類，諸器世間如是安布？何因緣故，地堅為相、水濕為相、火煖為相、風用輕動以為其相？何因緣故，諸蘊無常、諸法無我、涅槃寂靜？

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by the principle of natural law? It concerns questions such as: for what reason are the aggregates of just these kinds, and the physical world arranged just this way? For what reason does earth have solidity as its characteristic, water moisture, fire heat, and wind light movement? For what reason are the aggregates impermanent, all phenomena without self, and nirvana peaceful?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the principle of natural law as the treatise's acknowledgment that some facts are simply brute facts, not further derivable from anything more basic — asking "why" of them misses the point rather than deepening understanding. Why do the aggregates sort into exactly these five categories, and why is the physical universe structured the way it is (a question the treatise answers elsewhere at length, describing the world's formation, duration, destruction, and emptiness across the great eons, without ever explaining why the process is structured that way to begin with, only that it is)? Why does earth characteristically manifest as solid, water as wet, fire as hot, and wind as mobile — qualities that are simply each element's own nature, not the product of some prior cause? And why are the aggregates impermanent, all phenomena without self, and nirvana genuinely peaceful — the three seals of the Dharma, true not because some further argument proves them but because this is simply how things are? Cháng Bǎi's point is that recognizing certain truths as "just so" is itself an act of intellectual honesty, not a failure of explanation — some questions bottom out, and mistaking that bottoming-out for a puzzle still to be solved is itself a subtle form of confusion the treatise is trying to head off.

33. Milestone: Natural Law Closes the Four Principles, and Hearing-and-Reflecting Completes

The treatise closes the principle of natural law by applying it to the five aggregates' own defining marks, then closes the entire four-principle framework, the two methods of reflection, and — with them — the tenth and eleventh path-provisions together.

Chinese Source

何因緣故，色變壞相、受領納相、想等了相、行造作相、識了別相？由彼諸法本性應爾、自性應爾、法性應爾，即此法爾，說名道理瑜伽方便。如是名為法爾道理。……如由算數行相及稱量行相，觀察諸蘊相應言教，如是即由二種行相，觀察其餘所有言教。如是總名審正觀察思惟一切所說正法。如是名為聞思正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

For what reason does form have the mark of change and decay, feeling the mark of experiencing, perception the mark of apprehending and cognizing, formations the mark of constructing, and consciousness the mark of discerning? Because these phenomena's basic nature, their own nature, their essential nature is simply so — this very "simply so" is called a mode of correct reasoning, called the principle of natural law. … Just as the modes of enumeration and evaluation are used to examine teaching connected with the aggregates, so too these same two modes are used to examine every other teaching. All of this, together, is called the careful and correct examination and reflection upon everything the true teaching sets forth. This is called hearing and reflecting on the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes natural law with its final application: why does form specifically manifest as subject to change and obstruction, feeling as experiencing, perception as apprehending, formations as constructing, and consciousness as discerning? Simply because that is each aggregate's own essential nature — not derived from anything more fundamental, the same "just so" already applied to the four elements and the three seals. He adds a final nuance: even within natural law, things can be described three ways without contradiction — by their own intrinsic nature (earth simply is solid), by their functional role (earth specifically supports what rests upon it, distinct from its bare solidity), or by an appearance that seems to diverge from the underlying substance (a blazing flame's visible shimmer differing from fire's basic nature as heat) — yet all three descriptions, however they differ in emphasis, ultimately rest on and return to the same natural law. Recognizing this settles the mind rather than leaving it puzzled by apparent inconsistency.

With this, the four principles of reasoning — dependence, function, proof, and natural law — close, illustrated throughout using the five aggregates but explicitly generalizable to every other teaching (the sense-fields, dependent origination, and so on) via the same two methods of enumeration and evaluation. The treatise closes with a single formula: numerical enumeration plus fourfold evaluative reasoning, applied to everything the true teaching contains, together constitute genuine reflection on the true teaching — completing, together with hearing already covered, the tenth and eleventh path-provisions as one unified teaching.

34. Milestone: Freedom From Obstruction Opens — Internal and External Hindrances

Fascicle 25 continues directly into the twelfth path-provision, freedom from obstruction, opening with a broad twofold division: obstacles rooted within oneself, and obstacles arising from external circumstances.

Chinese Source

云何無障？謂此無障略有二種。一者、依內，二者、依外。我當先說依內外障，與彼相違，當知即是二種無障。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is freedom from obstruction? Briefly, this freedom is of two kinds: that which depends on internal factors, and that which depends on external factors. I shall first describe the internal and external obstructions themselves; understand that freedom from them, in each case, is simply their opposite.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the structure ahead: internal obstacles, rooted in one's own body and circumstances, break into three — obstacles to applying effort at all (five sub-causes: lacking basic provisions, being overwhelmed by strong affliction, being frequently ill, being troubled by recurring physical imbalance, and being weighed down by undigested food), obstacles to genuine seclusion (such as eating heavy food or indulging in idle talk), and obstacles to inner stillness (such as arrogance or dwelling in an unsuitable location). External obstacles break into two — bad company, an obstacle arising from other people, and an unsuitable environment, an obstacle arising from circumstance itself. The treatise's method throughout, Cháng Bǎi notes, is to define each obstacle in full first, since freedom from it is simply its straightforward absence — there's no need for a separate positive definition once the obstacle itself is clearly understood.

35. The First Internal Obstacle: Lacking Basic Provisions

Chinese Source

云何名依內障？謂如有一，於其先世不曾修福；不修福故，不能時時獲得隨順資生眾具，所謂衣、食、諸坐臥具、病緣醫藥及餘什具。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by an obstacle depending on internal factors? It means that one who, in a former life, never cultivated merit, is consequently unable, from time to time, to obtain the necessities that sustain life — namely, clothing, food, bedding, medicine for illness, and other requisites.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats material poverty itself as the first and most basic obstacle to practice — a practitioner constantly scrambling for food, clothing, and shelter has little capacity left for sustained meditation. He traces the cause back to a lack of merit cultivated in past lives, and reviews the treatise's classical threefold division of merit-making: giving-based merit (offering food, clothing, or medicine to those in need, or supporting genuine renunciants), discipline-based merit (keeping the precepts purely, extending impartial goodwill to all beings), and cultivation-based merit (wisdom gained through contemplation, or the sustained practice of the four immeasurables) — echoed in the Contemplation Sūtra's own three pure practices: filial devotion and reverence for teachers paired with the ten wholesome actions, taking the three refuges and keeping the precepts fully, and, at the highest level, awakening the aspiration for enlightenment, believing deeply in cause and effect, and encouraging others toward the same practice. A practitioner who neglected all of this in past lives now reaps the direct consequence: a present life spent short of the very provisions genuine practice depends on.

36. The Remaining Obstacles to Effort: Affliction, Illness, Imbalance, Undigested Food

Chinese Source

有猛利貪及長時貪，有猛利瞋及長時瞋，有猛利癡及長時癡。或於先世積集造作多疾病業。由彼為因，多諸疾病。或由現在行不平等。由是因緣，風熱痰癊數數發動。或有宿食住在身中。

Ru-Yi Translation

There is fierce and prolonged desire, fierce and prolonged aversion, fierce and prolonged delusion. Or one may have accumulated, in a former life, actions that bring about frequent illness — through that cause, illness now frequently arises. Or one may, in the present, live without balance — through that cause, disorders of wind, heat, and phlegm frequently arise. Or undigested food may remain lodged within the body.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the remaining four sub-causes of the first internal obstacle. Overwhelming, long-lasting affliction — desire, aversion, or delusion strong enough and persistent enough to occupy the mind continuously — leaves no real space for practice, whatever a practitioner's other circumstances. Frequent illness traced to past-life causes reflects the treatise's own teaching that cruelty toward others in a former life — striking, imprisoning, or otherwise harming them — tends to ripen as poor health in a later one, just as the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own converse teaching holds that caring for others' bodies and feeding them nourishing food in the past ripens as robust health later; even mocking someone else's strong build, Cháng Bǎi notes citing a related commentary, is said to ripen as chronic frailty. Illness from present carelessness — poor diet, irregular sleep, unchecked emotion — throws the body's elements out of balance and produces recurring disorders of wind, heat, and phlegm, distinct from illness rooted in past karma. And undigested food lodging in the body, the fifth cause, reflects simple neglect of physical activity; Cháng Bǎi cites a classical travel account praising walking meditation specifically for aiding digestion, warning that sitting too long without moving — a familiar hazard for modern people bent over a screen for hours — is itself a direct cause of this last obstacle.

37. Milestone: Ten Obstacles to Genuine Seclusion

The treatise's second category of internal obstacle, obstacles to seclusion, lists ten specific habits — from heavy eating to idle talk — each of which keeps a practitioner entangled with the world precisely when genuine withdrawal is needed.

Chinese Source

或食麤重，多事、多業、多有所作、多與眾會。樂著事業、樂著語言、樂著睡眠、樂著諠眾、樂相雜住、樂著戲論。

Ru-Yi Translation

One may eat heavily, be occupied with many affairs, many undertakings, many tasks, and much socializing. One may delight in busy activity, delight in talk, delight in sleep, delight in noisy crowds, delight in mixed company, and delight in idle speculation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi groups the ten into a coherent picture of a life too entangled to withdraw from. Heavy eating leaves the body sluggish and unfit for sustained practice. Being constantly occupied — with affairs, undertakings, tasks, and social obligations — leaves no actual time free. And six specific attachments compound the problem: delighting in busyness for its own sake, in talk (whether idle gossip or simply excessive speech, which drains energy and invites careless words), in sleep beyond what's needed, in noisy crowds, in mixed company with people uninterested in genuine practice (who, however well-meaning, keep pulling a practitioner back into worldly activity), and in idle speculation — talk or thought that serves no genuine purpose, which the treatise elsewhere calls "proliferation" (prapañca) and identifies as something even nirvana itself is defined by the complete absence of. None of these ten, Cháng Bǎi stresses, are dramatic vices; they are exactly the kind of small, habitual entanglements that quietly consume the time and stillness genuine seclusion requires.

38. Milestone: Obstacles to Stillness Close Internal Obstruction; Bad Company Opens the External

The treatise's third internal obstacle, obstacles to stillness, closes the full account of internal obstruction. External obstruction then opens with the first of its two causes: relying on an unwholesome companion.

Chinese Source

樂自恃舉、掉亂、放逸、居止非處。如是等類，應知一切名依內障。云何名依外障？謂如有一，依不善士；由彼因緣，不能時時獲得隨順教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

One may delight in self-conceit, be restless and scattered, be heedless, or dwell in an unsuitable place. All such things should be understood as obstacles depending on internal factors. What is meant by an obstacle depending on external factors? It means that one who relies on an unwholesome companion is, through that cause, unable to obtain, from time to time, the guidance and correction one needs.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi pairs the four obstacles to stillness against the two branches of meditation they specifically undermine: conceit — pride in one's own lineage, learning, or attainment — and the restless scatteredness that comes with it directly obstruct insight meditation, since a mind preoccupied with its own standing cannot settle into clear examination; heedlessness and dwelling in an unsuitable place directly obstruct calming meditation, since an undisciplined mind or a poorly chosen environment both work against genuine stillness. With this third and final internal obstacle named, the treatise closes its account of internal obstruction entirely — five causes undermining effort, ten undermining seclusion, four undermining stillness.

External obstruction then opens with its first cause: reliance on a poor companion, essentially the direct inversion of the good spiritual friend described at such length earlier in the fascicle. Cháng Bǎi summarizes the unwholesome counterpart briefly — someone who undermines right view, right conduct, and right livelihood, who delights in disorder, is habitually lazy, remains attached to saṃsāra, turns away from the path to awakening, or is overly attached to household life and family. Sustained closeness with such a person, he warns, works exactly like the proverbial dye shop — one comes away colored by whatever company one keeps — gradually eroding confidence in the Dharma, distorting view and conduct, and strengthening the very three poisons practice is meant to weaken.

39. Milestone: An Unsuitable Environment Closes External Obstruction Entirely

The treatise names the second and final external obstacle in vivid, concrete detail: a dwelling place plagued by noise, harsh weather, and genuine danger — closing the full account of both internal and external obstruction.

Chinese Source

或居惡處。於此住處，若晝日分，多有種種諠雜、眾集、諸變異事；若於夜分，多有種種高聲、大聲、大眾諠雜；復有種種猛利辛楚風日惡觸；或有種種人及非人怖畏驚恐。如是等類，應知一切名依外障。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or one may dwell in an unsuitable place. In such a dwelling, during the day, there may be much noise, crowding, and disruptive commotion; at night, much loud and clamorous noise, large crowds in an uproar; there may also be harsh, punishing wind and sun; or there may be various threats and terrors from human beings and non-human beings alike. All such things should be understood as obstacles depending on external factors.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi paints the unsuitable dwelling with real specificity: daytime commotion (a marketplace or noisy neighbor nearby), nighttime disturbance (machinery, a slaughterhouse's cries, a bustling night market), harsh climate (extreme altitude, cold, wind, or sun bringing constant physical strain), and genuine danger from both human sources (hostile neighbors, thieves) and non-human ones (whatever local tradition holds to trouble a place). None of these, he notes, are minor inconveniences to simply push through — a location that keeps the body sick, the nerves on edge, and the mind genuinely afraid is not a viable base for sustained practice, however admirable the effort to persevere there might seem.

With this, the treatise's full account of obstruction closes: internal obstacles across three categories (undermining effort, seclusion, and stillness) and external obstacles across two (poor company and an unsuitable environment) — the complete inventory of what freedom from obstruction, the twelfth path-provision, actually means to overcome.

40. Milestone: The Brief Summary Regroups Everything Into Three Obstacles

Having detailed internal and external obstruction at length, the treatise now offers a second, complementary grouping: three obstacles — to effort, to seclusion, and to stillness — that reorganize the same material by which part of practice each actually blocks.

Chinese Source

謂於此中略有三障。一、加行障，二、遠離障，三、寂靜障。云何加行障？謂若此障會遇現前，於諸善品所有加行皆無堪能，亦無勢力。……謂常疹疾、困苦、重病，風熱痰癊數數發動，或有宿食住在身中，或被蛇蝎、百足、蚰蜒之所蜇螫，或人非人之所逼惱，又不能得衣、食、臥具、病緣醫藥及餘什具。如是等類，應知一切名加行障。

Ru-Yi Translation

Briefly, there are three obstacles here: the obstacle to effort, the obstacle to seclusion, and the obstacle to stillness. What is the obstacle to effort? It means that when this obstacle is actually present, one has no capability at all — no strength — for any wholesome effort. … Namely: frequent minor ailments, distressing conditions, or serious illness; recurring disorders of wind, heat, and phlegm; undigested food lodged in the body; being stung or bitten by snakes, scorpions, centipedes, or millipedes; being harassed by human or non-human beings; or being unable to obtain clothing, food, bedding, medicine, or other necessities. All such things should be understood as the obstacle to effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this brief summary isn't simply repeating the internal/external division already given, but regrouping the same underlying material by function: the obstacle to effort gathers together everything that leaves a practitioner physically or materially unable to even begin sustained practice — illness in all its forms (minor, distressing, or serious), the specific bodily disorders and undigested food already detailed, venomous or biting creatures, harassment from people or spirits, and outright material lack. Where the earlier internal/external framework organized these causes by their source, this summary organizes them by their effect — all of it, whatever its origin, adding up to the same practical result: no capability and no strength left over for genuine practice.

41. Milestone: The Obstacle to Seclusion, Unpacked in Far Greater Depth

The treatise's second summary obstacle, the obstacle to seclusion, revisits the same ten habits named earlier but now unpacks each in considerably richer detail — heavy food's specific bad effects, a merchant's proverb about restless ambition, and a long, vivid list of exactly the kind of gossip that wastes a practitioner's days.

Chinese Source

云何遠離障？謂食麤重，多事、多業、多有所作。或樂事業。……或樂語言。……或樂睡眠。……或樂諠眾。由此因緣，樂與在家及出家眾談說種種王論、賊論、食論、飲論、妙衣服論、婬女巷論、諸國土論、大人傳論、世間傳論、大海傳論。……又多愛樂數與眾會……或樂雜住……或樂戲論。……如是等類眾多障法，應知一切名遠離障。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the obstacle to seclusion? It is eating heavily, being occupied with many affairs, many undertakings, many tasks. Or delighting in busy activity. … Or delighting in talk. … Or delighting in sleep. … Or delighting in noisy crowds — through this cause, delighting in discussing with householders and renunciants alike various talk of kings, of thieves, of food, of drink, of fine clothing, of the courtesans' quarter, of foreign lands, of famous people, of worldly gossip, of maritime adventure. Or delighting greatly in frequent socializing. … Or delighting in mixed company. … Or delighting in idle speculation. … All such many obstructive things should be understood as the obstacle to seclusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi fills out each of the ten with concrete texture. Heavy food specifically produces four distinct problems: a body too sluggish for real effort, a mind too dull to settle into concentration, breathing too labored for sustained practice (he notes even a pungent condiment can leave the breath briefly strained), and a mind repeatedly overtaken by drowsiness (sweets and warm milk both being cited as familiar culprits). Excessive business ventures — buying and selling, cultivating land, accumulating fine possessions — are illustrated with an old merchants' saying about needing to travel a thousand mountains, speak ten thousand words, devise a thousand schemes, and endure ten thousand hardships just to succeed in trade; a mind this occupied has nothing left for practice. Delighting in talk here specifically means a subtler trap: a practitioner genuinely capable of real seclusion and stillness who nonetheless settles for merely reciting and memorizing scripture — sometimes reinforced by a vivid experience during recitation, a fragrance or auspicious sign — mistaking that contentment for actual attainment and never advancing from mere hearing to genuine reflection and cultivation. Delighting in sleep breeds habitual laziness, treating drowsiness, reclining, and lying down each as a private pleasure worth indulging. Delighting in noisy crowds is illustrated with a specific, almost comically mundane list of topics — politics, crime, food, drink, fashion, the red-light district, foreign affairs, celebrity gossip, sea voyages — exactly the kind of talk that fills hours without producing anything of value. Frequent socializing scatters and agitates the mind through repeated gatherings; delighting in mixed company creates longing before a meeting and reluctance to part afterward; and delighting in idle speculation extends even to eagerness to be first in line for offerings, and — worst of all — casting off what the treatise calls the "yoke of goodness": refusing the genuinely humble lodgings (a cave, a straw hut, bare ground beneath a tree, a graveyard's edge) that austere, secluded practice actually requires, preferring comfort even at the cost of never leaving distraction behind.

42. Milestone: The Obstacle to Stillness Opens — Calming's Obstacle in Depth

The treatise's third summary obstacle, the obstacle to stillness, divides into two: an obstacle to calming meditation and an obstacle to insight meditation. Calming's obstacle opens first, tracing heedlessness through four distinct bad effects, then adding the disruption of an unsuitable dwelling.

Chinese Source

云何寂靜障？謂寂靜者，即奢摩他、毗鉢舍那。有奢摩他障、有毗鉢舍那障。云何奢摩他障？謂諸放逸及住非處。由放逸故，或惛沈睡眠纏繞其心，或唯得奢摩他便生愛味，或於下劣性心樂趣入，或於闇昧性其心樂著。由住如是非處所故，人或非人諠雜擾亂，他所逼惱，心外馳散。如是名為奢摩他障。當知此障能障寂靜。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the obstacle to stillness? Stillness means calming and insight; there is an obstacle to calming, and an obstacle to insight. What is the obstacle to calming? It is heedlessness and dwelling in an unsuitable place. Through heedlessness, the mind may be bound up in sloth and torpor; or, having attained calming alone, one may become attached to its flavor; or the mind may delight in turning toward base and inferior things; or the mind may become attached to a state of dullness. And because one dwells in such an unsuitable place, one is disturbed by the clamor of humans or non-humans, harassed by them, the mind scattering outward. This is called the obstacle to calming. Know that this obstacle obstructs stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi traces heedlessness to four distinct outcomes, each blocking calming meditation differently. First, sloth and torpor simply overtake the mind — the cumulative result of broken discipline, unguarded senses, poor eating habits, insufficient rest discipline, careless conduct, and half-hearted effort against affliction, leaving the body and mind too dull to sustain any real effort. Second, a practitioner who has genuinely attained the first absorption can become attached to its pleasure specifically — the ease of that state is so far beyond ordinary sensory pleasure that, without also cultivating insight, the attainment quietly degrades into just another comfortable worldly state, its owner unaware anything has been lost. Third, heedlessness lets the mind drift back toward base sensory indulgence, unable to restrain desire once vigilance relaxes. And fourth, it leaves the mind genuinely dull about the Dharma itself — unclear on the Four Noble Truths, dependent origination, or the emptiness of self and phenomena — not recognizing that calming meditation is the necessary support insight depends on, and so never taking it up with real seriousness. Beyond heedlessness, an unsuitable dwelling compounds the problem directly: disturbance from people or spirits, insects, weather, or genuine danger pulls the mind outward precisely when it most needs to turn inward, especially for a practitioner who hasn't yet developed enough concentration to hold steady regardless of surroundings.

43. Insight's Obstacle Opens: The Conceit of Birth, Wealth, and Learning

Chinese Source

云何毗鉢舍那障？謂樂自恃舉及以掉亂。……謂如有一，作是思惟：我生高族，淨信出家，非為下劣，諸餘苾芻則不如是。……如是我生富族……我具妙色……多聞、聞持、其聞積集，善巧言詞，語具圓滿，諸餘苾芻則不如是。由此因緣，自高自舉，陵懱於他。……彼由如是自高舉故，諸有苾芻耆年多智、積修梵行，不能時時恭敬請問。彼諸苾芻亦不時時為其開發未開發處……如是名為樂自恃舉毗鉢舍那障。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the obstacle to insight? It is delighting in self-conceit, and restlessness. … It is like one who thinks: I was born into a noble family, and went forth with genuine faith — not out of poverty, unlike other monks. … Likewise: I was born into a wealthy family … I possess a fine appearance … I am learned, retentive, my learning accumulated, skillful in speech, complete in expression — unlike other monks. Through this cause, one exalts oneself and looks down on others. … Because of this self-exaltation, one is unable to approach elder monks of great learning and long-cultivated purity with respect and questions from time to time. And those elder monks, in turn, do not, from time to time, open up for such a one what remains unopened … This is called delighting in self-conceit, the obstacle to insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this first cause of insight's obstacle as pride wearing five different masks — noble birth, wealth, physical beauty, broad learning genuinely retained and internalized, and eloquent, well-composed speech — each capable of curdling into the same quiet contempt for other practitioners perceived as less fortunate. The real damage isn't the pride itself so much as what it forecloses: a practitioner too self-satisfied to approach senior, learned monastics with genuine humility never receives what only humble inquiry unlocks — the deeper meaning still hidden beneath a passage's surface reading, or its subtler intended significance, the kind of guidance elder teachers only extend to a student who has shown up ready to actually listen. Cháng Bǎi illustrates "hidden meaning" with the Buddha's own teaching on emptiness in the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra, whose apparent claim that "all phenomena lack any nature of their own" turns out to rest on three distinct senses of "no nature" that a surface reading alone would never catch — exactly the kind of subtlety a proud, self-sufficient student forfeits by never genuinely asking.

44. Complacency and Restlessness Complete Insight's Obstacle

Chinese Source

又如有一，唯得少分下劣智見安隱而住。彼由如是少分下劣智見安住，便自高舉；自高舉故，便生喜足，更不上求。是名樂自恃舉所作毗鉢舍那障。……言掉亂者，謂如有一，根不寂靜，諸根掉亂、諸根囂舉。於一切時，惡思所思、惡說所說、惡作所作。不能安住思惟諸法，不能堅固思惟諸法。由此因緣，毗鉢舍那不能圓滿、不得清淨。是名掉亂毗鉢舍那障。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, it is like one who has attained only a small, inferior degree of knowledge and insight, and rests content in it. Resting in such a small, inferior attainment, one becomes conceited; and being conceited, one becomes complacent, seeking nothing higher. This is called the obstacle to insight caused by self-conceit. … Restlessness means being like one whose faculties are unsettled — agitated, unstable. At all times, one's thoughts, speech, and actions are unwholesome. One is unable to reflect on phenomena with any stability, unable to reflect on them with any firmness. Through this cause, insight cannot become complete, cannot become pure. This is called restlessness, the obstacle to insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies a second face of conceit here: not pride in birth or learning, but quiet satisfaction with a genuinely modest attainment — some small taste of insight into past or present conditions, or a settled sense of ease — mistaken for arrival rather than recognized as a single early step still far from the goal. Restlessness names a different personality altogether: the treatise's classical "discursive type," whose senses simply won't stay still, constantly reaching toward new objects, thoughts, and reactions, unable to sustain any consistent unwholesome pattern let alone a wholesome one. Cháng Bǎi cites Zhiyi's warning that excessive discursive thought is itself a direct obstacle to meditation, and notes the discursive type is proverbially harder to guide than even someone prone to open anger, since anger at least settles on a stable object while discursiveness never does. Without the capacity to reflect on the Dharma steadily and firmly enough to reach genuine conviction, insight simply cannot mature — leaving both conceit and restlessness as insight's two defining obstacles, alongside heedlessness and unsuitable dwelling as calming's two.

45. Milestone: Freedom From Obstruction Closes in Full

The treatise closes the obstacle to stillness by pairing its four causes against calming and insight, then closes the entire teaching on obstruction — detailed exposition and brief summary together — with a final, simple definition of what freedom from it actually means.

Chinese Source

如是二法障奢摩他，謂多放逸及住非處；二法能障毗鉢舍那，謂樂自恃舉及以掉亂。如是若奢摩他障、若毗鉢舍那障，總名寂靜障。……即此略義及前廣辯，總略為一，說名為障。此障相違，當知無障。謂即此障無性、遠離、不合、不會，說名無障。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus two factors obstruct calming: heedlessness, and dwelling in an unsuitable place. And two factors obstruct insight: self-conceit, and restlessness. Both the obstacle to calming and the obstacle to insight are together called the obstacle to stillness. … This brief summary, together with the detailed exposition given before, taken together as one, is called obstruction. Its opposite should be understood as freedom from obstruction — namely, that this obstruction has never taken root, is absent, does not coincide, and does not arise: this is called freedom from obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the pairing cleanly: heedlessness and an unsuitable dwelling obstruct calming; self-conceit and restlessness obstruct insight — together constituting the obstacle to stillness, the third of the three summary obstacles alongside effort and seclusion. With this, both the extraordinarily detailed exposition (internal obstacles across three categories, external across two) and the brief summary (effort, seclusion, and stillness) are declared to be, together, simply "obstruction" — two views of the same complete teaching, one exhaustive and one compact.

Freedom from obstruction is then defined with striking simplicity: not some separate positive achievement, but the straightforward absence of everything just described — never having planted the karmic causes for it in the first place, the obstruction genuinely not present, no coinciding conditions bringing it about, no actual occasion for it arising. Cháng Bǎi notes this simplicity is itself instructive: a practitioner does not need to manufacture some special state called "freedom from obstruction" — clearing away the causes already catalogued is the entire task. With this, freedom from obstruction, the twelfth of the fourteen path-provisions, is now complete.

46. Milestone: Generosity Opens — Giving That Serves the Path Itself

Fascicle 25 continues into the thirteenth path-provision, generosity, defining it specifically as giving undertaken for the sake of the path — adorning the mind, supporting it, aiding meditative cultivation, and reaching the highest good — distinct from giving for worldly reward.

Chinese Source

云何惠捨？謂若布施，其性無罪，為莊嚴心、為伴助心、為資瑜伽、為得上義而修布施，是名惠捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is generosity? It means that giving whose nature is blameless — undertaken to adorn the mind, to support the mind, to provide the resources for yogic practice, and to attain the highest good — is called generosity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as the treatise's classical "ultimate giving," the highest of eight traditional kinds of giving named in the Abhidharma treatises, defined by four purposes rather than by what's actually given: adorning the mind, since repeated giving directly weakens stinginess and leaves the mind genuinely more refined; supporting the mind, since the wholesome intention behind giving grows stronger and more expansive each time it's exercised; supplying the resources for yogic practice, since a mind free of stinginess and rich in accumulated merit is far better positioned to actually cultivate concentration; and attaining the highest good, since — for someone on the hearer-disciple's path — nothing in ordinary experience compares to the value of the undefiled path itself, which sustained giving directly supports.

Cháng Bǎi contrasts this with the Mahāyāna's own further refinement: a bodhisattva gives with a mind of non-attainment, seeking neither beauty nor wealth in return, nor even rebirth among gods or humans, but dedicating every act of giving — down to scraps of food left for ants on the grass — toward unsurpassed awakening itself, since it is the dedication of mind, not the size of the gift, that determines the merit produced. This fascicle's teaching, by contrast, is framed from the hearer-disciple's perspective, where giving serves specifically as one of the two provisions (worldly and world-transcending) for the path; the fuller Mahāyāna account will follow later, in the Bodhisattva-bhūmi.

The treatise now opens its detailed exposition with six classical questions that will structure everything to follow: who gives, to whom, with what, in what manner, how, and for what reason — the complete framework for understanding what makes an act of giving genuinely blameless.

47. Who Gives, and the First Recipients: Those in Genuine Hardship

Chinese Source

誰能施者，謂施者、施主，是名能施。云何施者？云何施主？謂若自手施，名為施者。若自物施、若欲樂施、非不樂施，名為施主。誰所施者，謂四種所施。一、有苦者……謂貧窮者、或乞丐者、或行路者、或希求者、或盲瞽者、或聾騃者、或無依者、或無趣者、匱乏種種資生具者。復有所餘如是等類，名有苦者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Who is able to give? The giver and the donor — these are called the able givers. What is a giver? What is a donor? Giving with one's own hand is called being a giver. Giving one's own property, willingly and not reluctantly, is called being a donor. To whom does one give? There are four kinds of recipients. First, those in hardship … the poor, beggars, travelers, seekers, the blind, the deaf, those without support, those without refuge, and those lacking the various necessities of life. And there are others of this same kind — these are called those in hardship.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes "giver" and "donor" as two roles that usually, but not always, coincide: a donor is whoever actually owns the gift and genuinely wishes to give it, willingly rather than under pressure; a giver is whoever physically carries out the act — most often the same person, but not when someone asks another to distribute a gift on their behalf, in which case the owner remains the donor while the one actually handing it over is the giver.

The first of four categories of recipient — those in hardship — gathers together anyone facing genuine material or physical difficulty: the poor, beggars, travelers away from home, those in earnest need of something specific, the blind, the deaf or otherwise impaired, orphans and the unsupported elderly, the homeless, and anyone lacking basic necessities or struck by disaster. Cháng Bǎi treats this category as the most straightforward: wherever suffering is visible and giving can genuinely relieve it, that alone is sufficient reason to give.

48. Two More Recipients: Those Who Showed Kindness, and Those Held Dear

Chinese Source

云何有恩者？謂或父母、或乳飲者、或養育者、或成長者、或於曠野沙磧等中能濟度者、或饑儉時能賑恤者、或怖怨敵而救援者、或被執縛而能解者、或遭疾病而救療者、教利益者、教安樂者、引利益者、引安樂者、隨所生起諸事務中為助伴者、同歡喜者、同憂愁者、遭厄難時不相棄者。……云何親愛者？謂諸親友，或於其處有愛有敬、或信順語、或數語言談論交往、或有親昵。

Ru-Yi Translation

Second, those who have shown kindness: parents; those who nursed one as an infant; those who raised one; those who helped one grow to maturity; those who rescued one in a wilderness or desert; those who gave relief during famine; those who saved one from a feared enemy; those who freed one from captivity; those who healed one during illness; those who taught what brings benefit or ease; those who guided one toward benefit or ease; those who assisted in whatever task arose; those who shared one's joy or one's sorrow; and those who did not abandon one in genuine crisis. Third, those held dear: relatives and friends — those toward whom one feels affection and respect, whose words one trusts and follows, with whom one converses often, or with whom one shares real intimacy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the second category as a genuinely wide net cast around anyone who has done real good for oneself — not only parents and those who physically raised and nursed one, but anyone who rescued one from danger (wilderness, famine, hostile enemies, captivity, illness), taught something genuinely beneficial, helped with any task, shared life's joys and sorrows alike, or simply stayed present through a real crisis rather than abandoning one to face it alone. Gratitude toward all of these, he notes, is itself a wholesome quality worth cultivating through giving, quite apart from any expectation of further benefit. The third category, those held dear, is comparatively simple: relatives and genuine friends, marked by real affection, mutual trust, frequent honest conversation, and real closeness — giving here strengthens bonds already worth strengthening.

49. Milestone: The Venerable, and a Digression on What Truly Determines Merit

The treatise names the fourth and final category of recipient — the venerable, those genuinely worthy of reverence — then Master Cháng Bǎi opens a rich digression on what actually determines the merit of a gift: the quality of the recipient, or the quality of the giver's own mind.

Chinese Source

云何尊勝？謂若沙門、若婆羅門，世間同許為賢善者、離損害者、極離害者、離貪欲者、為調伏貪而修行者、離瞋恚者、為調伏瞋而修行者、離愚癡者、為調伏癡而修行者。復有所餘如是等類，名尊勝者。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by the venerable? Renunciants or brahmins universally acknowledged by the world as genuinely virtuous — free of harm, utterly free of harm; free of desire, or practicing to overcome desire; free of aversion, or practicing to overcome aversion; free of delusion, or practicing to overcome delusion. And there are others of this same kind — these are called the venerable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this fourth category spans both accomplished noble ones who have genuinely eliminated desire, aversion, and delusion, and ordinary practitioners still earnestly working to overcome them — both equally worthy of support, since both are engaged in the same fundamental undertaking.

He then takes up a classical question this raises: does the merit of a gift depend on the worthiness of the recipient (the "field") or the quality of the giver's own mind? He illustrates with the well-known story of the Buddha and Śāriputra: when Śāriputra offered the Buddha food and the Buddha instead fed it to a dog, then asked whose merit was greater, Śāriputra answered that the Buddha's was — because although Śāriputra's own gift went to the most excellent field imaginable, the Buddha's compassion in that moment was itself more excellent still, showing that an inferior field met with a superior mind can outweigh a superior field met with a merely dutiful one. Cháng Bǎi also cites a second classical framework, "honoring the Dharma, valuing the person," under which giving to a stream-enterer outweighs giving to an ordinary person, giving to a once-returner outweighs a stream-enterer, and so on up through giving to a Buddha — a hierarchy that tracks how fully a given being embodies realized truth. And a third view holds that giving with a genuinely equal mind, regardless of who receives it, produces equal merit regardless of the field's actual rank. Cháng Bǎi doesn't resolve these three views against each other, but presents them together as complementary lenses: the field matters, the mind matters, and — at the deepest level — a mind of true equanimity can matter more than either.

50. What Is Given: Sentient Gifts, and the Beginning of Material Gifts

Chinese Source

用何施者，謂若略說，或用有情數物而行惠施，或用無情數物而行惠施。……謂或妻子、奴婢、作使，或象、馬、豬、牛、羊、雞、鴨、駝、騾等類，或有諸餘大男、大女、小男、小女……若有於彼諸有情類，或得自在，或有勢力，或能制伏……是名無罪有情數物持用惠施。云何無情數物持用惠施？謂若略說有三種物。一者、財物，二者、穀物，三者、處物。

Ru-Yi Translation

With what does one give? Briefly, one gives either using sentient possessions, or using non-sentient possessions. … Namely, one's spouse and children, servants, attendants; or elephants, horses, pigs, cattle, sheep, chickens, ducks, camels, or mules; or other men, women, boys, or girls in one's care. … If one has genuine authority, power, or control over such beings … this is called blameless giving of sentient possessions. What is giving of non-sentient possessions? Briefly, there are three kinds: wealth, grain, and dwelling places.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise permits giving away sentient possessions genuinely within one's authority — a household's dependents, livestock, or, in the ancient social context, servants and attendants — but only under three strict conditions: the giver must have genuine authority over the being in question, the gift must not itself be an act of wrongdoing, and it must not leave the one given away resentful or land them somewhere they'll actually be mistreated; a child given up for adoption, for instance, must go to a home that will genuinely care for them. He flags an important boundary here: the treatise also mentions bodhisattvas giving away parts of their own body — eyes, limbs, flesh, even marrow — but explicitly excludes this from what a hearer-disciple is expected or even permitted to do at this stage; that level of self-sacrifice belongs to the bodhisattva path described later in the treatise, not to the more modest giving appropriate here.

Non-sentient gifts then open with three broad categories — wealth, grain, and dwelling places — the treatise's classical way of organizing the world's remaining material goods, about to be unpacked one at a time.

51. Grain and Dwellings Close Material Gifts; the Manner of Giving

Chinese Source

云何處物？謂諸田宅、邸店、鄽肆，建立福舍及寺館等。……當知此中，有情數物、無情數物，一切總說名所用施。何相施者，謂無貪俱行思造作心意業，及此所起身業、語業，捨所施物。或自相續，或他相續。是名施相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are dwelling-places? Fields, houses, inns, shops, charitable halls, temples, and lodges. … Know that sentient and non-sentient possessions together are called what is given. In what manner does one give? It means the mental karma of volition accompanied by non-attachment, together with the bodily and verbal karma that arises from it, in relinquishing the gift — whether through one's own continuity, or through another's. This is called the manner of giving.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes grain covers the full range of edible staples — grains, legumes, produce, and prepared drinks and foods — while dwelling-places cover fields, houses, inns, shops, and buildings dedicated specifically to charitable or religious use, such as almshouses, temples, and libraries. With wealth, grain, and dwelling-places together, the "with what" question closes in full.

The "manner" of giving then turns from the object given to the mental act itself: genuine giving is defined by volition free of attachment, carried out through body and speech, whether the giver personally executes the act or delegates it to someone else acting on their behalf. Cháng Bǎi stresses this is what actually distinguishes a gift from a mere transaction — attachment or hidden expectation in the underlying volition disqualifies an act from counting as genuine giving at all, however generous it might look from the outside.

52. Milestone: How to Give — Seven Qualities That Make Giving Genuine

The treatise's fifth question, how one gives, names seven distinct qualities that together define genuinely excellent giving, then adds the purity of the gift itself and the inner psychology of joy that should accompany it from beginning to end.

Chinese Source

云何施者，謂由淨信而行惠施；由正教見而行惠施；由有果見而行惠施；由極殷重而行惠施；由恭敬心自手行施，而不輕慢；應時而施，濟他要用；不損惱他而行惠施。……如法平等……以清淨物而行惠施。……數數惠施制伏慳垢……先心歡喜而行惠施，於正施時其心清淨，施已無悔。如是而施。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one give? By giving with genuine faith; by giving with correct understanding; by giving with an understanding of its results; by giving with the utmost earnestness; by giving respectfully with one's own hand, never with contempt; by giving at the right time, meeting an actual need; by giving without causing distress. … Lawfully and impartially … giving with a genuinely pure object. … Giving again and again overcomes the taint of stinginess … with a joyful mind beforehand, a pure mind during the act itself, and no regret afterward. This is how one gives.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks all seven qualities in turn: genuine faith in the Three Jewels as the motivating ground; correct understanding — free of superstitious or violent notions about what "counts" as meritorious giving, such as the old practice of bloody sacrifice mistaken for religious merit; clear understanding of specific karmic results (food yielding future strength, clothing yielding future beauty, transport yielding future ease, light yielding future clear sight) held with genuine conviction rather than mere imitation of others; deep earnestness rather than a perfunctory gesture; personal, respectful delivery by one's own hand wherever possible, since a king who personally hands alms to the poor is said to generate greater merit than one who merely orders it done; timeliness — an umbrella offered exactly when it's raining, water when someone is thirsty, money when it's actually needed, rather than a promised gift indefinitely delayed or a gift that doesn't match the real need; and giving without any accompanying unkindness, since a gift preceded by a cutting remark can cost a recipient their dignity and undo the merit of the gift itself.

Beyond the act itself, the gift's own quality matters: obtained lawfully rather than through violence or fraud, offered fresh and clean rather than as unwanted leftovers, and genuinely fine rather than merely adequate. And the giver's inner state must remain consistent throughout — joy in anticipation, purity of mind during the act, and no regret afterward, since regretting a gift already given is said to bring exactly the opposite of prosperity: wealth that arrives but never stays. Cháng Bǎi notes repeated giving is itself the direct antidote to stinginess — an old, unexercised habit of clinging weakens each time it's deliberately overridden, until giving eventually becomes second nature rather than an effort of will.

53. Milestone: Why One Gives, and Generosity Closes in Full

The treatise's sixth and final question, why one gives, matches a specific motivation to each of the four recipient categories already named — then closes the entire teaching on generosity with its complete summary formula.

Chinese Source

何故施者，或慈悲故而行惠施，謂於有苦。或知恩故而行惠施，謂於有恩。或愛、或敬、或信順故而行惠施，謂於親愛。或為希求世出世間殊勝功德而行惠施，謂於尊勝。由是因緣，故修惠施。……由是行相，或在家者、或出家者修行布施，為莊嚴心、為伴助心、為資瑜伽、為得上義而行布施。由此因緣，施性無罪，是名惠捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Why does one give? Out of compassion, toward those in hardship. Out of gratitude, toward those who have shown kindness. Out of affection, respect, or trust, toward those held dear. Or in pursuit of excellent worldly and world-transcending qualities, toward the venerable. For these reasons, one practices giving. … By means of these characteristics, whether a householder or a renunciant practices giving, it is undertaken to adorn the mind, to support the mind, to provide the resources for yogic practice, and to attain the highest good. For this reason, the nature of such giving is blameless — this is called generosity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the circle back to the four recipients named at the very start of the detailed exposition: compassion is the natural motivation toward anyone in genuine hardship; gratitude is the natural motivation toward anyone who has already shown kindness; affection, respect, and trust are the natural motivations toward family and friends; and aspiration toward genuinely excellent qualities — both worldly virtue and the world-transcending path itself — is the natural motivation toward the venerable. Each motivation fits its object precisely, rather than a single generic sentiment applied indiscriminately.

With this, the treatise closes generosity in full: six questions — who gives, to whom, with what, in what manner, how, and why — together defining what makes an act of giving genuinely blameless, and, taken as a whole, constituting generosity, the thirteenth of the fourteen path-provisions, whether practiced by a householder or a renunciant. Cháng Bǎi notes this closes the loop opened at the section's start: giving undertaken this way adorns the mind, supports it, provides genuine resources for meditative cultivation, and ultimately serves the highest good — precisely the four purposes "ultimate giving" was defined by from the outset.

54. Milestone: Five Ways to Weigh a Gift, Closing Generosity's Teaching in Full

Master Cháng Bǎi closes generosity with five classical comparative frameworks for understanding what actually determines a gift's merit — the field versus the wealth given, the weight of mind versus the weight of wealth, emptiness versus substance, quantity versus quality, and the purity of giver and recipient — illustrated throughout with vivid traditional stories drawn from the Fǎjù Pìyù Jīng, the Dà Zhìdù Lùn, and related sources rather than from the root treatise itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the teaching on generosity with five paired lenses. Field against wealth: a superb field met with a modest gift (a child who once offered the Buddha a handful of sand and was later reborn a king; the elder Kāśyapa, who deliberately begged from the poorest rather than the richest, receiving only leftover rinse-water from a destitute woman he then taught to meditate, and who was later reborn in the heavens) can outweigh an ordinary field met with an impressive gift, though genuine compassion applied even to an ordinary recipient still yields real merit. Mind against wealth: a poor woman's single lamp, bought with her only coin and offered with total sincerity, is said to have burned all night unextinguished — "a small gift, a heavy heart" outweighing wealth given with an offhand or condescending attitude. Emptiness against substance: understanding emptiness intellectually while still clutching one's wealth when a genuine chance to give arrives still yields poverty in a future life, while giving freely — even without dwelling on emptiness as a concept — while genuinely expecting nothing back yields far greater merit; wisdom about non-attachment that never actually loosens the grip on possessions isn't yet functioning as wisdom at all. Quantity against quality, per the Fǎjù Pìyù Jīng's four combinations: much given for something worthless (an animal sacrifice, a lavish banquet serving only indulgence) yields little merit; little given with genuine reverence to those of real virtue yields much; little given out of stinginess to the genuinely undeserving yields little; and much given wisely — building genuine places of practice, supporting the monastic community properly — yields much, exemplified by the great donor Anāthapiṇḍika's own famous generosity. And purity of giver against purity of recipient, per the Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra's own fourfold analysis, ranges from an ordinary giver and an ordinary recipient (neither yet free of affliction) up to the rare case of Buddhas making offerings to Buddhas, purity meeting purity at the highest possible level. With this, generosity — the thirteenth path-provision — closes completely.

55. Milestone: The Final Provision Opens — The Ornaments of a Genuine Renunciant

Fascicle 25 opens its fourteenth and final path-provision: the ornaments of a genuine renunciant — seventeen qualities, announced first in a summary verse, that together define what makes someone a monastic worthy of the name, truly fit to receive offerings.

Chinese Source

云何名為沙門莊嚴？嗢柁南曰：正信而無諂，少病精進慧，具少欲喜足，易養及易滿，杜多德端嚴，知量善士法，具聰慧者相，忍柔和賢善。謂如有一，具足正信、無有諂曲、少諸疾病、性勤精進、成就妙慧、少欲、喜足、易養、易滿、具足成就杜多功德、端嚴、知量、具足成就賢善士法、具足成就聰慧者相、堪忍、柔和、為性賢善。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by the ornaments of a renunciant? A summary verse says: full faith and freedom from deceit; few illnesses, diligence, wisdom; few desires and contentment; ease of support and ease of satisfaction; the virtues of ascetic practice, dignified bearing; a sense of proper measure, the qualities of a good person; the marks of a wise person; patience, gentleness, and innate goodness. It is like one who is possessed of full faith, free of deceit, rarely ill, diligent by nature, accomplished in excellent wisdom, of few desires, contented, easy to support, easy to satisfy, fully accomplished in the virtues of ascetic practice, dignified in bearing, possessed of a sense of proper measure, fully accomplished in the qualities of a good person, fully accomplished in the marks of a wise person, patient, gentle, and good-natured by disposition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames these seventeen qualities as the treatise's complete portrait of what makes a monastic genuinely deserve support: not merely wearing the outward form, but embodying the substance the form is meant to represent, worthy of exactly the reverence and material support laypeople extend to renunciants on trust. He notes this closing provision pairs with a second, complementary list of sixteen behaviors to be actively avoided — restraint expressed negatively rather than positively — bringing the total picture of a genuine renunciant to thirty-three qualities together: seventeen to cultivate, sixteen to abstain from. The treatise now unpacks all seventeen positive qualities one by one, beginning with full faith.

56. Full Faith: Reverence Extended to Every Dimension of Practice

Chinese Source

云何名為具足正信？謂多淨信、多正敬順、多生勝解、多善欲樂。於諸善法及大師所深生淨信，無惑無疑；於大師所恭敬尊重承奉供養；既修如是恭敬尊重承奉供養，專心親附，依止而住。如於大師，如是於法同梵行者、於諸所學教授教誡、於修供養、於無放逸、於三摩地，當知亦爾。如是名為具足正信。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by full faith? It means abundant pure faith, abundant genuine reverence and compliance, abundant resolute understanding, and abundant wholesome aspiration. One holds deep, pure faith in wholesome teachings and in the great Teacher, free of doubt or uncertainty; one reveres, honors, serves, and makes offerings to the Teacher; and, having cultivated such reverence, honor, service, and offering, one draws close with a devoted mind and abides in reliance upon him. Just as toward the great Teacher, so too toward the Dharma, toward fellow practitioners of pure conduct, toward the instruction and guidance one has received, toward the practice of making offerings, toward vigilance, and toward meditative concentration — know that the same applies in each case. This is called full faith.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats full faith as devotion extended systematically across seven objects, not confined to the Buddha alone: reverence toward the Teacher himself, toward the Dharma he taught, toward fellow practitioners walking the same path, toward the specific instruction and correction received from teachers, toward the actual practice of making offerings, toward vigilant, undistracted conduct, and toward meditative concentration itself. He glosses the four qualifying terms precisely: pure faith is confidence in the Three Jewels so settled it cannot be shaken by rival teachings or persuasive opponents; genuine reverence and compliance is active honor and service, not passive assent; resolute understanding is deep confidence in the Buddha's inconceivable power and profound teaching, free of the fear or hesitation an incomplete understanding would otherwise produce; and wholesome aspiration is genuine delight in wholesome practices, wanting to undertake and actually complete them. Together, these define the first and foundational ornament of a genuine renunciant — everything else in the list builds on this settled confidence.

57. Freedom From Deceit, and Rarely Ill

Chinese Source

云何名為無有諂曲？謂有純質，為性正直，於其大師及諸有智同梵行所，如實自顯。如是名為無有諂曲。云何名為少諸疾病？謂性無病，順時變熟，平等執受，不極溫熱、不極寒冷，無所損害，隨時安樂。由是因緣，所食、所飲、所噉、所嘗，易正變熟。如是名為少諸疾病。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by freedom from deceit? It means being genuinely simple and upright by nature, showing oneself truthfully before the Teacher and before wise fellow practitioners of pure conduct. This is called freedom from deceit. What is meant by being rarely ill? It means being naturally healthy, digesting food properly in season, maintaining even bodily balance, neither too hot nor too cold, without harm, at ease at all times. Through this cause, what one eats, drinks, chews, and tastes is easily and properly digested. This is called being rarely ill.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes freedom from deceit as simple integrity: showing the same face to the Teacher and to wise companions that one shows in private, with no performance of devotion that doesn't match the inner state — the opposite of appearing reverent toward the Three Jewels while feeling nothing of the kind. Deceit is exactly this gap between outward show and inward reality; freedom from it means the gap simply doesn't exist.

Being rarely ill, he notes, traces back to a past free of killing and to a present life of genuinely disciplined eating — proper measure, food eaten only once previously digested, meals suited to the season — leaving the body's elements naturally balanced, neither overheated nor chilled, and free of the various illnesses catalogued so extensively back in fascicle 23's teaching on moderation in eating. A healthy monastic, unburdened by constant medical concerns, has correspondingly more genuine capacity left over for sustained practice.

58. Milestone: Diligence and Excellent Wisdom

The fourth and fifth ornaments name genuine diligence — the same five-fold energy already introduced in fascicle 24, now with a distinctive final nuance — and excellent wisdom, defined through three qualities: native cleverness, retentive memory, and realized insight.

Chinese Source

云何名為性勤精進？謂能安住有勢、有勤、有勇、堅猛，於善法中能不捨軛，翹勤無惰，起發圓滿，能有所作，於諸有智同梵行者躬自承奉。如是名為性勤精進。云何名為成就妙慧？謂聰、念、覺皆悉圓滿。根不闇鈍、根不頑愚，亦不瘖瘂，非手代言，有力能了善說、惡說所有法義，具足成就俱生覺慧，具足成就加行覺慧。如是名為成就妙慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by diligence by nature? It means abiding with strength, with sustained effort, with courage, with steadfastness, never abandoning the yoke of wholesome practice, ever diligent and never lazy, generating complete energy, seeing tasks through to completion, and personally attending upon wise fellow practitioners of pure conduct. This is called diligence by nature. What is meant by accomplished excellent wisdom? It means cleverness, mindfulness, and realization, all complete: faculties neither dull nor obstinate, neither mute nor reliant on gesture in place of speech, capable of distinguishing well-spoken teaching from poorly-spoken teaching, fully accomplished in both innate wisdom and wisdom cultivated through effort. This is called accomplished excellent wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps diligence onto the same five kinds of energy already detailed in fascicle 24's account of wakeful practice — armor-like resolve, applied effort, undaunted courage even facing the vastness of the path ahead, steadfastness unmoved by heat, cold, or insects, and, distinctively here, "never abandoning the yoke of wholesome practice" — refusing to settle for whatever small progress has already been made, continuing to till the same ground rather than unhitching early. A ninth quality closes the set: genuinely serving wise companions in person, not merely in sentiment.

Excellent wisdom then names three components. Cleverness combines innate aptitude (a naturally quick, discerning mind, present from birth in varying degrees the way some people are naturally strong in concentration and others in memory) with wisdom cultivated through sheer effort — someone slower by nature can still arrive at the same place through sufficient repetition. Mindfulness is simply strong retention — Cháng Bǎi illustrates it with the young Xuanzang, said to have grasped his father's teaching after a single hearing while his brothers needed it repeated, retaining what he heard so thoroughly that later biographers describe him as "mastering the point on a single pass, remembering what his eyes had merely crossed." And realization is genuine insight gained through actual practice — not simply knowing that all things are impermanent, empty, and without self as doctrine, but having directly seen it clearly enough that the knowledge changes how one meets every subsequent experience. All three, cultivated together through hearing, reflection, and cultivation, constitute the fifth ornament.

59. Few Desires, and Contentment Opens

Chinese Source

云何少欲？謂雖成就善少欲等所有功德，而不於此欲求他知。謂他知我具足少欲，成就功德。是名少欲。云何喜足？謂於隨一衣服、飲食、臥具等事，便生歡喜，生正知足。於所未得所有衣服，或麤或妙，更無希望、更無思慮；於所已得不染不愛，如前廣說而受用之。如於衣服，於其飲食、臥具等事，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is having few desires? It means that, although one has accomplished the qualities of having few desires and the like, one has no wish for others to know of it — no wish that others should know "I have few desires, I have accomplished these qualities." This is called having few desires. What is contentment? It means that, regarding whatever robe, food, bedding, or the like one happens to receive, one feels genuine joy and correct contentment. Regarding robes not yet received, whether coarse or fine, one has no further hope or concern for them; regarding those already received, one uses them without attachment or craving, exactly as explained at length before. As with robes, so too with food, bedding, and the like.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "few desires" here in a specific, almost paradoxical sense: not merely wanting little materially, but not even wanting recognition for wanting little — a monastic genuinely free of desire for possessions who still secretly hopes others will notice and admire that quality hasn't actually achieved it. Genuine humility keeps even one's own virtues quiet, since seeking praise for renunciation is itself a subtle form of the very craving being renounced.

Contentment then opens as few desires' natural companion: whatever robe, food, or bedding one happens to receive — coarse or fine — is met with genuine ease rather than restless hope for something better, and what has already been received is used without attachment, exactly as the treatise's earlier teaching on moderation in eating already described in detail. Cháng Bǎi notes the same equanimity extends identically across every category of material support, so that a monastic's peace of mind never actually depends on what happens to be offered.

60. Milestone: Easy to Support, Easy to Satisfy, and Ascetic Virtues Open

Two brief ornaments — easy to support and easy to satisfy — close out the "contentment" cluster. The treatise then opens its tenth and richest ornament, the virtues of ascetic practice, listing thirteen classical disciplines spanning food, robes, and dwelling.

Chinese Source

云何易養？謂能獨一自得怡養，不待於他，或諸僮僕、或餘人眾。又不追求餘長財寶，令他施者、施主等類謂為難養。是名易養。云何易滿？謂得微少，便自支持；若得麤弊，亦自支持。是名易滿。云何成就杜多功德？謂常期乞食、次第乞食、但一座食、先止後食、但持三衣、但持毳衣、持糞掃衣、住阿練若、常居樹下、常居迥露、常住塚間、常期端坐、處如常座。如是依止若食、若衣、若諸敷具杜多功德，或十二種，或十三種。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is being easy to support? It means being able, on one's own, to sustain oneself contentedly, without depending on others, on servants, or on any retinue; and not seeking further wealth beyond what is needed, so that donors never find one difficult to support. This is called being easy to support. What is being easy to satisfy? It means that, receiving only a little, one is content to sustain oneself with it; and receiving something coarse or poor, one is likewise content to sustain oneself with it. This is called being easy to satisfy. What is accomplishing the virtues of ascetic practice? Regularly begging for alms, begging in sequence, eating only one sitting's worth, eating only before a fixed time, keeping only three robes, wearing only wool, wearing only discarded rags, dwelling in the forest, dwelling constantly beneath a tree, dwelling constantly in the open, dwelling constantly near a charnel ground, always sitting upright, and remaining in one's fixed seat. Depending on whether counted by food, robe, or bedding, the virtues of ascetic practice number either twelve or thirteen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats "easy to support" and "easy to satisfy" as two faces of the same self-sufficiency: needing no attendants or retinue to feel genuinely at ease, and never chasing after wealth beyond actual necessity, so that supporting such a monastic is never a burden on generous donors; and being able to sustain oneself equally well on a little as on a lot, on the coarse as on the fine, so that satisfaction never depends on what happens to be offered.

He then introduces the treatise's tenth ornament, the classical thirteen (or, counted differently, twelve) ascetic practices — traditionally called dhūta, "shaking off" — organized into three groups: four concerning food (regular almsround rather than being invited to meals, begging door to door in strict sequence without favoring wealthier households, eating only what one sitting can consume, and eating only before a fixed hour), three concerning robes (owning only the three basic robes, made only of coarse wool rather than fine cloth, and sewn from discarded rags rather than new fabric), and six concerning dwelling and posture (living in the forest, constantly beneath a tree, constantly in the open air, constantly near a charnel ground, always sitting upright rather than lying down, and remaining wherever one's seat happens to be rather than choosing a preferred spot). Cháng Bǎi notes the count varies between twelve and thirteen only because two of the food practices are sometimes treated as a single combined discipline — the substance is identical either way, aimed at systematically stripping away every unnecessary attachment to comfort.

61. Begging for Food: Neither Favoring the Rich Nor Favoring the Poor

Chinese Source

於乞食中分為二種。一者、隨得乞食，二者、次第乞食。隨得乞食者，謂隨往還家，隨獲隨得而便受食。次第乞食者，謂入里巷，巡家而乞，隨得隨現而便受食。不高舉手越趣餘家，願我當獲精妙飲食，乃至期願多有所得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Begging for alms is of two kinds: begging as it comes, and begging in sequence. Begging as it comes means that, going to whatever households one customarily visits, one receives and eats whatever is obtained there. Begging in sequence means entering the lanes and alleys and going from house to house in order, receiving and eating whatever appears, without passing over one household in favor of another in hope of obtaining finer food, still less hoping to obtain a great deal.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the two forms of begging by their scope: begging as it comes simply means accepting whatever is received at one's customary households without complaint, free of preference for finer or coarser fare; begging in sequence is stricter still, requiring a monastic to proceed house to house in strict order through a neighborhood, never skipping a poor household in hopes of a wealthier one further along, nor — equally important, Cháng Bǎi stresses — favoring the poor out of pity while skipping the wealthy, a subtler error some well-meaning practitioners fall into. The Buddha's own teaching on this point applied to his followers generally: neither courting the rich nor showing special favor to the poor, but proceeding in strict, impartial sequence, letting whatever comes be received exactly as it comes.

62. Eating Once, Eating Before a Fixed Hour, and Owning Only Three Robes

Chinese Source

謂坐一座，乃至應食悉皆受食，從此座起必不重食。……謂為食故，坐如應坐，乃至未食先應具受諸所應食。應正了知：我今唯受爾所飲食，當自支持。又正了知：我過於此，定不當食。……謂但三衣而自支持。……一、僧伽胝，二、嗢怛羅僧伽，三、安怛婆參。除此三衣，終不貯畜過此長衣。

Ru-Yi Translation

Eating only one sitting's worth means receiving and eating whatever food is appropriate while seated, and once risen from that seat, never eating again. Eating only before a fixed time means sitting properly to eat, first fully receiving all the food to be eaten, and clearly resolving: I shall take only this much food to sustain myself, and beyond this I will certainly not eat. Owning only three robes means sustaining oneself with three robes alone: the great robe, the middle robe, and the inner robe — and never keeping any further robe beyond these three.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these three practices as disciplining appetite and possession together. Eating only once per sitting means no returning for seconds after rising, even if the meal is interrupted; eating only before a fixed hour means deciding the full measure of the meal before the first bite and holding firmly to that limit rather than continuing to eat as more becomes available. Owning only three robes revisits the classical set already introduced in fascicle 24 — the great ceremonial robe, the middle robe for assemblies, and the plain inner robe for daily wear and sleep — with the ascetic practice adding a stricter edge: no accumulation of any further robe beyond these three, however the ordinary allowances for extra or consecrated robes might otherwise permit.

63. Coarse Wool Only, and Robes Sewn From Discarded Rags

Chinese Source

謂所持衣或三衣數、或是長衣，一切皆用毛毳而作，終不貯蓄餘所作衣。……謂所有衣，他捨棄擲或街、或巷、或市、或鄽、或道非道，或雜便穢，或為便穢、膿血、洟唾之所塗染。取如是等不淨衣物，除去麤穢，堅執洗浣，縫染受持。

Ru-Yi Translation

Wearing only wool means that whatever robes one keeps — whether the three basic robes or any further robe — are all made of coarse animal hair, never any finer material kept in store. Wearing only discarded rags means taking cloth others have thrown away in the street, the alley, the marketplace, a shop, on a road or off it, cloth soiled with filth, blood, pus, mucus, or spittle — taking such unclean cloth, removing the coarsest dirt, washing it with real care, and sewing and dyeing it into a robe to be kept and worn.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes wearing only coarse wool means never keeping fine cloth in reserve even when it's available — deliberately choosing the roughest available material as ordinary dress. Wearing rag-robes goes further still: actively seeking out cloth already discarded as filthy or worthless, scavenged from streets, markets, or roadsides, soiled with genuine human waste or bodily fluids, then carefully cleaning, mending, and dyeing it into something wearable. Cháng Bǎi notes cloth was a genuinely precious commodity in the ancient setting, and this practice — literally the origin of "rag robe" as a term for a monastic's garment — is meant to sever any remaining attachment to appearance or comfort in dress as completely as possible.

64. Milestone: Six Ways of Dwelling Close All Thirteen Ascetic Practices

The treatise closes the ascetic virtues with six practices concerning where and how a practitioner dwells and sits — forest, tree, open air, charnel ground, an upright seated posture maintained without lying down, and staying wherever one's seat happens to already be arranged.

Chinese Source

謂住空閑山林坰野，受用邊際所有臥具，遠離一切村邑聚落。……謂常期願住於樹下，依止樹根。……謂常期願住於迥露，無覆障處。……謂常期願住塚墓間，諸有命過送尸骸處。……謂於大床、或小繩床、或草葉座端身而坐，推度時日，終不以背或以其脅，依倚大床、或小繩床、或壁、或樹、草葉座等。……謂所坐臥或諸草座、或諸葉座，如舊敷設草座、葉座而常坐臥，一敷設後，終不數數翻舉修理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Dwelling in the forest means living in quiet forests and open wilderness, using only the most minimal bedding, far from every village or town. Dwelling constantly beneath a tree means resolving always to dwell at the root of a tree. Dwelling constantly in the open means resolving always to dwell in open, unsheltered places. Dwelling constantly near a charnel ground means resolving always to dwell where the dead are brought and their remains laid to rest. Always sitting upright means sitting properly on a bed, rope-bed, or grass seat, passing the days this way, never reclining against a bed, wall, tree, or seat with one's back or side. Remaining in one's fixed seat means that, once a grass or leaf seat has been laid out, one sits and lies there as it already stands, never repeatedly rearranging or refashioning it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads all six as variations on the same discipline: deliberately choosing the least comfortable, least secure dwelling available, precisely because comfort and security are exactly what ordinary attachment clings to. Forest-dwelling and tree-dwelling recall the Buddha's own practice of seeking awakening at the foot of a tree; open-air dwelling, with no roof or even branches for shelter, is said to sharpen the mind toward the meditation on emptiness; charnel-ground dwelling puts death's reality in constant view, supporting the contemplation of impermanence and impurity already detailed at length in fascicle 24. Always sitting upright — never lying down, whatever the exhaustion — is the practice of "never lying down" still spoken of today, requiring genuine resolve to sustain. And remaining in one's fixed seat closes the list with something almost comically small in scale yet psychologically real: not endlessly rearranging one's cushion or seat in search of an ideal setup, since restlessness about physical comfort, Cháng Bǎi notes, is itself a sign the mind hasn't yet actually settled — genuine stillness in meditation makes the seat's arrangement essentially irrelevant. With this sixth dwelling practice, all twelve (or thirteen) ascetic practices — four concerning food, three concerning robes, and six concerning dwelling — are now complete.

65. Milestone: Why "Ascetic Virtues" — The Carded Wool Analogy

The treatise explains why these thirteen disciplines are called "ascetic virtues" (dhūta, literally "shaken off") through a vivid analogy: raw wool, tangled and unusable until beaten, plucked, and carded smooth — exactly like a mind still knotted with attachment to food, clothing, and comfort.

Chinese Source

答：譬如世間或毛或㲲，未鞭、未彈、未紛、未擘，爾時相著，不軟不輕，不任造作縷線氈蓐。若鞭、若彈、若紛、若擘，爾時分散，柔軟輕妙，堪任造作縷線氈蓐。如是行者，由飲食貪，於諸飲食令心染著；由衣服貪，於諸衣服令心染著；由敷具貪，於諸敷具令心染著。彼由如是杜多功德，能淨修治，令其純直，柔軟輕妙，有所堪任，隨順依止，能修梵行。是故名為杜多功德。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like wool or felt in the world: before it has been whipped, plucked, combed, or carded, it clings together, neither soft nor light, unfit to be made into thread, yarn, or felted cloth. Once whipped, plucked, combed, and carded, it comes apart, soft and light, fit to be made into thread, yarn, or felted cloth. In just the same way, a practitioner's mind clings, through craving for food, to food; through craving for clothing, to clothing; through craving for bedding, to bedding. Through these ascetic virtues, such a one is purified and refined, becoming genuinely simple and upright, soft, light, and capable — fit to rely upon in practicing pure conduct. This is why they are called ascetic virtues.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the analogy out precisely: raw wool clumped together is useless for weaving until deliberately beaten and combed apart; a mind still clumped together with attachment to food, clothing, and comfortable dwelling is equally unfit for genuine practice until these thirteen disciplines "comb" it loose. Once purified this way, the mind becomes simple, pliable, and light — exactly the qualities needed to actually sustain pure conduct and reach what hasn't yet been attained: entry into the first fruit of the path, and progressively the second, third, and finally arhatship itself. With this etymology given, the tenth ornament — the full thirteen ascetic virtues — is formally named and complete.

66. Milestone: Each Practice Matched to the Specific Craving It Cuts

The treatise now maps each ascetic practice back to the exact craving it was designed to counter — a precise, almost clinical diagnosis of attachment to food, clothing, and dwelling, with a specific remedy assigned to each.

Chinese Source

於飲食中，有美食貪及多食貪，能障修善。為欲斷除美食貪故，常期乞食、次第乞食。為欲斷除多食貪故，但一座食、先止後食。於衣服中，有三種貪，能障修善。一、多衣貪，二、軟觸貪，三、上妙貪。為欲斷除多衣貪故，但持三衣。為欲斷除於諸衣服軟觸貪故，但持毳衣。為欲斷除於諸衣服上妙貪故，持糞掃衣。於諸敷具有四種貪，能障修善。一、諠雜貪，二、屋宇貪，三、倚樂臥樂貪，四、敷具貪。為欲斷除諠雜貪故，住阿練若。為欲斷除屋宇貪故，常居樹下、迥露、塚間。又為斷除婬泆貪故，常住塚間。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding food, there is craving for delicious food and craving for eating too much, both obstructing wholesome cultivation. To cut off craving for delicious food, one practices regular and sequential begging. To cut off craving for eating too much, one eats only one sitting's worth and only before a fixed time. Regarding clothing, there are three cravings obstructing wholesome cultivation: craving for many robes, craving for soft texture, and craving for fine quality. To cut off craving for many robes, one keeps only three robes. To cut off craving for soft texture, one wears only coarse wool. To cut off craving for fine quality, one wears only discarded rags. Regarding bedding, there are four cravings obstructing wholesome cultivation: craving for lively company, craving for a proper dwelling, craving for the comfort of reclining and lying down, and craving for fine bedding itself. To cut off craving for lively company, one dwells in the forest. To cut off craving for a proper dwelling, one dwells constantly beneath a tree, in the open, or near a charnel ground. And to cut off craving rooted in lust specifically, one dwells constantly near a charnel ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this mapping as the treatise's own diagnostic key, showing that no ascetic practice is arbitrary — each addresses one specific, nameable craving. Two food-cravings pair with the four food practices: wanting delicious food specifically is cut by both forms of begging, which remove any choice in what's received; wanting to eat past genuine need is cut by eating only once and only within a set portion, removing any room for excess. Three clothing-cravings pair with the three robe practices: wanting many garments is cut by the strict three-robe limit; wanting soft fabric against the skin is cut by wearing only coarse wool; wanting fine, attractive material is cut by wearing salvaged rags. And bedding cravings begin their own fourfold breakdown: wanting lively company is cut by forest-dwelling's genuine solitude, and wanting a proper, secure building is cut by dwelling beneath a tree, in the open, or near a charnel ground instead — with charnel-ground dwelling specifically also serving as an antidote to lust, since nothing kills desire quite as directly as constant proximity to death's reality.
