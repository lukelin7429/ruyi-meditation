The Whole of the Holy Life

Fascicle 25 closes out the fourteen provisions for the path with the six that remain: good companionship, hearing the true teaching, reflecting on the true teaching, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the ornaments of a genuine renunciant — bringing the entire "provisions for detachment" section of the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation to completion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 25 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Good Companionship Opens: Eight Marks of a True Spiritual Friend

Fascicle 25 opens directly onto the ninth of the fourteen path-provisions, good companionship — naming eight qualities that together define a genuinely qualified spiritual friend, the kind of teacher and companion a practitioner should actually seek out.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之四……云何名善友性？謂八因緣故，應知一切種圓滿善友性。何等為八？謂如有一，安住禁戒、具足多聞、能有所證、性多哀愍、心無厭倦、善能堪忍、無有怖畏、語具圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, the fourth part of the third chapter … What is meant by the nature of a good friend? By eight causal factors, one should understand a good friend's nature as complete in every respect. What are the eight? It is like one who abides in restraint, is possessed of extensive learning, has genuine attainment, is deeply compassionate by nature, is tireless in mind, is well able to endure, is free of fear, and is complete in speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating fascicle 25 precisely: the last of four installments covering the "provisions for detachment" within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation — fascicle 22 covered detachment and moral discipline, 23 covered restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating, 24 covered wakeful practice and clear awareness in conduct, and this fascicle closes out the remaining six provisions, beginning with good companionship.

He grounds the topic's importance in a well-known exchange from the Āgamas: Ānanda once suggested to the Buddha that good spiritual friendship is "half of the holy life." The Buddha corrected him firmly — it is not half, but the entirety of the holy life, since a practitioner in genuine spiritual company can be counted on to progress diligently, to complete the roots of faith and the other wholesome qualities, and ultimately to escape birth, old age, sickness, and death. The Buddha adds that his own path to buddhahood depended on good companionship — without the guidance he himself received, he says, he would never have been the Dīpaṃkara Buddha's prophesied heir. Cháng Bǎi also cites a companion teaching, in which "good friend" is defined by an absence of comparison-driven pride: a genuine spiritual friend never thinks "I come from a nobler family than this other monk," or "I keep the precepts better," or "my concentration is deeper," or "I am wiser," or "I receive more support" — genuine equality of regard, whatever the actual differences in attainment, is itself part of what makes someone a good friend rather than merely an accomplished one.

The eight qualities named here — moral discipline, broad learning, genuine attainment, deep compassion, tireless energy, forbearance, fearlessness, and skillful speech — will each be unpacked individually in what follows. Cháng Bǎi notes parallel lists exist elsewhere in the treatise (four marks in the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's chapter on arousing the aspiration, eight in its chapter on serving with offerings), all converging on the same basic picture: a good spiritual friend is not simply someone knowledgeable, but someone whose entire character supports another person's genuine progress on the path.

2. The First Mark: Abiding in Restraint

Chinese Source

云何名為安住禁戒？謂安住具戒，善能守護別解律儀，如前廣說樂沙門性、樂婆羅門性，為自調伏、為自寂靜、為自涅槃，修行正行。如是名為安住禁戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by abiding in restraint? It means abiding fully in discipline, skillfully guarding the vows of individual liberation, as explained at length before — delighting in the nature of a true renunciant, delighting in the nature of one who has driven out unwholesome states, practicing correctly for the sake of taming oneself, for the sake of one's own peace, and for the sake of one's own nirvana. This is called abiding in restraint.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this first quality as a direct recap of moral discipline's own six-part structure, given in full in fascicle 22: fully abiding in the precepts received, skillfully guarding the vows of individual liberation across all seven categories of ordained and lay practitioners, complete deportment that never oversteps worldly or monastic convention, complete conduct that avoids the specific households and situations already named as unsuitable, genuine dread of even minor faults, and continual willingness to keep learning the training rules — undertaken for five underlying reasons: delighting in the noble eightfold path itself, delighting in having driven out unwholesome states, and taming, calming, and ultimately extinguishing one's own afflictions entirely. A good spiritual friend, in other words, must first and foremost actually live the discipline they would guide someone else toward — the remaining seven qualities build on this foundation rather than substituting for it.

3. Milestone: The Second Mark — Extensive Learning and Its Nine Qualities

The treatise's second mark of a genuine spiritual friend is extensive learning — not mere accumulation of facts, but a specific, ninefold quality of teaching mastered through hearing, reflection, and direct cultivation.

Chinese Source

云何名為具足多聞？謂若有法宣說開示，初中後善，文義巧妙，獨一、圓滿、清白、梵行。於如是類眾多妙法，能善受持，言善通利、意善尋思、見善通達。如是名為具足多聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by extensive learning? It means that, regarding a teaching proclaimed and explained — good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, its meaning subtle and its wording skillful, unique, complete, pure, and holy — one is able to receive and retain many such excellent teachings well: fluent in their language, skillfully investigating their meaning, and thoroughly penetrating what is seen. This is called extensive learning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the nine qualities named here as the treatise's own standard description of the Dharma itself. Good in the beginning means a teaching that brings genuine joy on first hearing; good in the middle means it can actually be practiced without undue hardship, following a middle way between indulgence and self-denial; good in the end means it leads all the way to the complete removal of defilement — arhatship for a hearer-disciple, buddhahood for a bodhisattva. Its meaning is subtle because it genuinely benefits and brings ease; its wording is skillful because it is well-composed and complete; it is unique because it shares nothing with non-Buddhist teachings; it is complete because it is boundless, pointing toward what ultimately exceeds language altogether; and it is pure and undefiled because the liberation it describes is free of affliction, both in itself and in its lasting continuity — all of it summed up as the noble eightfold path, called here "holy conduct."

Beyond simply being exposed to teaching of this quality, a genuine spiritual friend must actually master it through all three stages of wisdom: fluency in the language itself (built through hearing, called the wisdom of hearing), skillful investigation of its meaning through sustained reflection (the wisdom of reflection, grasping how a teaching's parts connect into a coherent whole), and direct, unmediated insight into the nature of things themselves (the wisdom of cultivation). Cháng Bǎi stresses that only someone who has genuinely completed all three — not merely memorized or intellectually understood a teaching — is qualified to guide someone else through it; anyone short of that level is still, in this respect, a student rather than a teacher, however much accumulated knowledge they may have.

4. Milestone: The Third Mark — Genuine Attainment's Twenty Perceptions

The treatise's third mark, genuine attainment, opens with twenty specific perceptions a qualified spiritual friend must have actually realized — not merely understood conceptually — spanning impermanence, suffering, non-self, and the direct contemplation of death and decay.

Chinese Source

云何名為能有所證？謂能證得勝無常想、無常苦想、苦無我想、厭逆食想、一切世間不可樂想、有過患想、斷想、離想、滅想、死想、不淨想、青瘀想、膿爛想、破壞想、膨脹想、噉食想、血塗想、離散想、骨鎖想、觀察空想。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by genuine attainment? It means being able to realize: the perception of surpassing impermanence, the perception of impermanence as suffering, the perception of suffering as non-self, the perception of revulsion toward food, the perception of the entire world as unlovable, the perception of its inherent flaws, the perception of cutting-off, the perception of detachment, the perception of cessation, the perception of death, the perception of impurity, and the perceptions of a corpse turning livid, festering, decaying, bloating, being devoured, smeared with blood, scattered apart, and reduced to bare bone — and, finally, the perception of examined emptiness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi groups these twenty into a clear progression. The first three — surpassing impermanence, impermanence as suffering, suffering as non-self — directly counter the three fundamental distortions of ordinary perception: mistaking what is impermanent for permanent, what is suffering for pleasurable, and what is without self for a genuine self, and together open onto all three marks of existence, including the unstated fourth, nirvana's peace. Revulsion toward food and the perception of the world as universally unlovable extend the same clarity to eating and to worldly experience generally, recognizing suffering's presence even in ordinary pleasure. The perception of flaws applies specifically to meditative attainment, recognizing that any lower absorption, however pleasant, still falls short of what lies beyond it. Cutting-off, detachment, and cessation name the three stages of liberation itself — the path of seeing that removes conceptually-based afflictions, the path of cultivation that removes the deeper, innate afflictions remaining after that, and the state of nirvana this progression aims at, whether the qualified "nirvana with residue" of an arhat still living, or the final "nirvana without residue" at life's end. Death-perception is simply sober awareness that death could come at any moment, life's remaining span never actually guaranteed. And the impurity contemplation — direct meditation on a corpse through its stages of livid discoloration, festering, decomposition, bloating, being devoured by animals, being smeared with blood, falling apart, and reduced finally to bare bone — is the same nine-stage practice already introduced in fascicle 24 as an antidote to desire, now named as a qualification a spiritual friend must have genuinely realized, not merely read about. The twentieth, examined emptiness, completes the sequence: direct seeing that no permanent, unified, controlling self can be found anywhere among the five aggregates — no one born, no one aging, no one dying, no one acting, and no one receiving the results of action, only causally arising processes empty of any such core.

5. Absorptions, Immeasurables, Fruits, Powers — Genuine Attainment Continues

Chinese Source

復能證得最初靜慮、第二靜慮、第三靜慮、第四靜慮、空無邊處、識無邊處、無所有處、最後非想非非想處。又能證得慈悲喜捨，或預流果、或一來果、或不還果、或神境通、或宿住通、或天耳通、或死生通、或心差別通、或阿羅漢。具八解脫靜慮等定，有大堪能，具大勢力，能善為他現三神變教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, one can realize the first, second, third, and fourth absorptions, the sphere of infinite space, the sphere of infinite consciousness, the sphere of nothingness, and finally the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. One can also realize loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; or the fruit of stream-entry, of once-returning, or of non-returning; or the supernormal power of miraculous ability, of recollecting past lives, of the divine ear, of seeing beings' death and rebirth, or of knowing others' minds; or arhatship itself. Possessing the eight liberations and such meditative attainments, one has great capability and great strength, and is able to skillfully instruct and guide others by manifesting three kinds of miraculous display.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a broad sweep across attainments the treatise has already detailed at length elsewhere: the four form-realm absorptions and four formless attainments (already described in the Ground of Meditative Equipoise); the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity (already unpacked in fascicle 24's antidote to ill-will); the four fruits of the path from stream-entry through arhatship; and five of the six supernormal powers — miraculous ability, recollection of past lives, the divine ear, seeing beings' death and rebirth according to their karma, and knowing others' minds (the sixth, the ending of defilements, is arhatship itself, already named). He highlights the eight liberations specifically as reserved for those with genuine meditative attainment beyond the third fruit, and closes with the three miraculous displays that make a spiritual friend's attainment actually useful to others: the display of miraculous power, which inspires genuine respect and thereby genuine attentiveness in a student; the display of mind-reading, which lets a teacher accurately diagnose a student's specific mental state; and the display of instruction, which lets a teacher give exactly the guidance a specific student's capacity and temperament actually call for. Together, Cháng Bǎi notes, these three displays are what makes "genuine attainment" more than a private accomplishment — they are what allows a spiritual friend's own realization to actually reach and benefit someone else.

6. Milestone: The Fourth Mark — Deep Compassion, Mapped to the Four Immeasurables

The treatise's fourth mark, deep compassion, unpacks into five specific things a genuine spiritual friend delights in giving — each traced to one of the four immeasurable states already introduced in earlier fascicles.

Chinese Source

云何名為為性哀愍？謂於他所常起悲憐，樂與其義、樂與其利、樂與其樂、樂與猗觸、樂與安隱。如是名為為性哀愍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by deep compassion by nature? It means constantly feeling compassion for others — delighting in giving them benefit, delighting in giving them advantage, delighting in giving them ease, delighting in giving them joyful contact, and delighting in giving them peace. This is called deep compassion by nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps each of the five delights to one of the four immeasurable states. Giving benefit — freeing someone from suffering — and giving advantage — guiding them toward wholesome conduct that brings lasting good — both flow from compassion (悲), the immeasurable specifically aimed at removing others' pain. Giving ease — conduct free of any wish to harm — flows from loving-kindness (慈), the immeasurable aimed at bringing others happiness. Giving joyful contact — genuine delight at another's success or virtue, however small — flows from sympathetic joy (喜). And giving peace — freeing someone from the agitation of craving and aversion — flows from equanimity (捨). Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own Bodhisattva-bhūmi simply defines "compassion" as the complete cultivation of all four immeasurables together: someone who has genuinely perfected loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity toward all beings is, by that very fact, what this passage means by "deeply compassionate by nature" — not sentimentality, but the settled fruit of real meditative cultivation.

7. The Fifth Mark: A Tireless Mind for Teaching

Chinese Source

云何名為心無厭倦？謂善能示現、善能教導、善能讚勵、善能慶慰，處於四眾宣說正法，不辭勞倦，翹勤無惰，起發圓滿，為性好樂，發勤精進。如是名為心無厭倦。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by a tireless mind? It means being skilled at demonstrating, skilled at instructing, skilled at encouraging, and skilled at rejoicing with others — teaching the true Dharma before the fourfold assembly without begrudging the effort, diligent and never lazy, arousing complete energy, delighting in it by nature, and generating sustained diligence. This is called a tireless mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the four verbs as four distinct teaching skills. Demonstrating means revealing the four principles of reasoning already introduced earlier in the treatise — dependence, function, proof, and natural law — so a student actually grasps how cause and effect, purity and defilement, genuinely work. Instructing means, once a student has developed real confidence in the teaching, formally establishing them in the training itself, so understanding turns into actual practice. Encouraging means recognizing when a student's resolve is flagging — discouraged by their own slow progress in knowledge, conduct, or attainment — and specifically bolstering their confidence so they can keep going. And rejoicing means genuinely celebrating a student's real progress when it happens, letting them know clearly that what they did was right. Cháng Bǎi notes the first two skills concern teaching the Dharma itself, while the latter two concern tending a student's morale — both equally necessary, and both requiring a teacher willing to keep showing up for the fourfold assembly of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen without fatigue ever becoming an excuse to stop.

8. Milestone: The Sixth Mark — Forbearance, Toward Both Hostility and Hardship

The treatise's sixth mark, forbearance, covers two distinct kinds of endurance: absorbing other people's hostility without retaliation, and enduring genuine physical hardship without complaint.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能堪忍？謂罵不報罵、瞋不報瞋、打不報打、弄不報弄，堪耐椎杵。……自推己過，以業異熟為所依趣，終不於他發生憤恚，亦不懷恨隨眠不捨。……又能堪忍寒熱、饑渴、蚊蝱、風日、蛇蝎惡觸，他所干犯、磣毒語言，身內所生猛利、堅勁、辛楚、切心、奪命苦受，為性堪忍，能有容納。如是名為善能堪忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by genuine forbearance? It means not repaying abuse with abuse, not repaying anger with anger, not repaying a blow with a blow, not repaying mockery with mockery, enduring even a beating. … Attributing the fault to oneself, taking it as the ripening of one's own past actions, one never gives rise to resentment toward another, nor harbors a lasting grudge. … One is also able to endure cold and heat, hunger and thirst, mosquitoes and gnats, wind and sun, the bite of snakes and scorpions, others' unjust intrusions, cruel and poisonous speech, and even severe, sharp, agonizing, life-threatening pain arising within the body itself — enduring all of it by nature, with genuine capacity to bear it. This is called genuine forbearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first form of forbearance — absorbing hostility — as resting on a specific reflection: whatever mistreatment comes, a spiritual friend attributes it to the ripening of their own past actions rather than to the other person's malice, treating the offender merely as the occasion through which an old karmic debt comes due. This reframing, he notes, is what actually makes "difficult to endure, yet endured" possible — not gritted-teeth suppression, but a genuine shift in how the harm is understood. He cites the Ratnakūṭa Sūtra's own extended list of specific reflections a bodhisattva uses to sustain this forbearance — that harsh words are as insubstantial as an echo, that the body struck is as insubstantial as a reflection, that the anger directed at oneself is itself illusory, that praise and blame alike leave one's true qualities unchanged, that worldly gain and loss cannot disturb a mind already settled, and more in the same vein — each pointing toward the same underlying clarity about the emptiness of both the harm and the one supposedly harmed.

The second form of forbearance — enduring genuine hardship — extends the same steadiness to physical circumstance: extreme weather, insect bites, hunger, hostile speech, and even severe bodily pain, all met without the mind buckling under them. Cháng Bǎi cites the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own reasoning for why this is possible: a practitioner who reflects honestly on how much suffering was once endured pointlessly, in pursuit of nothing more than fleeting sensual pleasure, realizes that enduring far less suffering now, in pursuit of the highest good, is a trade obviously worth making. Both forms of forbearance together, Cháng Bǎi notes, come not from raw willpower but from this kind of correct reflection — seeing clearly enough that endurance stops requiring effort at all.

9. The Seventh Mark: Fearlessness Before an Audience

Chinese Source

云何名為無有怖畏？謂處大眾說正法時，心無怯劣、聲無戰掉、辯無誤失，終不由彼怯懼因緣，為諸怖畏之所逼切，腋不流汗、身毛不豎。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by fearlessness? It means that, when teaching the true Dharma before a large assembly, the mind is not timid or weak, the voice does not tremble, and one's reasoning is without error — never oppressed by fear born of timidity, the armpits do not sweat, and the body's hair does not stand on end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this mark as the natural fruit of the marks already covered rather than a separate achievement: genuine command of the teaching (extensive learning), real inner realization (genuine attainment), and a mind purified of self-clinging together produce a composure that doesn't have to be performed — no trembling voice, no sweating, no visible signs of the nervous system's fight-or-flight response, because there's fundamentally nothing at stake for a self that isn't being clung to. A spiritual friend teaching from this place, he notes, isn't fearless through bravado, but simply has nothing left to protect.

10. Milestone: The Eighth Mark — Complete Speech, and All Eight Marks Close

The treatise closes the eight marks of a genuine spiritual friend with complete speech — eight further qualities describing exactly how such a person actually communicates — bringing the full eightfold definition of good companionship to its close.

Chinese Source

云何名為語具圓滿？謂彼成就最上首語、極美妙語、甚顯了語、易悟解語、樂欲聞語、無違逆語、無所依語、無邊際語。如是名為語具圓滿，言詞巧妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by complete speech? It means being accomplished in speech that leads toward nirvana above all else, speech of the utmost beauty, speech that is thoroughly clear, speech that is easily understood, speech people delight in hearing, speech free of anything objectionable, speech free of any hidden agenda, and speech of boundless scope. This is called complete speech, skillful in every word.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the eight: speech that leads toward nirvana above all else always keeps liberation as its ultimate destination, never mere entertainment or cleverness; speech of utmost beauty has a genuinely pleasing quality, likened to the sweet-voiced kalaviṅka bird; thoroughly clear speech chooses its words and phrasing with real precision; easily understood speech explains difficult things simply, moving skillfully from the accessible to the profound; speech people delight in hearing draws out genuine meaning that keeps listeners actually engaged; speech free of anything objectionable is offered without any personal wish to be believed or admired, purely for the listener's benefit; speech free of hidden agenda is measured correctly to the occasion — neither too much nor too little, since even good teaching, given at excessive length, can leave a listener overwhelmed rather than nourished; and speech of boundless scope draws on genuinely wide learning to open a teaching's full implications rather than a narrow slice of it. Cháng Bǎi illustrates the risk of over-measure with a familiar story: a preacher, delighted that even a single farmer showed up on an otherwise empty Sunday, poured out every teaching he knew at once — only for the farmer to gently point out afterward that even one chicken can't eat a feast meant for many. With this eighth mark named, all eight qualities of a genuine spiritual friend — moral discipline, extensive learning, genuine attainment, deep compassion, a tireless mind, forbearance, fearlessness, and complete speech — are now fully unpacked.

11. Milestone: Five Things a Qualified Spiritual Friend Can Do — Beginning With Skillful Admonishment

Having established all eight qualifying marks, the treatise names five specific capabilities a genuine spiritual friend brings to actually guiding another person, opening with the delicate art of pointing out someone else's fault well.

Chinese Source

成就如是八種因緣，善能諫舉、善作憶念、善能教授、善能教誡、善說正法。云何名為善能諫舉？謂若有餘，於增上戒毀犯尸羅，於增上軌毀犯軌則，由見聞疑能正諫舉，真實不以虛妄，應時不以非時，饒益不以衰損，柔軟不以麤獷，善友不以憎嫉。如是名為善能諫舉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having accomplished these eight causal factors, one is skilled at admonishing, skilled at reminding, skilled at instructing, skilled at guiding, and skilled at teaching the true Dharma. What is meant by skillful admonishment? It means that, when another has violated the precepts or broken the code of conduct, having seen it, heard of it, or come to suspect it, one admonishes them correctly: truthfully, not falsely; at the proper time, not an improper time; for their genuine benefit, not their harm; gently, not harshly; as a true friend, not out of resentment or envy. This is called skillful admonishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five capabilities as what the eight qualifying marks actually earn a practitioner the standing to do. Skillful admonishment specifically governs how a fault should be pointed out, whether personally witnessed, reported, or merely suspected — grounded in five deliberate choices: confirmed truth rather than rumor or assumption, private and well-timed delivery rather than public embarrassment or a poorly chosen moment, a genuine wish to help rather than to diminish, gentle rather than harsh language, and motivation rooted in real friendship rather than any trace of resentment or envy. The treatise elsewhere names the exact opposite of each as the mark of a botched, harmful confrontation: speaking at the wrong time, speaking falsely, speaking without any real benefit in view, speaking harshly, and speaking with anger already present — five ways an admonishment can do more damage than the original fault it addresses. Cháng Bǎi's point is that even correcting someone else is itself a skill requiring real discernment, not simply a license to criticize freely once a fault is noticed.

12. Skillful Reminding: Recalling a Forgotten Fault

Chinese Source

云何名為善作憶念？謂令憶念先所犯罪，或法、或義。云何名為令其憶念先所犯罪？謂若有餘，先起毀犯而不能憶，善作方便，令彼憶念。告言：長老！曾於某處、某事、某時，毀犯如是如是色類。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful reminding? It means causing someone to recall a fault previously committed, or a teaching, or its meaning. What is meant by causing someone to recall a fault previously committed? It means that, when another has committed a violation but cannot recall it, one skillfully finds a means to help them remember, saying: Venerable one, at such a place, on such an occasion, at such a time, you committed a fault of such and such a kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes skillful reminding from skillful admonishment by its specific purpose: not pointing out a fault someone is currently unaware of, but helping someone recall a fault they've genuinely forgotten — supplying the concrete details (the place, the occasion, the time, the specific nature of what was done) needed to jog a lapsed memory, addressed respectfully rather than accusingly. This same skill, he notes, extends to reminding someone of a teaching or its meaning they've simply let slip from memory — a second, equally necessary application of the same basic capacity: helping someone recover what they already once knew, rather than teaching them something wholly new.
