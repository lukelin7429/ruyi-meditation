The Whole of the Holy Life

Fascicle 25 closes out the fourteen provisions for the path with the six that remain: good companionship, hearing the true teaching, reflecting on the true teaching, freedom from obstruction, generosity, and the ornaments of a genuine renunciant — bringing the entire "provisions for detachment" section of the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation to completion.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 25 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Good Companionship Opens: Eight Marks of a True Spiritual Friend

Fascicle 25 opens directly onto the ninth of the fourteen path-provisions, good companionship — naming eight qualities that together define a genuinely qualified spiritual friend, the kind of teacher and companion a practitioner should actually seek out.

Chinese Source

本地分中聲聞地初瑜伽處出離地品第三之四……云何名善友性？謂八因緣故，應知一切種圓滿善友性。何等為八？謂如有一，安住禁戒、具足多聞、能有所證、性多哀愍、心無厭倦、善能堪忍、無有怖畏、語具圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, in the Ground of the Hearer-Disciples, the first yogic process, the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation, the fourth part of the third chapter … What is meant by the nature of a good friend? By eight causal factors, one should understand a good friend's nature as complete in every respect. What are the eight? It is like one who abides in restraint, is possessed of extensive learning, has genuine attainment, is deeply compassionate by nature, is tireless in mind, is well able to endure, is free of fear, and is complete in speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by locating fascicle 25 precisely: the last of four installments covering the "provisions for detachment" within the Ground of Setting Out Toward Liberation — fascicle 22 covered detachment and moral discipline, 23 covered restraint of the faculties and moderation in eating, 24 covered wakeful practice and clear awareness in conduct, and this fascicle closes out the remaining six provisions, beginning with good companionship.

He grounds the topic's importance in a well-known exchange from the Āgamas: Ānanda once suggested to the Buddha that good spiritual friendship is "half of the holy life." The Buddha corrected him firmly — it is not half, but the entirety of the holy life, since a practitioner in genuine spiritual company can be counted on to progress diligently, to complete the roots of faith and the other wholesome qualities, and ultimately to escape birth, old age, sickness, and death. The Buddha adds that his own path to buddhahood depended on good companionship — without the guidance he himself received, he says, he would never have been the Dīpaṃkara Buddha's prophesied heir. Cháng Bǎi also cites a companion teaching, in which "good friend" is defined by an absence of comparison-driven pride: a genuine spiritual friend never thinks "I come from a nobler family than this other monk," or "I keep the precepts better," or "my concentration is deeper," or "I am wiser," or "I receive more support" — genuine equality of regard, whatever the actual differences in attainment, is itself part of what makes someone a good friend rather than merely an accomplished one.

The eight qualities named here — moral discipline, broad learning, genuine attainment, deep compassion, tireless energy, forbearance, fearlessness, and skillful speech — will each be unpacked individually in what follows. Cháng Bǎi notes parallel lists exist elsewhere in the treatise (four marks in the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's chapter on arousing the aspiration, eight in its chapter on serving with offerings), all converging on the same basic picture: a good spiritual friend is not simply someone knowledgeable, but someone whose entire character supports another person's genuine progress on the path.

2. The First Mark: Abiding in Restraint

Chinese Source

云何名為安住禁戒？謂安住具戒，善能守護別解律儀，如前廣說樂沙門性、樂婆羅門性，為自調伏、為自寂靜、為自涅槃，修行正行。如是名為安住禁戒。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by abiding in restraint? It means abiding fully in discipline, skillfully guarding the vows of individual liberation, as explained at length before — delighting in the nature of a true renunciant, delighting in the nature of one who has driven out unwholesome states, practicing correctly for the sake of taming oneself, for the sake of one's own peace, and for the sake of one's own nirvana. This is called abiding in restraint.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this first quality as a direct recap of moral discipline's own six-part structure, given in full in fascicle 22: fully abiding in the precepts received, skillfully guarding the vows of individual liberation across all seven categories of ordained and lay practitioners, complete deportment that never oversteps worldly or monastic convention, complete conduct that avoids the specific households and situations already named as unsuitable, genuine dread of even minor faults, and continual willingness to keep learning the training rules — undertaken for five underlying reasons: delighting in the noble eightfold path itself, delighting in having driven out unwholesome states, and taming, calming, and ultimately extinguishing one's own afflictions entirely. A good spiritual friend, in other words, must first and foremost actually live the discipline they would guide someone else toward — the remaining seven qualities build on this foundation rather than substituting for it.

3. Milestone: The Second Mark — Extensive Learning and Its Nine Qualities

The treatise's second mark of a genuine spiritual friend is extensive learning — not mere accumulation of facts, but a specific, ninefold quality of teaching mastered through hearing, reflection, and direct cultivation.

Chinese Source

云何名為具足多聞？謂若有法宣說開示，初中後善，文義巧妙，獨一、圓滿、清白、梵行。於如是類眾多妙法，能善受持，言善通利、意善尋思、見善通達。如是名為具足多聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by extensive learning? It means that, regarding a teaching proclaimed and explained — good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, its meaning subtle and its wording skillful, unique, complete, pure, and holy — one is able to receive and retain many such excellent teachings well: fluent in their language, skillfully investigating their meaning, and thoroughly penetrating what is seen. This is called extensive learning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the nine qualities named here as the treatise's own standard description of the Dharma itself. Good in the beginning means a teaching that brings genuine joy on first hearing; good in the middle means it can actually be practiced without undue hardship, following a middle way between indulgence and self-denial; good in the end means it leads all the way to the complete removal of defilement — arhatship for a hearer-disciple, buddhahood for a bodhisattva. Its meaning is subtle because it genuinely benefits and brings ease; its wording is skillful because it is well-composed and complete; it is unique because it shares nothing with non-Buddhist teachings; it is complete because it is boundless, pointing toward what ultimately exceeds language altogether; and it is pure and undefiled because the liberation it describes is free of affliction, both in itself and in its lasting continuity — all of it summed up as the noble eightfold path, called here "holy conduct."

Beyond simply being exposed to teaching of this quality, a genuine spiritual friend must actually master it through all three stages of wisdom: fluency in the language itself (built through hearing, called the wisdom of hearing), skillful investigation of its meaning through sustained reflection (the wisdom of reflection, grasping how a teaching's parts connect into a coherent whole), and direct, unmediated insight into the nature of things themselves (the wisdom of cultivation). Cháng Bǎi stresses that only someone who has genuinely completed all three — not merely memorized or intellectually understood a teaching — is qualified to guide someone else through it; anyone short of that level is still, in this respect, a student rather than a teacher, however much accumulated knowledge they may have.

4. Milestone: The Third Mark — Genuine Attainment's Twenty Perceptions

The treatise's third mark, genuine attainment, opens with twenty specific perceptions a qualified spiritual friend must have actually realized — not merely understood conceptually — spanning impermanence, suffering, non-self, and the direct contemplation of death and decay.

Chinese Source

云何名為能有所證？謂能證得勝無常想、無常苦想、苦無我想、厭逆食想、一切世間不可樂想、有過患想、斷想、離想、滅想、死想、不淨想、青瘀想、膿爛想、破壞想、膨脹想、噉食想、血塗想、離散想、骨鎖想、觀察空想。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by genuine attainment? It means being able to realize: the perception of surpassing impermanence, the perception of impermanence as suffering, the perception of suffering as non-self, the perception of revulsion toward food, the perception of the entire world as unlovable, the perception of its inherent flaws, the perception of cutting-off, the perception of detachment, the perception of cessation, the perception of death, the perception of impurity, and the perceptions of a corpse turning livid, festering, decaying, bloating, being devoured, smeared with blood, scattered apart, and reduced to bare bone — and, finally, the perception of examined emptiness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi groups these twenty into a clear progression. The first three — surpassing impermanence, impermanence as suffering, suffering as non-self — directly counter the three fundamental distortions of ordinary perception: mistaking what is impermanent for permanent, what is suffering for pleasurable, and what is without self for a genuine self, and together open onto all three marks of existence, including the unstated fourth, nirvana's peace. Revulsion toward food and the perception of the world as universally unlovable extend the same clarity to eating and to worldly experience generally, recognizing suffering's presence even in ordinary pleasure. The perception of flaws applies specifically to meditative attainment, recognizing that any lower absorption, however pleasant, still falls short of what lies beyond it. Cutting-off, detachment, and cessation name the three stages of liberation itself — the path of seeing that removes conceptually-based afflictions, the path of cultivation that removes the deeper, innate afflictions remaining after that, and the state of nirvana this progression aims at, whether the qualified "nirvana with residue" of an arhat still living, or the final "nirvana without residue" at life's end. Death-perception is simply sober awareness that death could come at any moment, life's remaining span never actually guaranteed. And the impurity contemplation — direct meditation on a corpse through its stages of livid discoloration, festering, decomposition, bloating, being devoured by animals, being smeared with blood, falling apart, and reduced finally to bare bone — is the same nine-stage practice already introduced in fascicle 24 as an antidote to desire, now named as a qualification a spiritual friend must have genuinely realized, not merely read about. The twentieth, examined emptiness, completes the sequence: direct seeing that no permanent, unified, controlling self can be found anywhere among the five aggregates — no one born, no one aging, no one dying, no one acting, and no one receiving the results of action, only causally arising processes empty of any such core.

5. Absorptions, Immeasurables, Fruits, Powers — Genuine Attainment Continues

Chinese Source

復能證得最初靜慮、第二靜慮、第三靜慮、第四靜慮、空無邊處、識無邊處、無所有處、最後非想非非想處。又能證得慈悲喜捨，或預流果、或一來果、或不還果、或神境通、或宿住通、或天耳通、或死生通、或心差別通、或阿羅漢。具八解脫靜慮等定，有大堪能，具大勢力，能善為他現三神變教授教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, one can realize the first, second, third, and fourth absorptions, the sphere of infinite space, the sphere of infinite consciousness, the sphere of nothingness, and finally the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. One can also realize loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity; or the fruit of stream-entry, of once-returning, or of non-returning; or the supernormal power of miraculous ability, of recollecting past lives, of the divine ear, of seeing beings' death and rebirth, or of knowing others' minds; or arhatship itself. Possessing the eight liberations and such meditative attainments, one has great capability and great strength, and is able to skillfully instruct and guide others by manifesting three kinds of miraculous display.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this as a broad sweep across attainments the treatise has already detailed at length elsewhere: the four form-realm absorptions and four formless attainments (already described in the Ground of Meditative Equipoise); the four immeasurables of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity (already unpacked in fascicle 24's antidote to ill-will); the four fruits of the path from stream-entry through arhatship; and five of the six supernormal powers — miraculous ability, recollection of past lives, the divine ear, seeing beings' death and rebirth according to their karma, and knowing others' minds (the sixth, the ending of defilements, is arhatship itself, already named). He highlights the eight liberations specifically as reserved for those with genuine meditative attainment beyond the third fruit, and closes with the three miraculous displays that make a spiritual friend's attainment actually useful to others: the display of miraculous power, which inspires genuine respect and thereby genuine attentiveness in a student; the display of mind-reading, which lets a teacher accurately diagnose a student's specific mental state; and the display of instruction, which lets a teacher give exactly the guidance a specific student's capacity and temperament actually call for. Together, Cháng Bǎi notes, these three displays are what makes "genuine attainment" more than a private accomplishment — they are what allows a spiritual friend's own realization to actually reach and benefit someone else.

6. Milestone: The Fourth Mark — Deep Compassion, Mapped to the Four Immeasurables

The treatise's fourth mark, deep compassion, unpacks into five specific things a genuine spiritual friend delights in giving — each traced to one of the four immeasurable states already introduced in earlier fascicles.

Chinese Source

云何名為為性哀愍？謂於他所常起悲憐，樂與其義、樂與其利、樂與其樂、樂與猗觸、樂與安隱。如是名為為性哀愍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by deep compassion by nature? It means constantly feeling compassion for others — delighting in giving them benefit, delighting in giving them advantage, delighting in giving them ease, delighting in giving them joyful contact, and delighting in giving them peace. This is called deep compassion by nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps each of the five delights to one of the four immeasurable states. Giving benefit — freeing someone from suffering — and giving advantage — guiding them toward wholesome conduct that brings lasting good — both flow from compassion (悲), the immeasurable specifically aimed at removing others' pain. Giving ease — conduct free of any wish to harm — flows from loving-kindness (慈), the immeasurable aimed at bringing others happiness. Giving joyful contact — genuine delight at another's success or virtue, however small — flows from sympathetic joy (喜). And giving peace — freeing someone from the agitation of craving and aversion — flows from equanimity (捨). Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own Bodhisattva-bhūmi simply defines "compassion" as the complete cultivation of all four immeasurables together: someone who has genuinely perfected loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity toward all beings is, by that very fact, what this passage means by "deeply compassionate by nature" — not sentimentality, but the settled fruit of real meditative cultivation.

7. The Fifth Mark: A Tireless Mind for Teaching

Chinese Source

云何名為心無厭倦？謂善能示現、善能教導、善能讚勵、善能慶慰，處於四眾宣說正法，不辭勞倦，翹勤無惰，起發圓滿，為性好樂，發勤精進。如是名為心無厭倦。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by a tireless mind? It means being skilled at demonstrating, skilled at instructing, skilled at encouraging, and skilled at rejoicing with others — teaching the true Dharma before the fourfold assembly without begrudging the effort, diligent and never lazy, arousing complete energy, delighting in it by nature, and generating sustained diligence. This is called a tireless mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the four verbs as four distinct teaching skills. Demonstrating means revealing the four principles of reasoning already introduced earlier in the treatise — dependence, function, proof, and natural law — so a student actually grasps how cause and effect, purity and defilement, genuinely work. Instructing means, once a student has developed real confidence in the teaching, formally establishing them in the training itself, so understanding turns into actual practice. Encouraging means recognizing when a student's resolve is flagging — discouraged by their own slow progress in knowledge, conduct, or attainment — and specifically bolstering their confidence so they can keep going. And rejoicing means genuinely celebrating a student's real progress when it happens, letting them know clearly that what they did was right. Cháng Bǎi notes the first two skills concern teaching the Dharma itself, while the latter two concern tending a student's morale — both equally necessary, and both requiring a teacher willing to keep showing up for the fourfold assembly of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen without fatigue ever becoming an excuse to stop.

8. Milestone: The Sixth Mark — Forbearance, Toward Both Hostility and Hardship

The treatise's sixth mark, forbearance, covers two distinct kinds of endurance: absorbing other people's hostility without retaliation, and enduring genuine physical hardship without complaint.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能堪忍？謂罵不報罵、瞋不報瞋、打不報打、弄不報弄，堪耐椎杵。……自推己過，以業異熟為所依趣，終不於他發生憤恚，亦不懷恨隨眠不捨。……又能堪忍寒熱、饑渴、蚊蝱、風日、蛇蝎惡觸，他所干犯、磣毒語言，身內所生猛利、堅勁、辛楚、切心、奪命苦受，為性堪忍，能有容納。如是名為善能堪忍。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by genuine forbearance? It means not repaying abuse with abuse, not repaying anger with anger, not repaying a blow with a blow, not repaying mockery with mockery, enduring even a beating. … Attributing the fault to oneself, taking it as the ripening of one's own past actions, one never gives rise to resentment toward another, nor harbors a lasting grudge. … One is also able to endure cold and heat, hunger and thirst, mosquitoes and gnats, wind and sun, the bite of snakes and scorpions, others' unjust intrusions, cruel and poisonous speech, and even severe, sharp, agonizing, life-threatening pain arising within the body itself — enduring all of it by nature, with genuine capacity to bear it. This is called genuine forbearance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first form of forbearance — absorbing hostility — as resting on a specific reflection: whatever mistreatment comes, a spiritual friend attributes it to the ripening of their own past actions rather than to the other person's malice, treating the offender merely as the occasion through which an old karmic debt comes due. This reframing, he notes, is what actually makes "difficult to endure, yet endured" possible — not gritted-teeth suppression, but a genuine shift in how the harm is understood. He cites the Ratnakūṭa Sūtra's own extended list of specific reflections a bodhisattva uses to sustain this forbearance — that harsh words are as insubstantial as an echo, that the body struck is as insubstantial as a reflection, that the anger directed at oneself is itself illusory, that praise and blame alike leave one's true qualities unchanged, that worldly gain and loss cannot disturb a mind already settled, and more in the same vein — each pointing toward the same underlying clarity about the emptiness of both the harm and the one supposedly harmed.

The second form of forbearance — enduring genuine hardship — extends the same steadiness to physical circumstance: extreme weather, insect bites, hunger, hostile speech, and even severe bodily pain, all met without the mind buckling under them. Cháng Bǎi cites the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own reasoning for why this is possible: a practitioner who reflects honestly on how much suffering was once endured pointlessly, in pursuit of nothing more than fleeting sensual pleasure, realizes that enduring far less suffering now, in pursuit of the highest good, is a trade obviously worth making. Both forms of forbearance together, Cháng Bǎi notes, come not from raw willpower but from this kind of correct reflection — seeing clearly enough that endurance stops requiring effort at all.

9. The Seventh Mark: Fearlessness Before an Audience

Chinese Source

云何名為無有怖畏？謂處大眾說正法時，心無怯劣、聲無戰掉、辯無誤失，終不由彼怯懼因緣，為諸怖畏之所逼切，腋不流汗、身毛不豎。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by fearlessness? It means that, when teaching the true Dharma before a large assembly, the mind is not timid or weak, the voice does not tremble, and one's reasoning is without error — never oppressed by fear born of timidity, the armpits do not sweat, and the body's hair does not stand on end.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this mark as the natural fruit of the marks already covered rather than a separate achievement: genuine command of the teaching (extensive learning), real inner realization (genuine attainment), and a mind purified of self-clinging together produce a composure that doesn't have to be performed — no trembling voice, no sweating, no visible signs of the nervous system's fight-or-flight response, because there's fundamentally nothing at stake for a self that isn't being clung to. A spiritual friend teaching from this place, he notes, isn't fearless through bravado, but simply has nothing left to protect.

10. Milestone: The Eighth Mark — Complete Speech, and All Eight Marks Close

The treatise closes the eight marks of a genuine spiritual friend with complete speech — eight further qualities describing exactly how such a person actually communicates — bringing the full eightfold definition of good companionship to its close.

Chinese Source

云何名為語具圓滿？謂彼成就最上首語、極美妙語、甚顯了語、易悟解語、樂欲聞語、無違逆語、無所依語、無邊際語。如是名為語具圓滿，言詞巧妙。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by complete speech? It means being accomplished in speech that leads toward nirvana above all else, speech of the utmost beauty, speech that is thoroughly clear, speech that is easily understood, speech people delight in hearing, speech free of anything objectionable, speech free of any hidden agenda, and speech of boundless scope. This is called complete speech, skillful in every word.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the eight: speech that leads toward nirvana above all else always keeps liberation as its ultimate destination, never mere entertainment or cleverness; speech of utmost beauty has a genuinely pleasing quality, likened to the sweet-voiced kalaviṅka bird; thoroughly clear speech chooses its words and phrasing with real precision; easily understood speech explains difficult things simply, moving skillfully from the accessible to the profound; speech people delight in hearing draws out genuine meaning that keeps listeners actually engaged; speech free of anything objectionable is offered without any personal wish to be believed or admired, purely for the listener's benefit; speech free of hidden agenda is measured correctly to the occasion — neither too much nor too little, since even good teaching, given at excessive length, can leave a listener overwhelmed rather than nourished; and speech of boundless scope draws on genuinely wide learning to open a teaching's full implications rather than a narrow slice of it. Cháng Bǎi illustrates the risk of over-measure with a familiar story: a preacher, delighted that even a single farmer showed up on an otherwise empty Sunday, poured out every teaching he knew at once — only for the farmer to gently point out afterward that even one chicken can't eat a feast meant for many. With this eighth mark named, all eight qualities of a genuine spiritual friend — moral discipline, extensive learning, genuine attainment, deep compassion, a tireless mind, forbearance, fearlessness, and complete speech — are now fully unpacked.

11. Milestone: Five Things a Qualified Spiritual Friend Can Do — Beginning With Skillful Admonishment

Having established all eight qualifying marks, the treatise names five specific capabilities a genuine spiritual friend brings to actually guiding another person, opening with the delicate art of pointing out someone else's fault well.

Chinese Source

成就如是八種因緣，善能諫舉、善作憶念、善能教授、善能教誡、善說正法。云何名為善能諫舉？謂若有餘，於增上戒毀犯尸羅，於增上軌毀犯軌則，由見聞疑能正諫舉，真實不以虛妄，應時不以非時，饒益不以衰損，柔軟不以麤獷，善友不以憎嫉。如是名為善能諫舉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Having accomplished these eight causal factors, one is skilled at admonishing, skilled at reminding, skilled at instructing, skilled at guiding, and skilled at teaching the true Dharma. What is meant by skillful admonishment? It means that, when another has violated the precepts or broken the code of conduct, having seen it, heard of it, or come to suspect it, one admonishes them correctly: truthfully, not falsely; at the proper time, not an improper time; for their genuine benefit, not their harm; gently, not harshly; as a true friend, not out of resentment or envy. This is called skillful admonishment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five capabilities as what the eight qualifying marks actually earn a practitioner the standing to do. Skillful admonishment specifically governs how a fault should be pointed out, whether personally witnessed, reported, or merely suspected — grounded in five deliberate choices: confirmed truth rather than rumor or assumption, private and well-timed delivery rather than public embarrassment or a poorly chosen moment, a genuine wish to help rather than to diminish, gentle rather than harsh language, and motivation rooted in real friendship rather than any trace of resentment or envy. The treatise elsewhere names the exact opposite of each as the mark of a botched, harmful confrontation: speaking at the wrong time, speaking falsely, speaking without any real benefit in view, speaking harshly, and speaking with anger already present — five ways an admonishment can do more damage than the original fault it addresses. Cháng Bǎi's point is that even correcting someone else is itself a skill requiring real discernment, not simply a license to criticize freely once a fault is noticed.

12. Skillful Reminding: Recalling a Forgotten Fault

Chinese Source

云何名為善作憶念？謂令憶念先所犯罪，或法、或義。云何名為令其憶念先所犯罪？謂若有餘，先起毀犯而不能憶，善作方便，令彼憶念。告言：長老！曾於某處、某事、某時，毀犯如是如是色類。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful reminding? It means causing someone to recall a fault previously committed, or a teaching, or its meaning. What is meant by causing someone to recall a fault previously committed? It means that, when another has committed a violation but cannot recall it, one skillfully finds a means to help them remember, saying: Venerable one, at such a place, on such an occasion, at such a time, you committed a fault of such and such a kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes skillful reminding from skillful admonishment by its specific purpose: not pointing out a fault someone is currently unaware of, but helping someone recall a fault they've genuinely forgotten — supplying the concrete details (the place, the occasion, the time, the specific nature of what was done) needed to jog a lapsed memory, addressed respectfully rather than accusingly. This same skill, he notes, extends to reminding someone of a teaching or its meaning they've simply let slip from memory — a second, equally necessary application of the same basic capacity: helping someone recover what they already once knew, rather than teaching them something wholly new.

13. Skillful Reminding Completes: Forgotten Teachings and Forgotten Good Deeds

Chinese Source

云何名為令憶念法？謂若有餘，於先所聞、所受正法，獨處思念。……彼若不憶，令其憶念，或復稱述、授與令憶，或興請問、詰難令憶。……云何名為令憶念義？謂若有餘，於先所聞、所受正義，有所忘失，為作憶念，宣說開示，令新令顯。又若有善能引義利、能引梵行，久時所作、久時所說，彼若忘失，亦令憶念。如是名為善作憶念。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by causing someone to recall a teaching? It means that, when another, reflecting alone on a teaching previously heard and received, cannot recall it, one skillfully helps them remember — repeating it back, supplying it directly, or drawing it out through questions and probing inquiry. What is meant by causing someone to recall its meaning? It means that, when another has forgotten the meaning of a teaching previously heard and received, one helps them recall it, explaining and clarifying it so that it appears fresh and clear once more. And if there is some wholesome deed or word, capable of bringing real benefit and supporting pure conduct, done or spoken long ago, which they have forgotten, one likewise helps them recall it. This is called skillful reminding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi completes the full scope of skillful reminding across three objects: a forgotten fault (covered previously), a forgotten teaching or its meaning, and — perhaps the most quietly encouraging of the three — a forgotten good deed or wholesome word from one's own past, one genuinely capable of bringing real benefit if only it were remembered and built upon again. He cites the Lotus Sūtra's own image of this role: the bodhisattva Samantabhadra promising to protect anyone who forgets even a single verse of the sūtra by reciting it back to them until it's fluent again — the same basic gesture the treatise describes here in more general terms. A genuine spiritual friend, in other words, isn't only correcting what's gone wrong; a significant part of the role is simply helping someone hold onto what they already, at some point, got right.

14. Milestone: Skillful Instruction — Thirteen Kinds of Discourse for Calming and Insight

The treatise's third capability, skillful instruction, concerns specifically guiding a student into seclusion, stillness, and the meditative attention that supports calming and insight — unpacked through thirteen distinct kinds of discourse a qualified spiritual friend draws on.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能教授？謂於遠離、寂靜瑜伽作意止觀，時時隨順教授而轉，時時宣說與彼相應無倒言論。所謂能趣心離障蓋甚可愛樂尸羅言論、等持言論、聖慧言論、解脫言論、解脫智見言論、少欲言論、喜足言論、永斷言論、離欲言論、寂滅言論、損減言論、無雜言論、隨順緣性緣起言論。如是名為善能教授。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful instruction? It means constantly guiding a student, in keeping with seclusion, stillness, and meditative attention to calming and insight, and constantly setting forth discourse that accords with these, free of error — discourse capable of leading the mind toward freedom from the obstructions and hindrances, and deeply delightful in itself: discourse on discipline, on concentration, on noble wisdom, on liberation, on the knowledge and vision of liberation, on having few desires, on contentment, on permanent abandonment, on freedom from craving, on cessation, on diminishment, on freedom from admixture, and on conformity with conditionality and dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes that genuine seclusion — the foundation this entire teaching rests on — itself has three components already detailed in fascicle 30: a suitable place (quiet, dignified, safe, well-provisioned, with access to a genuine teacher and fellow practitioners), proper deportment (correctly timed walking and sitting meditation, posture, and resolve), and actual withdrawal, both physical (not living entangled with others) and mental (setting aside every defiled or neutral state of mind in favor of wholesome, meditation-supporting attention).

He then groups the thirteen kinds of discourse into three broader categories, following the Compendium of Determinations. The first five — on discipline, concentration, noble wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation — together form the "noble teaching proper," describing the five-part body of qualities (discipline, concentration, wisdom, liberation, and liberation's own self-knowledge) that mark a fully accomplished practitioner. The next two — on having few desires and on contentment — form the "teaching of renunciation," addressing desire for what's not yet obtained and satisfaction with what already has been. The remaining six form the "teaching that frees the mind from hindrances," itself divided three ways: discourse on permanent abandonment, freedom from craving, and cessation addresses the afflictions generally, tracing the path from the initial removal of conceptually-based afflictions through the deeper removal of innate ones to nirvana itself; discourse on diminishment and freedom from admixture addresses the five hindrances specifically; and discourse on conformity with dependent origination addresses the subtlest obstruction of all — fundamental ignorance — by directly explaining causality's fourfold conditions and the twelve links, so a student sees clearly that phenomena arise lawfully from causes, without either a controlling self or pure chance behind them. Cháng Bǎi's summary point: skillful instruction is not generic encouragement, but a precisely calibrated body of teaching, aimed exactly at whatever is standing between a specific student and the calming and insight they're working to develop.

15. Milestone: Skillful Guidance — Correcting, Reintegrating, and Directing a Community

The treatise's fourth capability, skillful guidance, covers a teacher's full disciplinary role: correcting a student who has genuinely erred, welcoming them back once corrected, and steadily directing a whole community toward what should be cultivated and away from what should not.

Chinese Source

云何名為善能教誡？謂於大師所說聖教，能以正法、以毗奈耶平等教誨。……如實知彼隨於一處違越毀犯，便於時時如法訶責、治罰、驅擯，令其調伏。既調伏已，如法平等受諸利養，和同、曉悟、收斂、攝受。於所應作及不應作，為令現行不現行故。於其積習及不積習，教導教誨。如是名為善能教誡。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful guidance? It means teaching with even-handed instruction, according to the true teaching and the discipline the great Teacher himself set forth. … Knowing truthfully that someone has, in some respect, transgressed or violated the training, one rightly censures, disciplines, or, if necessary, expels them, so as to bring about their correction. Once corrected, one allows them to share equally in the community's support once more, working in harmony with them, helping them understand clearly, restraining what should be restrained, and welcoming them back. Regarding what should be done and what should not, one ensures the one occurs and the other does not; regarding habits already formed and habits not yet formed, one instructs and guides accordingly. This is called skillful guidance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes skillful guidance from skillful admonishment by scope: admonishment addresses one instance of wrongdoing directly, while guidance covers a teacher's full disciplinary authority — the various roles of preceptor, instructor, and respected senior within a monastic community, correcting genuine violations through censure, formal discipline, or, in serious cases, expulsion, always guided by the same five qualities already named for admonishment (truthful, well-timed, beneficial, gentle, and rooted in genuine goodwill, never punitive anger). Correction is never the end point, though — once genuinely reformed, the corrected student is deliberately welcomed back into full, equal participation in communal life and support, helped to understand clearly what was and wasn't actually a violation, and guided to restrain whatever in their own conduct still risks harming themselves or others. Beyond individual correction, this same guidance extends to shaping an entire community's habits over time — steadily encouraging what should be cultivated (discipline, concentration, wisdom) and discouraging what has become habitual through countless past lives of unwholesome conditioning (the deeply worn grooves of greed, hatred, and delusion) — the long, patient work of turning ingrained bad habits into good ones.

16. Milestone: Skillful Teaching of the True Dharma — From Worldly Good to the Four Noble Truths

The treatise's fifth and final capability, skillful teaching, moves through two levels: worldly teaching suited to newcomers, and the supramundane teaching of the Four Noble Truths that surpasses anything found outside the Buddha's own tradition.

Chinese Source

云何名為善說正法？謂於時時能善宣說初時所作無倒言論。所謂施論、戒論、生天之論。於諸欲中，能廣開示過患出離清淨品法。又於時時宣說超勝四種聖諦相應言論。所謂苦論、集論、滅論、道論。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by skillful teaching of the true Dharma? It means being able, from time to time, to teach correctly what a newcomer should first take up — namely, discourse on giving, discourse on discipline, and discourse on rebirth in the heavens — and, regarding sensual desires, broadly revealing their dangers, and the pure qualities found in renouncing them. And it further means, from time to time, teaching discourse connected with the four noble truths, which surpasses all else — namely, discourse on suffering, on its origin, on its cessation, and on the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies this as the treatise's classic two-tier teaching sequence. For someone new to the path, teaching begins with what's immediately actionable and encouraging: the value of generosity, the value of moral discipline, and the prospect of a favorable rebirth through both — before turning, gently but honestly, to the real dangers hidden inside sensual pleasure, which is impermanent, insubstantial, and ultimately deceptive, "like a magician's illusion beguiling the unlearned," and to the genuine purity found in letting go of it. Only once this groundwork is laid does teaching turn to what "surpasses all else" — the Four Noble Truths, unique to the Buddha's own realization: suffering, its origin in craving, its actual cessation, and the eightfold path that leads there.

Cháng Bǎi illustrates the Four Noble Truths' power with a story from King Aśoka's biography. The king had arranged for a curtain to separate the women of his court from a visiting Dharma teacher, who accordingly taught them only the gentle, preliminary topics of giving, discipline, and heavenly rebirth. One woman, having broken the rule against approaching him directly, pushed the curtain aside and asked what the Buddha himself had actually realized beneath the Bodhi tree — was it only giving and discipline, or something more? The teacher answered plainly: the Buddha realized that all conditioned, defiled phenomena are suffering, like molten iron; that this suffering arises from craving, like a poisonous tree; and that the eightfold path extinguishes both the suffering and its cause. Hearing this, the woman attained stream-entry on the spot, then presented herself to the king with a knife at her own throat, confessing she had broken his rule out of genuine thirst for the Dharma, "the way a parched ox will not avoid death to reach water." The king, moved rather than angered on learning she had gained the Dharma-eye through this exchange, abolished the curtain rule entirely, decreeing that any woman who wished to hear the teaching directly could do so from then on. Cháng Bǎi's point in retelling it: the Four Noble Truths, taught plainly and directly, can accomplish in a single hearing what gentler preliminary teaching alone cannot.

17. Milestone: Skillful Teaching Completes, and All Eight Marks Define a "Good Friend"

The treatise finishes describing what makes teaching genuinely skillful — well-composed speech, teaching given in accordance with the listener, and teaching offered with a pure heart — then closes the entire account of good companionship with a single formula naming all eight marks and five capabilities together.

Chinese Source

又此言論應時而發、殷重、漸次、相續、俱有、令其欣慶、令其愛樂、令其歡喜、令其勇悍、無所訶擯、相應、助伴、無亂、如法、稱順眾會。有慈憐心，有利益心，有哀愍心，不依利養恭敬讚頌，不自高舉、不陵懱他。如是名為善說正法。由彼成就如是八支，於時時間善能諫舉、善作憶念、善能教授、善能教誡、善說正法，是故說彼名為善友。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, this discourse is offered at the proper time, with earnest seriousness, in proper sequence, without interruption, complete in both structure and content, bringing about delight, fondness, joy, and courageous resolve in the listener, never leaving the deserving unpraised or the correctable uncorrected, in accordance with reason, mutually supportive, free of confusion, in keeping with the true teaching, and suited to the assembly at hand. It is offered with a heart of loving-kindness, a heart seeking the listener's benefit, and a heart of compassion, never for the sake of gain, honor, or praise, never with arrogance, and never belittling another. This is called skillful teaching of the true Dharma. Having accomplished these eight qualities of speech, one is, from time to time, skilled at admonishing, skilled at reminding, skilled at instructing, skilled at guiding, and skilled at teaching the true Dharma — and it is for this reason that such a one is called a good friend.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifteen qualities of "teaching in accordance" and the five qualities of "pure-minded teaching" as two final refinements layered onto the eight qualities of well-composed speech already given. Teaching in accordance means matching the delivery itself to the listener and occasion — properly timed and dignified in bearing, treating the teaching with genuine seriousness, delivered in clear sequence without interruption, complete in both outline and detail, and calibrated to produce real delight, fondness, joy, and courage in the listener rather than discouragement; it also means never leaving virtuous effort unacknowledged or a correctable fault unaddressed, reasoning drawn from direct perception, inference, and scriptural authority together, mutually reinforcing rather than scattered, unambiguous rather than needlessly obscure, faithful to the Four Noble Truths and the rest of genuine doctrine, and pitched to whatever audience is actually present — encouraging the already-faithful, persuading the uncertain, and gently opening even a hostile listener toward faith. Pure-minded teaching, meanwhile, governs the teacher's inner motivation regardless of audience: loving-kindness even toward someone who bears them ill will, a genuine wish for benefit even toward someone of poor conduct, and compassion for anyone caught in pleasure, pain, heedlessness, or degraded circumstance — all of it offered without any hidden wish for material support, praise, or reputation, and without a trace of arrogance or contempt for the listener.

With this, the treatise closes its entire account of good companionship (provision nine of the fourteen path-provisions) with a single unifying formula: someone who has genuinely accomplished all eight qualifying marks — discipline, learning, attainment, compassion, tirelessness, forbearance, fearlessness, and skillful speech — and who can, on that foundation, actually admonish, remind, instruct, guide, and teach as just described, is what the treatise means, in full, by a "good friend." Cháng Bǎi stresses that eloquence alone, without the underlying eight qualities, would be empty performance; it is only the combination of genuine inner accomplishment and genuinely skillful expression that makes someone qualified to guide another person's practice.

18. The Brief Summary of Good Companionship Opens

Chinese Source

如是廣辯善友性已。復云何知此中略義？

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus has good companionship been explained in detail. How, then, should the brief summary of this teaching be understood?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise now follows its familiar three-part pattern — brief outline, detailed exposition, brief summary — already used for wakeful practice and clear awareness in conduct in the previous fascicle. Having just completed the extraordinarily detailed exposition of good companionship's eight marks and five capabilities, the treatise now turns to condense everything covered into a compact, memorable summary, the same closing move that brought each earlier provision to completion.

19. Milestone: Good Companionship Closes — Four Factors, and the Whole Teaching Complete

The treatise condenses everything covered about good companionship into four essential factors, then closes the entire ninth provision with its standard formula uniting detailed exposition and brief summary.

Chinese Source

謂若善友心善稠密，為性哀愍，最初於彼樂為利益、樂為安樂。又即於此利益安樂，如實了知，無有顛倒，離顛倒見。又即於此利益安樂，有大勢力、方便善巧，能令積集、能令引發。又即於此利益安樂，翹勤無惰，起發圓滿，為性好樂，發勤精進。當知由此四因緣故，攝一切種總、略圓滿善知識性。如是名為此善友性所有略義。若前所說廣分別義，若此所說所有略義，一切總說為善友性。

Ru-Yi Translation

A good friend's mind is thoroughly settled and compassionate by nature, from the very outset delighting in bringing others benefit and ease. Regarding that very benefit and ease, one understands it truly, free of distortion, free of any mistaken view. Regarding that very benefit and ease, one has great capability and skillful means, able to help others accumulate it and bring it forth. And regarding that very benefit and ease, one is diligent and never lazy, arousing complete energy, delighting in it by nature, generating sustained effort. Know that by these four causal factors, the nature of a good friend is entirely and completely encompassed, in full and in brief alike. This is the brief summary of good companionship. Both what was explained in detail before, and this brief summary given now, are together called good companionship.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps these four factors directly onto the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own parallel list, noting they correspond to its first four (of five) marks of a good spiritual friend: genuinely wishing to benefit another from the start (drawing on compassion), correctly understanding what actually constitutes their benefit (drawing on extensive learning), having the real capability to bring that benefit about (drawing on genuine attainment), and never tiring in the effort (drawing directly on the fifth of the eight original marks). Though stated as only four factors here, Cháng Bǎi notes each still depends on the fuller eightfold structure already detailed — compassion without learning could easily misfire, learning without attainment stays merely theoretical, and neither means anything without the tireless follow-through to actually see it through.

With this brief summary, good companionship — the ninth of the fourteen path-provisions — closes in full: its brief outline, its extraordinarily detailed exposition across eight marks and five capabilities, and now its four-factor summary, together completing the teaching. Cháng Bǎi closes by naming the four classical steps of genuine practice this entire provision serves: first, drawing near a good spiritual friend; second, actually hearing the true teaching from them; third, reflecting on it correctly; and fourth, practicing in accordance with what has been understood — the exact sequence the treatise turns to next, as provisions ten and eleven, hearing and reflecting on the true teaching, open immediately.

20. Milestone: Hearing and Reflecting on the True Teaching Opens

Fascicle 25 continues directly into the tenth and eleventh path-provisions, treated together: hearing the true teaching and reflecting on it. The treatise opens by defining exactly what counts as "the true teaching" and who is qualified to proclaim it, then works through the classical twelvefold division of scripture in unusually rich technical detail, beginning with the discourses themselves.

Chinese Source

云何名為聞思正法？謂正法者，若佛世尊、若佛弟子、正士、正至、正善丈夫，宣說開顯分別照了。此復云何？所謂契經、應頌、記別，廣說如前十二分教。是名正法。……云何契經？謂薄伽梵，於彼彼方所，為彼彼所化有情，依彼彼所化諸行差別，宣說無量蘊相應語、處相應語、緣起相應語、食相應語、諦相應語、界相應語、聲聞乘相應語、獨覺乘相應語、如來乘相應語、念住正斷神足根力覺支道支等相應語、不淨息念諸學證淨等相應語。結集如來正法藏者，攝聚如是種種聖語，為令聖教久住世故，以諸美妙名句文身，如其所應次第安布、次第結集。謂能貫穿縫綴種種能引義利、能引梵行真善妙義。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is meant by hearing and reflecting on the true teaching? The true teaching is what is proclaimed, revealed, analyzed, and clarified by the Blessed One, by the Buddha's disciples, by upright persons, by those who have reached correctness, and by genuinely good persons. What, further, is this teaching? Namely, discourses, verse restatements, prophecies, and so on through the twelve divisions of scripture explained before. This is called the true teaching. … What are discourses? It means that the Blessed One, in various places, for various beings to be taught, according to the various differences in their conduct, proclaimed countless forms of teaching connected with the aggregates, connected with the sense-fields, connected with dependent origination, connected with nutriment, connected with the truths, connected with the elements, connected with the hearer-disciple vehicle, connected with the solitary-realizer vehicle, connected with the vehicle of the Tathāgata, connected with the foundations of mindfulness, right exertions, bases of miraculous power, faculties, powers, factors of awakening, and path-factors, and connected with the training in impurity, mindfulness of breathing, and the confirmed faiths. Those who compiled the treasury of the Tathāgata's true teaching gathered together these various holy sayings and, so that the sacred teaching might long abide in the world, arranged them in proper sequence with excellent words, phrases, and letters, compiling them in due order — that is, stringing and stitching together their various meanings, capable of bringing benefit, capable of supporting pure conduct, genuinely good and subtle.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first clarifies who is qualified to proclaim "the true teaching": the Buddha himself; his ordained and lay disciples; "upright persons" who have simply set aside wrong conduct for correct conduct; "those who have reached correctness," meaning stream-enterers through arhats who have each cut off their own share of affliction; and "genuinely good persons" — precisely the spiritual friends just described in full, possessing all eight marks and five capabilities. The teaching itself is then organized into the traditional twelve divisions, and the treatise gives the discourses (the first and broadest division) an unusually detailed account of their actual subject matter: teachings connected with the five aggregates, the twelve sense-fields, dependent origination, the four kinds of nutriment, the Four Noble Truths, the elements, and each of the three vehicles (hearer-disciple, solitary-realizer, and bodhisattva) in turn, as well as teachings connected with the entire thirty-seven factors of awakening (the four foundations of mindfulness, four right exertions, four bases of power, five faculties, five powers, seven factors of awakening, and eightfold path) and with specific meditative trainings such as the contemplation of impurity, mindfulness of breathing, and the "four confirmed faiths" in the Buddha, the teaching, the community, and the precepts.

He then explains why these teachings are called a "sutra" (契經) at all: the Chinese term literally means "stringing together," evoking a thread running through beads — after the Buddha's passing, his disciples convened to recite everything they had heard, cross-checked it collectively to confirm its authenticity, and arranged it in careful sequence using memorable summary verses, precisely so the teaching could survive intact rather than scatter and be lost. "Sutra" in the fuller sense carries two meanings: compilation, the actual editorial work of gathering and ordering the material, and confirmation, the collective verification that what was preserved was genuinely the Buddha's own word. Cháng Bǎi frames the term "contact" (the other classical gloss on 契) as pointing in two directions at once: upward, in perfect accord with the Buddha's own realization, and downward, perfectly suited to the capacities of the beings receiving it — a teaching genuinely worth preserving this carefully precisely because it manages both at once.

21. Three More Divisions: Verse Restatements, Prophecies, and Standalone Verses

Chinese Source

云何應頌？謂於中間、或於最後宣說伽陀，或復宣說未了義經。是名應頌。云何記別？謂於是中，記別弟子命過已後當生等事，或復宣說已了義經。是名記別。云何諷頌？謂非直說，是結句說。或作二句、或作三句、或作四句、或作五句、或作六句等。是名諷頌。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are verse restatements? They are verses spoken in the middle or at the end of a teaching, or verses that further express a teaching not yet fully drawn out. This is called a verse restatement. What are prophecies? They are declarations of where a disciple will be reborn after death, or the concise restatement of a teaching already fully explained. This is called a prophecy. What are standalone verses? They are not spoken as prose, but composed in verse — in couplets, triplets, quatrains, or stanzas of five or six lines and more. This is called a standalone verse.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes these three closely related verse forms carefully. Verse restatement (應頌, "corresponding verse") is poetry that echoes or completes a prose passage already given — sometimes simply restating it more memorably, sometimes drawing out a meaning the prose alone left incomplete. Prophecy (記別) carries two quite different senses in the treatise: the more famous sense, a prediction of a disciple's future rebirth or eventual buddhahood, but also a second, more technical sense — a teaching stated briefly in outline being restated with its meaning fully drawn out, or a fully complete teaching being condensed back into brief statement. Standalone verse (諷頌, sometimes called "rhinoceros verse" after a famous early example about solitary practice) differs from verse restatement in having no prose counterpart at all — verses like those found in the Dhammapada or the Verses of the Elder Monks and Nuns, complete units of teaching in their own right, not echoing or condensing anything spoken in prose nearby.

22. Unprompted Teachings and the Opening of Circumstance-Based Teachings

Chinese Source

云何自說？謂於是中，不顯能請補特伽羅名字、種性，為令當來正法久住、聖教久住，不請而說。是名自說。云何因緣？謂於是中，顯示能請補特伽羅名字、種性，因請而說；及諸所有毗奈耶相應，有因有緣別解脫經。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are unprompted teachings? They are teachings in which the name and lineage of the one who requested them is not given, spoken without being requested at all, so that the true teaching and the sacred doctrine might long endure. This is called an unprompted teaching. What are circumstance-based teachings? They are teachings in which the name and lineage of the one who requested them is given, spoken in response to that request; and they also include all the discipline-related teachings, the rules of individual liberation, each arising from some specific cause and circumstance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Sūtra of Amitābha as the classic example of an unprompted teaching: the Buddha simply began speaking without any disciple having asked, moved to share it purely so the teaching would endure for the benefit of future generations — the treatise notes he did the same on seeing a wild elephant roaming freely (composing a verse on solitary contentment) and on seeing an old couple in poverty (composing a verse on the cost of a wasted youth), moments of spontaneous teaching arising from what he simply observed. Circumstance-based teachings are the mirror image: teachings that name exactly who asked the question that prompted them — sutras named for the bodhisattva or disciple whose inquiry occasioned them — and, more broadly, the entire body of monastic discipline, since every single training rule in the Vinaya was established only after some specific incident actually occurred, never speculatively in advance. Cháng Bǎi notes this is why the rules of individual liberation are always transmitted together with the story of their origin — the "cause and circumstance" is treated as inseparable from the rule itself.
