The Five Marks of a True Bodhisattva

With chapter eighteen closed, the Bodhisattva Stage opens its second major section — the yoga foundation of sustained practice — beginning with the chapter on a bodhisattva's true marks: compassion, loving speech, valor, open-handed giving, and understanding the teaching's own profound hidden meaning, each examined through five further angles of intrinsic nature, basis, benefit, sequence, and how it relates to the six perfections.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 47 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: The Bodhisattva Stage's Second Section Opens — Five True Marks of a Bodhisattva, Each to Be Examined Five Ways

With chapter eighteen now closed, the treatise opens the Bodhisattva Stage's second of four major yoga sections — the yoga foundation concerned with sustained practice in accordance with the teaching, comprising four chapters: the chapter on a bodhisattva's true marks, the chapter on the two divisions of householder and renunciate bodhisattva, the chapter on excellent aspiration, and the chapter on the bodhisattva abodes, describing the thirteen stages of bodhisattva practice — this fascicle covering the first three chapters in full and opening the fourth as far as its first abode, the abode of excellent aspiration. The first chapter, on true marks, opens by asking what genuinely constitutes a true bodhisattva's mark, then answers in summary verse: five marks — compassion, loving speech, valor, open-handed giving, and understanding the teaching's own profound hidden meaning — each of which is, in turn, to be examined from five further angles: its own intrinsic nature, the basis it depends on, the beneficial fruit it produces, the sequence in which the five marks are cultivated, and how each mark relates to and is included within the six perfections.

Ru-Yi Translation

Within the Basic Section, the Bodhisattva Stage, the second yoga foundation of sustained practice, chapter one: the marks of a bodhisattva.

What are the true marks of a bodhisattva? A summary verse states: "Bodhisattvas' true marks; one should know they are five kinds; intrinsic nature, basis, and fruit; sequence, and inclusion — five turnings."

This means that bodhisattvas have five true marks; anyone who has accomplished them falls within the number of bodhisattvas. What are these five? First, compassion; second, loving speech; third, valor; fourth, open-handed giving; fifth, understanding profound meaning and hidden intent.

One should understand that each of these five factors, in turn, has five further turnings: first, intrinsic nature; second, basis; third, beneficial fruit; fourth, sequence; fifth, mutual inclusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by orienting the reader to a genuine structural transition: the Bodhisattva Stage's first major section, the initial yoga foundation just completed across fascicles 44 through 46, comprised eighteen chapters covering everything from the bodhisattva lineage through the qualities that define a bodhisattva's character. Now begins the second of four such sections, this one addressing the yoga foundation of sustained, ongoing practice in accordance with the teaching, and comprising four further chapters: the marks of a bodhisattva (this chapter), the divisions between householder and renunciate bodhisattvas, excellent aspiration, and the bodhisattva abodes — the last of which unfolds the full thirteen stages of bodhisattva practice, though this fascicle will only reach its very first stage before concluding. He notes in passing that the treatise's overall architecture continues beyond even this second section into two further major divisions, covering birth, being gathered in by a teacher, the bodhisattva grounds in their fullest technical detail, and finally bodhisattva conduct itself — a vast structure of which this fascicle opens only the next modest portion.

He then previews the chapter's own inner logic: five marks make a bodhisattva genuinely a bodhisattva rather than merely someone who has taken the name — compassion, meaning not merely an inward feeling of sympathy but a disposition that actually issues in concrete benefit and ease for beings; loving speech, meaning language that goes beyond simply comforting words to include teaching the profound, genuinely beneficial truths beings actually need to hear; valor, meaning vigorous, undaunted diligence in bringing both oneself and others to spiritual maturity; open-handed giving, meaning a readiness to give material wealth, the teaching itself, and freedom from fear alike, without restriction; and understanding profound meaning and hidden intent, meaning the four kinds of unobstructed understanding that let a bodhisattva penetrate the deepest teachings of emptiness and dependent origination in the scriptures, the subtleties of what may be transgressed and restored in the monastic code, and the specific technical vocabulary the treatises use to remove beings' doubts about the profound intentions underlying the Buddha's own words. He stresses that a bodhisattva lacking any one of these five falls short of being a bodhisattva in truth, however sincerely the aspiration for awakening may have been aroused — the same insistence on substance over mere title that closed chapter eighteen now becomes, in this new chapter, the entire organizing question. The treatise turns next to examining each of these five marks in turn, beginning with the first of the five angles, intrinsic nature, applied first to compassion.

2. Milestone: The Intrinsic Nature of Compassion — Both an Inward Wish for Beings' Benefit and Its Actual Expression in Conduct

The treatise begins examining the first of the five true marks, compassion, from the angle of its own intrinsic nature, dividing it in brief into two components: one residing in aspiration, and one residing in actual conduct. The component residing in aspiration is that bodhisattvas hold, toward all sentient beings, both the aspiration to bring benefit and the aspiration to bring ease — sincerely wishing, in their own hearts, that beings' thinking would improve for the better and that beings' bodies and minds would find rest from suffering; this inward wish alone is already called compassion. The component residing in actual conduct is that bodhisattvas, in accordance with that very aspiration, according to whatever strength and capacity they actually possess, bring concrete benefit to beings through body and speech — not merely wishing beings well but actually acting, through physical deeds and spoken words, to bring about the benefit and ease wished for; this too is called compassion.

Ru-Yi Translation

One should understand that, here, compassion's own intrinsic nature is, in brief, of two kinds: first, residing in aspiration; second, residing in actual conduct.

Residing in aspiration means that bodhisattvas hold, toward all sentient beings, the aspiration to benefit them and the aspiration to bring them ease. This is called compassion.

Residing in actual conduct means that bodhisattvas, in accordance with that very aspiration, according to their own strength and capacity, bring concrete benefit to beings through body and speech. This too is called compassion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that this twofold division of compassion's own intrinsic nature — aspiration and conduct together — is not incidental but central to the entire chapter's insistence on substance over mere sentiment: a bodhisattva's compassion must first exist as a genuine inward wish that beings' own thinking improve and that their bodies and minds find rest, but a wish alone, however sincere, remains incomplete without the second component, actual conduct that puts that wish into practice through real bodily and verbal action.

He explains why the aspiration itself is framed specifically around beings' thinking rather than only their circumstances: a bodhisattva rescuing beings leads always with wisdom, recognizing that most of the poverty, disaster, and suffering beings encounter traces back, at root, to the karma generated by their own mistaken thinking — so genuine compassion does not stop at solving a being's immediate material problem but reaches further, guiding that being's own thinking and outlook toward abandoning the unwholesome and cultivating the wholesome, thereby resolving the problem at its actual root rather than merely its surface symptom. He notes that these two components, the aspiration to benefit thinking and the aspiration to bring bodily and mental ease, together constitute what the treatise means by a bodhisattva's compassionate mind, while the conduct-component simply describes how that same compassionate aspiration, once genuinely held, naturally and necessarily expresses itself outwardly whenever a bodhisattva's own wisdom and merit provide the actual capacity to help. The treatise turns next to the intrinsic nature of the second mark, loving speech.

3. Milestone: The Intrinsic Nature of Loving Speech, of Valor, and of Open-Handed Giving

The second mark, loving speech, has, as its own intrinsic nature, the three kinds already explained earlier in the treatise's own chapter on the means of gathering beings: comforting speech, joyfully congratulatory speech, and beneficial speech. Comforting speech follows ordinary worldly courtesy — greeting others, inquiring after their well-being, offering words of consolation whenever something unwelcome has occurred, so that a being's spirits are lifted. Joyfully congratulatory speech offers genuine words of celebration whenever something wonderful or fortunate has happened to a being, bringing forth genuine joy. And beneficial speech actually teaches the profoundly beneficial truths of the teaching itself, so that a being who hears it gains real, lasting benefit — a gift of the teaching bringing benefit and ease to all beings. The third mark, valor, has as its own intrinsic nature a resolute, unshakeable firmness, entirely without timidity or weakness, possessed of tremendous inner strength — a bodhisattva who, faced with whatever difficulty arises in bringing both self and others to spiritual maturity, remains utterly undaunted, never retreating into cowardice or settling for the lesser vehicles, firmly resolved on nothing less than buddhahood itself. And the fourth mark, open-handed giving, has as its own intrinsic nature both a vast, expansive disposition toward giving and a disposition toward giving entirely free of any taint — giving broadly to every being without distinguishing friend from foe from stranger, and giving with a mind untroubled by affliction, unattached to either love or hatred, giving with the threefold emptiness of giver, gift, and recipient equally in mind.

勇猛自性，謂諸菩薩剛決堅固，無所怯劣，有大勢力。

若諸菩薩廣大施性、無染施性，是名舒手惠施自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Loving speech's own intrinsic nature is, as already explained earlier, comforting speech, joyfully congratulatory speech, and beneficial speech; one should understand this exactly as explained earlier in the Chapter on the Means of Gathering Beings.

Valor's own intrinsic nature is that bodhisattvas are resolute and firm, entirely without timidity or weakness, possessed of tremendous strength.

If bodhisattvas possess both a vast, expansive disposition toward giving and a disposition toward giving free of any taint, this is called open-handed giving's own intrinsic nature.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi refers the loving-speech mark directly back to the treatise's own fuller treatment in fascicle 43's chapter on the means of gathering beings, reprising the threefold distinction briefly: comforting speech as ordinary, courteous inquiry after another's well-being and comfort in adversity; joyfully congratulatory speech as genuine celebration of another's good fortune; and beneficial speech as the gift of the teaching itself, the most valuable of the three since it alone offers lasting rather than merely momentary benefit.

He explains valor as combining wisdom with an unbending resolve toward the six perfections and toward the twin tasks of maturing both oneself and others — such a bodhisattva, encountering hardship, neither wavers nor shrinks back into timidity, and above all never settles for the lesser attainment of the disciple's or solitary awakener's own more limited vehicles, holding fast instead to nothing short of complete buddhahood; this unshakeable inner strength is precisely what "valor" names. He explains open-handed giving's two components as, respectively, breadth and purity: a truly vast giving disposition draws no distinction between friend, enemy, or stranger, extending generosity to every being alike, while an untainted giving disposition gives with a mind free of the afflictions of attachment and aversion, ideally accomplished with the classical threefold emptiness — no fixed notion of a giver, no fixed notion of a gift, and no fixed notion of a recipient — in mind throughout the act of giving itself. The treatise turns next to the fifth and most technically demanding mark, understanding profound meaning and hidden intent, unpacked through the classical four kinds of unobstructed understanding.

4. Milestone: Understanding Profound Meaning and Hidden Intent — the Four Kinds of Unobstructed Understanding in Full

The fifth mark, understanding profound meaning and hidden intent, has as its own intrinsic nature the bodhisattva's own four kinds of unobstructed understanding, together with the correct, applied wisdom that these four undistortedly bring forth. Unobstructed understanding of the teaching means that bodhisattvas, with respect to every teaching and every one of its varied gateways, possess an unobstructed, irreversible wisdom born of cultivation regarding both its full range of particular characteristics and its underlying universal nature — equally described as the undeluded insight, arising from correctly realizing the differing names given to every kind of phenomenon, together with the meditative states of mind joined with it. Unobstructed understanding of meaning means the same unobstructed, irreversible cultivated wisdom applied instead to every phenomenon's varied particular marks, rather than to its names — the undeluded insight arising from correctly realizing the many differing characteristics found within each and every phenomenon. Unobstructed understanding of language means this same wisdom applied to the etymological explanation of every term used for every phenomenon — the undeluded insight arising from correctly realizing the explanatory derivation of technical vocabulary itself. And unobstructed understanding of eloquent exposition means this same wisdom applied to distinguishing and expounding every category of phenomena without exception — the undeluded insight, joined with meditative states of mind, that arises from correctly realizing how thoroughly to penetrate all phenomena, engaged in extensively for the sake of benefiting others through actual teaching.

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas possess the four kinds of unobstructed understanding, together with the correct, applied wisdom undistortedly brought forth by them, this is called understanding profound meaning and hidden intent's own intrinsic nature.

Commentary: Regarding "if bodhisattvas possess the four kinds of unobstructed understanding" and the like — the bodhisattva's own cultivation of the four kinds of unobstructed understanding has already had its characteristics explained in the chapter on the factors of awakening. Because of this, a bodhisattva is skillfully able to expound the true teaching, giving rise to applied wisdom with respect to all phenomena — this is called the correct, applied wisdom undistortedly brought forth by them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own two earlier discussions of this same correct, applied wisdom — in fascicle 45's chapter on the factors of awakening and again in fascicle 48 — as the wisdom a bodhisattva brings forth specifically after having already accomplished the four kinds of unobstructed understanding, skillfully able to expound phenomena as a result, while noting that the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra, fascicle 180, frames the same relationship in the opposite causal direction: a practitioner's own sustained habituation with the Buddha's words — their names, their meaning, their etymological explanation, and their exposition — is precisely what gives rise to the four kinds of unobstructed understanding as applied wisdom's own result. He treats both framings as simply describing the same tight, mutually reinforcing relationship between the four kinds of understanding and their fruit in actual skillful teaching, rather than a genuine disagreement.

He then draws out, following the treatise's own fuller explanation in fascicle 38, two complementary formulations the treatise gives for each of the four unobstructed understandings. Unobstructed understanding of the teaching, in its first formulation, is the cultivated wisdom, unobstructed and never regressing, that bodhisattvas bring to every teaching and its varied approaches, with respect to both a phenomenon's full range of particular qualities and its shared universal nature; in its second formulation, it is simply the undeluded insight born of correctly realizing the differing names attached to every category of phenomenon, together with the corresponding states of concentrated mind. Unobstructed understanding of meaning, in its first formulation, is this same unobstructed cultivated wisdom applied to every phenomenon's varied particular marks rather than to its name; in its second, it is the undeluded insight born of correctly realizing the many different characteristics found within each phenomenon.

Unobstructed understanding of language, in its first formulation, is this same cultivated wisdom applied to the etymological derivation of every term describing every phenomenon; in its second, it is simply the undeluded insight born of correctly realizing how technical vocabulary itself is derived and explained. And unobstructed understanding of eloquent exposition, in its first formulation, is this same cultivated wisdom applied to every category and classification of phenomena without exception; in its second, it is the undeluded insight, joined with meditative absorption, born of correctly realizing how to penetrate all phenomena thoroughly, and is specifically exercised at length whenever a bodhisattva teaches for the direct benefit of others. He notes that a bodhisattva who has genuinely cultivated all four — through the four investigations, the four kinds of genuine wisdom, sustained correct reflection, and the calm-abiding and insight already explained at length in earlier fascicles regarding the profound emptiness and dependent origination of phenomena, the subtleties of what constitutes transgression and restoration within the monastic precepts, and the specific technical vocabulary and interpretive method the treatises use to resolve beings' doubts about the Buddha's own deepest intentions, exactly as the Compendium on Textual Exposition explains in fascicles 81 and 82 — gradually perfects all four kinds of unobstructed understanding together, and this fully accomplished capacity is precisely what the fifth mark, understanding profound meaning and hidden intent, describes. With this, the intrinsic nature of all five true marks of a bodhisattva has now been explained in full; the treatise turns next to the second of the five angles, the basis each of these five marks depends upon.

5. Milestone: The Basis of Compassion Opens — Beings Who Suffer, and Beings Who Delight in Wrongdoing

The treatise turns to the second of the five angles, the basis each mark depends on, beginning with compassion: one should understand that a bodhisattva's compassion rests, in brief, on five kinds of beings — beings who suffer, beings of wrong conduct, heedless beings, wrongly-practicing beings, and beings still latent with affliction, the first four common to ordinary beings and non-Buddhist practitioners alike, the fifth spanning both ordinary and noble beings within the Buddhist teaching itself. The first basis, beings who suffer, names those in the hells, among the hungry ghosts, and among animals, who are ceaselessly, continuously oppressed by unrelenting painful sensation, their bodies and minds harmed without any respite — such suffering beings are the very first object of a bodhisattva's compassion. The second basis, beings of wrong conduct, names beings whose present circumstances may not be fixed toward suffering but who habitually engage in unwholesome conduct of body, speech, and mind, delighting and dwelling contentedly in wrongdoing — exemplified by those who raise and then slaughter sheep, pigs, and chickens as their very livelihood, undisciplined in conduct, whose determined commitment to such unwholesome deeds guarantees a future fall into the lower realms.

Ru-Yi Translation

One should understand that a bodhisattva's basis for compassion is, in brief, five kinds. What are these five? First, beings who suffer; second, beings of wrong conduct; third, heedless beings; fourth, wrongly-practicing beings; fifth, beings still latent with affliction.

All beings in the hells and the like are ceaselessly, continuously oppressed and turned about by painful sensation. Such beings are called beings who suffer.

There are also beings who, though not fixed toward suffering, frequently engage in unwholesome conduct of body, unwholesome conduct of speech, and unwholesome conduct of mind, delighting and dwelling contentedly in wrongdoing — such as those undisciplined in conduct who raise and slaughter sheep, pigs, chickens, and the like. Such beings are called beings of wrong conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens by observing that beings and buddhas exist only in mutual dependence — without beings, there could be no bodhisattva at all, and without a bodhisattva's path, no buddha; a bodhisattva's compassion itself only ever arises in dependence on some actual being toward whom it is directed, which is precisely why this second angle asks specifically what beings serve as that necessary basis. He notes the treatise's own observation that the first four kinds of beings named here are found equally among ordinary worldly people and non-Buddhist practitioners, while the fifth kind spans both ordinary and noble beings specifically within the Buddhist teaching — a structure he will return to once all five have been explained.

He identifies the first basis, beings who suffer, as specifically the inhabitants of the three lower realms — hell-beings, hungry ghosts, and animals — whose suffering is not occasional but genuinely unrelenting, one moment of pain flowing into the next without any actual relief, and it is precisely this unbroken intensity of suffering that makes such beings the most immediate object of a bodhisattva's compassion. He then illustrates the second basis, beings of wrong conduct, with the treatise's own example of professional butchers, and draws out the Decisions section's own threefold grading of undisciplined conduct from fascicle 53: the lowest grade describes someone simply born into a family whose livelihood happens to be butchery, without any strong personal inclination toward such unwholesome work; the middling grade describes someone who has come to personally endorse taking up such a livelihood but has not yet actually begun; and the highest grade describes someone who, purely to sustain their own life, actively delights in, approves of, and commits to killing as a means of livelihood through deliberately harsh methods, continuing this practice for as long as they live. Beings of any of these three grades, having sown the causes of future suffering right now, remain, he stresses, no less an object of a bodhisattva's compassion than those already suffering the results of past unwholesome karma.

6. Milestone: Heedless Beings, Wrongly-Practicing Beings, and Beings Still Latent With Affliction — Closing the Basis of Compassion

The third basis, heedless beings, names those who, though neither fixed toward suffering nor engaged in unwholesome conduct, remain infatuated with and absorbed in sensory pleasure, forever delighting in dwelling amid entertainers, song and dance, and playful glances to amuse themselves — those given over to the pleasures of the senses, easily mistaking such entertainment for genuine art while it in fact stirs up craving and becomes the very root of continued samsaric wandering. The fourth basis, wrongly-practicing beings, names those who, neither suffering, unwholesome, nor heedless, nonetheless rely on false views to practice all manner of painful, misguided paths toward what they mistakenly believe is liberation — renouncing sensory desire and taking ordination within a wrongly-taught doctrine and discipline, holding one or another of the sixty-two false views concerning self and person. And the fifth basis, beings still latent with affliction, names those who, though free of the first four conditions altogether, remain, whether fully bound or not fully bound, still followed by the latent seeds of affliction — specifically genuinely practicing, wholesome ordinary beings and noble ones still in training within the Buddhist teaching itself. With this fifth basis, the treatise concludes: these five kinds of beings together comprise the entire basis and object upon which a bodhisattva's compassion depends and arises, with nothing beyond these five to be added or found lacking.

Ru-Yi Translation

There are also beings who, though not fixed toward suffering nor engaged in unwholesome conduct, are infatuated with and given over to sensory desires, forever delighting in dwelling amid entertainers, song and dance, and playful glances, amusing themselves. These are beings absorbed in the pleasures of the senses. Such beings are called heedless beings.

There are also beings who, though not fixed toward suffering, unwholesome conduct, or heedlessness, rely on false views to practice all manner of painful paths toward what they take to be liberation — renouncing sensory desires and taking ordination within a wrongly-taught doctrine and discipline. Such beings are called wrongly-practicing beings.

There are also beings who, though not fixed toward suffering, and so on up through not practicing wrong paths, are nevertheless, whether fully bound or not fully bound, followed by the latent seeds of affliction. These are genuinely practicing, wholesome ordinary beings and noble ones still in training. These are called beings still latent with affliction.

Commentary: Regarding "genuinely practicing, wholesome ordinary beings" and the like — those who follow through faith or follow through the teaching, heading toward direct contemplation of the truths but not yet having seen the truths, are called genuinely practicing, wholesome ordinary beings. Those from the fruit of stream-entry through the path toward arhatship are called noble ones still in training. "Fully bound" and "not fully bound" should be understood as explaining these two in turn, respectively.

These, then, are the bodhisattva's five bases for compassion. It is by depending on these bases, by taking these as objects, that compassion turns and functions. Beyond these five, there is nothing to be added and nothing lacking.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes how easily the third basis, heedless beings, can be mistaken for something harmless or even admirable by worldly standards — ordinary people often regard the entertainments named here, performance, song, dance, and playful glances, as legitimate art, yet examined through the lens of the teaching, such entertainments readily stir up craving in the mind, leading beings to become lost in and infatuated with sensory pleasure precisely because they seem so innocuous; since craving is itself the root of continued wandering through birth-and-death, a bodhisattva's compassion extends with equal seriousness to beings absorbed in such heedless pleasure-seeking, not only to those obviously suffering or doing evident harm.

He explains the fourth basis, wrongly-practicing beings, as specifically non-Buddhist renunciates who have genuinely given up worldly desire and even taken up an ascetic discipline, yet do so grounded in one or another of the classical sixty-two false views regarding self and person — such beings' very sincerity and discipline make them no less deserving of a bodhisattva's compassionate outreach, since sincere effort misdirected by wrong view still leads only to further suffering rather than genuine liberation. For the fifth basis, he unpacks the commentary's own careful distinction between two kinds of practitioner following two different temperaments — those who follow primarily through faith, whose strong confidence lets them take up practice simply on a good teacher's word, and those who follow primarily through the teaching itself, whose stronger wisdom-faculty leads them to study the scriptures directly and only commit to practice after their own careful reflection — both alike still heading toward, but not yet having attained, direct realization of the four noble truths, and both alike called genuinely practicing, wholesome ordinary beings; while noble ones still in training span every stage from stream-entry through the very threshold of arhatship, the fifteen mind-moments preceding stream-entry itself already counted as heading toward that first fruit. He notes that "fully bound," used earlier of ordinary beings generally, here refers specifically to these genuinely practicing, wholesome ordinary beings, still bound by every affliction of the three realms, while "not fully bound" refers to the noble ones still in training, who have already begun severing them.

Surveying the completed fivefold scheme, he observes that these five bases move in a certain natural progression from the most obviously suffering to the most subtly still-imperfect: from beings openly suffering in the lower realms, through beings actively creating future suffering, through beings dulled by pleasure, through beings sincerely but wrongly seeking liberation, to beings genuinely on the Buddhist path yet still carrying affliction's own latent seeds — a bodhisattva's compassion, properly cultivated, must extend to every one of these five without exception, since no being, however apparently advanced or apparently comfortable, is yet entirely free of some condition calling for compassionate concern. The treatise turns next to the basis of the second mark, loving speech.

7. Milestone: The Basis of Loving Speech — Five Kinds of Speech Grounded in the Four Unobstructed Understandings, Closing the Second Mark's Basis in Full

The treatise turns to the basis of the second mark, loving speech, which likewise rests on five kinds: correct doctrinal speech, correct congratulatory speech, correct comforting speech, correct speech of open, unrestricted generosity, and speech that accords with reason — all five already explained at length in the treatise's own earlier chapter on the means of gathering beings. 8. Milestone: The Basis of Valor — Five Grounds Already Named in the Chapter on the Factors of Awakening, Three for Others and Two for Oneself

The treatise turns to the basis of the third mark, valor, referring directly back to the five bases of the bodhisattva's own firm, sustaining resolve already explained in the earlier chapter on the factors of awakening — these same five, the treatise says, should be understood as valor's own five bases here as well. 9. Milestone: The Basis of Open-Handed Giving — Five Qualities of Genuine Generosity, From Frequent Giving to Giving Grounded in Nothing at All

Frequent giving means that a bodhisattva gives away whatever is received the moment the opportunity arises, without stinginess, rather than waiting to accumulate a large sum before giving all at once. Untainted giving means giving without anger or agitation even toward a recipient of poor conduct, and without any superstitious clinging to false omens about when giving is or is not auspicious. Turning to dependent origination's own depth, he explains that the links of dependent origination, however genuinely impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self they actually are, nonetheless present an appearance closely resembling permanence, ease, self, and purity — a resemblance that easily deceives an unexamined mind into mistaking passing appearance for lasting substance, exactly as detailed in the Stage With Both Investigation and Analysis, fascicle 10. 11. Milestone: The Monastic Code, the Treatises, Hidden Intent, and Unobstructed Understanding — Closing the Basis of All Five Marks Entirely

The second basis is skill regarding transgression and skill regarding restoration to purity within the monastic code — a bodhisattva who correctly understands both what constitutes a violation of the precepts and how genuine confession restores purity afterward, since the Buddha's own precepts exist not to punish beings but to guide them toward genuine freedom from suffering, and therefore necessarily allow for restoration. The third basis is using language to establish, without any distortion, the very characteristics of phenomena found within the treatises — the great bodhisattva authors from Nagarjuna through Maitreya and Asanga having themselves skillfully composed such treatises precisely through this same capacity. The fourth basis is correctly disclosing and dispelling doubt about phenomena whose underlying intent is difficult to grasp — every tathagata's own hidden intent sometimes remaining concealed within scripture rather than stated outright, as when the scriptures speak of an arhat who has "killed father and mother," a riddle resolved only by understanding that the true referents are ignorance, the father, and craving, the mother. With this fifth basis, the treatise concludes: these five — the discourses, the monastic code, the treatises, hidden intent, and unobstructed understanding — together comprise the entire basis on which a bodhisattva's capacity to understand profound meaning and hidden intent depends, with nothing beyond these five to add or find lacking — closing the second of the five angles, the basis of every one of the five true marks, entirely in full.

22. Milestone: Thorough, Constant, and Faultless Giving — Withholding Nothing, Never Growing Weary, and Giving Only What Genuinely Benefits

34. Milestone: Fearless Compassion, and Reasoned Compassion Grounded in Genuinely Wholesome Conduct

The first aspect, fearless compassion, describes a bodhisattva who gives rise to compassion toward beings not out of fear or dread but from genuine care, manifesting conduct of body, speech, and mind that accords with beings' own needs, fitting their hearts and bringing them benefit and ease. 36. Milestone: Vast Compassion That Never Retaliates, and Equal Compassion Extending Without Limit — Closing All Seven Aspects in Full

With this seventh aspect, the treatise concludes: a bodhisattva joined with these seven aspects of compassion is thereby called possessed of wholesome aspiration, or utterly wholesome aspiration — wholesome aspiration at the stage prior to the noble path, and utterly wholesome aspiration once the noble grounds, all the way through the tenth and most ultimate, have been reached — closing the first of chapter three's three topics, the seven aspects of compassion, entirely in full.

Commentary: Regarding "toward these very same sentient beings, hold no attached, craving mind" — this means that the wish to give beings benefit and ease does not arise from any attachment or craving in the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the sixth aspiration as a bodhisattva's wish to teach beings the classical formula "refrain from every evil, undertake every good, purify one's own mind — this is what every buddha teaches," guiding beings out of unwholesome ground and into wholesome ground through the refuges, the five precepts, and the eight precepts, gradually leading them toward the noble path itself. He explains the seventh as a bodhisattva's wish to relieve beings of poverty, want, and the helplessness of having no one to depend on, providing whatever material support actually eases their present suffering.

He explains the eighth aspiration as the crucial qualification binding the sixth and seventh together properly: a bodhisattva who gives beings both wholesome teaching and material ease does so without any attachment clinging to the beings themselves, and without regard for whether those beings ever cause the bodhisattva harm or hardship in return, and equally without any mind fixed on some pleasant karmic reward the giving itself might eventually produce — genuine liberation from both objects of attachment, the beings helped and the fruit of helping them. Without this eighth aspiration, he notes, even the sixth and seventh, however outwardly generous, could still remain subtly tainted by self-interest; only together do all three describe a bodhisattva's genuinely free and complete generosity. The treatise turns next to the ninth and tenth aspirations, firmness of resolve and freedom from distorted wisdom.
