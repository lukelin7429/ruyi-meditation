The Embrace of the Elements Begins

Fascicle 96 opens the embrace of the elements (界攝), the seventh and final gate of the dependent-origination/food/truths/elements section (緣起食諦界擇攝), following directly upon the truths' embrace closed entirely in fascicle 95. Its own general summary verse names three matters — general meaning and the rest, radiance and the rest, and feeling and the rest, the last split into two further verses — while its first particular verse names four: general meaning, distinct kinds, similar turning, and the three later seekings. This fascicle opens with general meaning (the two kinds of elements — abiding-nature and habituation-increased), distinct kinds (the eighteen elements' innumerable sub-varieties), and similar turning (how sentient beings of like disposition, wholesome or unwholesome, gravitate toward and reinforce one another).

3. Section: The Long-Form Explanation Begins — General Meaning: Marking and Listing

One should know that the elements are, briefly, of two kinds. First, elements abiding in their intrinsic nature; second, elements increased through habituation.

Chinese Source

當知諸界略有二種。一、住自性界，二、習增長界。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that the elements are, briefly, of two kinds. First, elements abiding in their intrinsic nature; second, elements increased through habituation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the long-form explanation (長行釋), the second of the general-meaning verse's four matters, beginning with general meaning (總義), the first, beginning with marking and listing (標列) — an element abiding in its intrinsic nature is a naturally-so seed; an element increased through habituation is one built up through repeated conditioning. "Element" (界, dhātu) is elsewhere explained as the cause of all dharmas — the eighteen elements of six sense-faculties, six objects, and six consciousnesses — or as a boundary (as when a affliction is bounded to the desire, form, or formless realm), or as a category, or, in its oldest sense, as an intrinsic characteristic that never dissolves, hence also rendered "that which sustains" — as water sustains water's nature and fire sustains fire's, so earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness together constitute every sentient being's own basis, and so "element" is also called "causal nature," since different causes give rise to different realms of existence, just as a human's realm of existence differs from an animal's.

4. Section: Explaining Each in Turn Begins — Elements Abiding in Their Intrinsic Nature

Elements abiding in their intrinsic nature means: the eighteen elements, falling within one's own continuum, each having its own determined, distinct seed.

Chinese Source

住自性界者，謂十八界，墮自相續，各各決定差別種子。

Ru-Yi Translation

Elements abiding in their intrinsic nature means: the eighteen elements, falling within one's own continuum, each having its own determined, distinct seed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining each in turn (隨釋), beginning with elements abiding in their intrinsic nature (住自性界) — the six sense-faculties, six objects, and six consciousnesses each continue seed-to-seed within one's own continuum, each distinct from the others.

5. Section: Elements Increased Through Habituation, Closing "Explaining Each in Turn" and "General Meaning" Entirely

Elements increased through habituation means: dharmas, whether wholesome or unwholesome, that have already been repeatedly practiced in a former life, causing them to currently manifest — so that, at present, their seeds are vigorous and strong, attached to and continuing within the mental continuum. By this cause, upon meeting even a small condition, they are immediately able to manifest, and are certainly not to be altered.

Chinese Source

習增長界者，謂則諸法或是其善，或是不善，於餘生中先已數習，令彼現行，故於今時種子彊盛，依附相續。由是為因，暫遇小緣便能現起，定不可轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Elements increased through habituation means: dharmas, whether wholesome or unwholesome, that have already been repeatedly practiced in a former life, causing them to currently manifest — so that, at present, their seeds are vigorous and strong, attached to and continuing within the mental continuum. By this cause, upon meeting even a small condition, they are immediately able to manifest, and are certainly not to be altered.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

With this, elements increased through habituation (習增長界) closes entirely, closing explaining each in turn (隨釋2: 住自性界/習增長界) entirely, closing general meaning (總義2: 標列/隨釋) entirely — a person naturally skilled at something without ever having studied it usually carries strong habituated seeds from past lives; per the 披尋記, "certainly not to be altered" means there is no power to transform such a seed and prevent its manifestation once conditions ripen. Whether a person becomes contaminated by bad company or resists it despite exposure to it depends on which seeds, wholesome or unwholesome, already run stronger within them — the surrounding environment is not itself the deciding cause.

6. Section: Distinct Kinds Begins — General Marking

Next, to speak of the essential point: although the elements' kinds number eighteen as can be obtained, yet, because each single element's karmic destiny and the sentient beings within it have distinct varieties of many different sorts, one should know they are truly innumerable.

Chinese Source

復次，以要言之，雖界種類十八可得，然一一界業趣、有情，種種品類有差別故，當知無量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, to speak of the essential point: although the elements' kinds number eighteen as can be obtained, yet, because each single element's karmic destiny and the sentient beings within it have distinct varieties of many different sorts, one should know they are truly innumerable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens distinct kinds (自類別), the second of the general-meaning verse's four matters, explaining that each of the eighteen elements itself contains distinct varieties, beginning with general marking (總標) — briefly, the elements number eighteen; but told in full, since beings' karmic destinies vary so widely, their true number is beyond counting.

7. Section: Raising a Simile

It is like a great cluster of akṣa seeds in the world — within this cluster there are many varieties; though the kind is spoken of as one, it is truly innumerable.

Chinese Source

譬如世間大惡叉聚，於此聚中有多品類，種類一故，雖說為一，而有無量。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like a great cluster of akṣa seeds in the world — within this cluster there are many varieties; though the kind is spoken of as one, it is truly innumerable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens raising a simile (舉喻), the second — the akṣa (惡叉) is a tree whose fruit grows in clusters of three seeds fused at a single base, falling to the ground still clustered together, and is a stock simile in the treatises for interdependent afflictions, karma, and suffering, or simply for great multiplicity; though called one cluster, or one kind, it in fact contains countless individual seeds.

8. Section: Matching the Simile to the Dharma, Closing "Distinct Kinds" Entirely

Thus, within each and every element, each has innumerable, distinct varieties; because the kind is one, though each is spoken of as one, it is in truth innumerable.

Chinese Source

如是於其一一界中，各有無量品類差別，種類一故，雖各說一，而實無量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, within each and every element, each has innumerable, distinct varieties; because the kind is one, though each is spoken of as one, it is in truth innumerable.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing a companion sūtra on innumerable elements

With this, matching the simile to the dharma (合法) closes entirely, closing distinct kinds (自類別3: 總標/舉喻/合法) entirely — the eye-element, though spoken of as one single element shared by all who possess an eye, in fact varies innumerably: one person's eyesight is sharp, another's dim; an eagle's eye differs entirely from a human's, in both appearance and visual power. Every one of the eighteen elements is like this — nominally one kind, in truth innumerable varieties.

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a companion sūtra teaching that the elements' number is truly innumerable, illustrated by a ball of eye-ointment a league deep and wide: however swiftly one tried to portion this ointment out, one measure to each element, the elements themselves would run out of ointment to receive, since each element in turn contains its own innumerable sub-elements without end — taking the eye-element alone, human eyes already differ innumerably from one another, let alone across every other kind of being. Therefore, the sūtra concludes, monks should skillfully know the elements are innumerable, and should train themselves to cultivate every kind of wholesome element and skillfully penetrate every kind of element there is.

9. Section: Similar Turning Begins — Marking the Distinction

Next, these elements are gathered and formed by the power of decisive understanding. A former evil decisive understanding gathers and forms an evil element; a former good decisive understanding gathers and forms a good element.

Chinese Source

復次，如是諸界，由勝解力之所集成。先惡勝解，集成惡界；先善勝解，集成善界。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, these elements are gathered and formed by the power of decisive understanding. A former evil decisive understanding gathers and forms an evil element; a former good decisive understanding gathers and forms a good element.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens similar turning (似轉), the third of the general-meaning verse's four matters, explaining how similar elements arise and reinforce one another, beginning with marking the distinction (標差別) — the seeds stored in the store-consciousness are themselves gathered through the power of decisive understanding built up in past lives: repeated evil understanding forms an evil element, repeated good understanding forms a good element, and this in turn shapes what a person is naturally drawn toward now.

10. Section: Clarifying Similar Turning Begins — Marking

Following whatever has been gathered and formed, one still turns together with sentient beings similar to oneself, sharing the same dharma.

Chinese Source

隨所集成，還與如是相似有情同法而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Following whatever has been gathered and formed, one still turns together with sentient beings similar to oneself, sharing the same dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens clarifying similar turning (明似轉), the second, beginning with marking (標) — those who share wholesome dispositions naturally become friends, as renunciants gravitate toward fellow renunciants, and those uninterested in the Dharma gravitate toward others likewise uninterested; the same holds for those sharing unwholesome dispositions.

11. Section: Explaining, Closing "Clarifying Similar Turning" and "Similar Turning" Entirely

Namely, they travel back and forth together, gather together, dwell together, share the same views, and share a similar decisive understanding of intention. For this reason it is said: sentient beings' various elements mutually nourish one another and turn in similar fashion.

Chinese Source

謂相往來、同聚、同住、同見、同意勝解相似。由是故言：有情諸界共相滋潤、相似而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely, they travel back and forth together, gather together, dwell together, share the same views, and share a similar decisive understanding of intention. For this reason it is said: sentient beings' various elements mutually nourish one another and turn in similar fashion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing an extensive series of companion sūtras

With this, explaining (釋) closes entirely, closing clarifying similar turning (明似轉2: 標/釋) entirely, closing similar turning (似轉2: 標差別/明似轉) entirely — birds of a feather flock together: teachers form their own circles, physicians their own, merchants their own, and so it is with any shared wholesome or unwholesome disposition, mutually reinforcing whatever element already runs strong within each member.

Master Cháng Bǎi cites an extensive series of companion sūtras illustrating this teaching. One sūtra simply states that beings constantly turn together with their element — an unwholesome mind turns with the unwholesome element, a wholesome mind with the wholesome element, a superior mind (as in form-realm absorption) with the superior element, and a lesser mind (as in desire-realm attachment) with the lesser element. A companion verse-sūtra develops this further: those who delight in company delight in further company, and being separated from it, that bond breaks — like a person clutching a small piece of wood who plunges into the vast ocean, both person and wood sink together, exactly as one mired in laziness sinks together with laziness; one should therefore forsake laziness and every inferior form of exertion, since the noble ones never grow lax, abiding instead in solitude, diligently absorbed in meditation, crossing over the stream of birth-and-death. As glue and lacquer share their sticking nature, as fire blazes brighter fed by wind, as conch-shell and milk share one white color, so sentient beings gather with their matching element, reinforcing one another the more. A further sūtra, addressed to the assembly at Rājagṛha, illustrates this with the Buddha's own ten great disciples, each excelling in a different specialty and each naturally drawing to himself companions of the same bent: Kauṇḍinya's circle of senior, widely-learned elders long free of desire; Mahākāśyapa's circle of ascetic practitioners content with little, eating once a day and sleeping beneath trees; Śāriputra's circle famed for wisdom and eloquence; Mahāmaudgalyāyana's circle of those with great supernormal powers; Aniruddha's circle possessing the heavenly eye; Śroṇa Koṭikarṇa's circle of the vigorously diligent (Śroṇa himself, discouraged after fruitless austerities, was guided back to balanced practice through the Buddha's lute-string simile — neither too taut nor too slack — and thereby attained arhatship); Dravya-Mallaputra's circle of those glad to serve the community's material needs; Upāli's circle of vinaya specialists; Pūrṇa's circle of skilled Dharma-teachers; Mahākātyāyana's circle of doctrinal analysts; Ānanda's circle renowned for retentive memory; and Rāhula's circle of those who guard their practice quietly, without seeking recognition — set finally against Devadatta, whose own circle gathered around him precisely because they shared his taste for wrong views. The lesson drawn is that monks should watch closely which circle — the noble disciples', or Devadatta's — they themselves are drawn toward.

12. Section: The Three Seekings Begin — Seeking Along the Holy Life: Bringing Forth the Gradual Order of Training: Distinguishing the Mark Individually

Next, by the dominant power of seeking along the holy life, one is first said to give rise to faith; next, one turns toward receiving and studying the precepts; next, toward whatever faults currently manifest, one gives rise to shame by observing oneself and observing others; next, one cultivates wholesome dharmas without interruption, generating diligent effort, able to remain unforgetful toward what was long ago done and long ago spoken; relying on these two, the mind attains concentration; because the mind is concentrated, one attains wisdom in accordance with truth.

Chinese Source

復次，由梵行求增上力故，先說起信；次於尸羅受學而轉；次於現行所有過罪，觀自、觀他而生羞恥；次於善法無間修習，發勤精進，於久所作及久所說，能無忘失；是二為依，令心得定；由心定故，得如實智。

Ru-Yi Translation

Next, by the dominant power of seeking along the holy life, one is first said to give rise to faith; next, one turns toward receiving and studying the precepts; next, toward whatever faults currently manifest, one gives rise to shame by observing oneself and observing others; next, one cultivates wholesome dharmas without interruption, generating diligent effort, able to remain unforgetful toward what was long ago done and long ago spoken; relying on these two, the mind attains concentration; because the mind is concentrated, one attains wisdom in accordance with truth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the three seekings (三求), the fourth and final matter of the general-meaning verse, explaining how sentient beings in the world pursue one of three seekings — along the holy life, toward existence, or toward desire — beginning with seeking along the holy life (依梵行求), the first of three, explaining beings who seek the uncontaminated noble path, beginning with bringing forth the gradual order of training (出學漸次), the first, beginning with distinguishing the mark individually (別辨相) — faith in the Three Jewels leads to going forth and receiving precepts, then to shame at one's own faults, then to unforgetting diligence in wholesome cultivation, and, resting on precepts and mindfulness together, to concentration, and finally, resting on concentration, to wisdom in accordance with truth.

13. Section: Briefly Gathering the Meaning, Closing "Bringing Forth the Gradual Order of Training" Entirely

Thus, for the time being, it is said that, by the dominant power of faith, one gradually cultivates the three kinds of training. First, the training in superior precepts; second, the training in superior mind; third, the training in superior wisdom.

Chinese Source

如是且說信增上力，漸次修習三種所學。一、增上戒，二、增上心，三、增上慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, for the time being, it is said that, by the dominant power of faith, one gradually cultivates the three kinds of training. First, the training in superior precepts; second, the training in superior mind; third, the training in superior wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, briefly gathering the meaning (略攝義) closes entirely, closing bringing forth the gradual order of training (出學漸次2: 別辨相/略攝義) entirely — the entire gradual path just described reduces to the three trainings: precepts (abiding in the precepts, guarding the code of conduct, perfected demeanor), mind (the four form-realm absorptions and four formless attainments), and wisdom (directly seeing the four truths or the twelve links of dependent origination).

14. Section: Named "Fulfillment Attained"

Thus, the three trainings' superior provisions of the path are: worldly right view, delighting in generosity, being easy to support, easy to satisfy, having few desires, contentment, and the four means of attraction. The distinct meanings of "easy to support" and the rest are as already explained in their marks in the Śrāvaka Ground. One should know this is called seeking along the holy life having already attained fulfillment.

Chinese Source

如是三學勝資糧道，謂世正見、好行惠捨、易養、易滿、少欲、喜足及四攝事。其易養等句義差別，如聲聞地已說其相。如是當知名梵行求已得圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, the three trainings' superior provisions of the path are: worldly right view, delighting in generosity, being easy to support, easy to satisfy, having few desires, contentment, and the four means of attraction. The distinct meanings of "easy to support" and the rest are as already explained in their marks in the Śrāvaka Ground. One should know this is called seeking along the holy life having already attained fulfillment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and the Śrāvaka Ground

This opens named "fulfillment attained" (名得圓滿), the second of seeking along the holy life's three matters — worldly right view arises from mental attention in accord with the Buddha's own teaching; generosity, free of craving, aversion, and delusion, adorns and assists the mind and provisions the path toward calming and contemplation; being easy to support and easy to satisfy, having few desires, and contentment together constitute a renunciant's adornment; and the four means of attraction (giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and cooperation) round out the seven qualities. Per the 披尋記, these match the Śrāvaka Ground's own verse on a śramaṇa's adornment: sincere faith without flattery, little illness, diligence and wisdom, few desires and contentment, easy to support and easy to satisfy, ascetic virtue and dignity, knowing moderation as a good person's way, the marks of intelligence, and patience, gentleness, and goodness.

15. Section: Clarifying the Establishment of Elements Begins — Displaying the Distinction

One who accomplishes such a seeking along the holy life, in turn, together with the sentient beings of this same element, mutually nourishes and turns in similar fashion. One who departs from this element, in turn, together with beings who have departed from this element, mutually nourishes and turns in similar fashion.

Chinese Source

成就如是梵行求者，還與此界諸有情類共相滋潤、相似而轉。離此界者，還與遠離此界有情共相滋潤、相似而轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

One who accomplishes such a seeking along the holy life, in turn, together with the sentient beings of this same element, mutually nourishes and turns in similar fashion. One who departs from this element, in turn, together with beings who have departed from this element, mutually nourishes and turns in similar fashion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens clarifying the establishment of elements (明安立界), the third of seeking along the holy life's three matters, explaining that sentient beings constantly turn together with their element, gathering with like kind, beginning with displaying the distinction (顯差別) — practitioners of the holy life gather with fellow practitioners; those who abandon it gather with others who likewise abandon it.

16. Section: Explaining What Is Difficult, Closing "Clarifying the Establishment of Elements" and "Seeking Along the Holy Life" Entirely

One should know that, here, the fruit depends on the cause, not the cause on the fruit.

Chinese Source

當知此中，果依於因，非因依果故。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, here, the fruit depends on the cause, not the cause on the fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and an extensive series of twelve companion sūtras

With this, explaining what is difficult (隨難釋) closes entirely, closing clarifying the establishment of elements (明安立界2: 顯差別/隨難釋) entirely, closing **seeking along the holy life (依梵行求3: 出學漸次/名得圓滿/明安立界), the first of the three seekings, entirely**. Per the 披尋記, gathering with like-minded companions ("similar turning") is the fruit, and whether one has accomplished or abandoned the holy-life seeking is the cause — the fruit depends on the cause, not the reverse, meaning one does not first gather with companions and thereby come to seek or abandon the holy life.

Master Cháng Bǎi cites a companion sūtra called the "Elements in Harmony Sūtra," restating the same teaching for disbelief and its element, precept-breaking and its element, and shamelessness and its element, and a following series of eleven further companion sūtras applying the identical pattern to further pairs arising along the holy-life seeking's path: diligence versus non-diligence in wholesome cultivation, forgetfulness versus unforgetfulness toward what was long practiced and taught, absorption versus non-absorption in meditation, much versus little learning in cultivating the threefold training, stinginess versus generosity in cultivating giving, poor versus good wisdom in cultivating worldly right view, and, in cultivating a renunciant's adornment, difficulty versus ease of support, difficulty versus ease of satisfaction, much versus few desires, discontent versus contentment, and finally embracing versus not embracing others through the four means of attraction — each pair gathering its own matching element of companions.

17. Section: Seeking Toward Existence Begins — Distinguishing the Conditions

Because of the elements of the six bases, followed by the element of ignorance, differing in the condition they depend upon, various kinds of ignorance-contact arise; taking these various kinds of ignorance-contact as condition, various kinds of feeling produced by ignorance-contact arise; taking these various kinds of feeling produced by ignorance-contact as condition, craving conditioned by feeling produced by ignorance-contact arises; taking craving as condition, there is clinging — extensively said, up to the accumulation of the great mass of suffering.

Chinese Source

無明界所隨六處諸界為緣所依別故，起無明觸種種品類；其無明觸種種品類以為緣故，起無明觸所生諸受種種品類；其無明觸所生諸受種種品類以為緣故，起無明觸所生諸受為緣貪愛；愛為緣故，而有其取；廣說乃至大苦蘊集。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of the elements of the six bases, followed by the element of ignorance, differing in the condition they depend upon, various kinds of ignorance-contact arise; taking these various kinds of ignorance-contact as condition, various kinds of feeling produced by ignorance-contact arise; taking these various kinds of feeling produced by ignorance-contact as condition, craving conditioned by feeling produced by ignorance-contact arises; taking craving as condition, there is clinging — extensively said, up to the accumulation of the great mass of suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra

This opens seeking toward existence (依有求), the second of the three seekings, explaining beings who seek the form and formless realms' absorptions, beginning with distinguishing the conditions (辨為緣) — relishing meditative absorption's bliss and equanimity, still driven by the latent seed of ignorance, leads one to seek rebirth in the form or formless realms, tracing the same causal chain as dependent origination toward the great mass of suffering. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra's simile: on a perilous cliff overgrown with grass lies ambrosia that grants a thousand years of life and freedom from illness, yet the foolish, greedy for its taste, fail to notice the deep pit hidden just beneath and fall to their deaths, while the wise know to stay away entirely — however long a lifespan, so long as it remains within birth-and-death, it remains foul.

18. Section: Concluding the Establishment, Closing "Seeking Toward Existence" Entirely

One should know this is called establishing the elements based on seeking toward existence.

Chinese Source

當知是名依有求故，建立諸界。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know this is called establishing the elements based on seeking toward existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記

With this, concluding the establishment (結建立) closes entirely, closing seeking toward existence (依有求2: 辨為緣/結建立) entirely — per the 披尋記, the six bases' seeds are themselves followed by the seed of ignorance, and so the six bases become both ignorance's object and its basis, giving rise to ignorance-contact at each of the six sense-doors; lacking transcendent right view, ignorance pervades every link of dependent origination, and the cycle of birth-and-death continues unbroken.

19. Section: Seeking Toward Desire Begins — Distinguishing the Conditions

Per the 披尋記, consciousness's mind-attachment to the four stations of consciousness (form, feeling, perception, and formation) and its four kinds of clinging (to sense-desire, views, rites and vows, and the doctrine of self) are each categorically fixed at exactly four — never more, never fewer — precisely the kind of question the Buddha always answers decisively, never treating as indefinite. Master Cháng Bǎi closes this entire division with an extraordinary companion sūtra: newly-ordained Śāriputra teaches that a monk complete in precepts, concentration, and wisdom can enter and exit the cessation of feeling and perception in this very life, or, failing full liberation, will be reborn in a mind-made heaven and continue entering it there — a claim the elder Udāyin disputes three times over, mistaking Śāriputra's "mind-made heaven" for the formless realm's peak of existence (where no cessation attainment is possible), rather than the form-realm heaven Śāriputra actually meant. Unable to resolve the dispute among the assembly, Śāriputra brings the matter before the Buddha, who confirms Śāriputra's teaching, rebukes both Udāyin for his confusion and Ānanda for failing to intervene and support his elder, and, prompted by the monk Bhaddāli, has the assembly recite the five qualities that earn a senior monastic the community's genuine respect: pure precepts, broad learning of the three-fold teaching, mastery of the four absorptions, wisdom that sees worldly impermanence clearly, and the liberation of one who has exhausted every contaminant — concluding that Śāriputra himself embodies all five. The Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā-śāstra's own extended commentary on this same exchange, cited alongside, explains Udāyin's confusion as arising from his own narrower experience of an unretrogressing cessation attainment (contrasted with Śāriputra's account of a still-retrogressible one, requiring renewed cultivation after rebirth) and catalogues four distinct reasons for Ānanda's rebuke, closing this entire section of the treatise on a note of the Saṅgha's own collective wisdom, patiently working through disagreement to arrive together at the truth.
