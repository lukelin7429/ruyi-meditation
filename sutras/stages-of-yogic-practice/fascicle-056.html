Fascicle 56 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十六 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Continues

Neither Form Nor Mind, Yet Real Enough to Matter

The treatise turns to the twenty-four mind-dissociated formations — attainment, the two mindless meditative states, the life faculty, and the rest — phenomena that are neither form nor mind, yet still provisionally established upon their shifting relationship, before completing the entire skillful understanding of the aggregates and turning at last toward the elements, the sense-fields, and dependent origination.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 56 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Attainment Itself — Neither Form Nor Mind, Yet Established Upon Both

The treatise opens its second topic within this stage of analysis, the aggregates' own successive stages, revisiting the twenty-four mind-dissociated formations already introduced at length in an earlier fascicle, now consolidated together in a single, focused discussion. Asked how many kinds of stage constitute the aggregates, the treatise answers that there are many, beginning with attainment and the two mindless meditative absorptions, referring back to its own earlier, more extensive treatment. It then turns to attainment specifically, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the joint basis of cause, mastery, and actual manifestation — attainment being, in every case, neither a form phenomenon nor a mental one in its own right, but a provisional label placed upon the shifting relationship between a seed's own causal potential, the settled strength that potential has secured, and its own occasional manifestation in actual experience. The treatise names three specific kinds of attainment corresponding to these same three bases: attainment through seed, meaning simply that a given seed, not yet suppressed or severed, retains the potential to produce its own result at some future point; attainment through mastery, meaning that the causal conditions capable of bringing some quality into being have secured sufficient, unobstructed strength to do so; and attainment through manifestation, meaning that some phenomenon's own distinctive character is presently, actively appearing. The lecture notes that this same concept of "attainment," used by non-Mahāyāna schools lacking any doctrine of seeds to explain how a quality once gained is not simply lost, is refined here into something considerably broader, spanning both newly secured and long-standing qualities alike, precisely because it is grounded in the storehouse consciousness's own seeds rather than treated as some free-floating, independently real force.

It is further of three kinds: attainment through seed, attainment through mastery, and attainment through manifestation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening return to the twenty-four mind-dissociated factors as a deliberate act of consolidation rather than mere repetition — the earlier fascicle introduced these same phenomena scattered among a broader discussion of provisionally established categories, while this fascicle gathers them together for their own dedicated, systematic treatment, attainment itself serving as the natural first example of a phenomenon that is neither form nor mind, yet still meaningfully real enough to explain why a cultivated quality persists rather than evaporating the instant attention moves elsewhere. Having established what attainment actually is, he notes, the treatise turns to two considerably more dramatic examples of the same basic principle — the mindless meditative absorption and the deeper cessation attainment, both provisionally established upon the temporary halting of ordinary mental activity itself.

2. Section: The Mindless Attainment — Ordinary Beings Mistaking Silence for Liberation

The treatise turns to the mindless meditative attainment, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the specific stage in which a practitioner has already overcome craving for the third absorption's own refined equanimity, yet has not yet overcome craving for the fourth absorption above it, and who, weary of mental activity itself, deliberately brings the six operating consciousnesses to a halt. Regarding mental activity as a kind of sickness, an abscess, or a dart, and imagining stillness itself to be a wondrous, peaceful refuge, such a practitioner forms the deliberate resolve to obstruct all thought for a day, seven days, an eon, or more, and through this same aversion toward thought, gradually brings the six consciousnesses' own activity from coarse to increasingly subtle until, at the very threshold of full absorption, they cease outright — this cessation of the first six consciousnesses' own activity is what the mindless attainment is established upon. The treatise then names three further distinctions: its own essential nature is invariably wholesome, since it arises specifically through deliberately cultivated, effortful wholesome intention grounded in the fourth absorption; the beings who cultivate it are always ordinary, unenlightened practitioners, mistakenly believing this attainment to be nirvāṇa itself rather than the deeper, genuinely liberating cessation it merely resembles; and its result is that a practitioner who cultivates it in the desire realm is subsequently reborn in the form realm's own fourth absorption, specifically in the heaven belonging to those without perception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the mindless attainment's own defining error — mistaking the mere absence of mental activity for genuine liberation — as a cautionary tale worth returning to whenever meditation itself starts to feel like the goal rather than the means: silencing thought is not the same as severing affliction at its root, and a practitioner who confuses the two, however deep their concentration, remains, in this treatise's own account, still an ordinary, unenlightened being rather than a liberated one. Having explained this first mindless attainment, cultivated only by the mistaken, he notes, the treatise turns to its far more advanced counterpart — the cessation attainment, reserved specifically for noble ones who have already secured genuine liberating insight.

3. Section: Cessation Attainment — Reserved for the Noble, and a Debate Over Whether It Needs a Body at All

The treatise turns to the cessation attainment, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the specific stage in which a practitioner has already overcome craving for the sphere of nothingness, and, wearied even by the subtle activity of feeling and perception that remains, deliberately brings this same activity to a halt. Unlike the mindless attainment, the treatise specifies that those capable of cultivating cessation attainment belong exclusively to the continuity of noble ones, spanning both learners who have already secured the eight liberations and the arhat who has secured liberation in both respects — ordinary beings, however accomplished their concentration, remain entirely incapable of it. Regarding how cessation attainment actually arises, the treatise records a genuine doctrinal disagreement reflecting two different stages of the tradition's own development: according to teachings that had not yet established the doctrine of the storehouse consciousness, a practitioner must first cultivate this attainment in the desire realm and then manifest it again in the form realm, since mind and its mental factors, once suspended, still require some physical basis to depend upon, without which the very continuity of a living being would appear to be severed altogether — a concern this same earlier teaching addresses by insisting cessation attainment can only actually manifest where a physical body is present. Once the storehouse consciousness's own doctrine has been established, however, the treatise notes, this same attainment may manifest anywhere at all, including the formless realm, since the storehouse consciousness itself, holding every phenomenon's own seed, never actually departs even where no physical form remains, removing any risk that a being's continuity might be broken by cessation attainment's own temporary suspension of ordinary mental activity.

Once this doctrine is established, it may manifest anywhere at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this candid acknowledgment of two competing explanations for how cessation attainment actually manifests as a rare and valuable window into the tradition's own historical development — earlier Buddhist schools, lacking the storehouse consciousness's own doctrine, genuinely worried that a being's continuity might snap entirely once ordinary mental activity stopped without any accompanying physical form to anchor it, a worry the Yogācāra tradition's own signature innovation, the storehouse consciousness, was specifically developed to resolve. He treats the sheer rarity of this attainment — reserved only for the most advanced noble ones, unlike the mindless attainment any sufficiently deluded ordinary being can reach — as this passage's own quiet reminder that outward stillness alone proves nothing about a practitioner's actual spiritual attainment, since the very same silence can be either a beginner's mistake or an arhat's own hard-won accomplishment depending entirely on what understanding underlies it. Having explained both mindless meditative states, he notes, the treatise turns to the third, related phenomenon — the mindless heaven itself, the actual rebirth destination such practice, if mistaken for liberation, eventually leads to.

4. Section: The Mindless Heaven Itself — A Rebirth Destination, Purely Neutral, Reserved for the Mistaken

The treatise turns to the mindless heaven itself, the actual rebirth destination the mindless attainment leads to, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the very stage already reached by beings already born into that heaven, where the first six consciousnesses' own activity has ceased entirely. Unlike the mindless attainment that leads to it, whose own nature is invariably wholesome, the resulting heavenly rebirth's own nature is neutral, being simply karma's automatic, unobscured result rather than an actively cultivated wholesome state in its own right. Those born there belong exclusively to the continuity of ordinary, unenlightened beings, since no noble one, understanding this state for what it actually is, would ever mistake it for genuine liberation and aim toward it. And its own characteristic unfolding is this: a being who has cultivated the volition capable of generating this same attainment experiences its karmic result upon rebirth there, remaining without perception for the great majority of an extraordinarily long lifespan, yet with perception present during a brief span both immediately after birth and immediately before death — this brief return of perception at the very end being precisely what causes such a being to pass away from that same heavenly state at last.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp contrast between the mindless attainment's own wholesome nature and the mindless heaven's own neutral nature as a precise illustration of how cause and effect can differ in moral quality: the deliberate cultivation that leads there is genuinely wholesome effort, however misguided its underlying goal, while the resulting rebirth itself is simply karma's automatic unfolding, carrying no further moral charge of its own. He notes the poignant detail that perception's own brief return at the very end of this extraordinarily long lifespan is what actually ends it — a being who spent nearly the entire span unconscious of anything at all finds thought itself returning only to immediately precipitate their own departure from that same state, a fittingly ironic close to an existence built entirely on avoiding thought in the first place. Having completed the mindless heaven, he notes, the treatise turns to the third of the twenty-four mind-dissociated factors — the life faculty itself, the provisional label placed upon the sheer duration a given karmic result is fated to last.

5. Section: The Life Faculty — Fixed or Uncertain, Welcome or Unwelcome, Counted in Years or in Eons

The treatise turns to the life faculty, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the settled duration karma itself has already determined a given karmic result will last — the life faculty being simply this same fixed span of time, provisionally treated as a distinct phenomenon. It names three further distinctions. Some lifespans are fixed and others are not: beings in the northern continent of Uttarakuru, for instance, live exactly a thousand years without exception, while beings in the southern continent — this present human world — may live anywhere from a fleeting, unformed existence in the womb up to extraordinarily long spans, with no fixed measure at all, subject to premature death at any point along the way. Some lifespans are desirable and others are not, according to which of the five destinies a being is reborn into — human and heavenly rebirth counting as desirable, the three lower realms as undesirable. And lifespans are measured in different units depending on the realm: desire-realm beings, human and heavenly alike, count their lives in years, while form- and formless-realm beings count theirs in eons instead — the lecture noting further, more technical distinctions still, such as whether a given lifespan has any outer limit at all, as an ordinary being's does, or, like an arhat's or advanced bodhisattva's, can be extended through the practitioner's own spiritual power beyond what karma alone would have determined.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the contrast between Uttarakuru's own fixed thousand-year lifespan and the human world's own wildly variable one — from a lifetime cut short in the womb to extraordinary old age — as a pointed reminder of just how much uncertainty ordinary human life actually involves compared to other realms of rebirth, a fact worth sitting with rather than taking one's own continued existence for granted. He notes that the life faculty's own provisional status — a mere label placed on a duration rather than any independently findable substance — fits the same pattern already established for attainment and the two mindless states: none of these twenty-four factors names a genuine, freestanding reality, only a useful way of speaking about how form and mind actually behave over time. Having explained the life faculty, he notes, the treatise turns to the fourth mind-dissociated factor — species-similarity, the shared characteristics that let beings of the same general kind recognize one another as belonging together.

6. Section: Species-Similarity, and Three Faces of Arising — Instant by Instant, Life to Life, Mood to Mood

The treatise turns to species-similarity, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon whatever shared resemblance links different beings together — the provisional recognition that certain individuals belong to the same broader category. It names three kinds: similarity of category, meaning the shared characteristics linking beings born within the same realm or destiny, such as belonging equally to the desire realm or equally to the animal realm; similarity of essential character, meaning a finer-grained resemblance in condition, temperament, appearance, form, or voice; and similarity through shared trade or livelihood, meaning the resemblance linking beings who practice the same profession or skill, such as fellow teachers, physicians, or craftsmen. The treatise then turns to arising itself, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the present moment in which a given phenomenon actually manifests. It names three kinds of arising: momentary arising, the ceaseless, instant-by-instant manifestation of every conditioned phenomenon whatsoever; continuous arising, the ongoing transition from one being's own life, once its span concludes, into the next; and arising through distinct stages, itself of two further kinds — growth-arising, the gradual unfolding from infancy through advancing age, and mental arising, the constantly shifting parade of mental states a being passes through moment to moment, sometimes accompanied by pleasant feeling, sometimes by painful, sometimes by neither, sometimes marked by craving, sometimes by its absence, extending all the way to the difference between a mind genuinely liberated and one still unliberated.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats species-similarity's own threefold breakdown — category, essential character, and shared trade — as capturing three genuinely different ways beings actually recognize kinship with one another, from the broadest possible grouping by realm of rebirth down to the narrowest, most specific bond of sharing the same daily profession. He notes that mental arising's own extension all the way to the difference between a liberated and unliberated mind is a quiet reminder that even the deepest spiritual transformation a being undergoes is still, technically, just one more instance of this same basic phenomenon — arising, moment by moment, like everything else. Having explained species-similarity and arising, he notes, the treatise turns to a phenomenon inseparable from arising's own opposite number — aging, illustrated through one of this fascicle's most consoling teachings on how the body may suffer even while the mind remains genuinely untroubled.

7. Section: Aging in Three Faces — And Why the Body May Suffer While the Mind Need Not

The treatise turns to aging, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the difference between an earlier and a later stage of the very same continuity. It names three kinds: aging through changed character, the moment-to-moment alteration every conditioned phenomenon undergoes, giving rise to bodily aging, mental aging, aging of the lifespan itself, aging through decay, and aging through the transformation of one's own being; aging through transformation, the more dramatic shift a being undergoes across an entire lifetime or across rebirths, as when a being who dies in a fortunate realm is reborn instead into one of the three lower realms, the aggregates themselves utterly transformed; and aging through diminished capacity, the gradual decline of the sense faculties' own ability to engage their objects, manifesting as graying hair, wrinkled skin, diminished vitality, frequent illness, a stooped spine, labored breathing, a bent frame, dependence on a cane, dulled memory and judgment, and the general breakdown of every faculty's own former function. The lecture illustrates this teaching with a story from the Ekottara Āgama: an elderly, ailing layman once approached the Buddha seeking guidance, and the Buddha told him simply that old age inevitably brings illness — birth, aging, sickness, and death are unavoidable for everyone — but that a genuine practitioner can learn to suffer in body while remaining untroubled in mind. Not fully understanding this teaching, the layman sought out Śāriputra, who explained more fully: the body, like feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, is impermanent and constantly breaking down regardless, this being simply how the four elements naturally behave — but so long as one does not cling to this same impermanent body and mind as a genuine, fixed self or as something belonging to oneself, no suffering actually follows from witnessing its own inevitable decline, exactly as clouds must eventually become rain, one transient form simply becoming another. A mind that has genuinely realized selflessness, the lecture concludes, need not age or suffer even while the body it inhabits visibly does, since suffering was never a necessary consequence of aging itself, only of clinging to what was never truly one's own to begin with.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own deceptively simple teaching to the elderly layman — that the body inevitably suffers while the mind need not — as one of this entire treatise's most practically valuable insights, precisely because it requires no advanced meditative attainment to begin applying, only a genuine, sustained willingness to stop identifying the aggregates' own inevitable decline with "myself" or "my own." He notes that Śāriputra's own fuller explanation grounds this teaching directly in the selflessness doctrine this treatise has returned to repeatedly throughout its analysis of the aggregates — the body's aging is simply the four elements behaving exactly as they always do, and it is only the added layer of self-clinging, not the physical process itself, that actually generates suffering. Having explained aging in its three forms, he notes, the treatise turns to abiding, the temporary persistence any phenomenon shows before it too eventually passes away.

8. Section: Abiding and Impermanence — Momentary Persistence, and Three Ways Everything Eventually Falls Apart

The treatise turns to abiding, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the very same moment of arising already discussed, since abiding simply names conditioned phenomena's own brief persistence immediately following that moment. It names three kinds: momentary abiding, the instant-by-instant persistence belonging to any already-arisen phenomenon; continuous abiding, the ongoing sustaining of a being's own five aggregates through nourishment, or the physical world's own persistence across the long stretch of time before a cosmic age finally begins to decline; and abiding through established convention, meaning the laws, systems, and institutions different peoples and regions establish to secure order and stability for their own communities. The treatise then turns to impermanence itself, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the stage in which a phenomenon, having already arisen, subsequently breaks apart and ceases. It names three kinds: impermanence through outright destruction, the plain cessation any conditioned phenomenon undergoes the instant after it arises; impermanence through transformation, the alteration by which a once-pleasing state turns into a displeasing one, whether through the body's own many internal changes or the physical world's own many external ones; and impermanence through separation, the loss of wealth, status, or the people one loves, each of these three together exhausting the ways in which everything that arises must, sooner or later, also come apart.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sequence — arising, abiding, aging, and now impermanence — as together tracing the same four-phase cycle any conditioned phenomenon inevitably passes through, each phase provisionally established as its own distinct factor purely for the sake of careful analysis, even though, taken together, they describe nothing more than a single continuous, ever-changing process. He notes that impermanence through separation — losing wealth, status, or loved ones — connects this abstract philosophical category directly back to lived human experience, the very same losses that provoke genuine grief being simply this treatise's own carefully named third variety of a universal law nothing whatsoever escapes. Having completed this four-phase cycle of arising, abiding, aging, and impermanence, he notes, the treatise turns to a rather different kind of mind-dissociated factor — names, phrases, and syllables themselves, the building blocks of language through which all of this analysis has been expressed in the first place.

9. Section: Names, Phrases, and Syllables — And Why a Raised Eyebrow Can Teach the Dharma Too

The treatise turns to names, phrases, and syllables together, asking upon what basis a name-group is established, and answering: upon whatever provisional linguistic convention allows a word to designate something. It names three kinds: provisional names, such as designating a body as "I" or "a being," or designating some object as "a vase" or "a robe," none of these labels corresponding to any independently real substance; substantive names, such as designating the actual sense faculties and their objects — eye, ear, form, sound — categories genuinely grounded in dependently arisen phenomena; and names either commonly understood or not commonly understood by ordinary people, some conventions being widely shared and others obscure. Phrases and syllables, the treatise explains, follow this same threefold pattern exactly, each likewise divisible into provisional, substantive, and more-or-less commonly understood forms, differing only in scale — a phrase being a complete statement rather than a single word, and a syllable being the individual sound or written character phrases and names are themselves built from. The treatise adds a further distinction: phrases divide into declarative phrases, which simply state some quality, and explanatory phrases, which elaborate on that same quality's own meaning, while syllables divide into those grasped through sound and those grasped through written form. The lecture notes that this entire threefold linguistic system is itself only provisionally grounded in whichever sense object a given world's beings happen to perceive most keenly — in this present world, where sight, hearing, and thought are sharpest, names and phrases are built primarily from visible form, sound, and mental images, but in other buddha-lands, beings with especially keen smell, taste, or touch might just as validly build their own entire language from fragrance, flavor, or texture instead, since ultimately any of the six sense objects, skillfully employed, can serve as a vehicle for teaching — a raised eyebrow, a smile while holding up a flower, a sigh, a cough, or simply a remembered image can all convey the Dharma every bit as effectively as spoken words or written characters, teaching never being strictly confined to language in the first place.

Having explained the linguistic building blocks through which every other teaching in this treatise has itself been expressed, he notes, the treatise turns to the tenth mind-dissociated factor — ordinary-being status itself, the provisional label marking anyone who has not yet directly realized the supramundane path.

10. Section: Ordinary-Being Status Across Three Realms, and Continuity's Forward and Reverse Flow

The treatise turns to ordinary-being status, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the stage in which every supramundane, noble quality remains as yet unrealized. It names three kinds, corresponding to the three realms: ordinary-being status bound to the desire realm, to the form realm, and to the formless realm respectively — since a being still bound by any one of the three realms' own afflictions, whichever realm it happens to inhabit, remains, by that very fact, an ordinary being rather than a noble one. The treatise then turns to continuity, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the uninterrupted succession of cause and effect. It names three kinds: momentary continuity, the ceaseless succession of arising and ceasing within a single mental stream; continuity through rebirth, an ordinary being's own uninterrupted passage back and forth through the three realms, life after life; and continuity through defilement and purification, the ongoing succession of defiled and pure states of mind alternating within a single being's own experience — the lecture connecting this last kind directly to the twelve links of dependent origination, forward continuity following the defiled direction from ignorance through formations all the way to aging and death, exactly as this condition existing gives rise to that one, and reverse continuity following the purifying direction instead, ignorance's own cessation bringing the cessation of formations, all the way to the cessation of aging and death, exactly as this condition ceasing brings that one's own cessation as well.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats continuity's own forward-and-reverse structure, mapped directly onto dependent origination's own twelve links, as this treatise's own reminder that the very same causal chain binding a being to saṃsāra can, once properly understood and reversed, become the exact mechanism of that being's own liberation — nothing extra needs to be added to the process, only the same links traced backward, each cause's own cessation bringing its effect's cessation in turn. Having explained ordinary-being status and continuity, he notes, the treatise turns to a further pair of factors — determinate distinction, ensuring cause and effect never blur into one another, and correspondence, the fitting match between a cause and its own proper effect.

11. Section: Cause Never Blurs With Effect — Determinate Distinction and the Beginning of Correspondence

The treatise turns to determinate distinction, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the fact that each phenomenon possesses its own distinct character, never blurring into confusion with any other. It names three kinds: distinction of characteristic, meaning that cause and effect always retain their own separate, non-interchangeable identities — a wholesome cause always yielding a pleasant result, an unwholesome cause always yielding a painful one, the two never actually merging into one another, exactly as the truths of suffering and origin, describing saṃsāra's own forward flow, remain forever distinct from the truths of cessation and the path, describing liberation's own reverse flow, or as the twelve sense-fields each retain their own separate identity, or as the three kinds of feeling, or the varying lifespans and dimensions of different realms of rebirth, never actually collapsing into one another despite their shared status as conditioned phenomena; distinction of cause, meaning that wholesome and unwholesome karma remain forever distinct in their own causal power, wholesome karma alone capable of yielding a favorable rebirth, unwholesome karma alone capable of yielding an unfavorable one; and distinction of effect, meaning that painful and pleasant results themselves remain forever distinct, painful results always accompanied by painful sensation, pleasant results always accompanied by pleasant sensation, cause and effect corresponding reliably rather than randomly. The treatise then opens a further factor, correspondence, asking upon what basis it is established — the fitting match ensuring a given cause reliably brings about its own proper effect rather than some other, unrelated one.

Having established why cause and effect never actually merge, he notes, the treatise turns to correspondence itself — the equally important guarantee that a cause, once distinct from its effect, still actually connects to that effect in a reliable, fitting way rather than producing results at random.

12. Section: Correspondence's Three Faces, and Three Speeds — From a Single Thought to a Buddha's Own Reach

The treatise closes correspondence, explaining that it is established upon the fitting match between cause and effect, itself of three kinds: correspondence through combination, meaning simply that wholesome causes reliably yield pleasant results and unwholesome causes reliably yield painful ones; correspondence through skillful means, meaning the fitting relationship between preparatory practice — reciting scripture, prostrating, cultivating the foundations of mindfulness — and the deeper realization such practice is meant to prepare the way for; and correspondence through conformity with principle, meaning wisdom's own fitting alignment with the four classical modes of reasoning a phenomenon can be properly understood through. The treatise then turns to speed, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the swift, unceasing flow that both form and mind display without exception. It names three kinds: the speed of conditioned phenomena themselves, referring to the traditional teaching that a single finger-snap already contains six hundred arisings and ceasings, every conditioned thing changing far too quickly for ordinary perception to track; the speed of embodied beings, referring to how swiftly humans, elephants, horses, birds, and especially yakṣas and gods can move from place to place; and the speed of supernatural power, referring to the tradition's own account of a being capable of reaching the highest heaven in the time it takes to bend and extend an arm.

13. Section: Sequence From Sunrise to Sunset, and Time Itself — Relative Even Among the Heavens

The treatise turns to sequence, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the orderly succession any given process of conditioned arising displays. It names three kinds: momentary sequence, the strict, uninterrupted order in which each successive mental moment follows the one before it; the sequence of bodily activity, meaning the customary order of a day's own routine — for a layperson, waking, washing, dressing, working, bathing, and resting in turn; for a monastic, arranging one's robes, entering the village for alms, eating properly, washing the bowl and feet, retiring to a quiet cell to recite scripture and reflect, sitting in meditation through the day, resting during the deepest hours of night, and rising again before dawn to resume walking meditation and further reflection; and the sequence by which the physical universe itself was originally arranged at the very start of a cosmic age. The treatise then turns to time, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the uninterrupted succession of cause and effect itself, time being nothing more than a provisional label placed on this same succession — what has already arisen and ceased is called the past, what has not yet arisen is called the future, and what has arisen but not yet ceased is called the present. It names three kinds accordingly: past, future, and present, the lecture noting that time itself, far from being any fixed, independent measure, is entirely relative — a single day and night for humans lasts twenty-four hours, yet the very same single day and night for the Heaven of the Four Great Kings equals fifty human years, and for the Heaven of the Thirty-Three equals a full hundred, proof that "time" names nothing more than this same relative succession of cause and effect, provisionally measured differently depending on where one happens to stand within it.

14. Section: Place in Three Directions, and Number Built From Nothing but Comparison

The treatise turns to place, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the position occupied by whichever internal or external forms are actually being considered. It names three kinds: above, below, and to the side — the lecture noting that "to the side" here loosely covers every intermediate direction as well, east, west, south, north, and the points between, however finely such directions might otherwise be subdivided. The treatise then turns to number, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon whatever measure allows a phenomenon's own quantity to be expressed and understood. It names three kinds: the singular, the dual, and the plural. The lecture explains that number itself arises entirely through relative comparison among phenomena — one thing simply being taller than another, one day simply hotter than the previous one, one distance simply farther than a shorter one — this same comparative relationship being what actually generates the entire counting system, from the simplest single digits through the traditional "lower," "middle," and "higher" systems of enumeration, culminating in India's own ten traditional terms for numbers beyond ordinary reckoning altogether, among them the immeasurable, the boundless, the incomparable, the uncountable, and the inconceivable — vast quantities named specifically to indicate they exceed what any ordinary counting system could actually express.

15. Section: Union and Disunion Close the List — Twenty-Four Factors That Are Neither Form Nor Mind, Fully Explained

The treatise turns to union, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the stage in which every necessary supporting condition for a given undertaking is complete, without a single one lacking. It names three kinds: union through gathering, meaning simply people or conditions assembling together in one place, as students gathering to study this very treatise; union through shared purpose, meaning multiple minds and mental factors converging on the very same object, as an entire class attending to the same teaching together; and union through completion, meaning an undertaking reaching its own final, successful conclusion, as a house, once built, stands finished. The treatise then turns to disunion, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering simply: the exact reverse of union — wherever a necessary supporting condition is lacking, disunion is established instead, itself likewise of three kinds, mirroring union's own three in reverse: failure to gather, failure to share a common object, and failure to reach completion. The lecture notes the practical stakes of this final pair directly: a household that achieves genuine union flourishes, while one riven by disunion falls apart, and a monastic community that achieves genuine union sustains the teaching itself for the benefit of all, while one riven by disunion allows the genuine teaching to fade and corrupted teachings to flourish in its place. With this, the treatise concludes its entire survey of the twenty-four mind-dissociated formations — from attainment and the two mindless meditative states through the life faculty, species-similarity, arising, aging, abiding, impermanence, the linguistic units of names, phrases, and syllables, ordinary-being status, continuity, determinate distinction, correspondence, speed, sequence, time, place, and number, concluding now with union and disunion — bringing the second of four topics within this stage of analysis to its complete close, and the treatise turns to its third topic: an extensive, twenty-nine-angle examination distinguishing exactly which of the five aggregates possess which properties.

16. Section: Twenty-Nine Angles Begin — Which Aggregate Has Form, and Which Portion Can Actually Be Seen

17. Section: Resistance, Contamination, and Affliction's Four Genuine Faults

The treatise continues through its twenty-nine angles. Resistance means the capacity of coarse matter to occupy space and mutually obstruct other matter — free of the three kinds of subtlety already discussed elsewhere — and applies to only a portion of form aggregate, specifically the ten physical sense-fields, excluding the intermediate being and matter produced through meditative mastery, both too subtle to obstruct anything. Contamination means being followed by affliction's own latent heaviness, not yet countered, and arising from affliction in the first place — nearly the whole of the five aggregates qualifies, apart from what has already been purified. The treatise elaborates contamination's own meaning further: wherever affliction is present, four specific faults follow — the fault of restlessness, since affliction's own defining character is an inability to remain still, agitating body and mind alike; the fault of internal and external alteration, since affliction distorts both one's own inner state and one's perception of the outer world, each feeding the other in an escalating spiral; the fault of generating unwholesome conduct, since affliction directly provokes killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech; and the fault of securing a future rebirth, since every arising affliction is immediately stored as a seed destined to ripen into some future result. The lecture urges a direct, honest self-examination here: of the countless thoughts arising across a single day, how many are genuinely wholesome, and how many merely circle around sensory indulgence — a private audit, it suggests, that already reveals which realm of rebirth a person is quietly preparing for.

19. Section: Worldliness, the Three Realms — And Why One Single Instant Already Contains Past, Present, and Future

The treatise turns to worldliness, explaining that it means simply serving as the basis for conceptual proliferation — a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, since it is precisely the contaminated aggregates that give rise to every kind of discursive elaboration a being generates. Confinement to a realm means being subsumed under the desire, form, or formless realm as "the world" in this same sense — again a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, wherever a being's own experience is actually colored by whichever realm's conceptual proliferation currently governs it. The treatise then turns to time itself: the past means whatever cause and effect have already been fully experienced — all five aggregates qualify; the future means whatever cause and effect have not yet been experienced — all five aggregates likewise qualify; and the present means whatever cause has already been experienced while its own effect has not yet been — again all five aggregates qualify. The lecture draws out the striking implication here: since the present aggregates are simultaneously the ripened effect of past causes and the freshly planted cause of future effects, a single present moment already contains all three times at once, past, present, and future collapsed into this same instant — which is precisely why, the lecture explains, this entire meticulous sorting of the aggregates across time ultimately serves selflessness itself: neither the past self, nor the present self, nor the future self is ever truly found, each merely a temporary configuration of causes and conditions, so that the whole of this treatise's exhaustive analysis amounts, in the end, to one extended contemplation of no-self.

21. Section: Confinement to a Realm, Explained Two Ways — By Where It Arises, and By Whether Pliancy Accompanies It

The treatise offers two distinct explanations of what confines the aggregates to a particular realm. According to the first, confinement to the desire realm means being born there and not yet possessing the meditative antidote capable of transcending it, or having already attained and then emerged from such an antidote — the aggregates, across past, future, and present, manifesting under these conditions all belong to the desire realm. Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second explanation's own simpler criterion — whether pliancy-accompanied concentration is actually present — as a practically useful shortcut for the same conclusion the first, more elaborate explanation reaches by tracing a being's own history of attainment and emergence: wherever genuine meditative pliancy is absent, the mind remains desire-realm territory regardless of outward circumstance, and wherever it is present, the mind has already, in that moment, transcended into form-realm or subtler territory. Having explained confinement to a realm through both approaches, he notes, the treatise turns to moral quality itself, likewise explained through two distinct approaches — the familiar threefold division into wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral.

22. Section: Wholesome, Unwholesome, Neutral — Explained Twice, Once by Result and Once by Fault

The treatise offers two explanations of moral quality as well. According to the first, wholesomeness means whatever secures a pleasant future result, together with whatever uncontaminated virtue permanently counters and severs affliction's own suffering — a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, spanning both contaminated and uncontaminated wholesome states alike. Unwholesomeness means whatever secures a painful future result and gives rise to unwholesome conduct — again a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, wherever root or secondary affliction is actually present. And neutrality means simply whatever is neither of these — a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, including karma-ripened neutral states, meditatively produced neutral states, and the neutral qualities inherent in external objects and in ordinary bodily growth. According to the second explanation, wholesomeness means freedom from fault together with whatever counters fault and accords with genuine virtue — the first half of this definition describing uncontaminated wholesomeness specifically, the second half describing contaminated wholesomeness, which still carries the capacity to counter fault and give rise to further uncontaminated virtue; unwholesomeness, by direct contrast, means the opposite of this; and neutrality means simply whatever is neither wholesome nor unwholesome by either measure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two explanations of wholesomeness as complementary rather than competing — the first defines wholesomeness by its own consequences, a pleasant result or genuine liberation from suffering, while the second defines it by its own internal character, freedom from fault, the two perspectives converging on the same underlying reality from outside and from within. Having explained moral quality through both approaches, he notes, the treatise turns to a further pair of properties directly relevant to the path itself — the status of a learner still progressing toward full liberation, and one who has already gone beyond all further learning.

23. Section: Neither Learner Nor Beyond, Severed by Insight or Cultivation — Closing the Twenty-Nine Angles

The treatise completes its twenty-nine-angle analysis with its final properties. Being neither a learner nor beyond learning means simply falling outside both of these categories — every ordinary, unenlightened being's own contaminated aggregates qualify, together with whatever neutral or obscured qualities even a noble one's own continuity still contains beyond the specifically liberating qualities already counted as learning or its completion. Being severed by insight means whatever the direct realization achieved at the first stage of awakening is capable of cutting — roughly the hundred and twelve conceptually generated afflictions tied to wrong view, doubt, and their companions. Being severed by cultivation means whatever the sustained cultivation following that same initial realization is capable of cutting — the sixteen innate afflictions still remaining across the three realms. And being beyond all severance entirely means whatever has already been permanently countered and eliminated for good — an arhat's own aggregates, once every defilement has been exhausted, leaving only wholesome and neutral seeds behind, qualify as beyond all further severance. The treatise then confirms that this same twenty-nine-angle analysis, developed in such detail for form aggregate, applies equally, by direct contrast, to the four formless aggregates as well — wherever form aggregate was shown to possess some property, the formless aggregates are understood to lack it, and vice versa. With this, the treatise concludes its third topic within this stage of analysis — this exhaustive inventory of exactly which aggregates possess which of twenty-nine distinct properties — and turns to its fourth and final topic: how the very meaning of "aggregate" itself, and of "form" and "name" specifically, came to be established in the first place.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the completion of this twenty-nine-angle inventory as a genuine achievement of sustained, systematic analysis — every one of the five aggregates now cross-referenced against every property the tradition considers worth distinguishing, from something as basic as visibility to something as subtle as which specific stage of the path actually eliminates it. He notes that the closing instruction to simply reverse everything already established for form when considering the formless aggregates is a fittingly economical way to close out such an exhaustive inventory, rather than repeating the entire analysis a second time from scratch. Having completed this third topic, he notes, the treatise turns to its fourth and final topic within this stage — establishing what the very terms "aggregate," "form," and "name" actually mean, opening with a rich discussion of aggregation itself, illustrated through one of this fascicle's most moving teachings on how a noble one experiences pain.

24. Section: What "Aggregate" Actually Means — And Why a Noble One Feels Bodily Pain Without Mental Suffering

The treatise turns to its fourth and final topic, establishing what the word "aggregate" itself actually means, asking what constitutes aggregation, and answering with four distinct senses. The first, designating a collective entity, names how a great variety of individual phenomena — every kind of matter from the eye through mind-object form, for instance — are gathered together under a single collective label, "form aggregate," itself nothing more than a convenient designation for the whole collection rather than an additional, separately real thing. The second, mutual intermixing, names how the members of any given aggregate never actually occur in isolation, but always arise together, interwoven — the four elements always intermixed within any physical cluster, and the four formless aggregates always co-arising within any single moment of mind, nothing ever standing entirely alone. The third, a single unified category, names how an entire, highly varied range of related phenomena gets summarized under one simple heading — the eleven kinds of matter simply called "form," the many varieties of feeling simply called "feeling," and so through perception, formations, and the eight consciousnesses. And the fourth, increase and decrease, names how every aggregate, being conditioned, inevitably grows or diminishes over time — a body gaining or losing weight, a mind's wholesome qualities waxing or waning. The lecture illustrates this fourfold analysis with the Buddha's own teaching to his son Rāhula in the Saṃyukta Āgama: contemplate every form — past, future, present, internal, external, coarse, subtle, inferior, excellent, distant, or near — as neither self, nor different from self, nor mutually contained within self, and apply this same eleven-angle contemplation equally to feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, since it is precisely nostalgic attachment to the past aggregates, hopeful longing for future ones, clinging to the present ones, and craving toward the internal, the external, the coarse, the subtle, the inferior, the excellent, the distant, and the near alike that keeps a being bound to suffering — eleven distinct doorways through which the very same underlying craving operates, requiring eleven distinct angles of contemplation to fully see through. The lecture closes with a related teaching from the same collection: an unlearned ordinary being, struck by pain, suffers doubly, in both body and mind, wailing and despairing, while a well-taught noble disciple, struck by the very same physical pain, suffers only in body, the mind itself remaining free of aversion — not because the noble one somehow avoids all sensation, but because craving, aversion, and delusion no longer accompany whatever sensation does arise, leaving only the bare, unavoidable bodily fact without the additional, avoidable mental anguish an unenlightened being invariably layers on top of it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the teaching to Rāhula as this entire twenty-nine-angle analysis's own hidden purpose finally made explicit: every one of those properties — past and future, internal and external, coarse and subtle — was never simply an academic exercise in classification, but eleven distinct doorways through which craving specifically operates, each requiring its own deliberate contemplation before genuine selflessness can take hold across the board. He treats the teaching on bodily versus mental pain as this treatise's own most practically applicable insight into what liberation actually feels like moment to moment — not an absence of sensation, which remains as real for a noble one as for anyone else, but a mind that has genuinely stopped adding craving, aversion, and delusion on top of whatever sensation simply arises, the difference between suffering doubly and suffering only once. Having explained aggregation's own fourfold meaning so richly, he notes, the treatise turns to a more focused question: why form specifically is called "form" at all.

25. Section: Why Form Is Called "Form" — Growth, and Two Kinds of Obstruction

The treatise asks why form aggregate specifically earns the name "form," answering with two meanings: growth in various locations, and obstruction. Growth names how form is nourished and expands over time, not merely external crops sown and cultivated in soil, but the body's own internal growth as well, the elements sustaining and expanding the very faculties and their objects through food and other nourishment, this same growth then producing further growth in turn. Obstruction names how form displays a changeable, resistant character, whether a body's own complexion visibly shifting with anger, joy, fear, or injury, or external matter like grass and trees visibly damaged by wind and frost. The treatise elaborates this same obstruction further into two more specific senses: alteration through direct contact, meaning that the five sense faculties and their four physical objects can be struck and damaged by a hand, foot, or stick; and distinction through occupied position, meaning that matter, possessing genuine volume, always occupies its own distinct location in space, mutually obstructing whatever else might otherwise occupy that same space.

Having explained why form earns its own name, he notes, the treatise turns to the parallel question for the four formless aggregates — why they are collectively called "name" instead.

26. Section: Why the Four Formless Aggregates Are Collectively Called "Name" — And Why the Mind Is Basically a Pile of Labels

The treatise asks why feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness together earn the collective title "name," answering with two reasons: they incline toward and engage whatever object they encounter, and they depend on conventional designation to actually distinguish and make sense of that same object. Every act of cognition, the treatise explains, ultimately depends on naming — a being cannot engage any object at all without first grasping it through some conventional label, feeling, perceiving, and constructing responses only in relation to whatever has already been named and thereby singled out from everything else. The lecture draws out a striking implication here: since all four formless aggregates depend this thoroughly on naming to function, the mind itself, examined honestly, turns out to be little more than an elaborate structure built from names — nearly every act of thinking, remembering, or planning proceeds by manipulating labels rather than engaging reality directly, and a name, the lecture stresses, is nothing more than a name, carrying no genuine substance of its own; the mind that manipulates these same names is itself merely a dependently arisen process, without any fixed, independent self behind it — a reflection that, sustained carefully, can lead directly toward suchness itself, the quiescent, unconditioned reality words alone can never actually reach.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the observation that the mind is basically a structure built from names as one of this fascicle's most quietly disorienting insights, precisely because it is so easily verified through direct introspection: try to think about anything at all without relying on some label for it, and the difficulty of doing so reveals just how thoroughly naming actually structures ordinary mental life, a dependence this treatise treats as itself further evidence of the mind's own lack of any fixed, independent essence. Having explained why form is called "form" and the four formless aggregates are called "name," he notes, the treatise turns to a further, related question — why the aggregates are said to be "subsumed" under their own essential nature specifically, rather than under something else entirely.

27. Section: Each Aggregate Belongs to Its Own Nature Alone — And Opens the Meaning of Correspondence

The treatise asks to what the aggregates actually belong, and for what purpose the very notion of "subsumption" is established in the first place, answering that each aggregate belongs to its own essential nature, never to some other aggregate's nature instead — form aggregate's own subsumption is form aggregate itself, and so for each of the remaining four — and that this notion of subsumption exists specifically so that a practitioner can come to comprehensively understand each aggregate's own distinct category, thereby breaking through the false appearance of the aggregates as some single, unified, aggregated whole and opening the way toward the liberating insight of the emptiness gate. The treatise then turns to its final question within this stage of analysis: to what do all phenomena actually correspond, and for what purpose is correspondence itself established? The lecture notes a fivefold classical account of correspondence worth keeping in mind here: correspondence with another nature rather than one's own; correspondence, among such other natures, specifically with what does not contradict one's own; correspondence, among what does not contradict, specifically between matching grades of intensity — soft with soft, medium with medium, superior with superior, never across mismatched grades; correspondence occurring at the very same time rather than different times; and correspondence occurring within the very same realm rather than different realms — five successively narrower conditions together defining what makes any two phenomena genuinely capable of corresponding to one another at all.
