Fascicle 56 · 瑜伽師地論卷第五十六 · The Chapter of Deciding Discernment Continues

Neither Form Nor Mind, Yet Real Enough to Matter

The treatise completes three of the six kinds of skillful understanding in a single fascicle — the aggregates, the elements, and the sense-fields — then opens the fourth, dependent origination, tracing ignorance down to the five afflictions it generates and closing with Venerable Sudarśana's own hard-won discovery that rainmaking power is not the same thing as arhatship.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete (source-limited) Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 56 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Attainment Itself — Neither Form Nor Mind, Yet Established Upon Both

The treatise opens its second topic within this stage of analysis, the aggregates' own successive stages, revisiting the twenty-four mind-dissociated formations already introduced at length in an earlier fascicle, now consolidated together in a single, focused discussion. Asked how many kinds of stage constitute the aggregates, the treatise answers that there are many, beginning with attainment and the two mindless meditative absorptions, referring back to its own earlier, more extensive treatment. It then turns to attainment specifically, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the joint basis of cause, mastery, and actual manifestation — attainment being, in every case, neither a form phenomenon nor a mental one in its own right, but a provisional label placed upon the shifting relationship between a seed's own causal potential, the settled strength that potential has secured, and its own occasional manifestation in actual experience. The treatise names three specific kinds of attainment corresponding to these same three bases: attainment through seed, meaning simply that a given seed, not yet suppressed or severed, retains the potential to produce its own result at some future point; attainment through mastery, meaning that the causal conditions capable of bringing some quality into being have secured sufficient, unobstructed strength to do so; and attainment through manifestation, meaning that some phenomenon's own distinctive character is presently, actively appearing. The lecture notes that this same concept of "attainment," used by non-Mahāyāna schools lacking any doctrine of seeds to explain how a quality once gained is not simply lost, is refined here into something considerably broader, spanning both newly secured and long-standing qualities alike, precisely because it is grounded in the storehouse consciousness's own seeds rather than treated as some free-floating, independently real force.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening return to the twenty-four mind-dissociated factors as a deliberate act of consolidation rather than mere repetition — the earlier fascicle introduced these same phenomena scattered among a broader discussion of provisionally established categories, while this fascicle gathers them together for their own dedicated, systematic treatment, attainment itself serving as the natural first example of a phenomenon that is neither form nor mind, yet still meaningfully real enough to explain why a cultivated quality persists rather than evaporating the instant attention moves elsewhere. Having established what attainment actually is, he notes, the treatise turns to two considerably more dramatic examples of the same basic principle — the mindless meditative absorption and the deeper cessation attainment, both provisionally established upon the temporary halting of ordinary mental activity itself.

2. Section: The Mindless Attainment — Ordinary Beings Mistaking Silence for Liberation

The treatise turns to the mindless meditative attainment, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the specific stage in which a practitioner has already overcome craving for the third absorption's own refined equanimity, yet has not yet overcome craving for the fourth absorption above it, and who, weary of mental activity itself, deliberately brings the six operating consciousnesses to a halt. Regarding mental activity as a kind of sickness, an abscess, or a dart, and imagining stillness itself to be a wondrous, peaceful refuge, such a practitioner forms the deliberate resolve to obstruct all thought for a day, seven days, an eon, or more, and through this same aversion toward thought, gradually brings the six consciousnesses' own activity from coarse to increasingly subtle until, at the very threshold of full absorption, they cease outright — this cessation of the first six consciousnesses' own activity is what the mindless attainment is established upon. The treatise then names three further distinctions: its own essential nature is invariably wholesome, since it arises specifically through deliberately cultivated, effortful wholesome intention grounded in the fourth absorption; the beings who cultivate it are always ordinary, unenlightened practitioners, mistakenly believing this attainment to be nirvāṇa itself rather than the deeper, genuinely liberating cessation it merely resembles; and its result is that a practitioner who cultivates it in the desire realm is subsequently reborn in the form realm's own fourth absorption, specifically in the heaven belonging to those without perception.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the mindless attainment's own defining error — mistaking the mere absence of mental activity for genuine liberation — as a cautionary tale worth returning to whenever meditation itself starts to feel like the goal rather than the means: silencing thought is not the same as severing affliction at its root, and a practitioner who confuses the two, however deep their concentration, remains, in this treatise's own account, still an ordinary, unenlightened being rather than a liberated one. Having explained this first mindless attainment, cultivated only by the mistaken, he notes, the treatise turns to its far more advanced counterpart — the cessation attainment, reserved specifically for noble ones who have already secured genuine liberating insight.

3. Section: Cessation Attainment — Reserved for the Noble, and a Debate Over Whether It Needs a Body at All

The treatise turns to the cessation attainment, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the specific stage in which a practitioner has already overcome craving for the sphere of nothingness, and, wearied even by the subtle activity of feeling and perception that remains, deliberately brings this same activity to a halt. Unlike the mindless attainment, the treatise specifies that those capable of cultivating cessation attainment belong exclusively to the continuity of noble ones, spanning both learners who have already secured the eight liberations and the arhat who has secured liberation in both respects — ordinary beings, however accomplished their concentration, remain entirely incapable of it. Regarding how cessation attainment actually arises, the treatise records a genuine doctrinal disagreement reflecting two different stages of the tradition's own development: according to teachings that had not yet established the doctrine of the storehouse consciousness, a practitioner must first cultivate this attainment in the desire realm and then manifest it again in the form realm, since mind and its mental factors, once suspended, still require some physical basis to depend upon, without which the very continuity of a living being would appear to be severed altogether — a concern this same earlier teaching addresses by insisting cessation attainment can only actually manifest where a physical body is present. Once the storehouse consciousness's own doctrine has been established, however, the treatise notes, this same attainment may manifest anywhere at all, including the formless realm, since the storehouse consciousness itself, holding every phenomenon's own seed, never actually departs even where no physical form remains, removing any risk that a being's continuity might be broken by cessation attainment's own temporary suspension of ordinary mental activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this candid acknowledgment of two competing explanations for how cessation attainment actually manifests as a rare and valuable window into the tradition's own historical development — earlier Buddhist schools, lacking the storehouse consciousness's own doctrine, genuinely worried that a being's continuity might snap entirely once ordinary mental activity stopped without any accompanying physical form to anchor it, a worry the Yogācāra tradition's own signature innovation, the storehouse consciousness, was specifically developed to resolve. He treats the sheer rarity of this attainment — reserved only for the most advanced noble ones, unlike the mindless attainment any sufficiently deluded ordinary being can reach — as this passage's own quiet reminder that outward stillness alone proves nothing about a practitioner's actual spiritual attainment, since the very same silence can be either a beginner's mistake or an arhat's own hard-won accomplishment depending entirely on what understanding underlies it. Having explained both mindless meditative states, he notes, the treatise turns to the third, related phenomenon — the mindless heaven itself, the actual rebirth destination such practice, if mistaken for liberation, eventually leads to.

4. Section: The Mindless Heaven Itself — A Rebirth Destination, Purely Neutral, Reserved for the Mistaken

The treatise turns to the mindless heaven itself, the actual rebirth destination the mindless attainment leads to, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the very stage already reached by beings already born into that heaven, where the first six consciousnesses' own activity has ceased entirely. Unlike the mindless attainment that leads to it, whose own nature is invariably wholesome, the resulting heavenly rebirth's own nature is neutral, being simply karma's automatic, unobscured result rather than an actively cultivated wholesome state in its own right. Those born there belong exclusively to the continuity of ordinary, unenlightened beings, since no noble one, understanding this state for what it actually is, would ever mistake it for genuine liberation and aim toward it. And its own characteristic unfolding is this: a being who has cultivated the volition capable of generating this same attainment experiences its karmic result upon rebirth there, remaining without perception for the great majority of an extraordinarily long lifespan, yet with perception present during a brief span both immediately after birth and immediately before death — this brief return of perception at the very end being precisely what causes such a being to pass away from that same heavenly state at last.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sharp contrast between the mindless attainment's own wholesome nature and the mindless heaven's own neutral nature as a precise illustration of how cause and effect can differ in moral quality: the deliberate cultivation that leads there is genuinely wholesome effort, however misguided its underlying goal, while the resulting rebirth itself is simply karma's automatic unfolding, carrying no further moral charge of its own. He notes the poignant detail that perception's own brief return at the very end of this extraordinarily long lifespan is what actually ends it — a being who spent nearly the entire span unconscious of anything at all finds thought itself returning only to immediately precipitate their own departure from that same state, a fittingly ironic close to an existence built entirely on avoiding thought in the first place. Having completed the mindless heaven, he notes, the treatise turns to the third of the twenty-four mind-dissociated factors — the life faculty itself, the provisional label placed upon the sheer duration a given karmic result is fated to last.

5. Section: The Life Faculty — Fixed or Uncertain, Welcome or Unwelcome, Counted in Years or in Eons

The treatise turns to the life faculty, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the settled duration karma itself has already determined a given karmic result will last — the life faculty being simply this same fixed span of time, provisionally treated as a distinct phenomenon. It names three further distinctions. Some lifespans are fixed and others are not: beings in the northern continent of Uttarakuru, for instance, live exactly a thousand years without exception, while beings in the southern continent — this present human world — may live anywhere from a fleeting, unformed existence in the womb up to extraordinarily long spans, with no fixed measure at all, subject to premature death at any point along the way. Some lifespans are desirable and others are not, according to which of the five destinies a being is reborn into — human and heavenly rebirth counting as desirable, the three lower realms as undesirable. And lifespans are measured in different units depending on the realm: desire-realm beings, human and heavenly alike, count their lives in years, while form- and formless-realm beings count theirs in eons instead — the lecture noting further, more technical distinctions still, such as whether a given lifespan has any outer limit at all, as an ordinary being's does, or, like an arhat's or advanced bodhisattva's, can be extended through the practitioner's own spiritual power beyond what karma alone would have determined.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the contrast between Uttarakuru's own fixed thousand-year lifespan and the human world's own wildly variable one — from a lifetime cut short in the womb to extraordinary old age — as a pointed reminder of just how much uncertainty ordinary human life actually involves compared to other realms of rebirth, a fact worth sitting with rather than taking one's own continued existence for granted. He notes that the life faculty's own provisional status — a mere label placed on a duration rather than any independently findable substance — fits the same pattern already established for attainment and the two mindless states: none of these twenty-four factors names a genuine, freestanding reality, only a useful way of speaking about how form and mind actually behave over time. Having explained the life faculty, he notes, the treatise turns to the fourth mind-dissociated factor — species-similarity, the shared characteristics that let beings of the same general kind recognize one another as belonging together.

6. Section: Species-Similarity, and Three Faces of Arising — Instant by Instant, Life to Life, Mood to Mood

The treatise turns to species-similarity, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon whatever shared resemblance links different beings together — the provisional recognition that certain individuals belong to the same broader category. It names three kinds: similarity of category, meaning the shared characteristics linking beings born within the same realm or destiny, such as belonging equally to the desire realm or equally to the animal realm; similarity of essential character, meaning a finer-grained resemblance in condition, temperament, appearance, form, or voice; and similarity through shared trade or livelihood, meaning the resemblance linking beings who practice the same profession or skill, such as fellow teachers, physicians, or craftsmen. The treatise then turns to arising itself, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the present moment in which a given phenomenon actually manifests. It names three kinds of arising: momentary arising, the ceaseless, instant-by-instant manifestation of every conditioned phenomenon whatsoever; continuous arising, the ongoing transition from one being's own life, once its span concludes, into the next; and arising through distinct stages, itself of two further kinds — growth-arising, the gradual unfolding from infancy through advancing age, and mental arising, the constantly shifting parade of mental states a being passes through moment to moment, sometimes accompanied by pleasant feeling, sometimes by painful, sometimes by neither, sometimes marked by craving, sometimes by its absence, extending all the way to the difference between a mind genuinely liberated and one still unliberated.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats species-similarity's own threefold breakdown — category, essential character, and shared trade — as capturing three genuinely different ways beings actually recognize kinship with one another, from the broadest possible grouping by realm of rebirth down to the narrowest, most specific bond of sharing the same daily profession. He notes that mental arising's own extension all the way to the difference between a liberated and unliberated mind is a quiet reminder that even the deepest spiritual transformation a being undergoes is still, technically, just one more instance of this same basic phenomenon — arising, moment by moment, like everything else. Having explained species-similarity and arising, he notes, the treatise turns to a phenomenon inseparable from arising's own opposite number — aging, illustrated through one of this fascicle's most consoling teachings on how the body may suffer even while the mind remains genuinely untroubled.

7. Section: Aging in Three Faces — And Why the Body May Suffer While the Mind Need Not

The treatise turns to aging, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the difference between an earlier and a later stage of the very same continuity. It names three kinds: aging through changed character, the moment-to-moment alteration every conditioned phenomenon undergoes, giving rise to bodily aging, mental aging, aging of the lifespan itself, aging through decay, and aging through the transformation of one's own being; aging through transformation, the more dramatic shift a being undergoes across an entire lifetime or across rebirths, as when a being who dies in a fortunate realm is reborn instead into one of the three lower realms, the aggregates themselves utterly transformed; and aging through diminished capacity, the gradual decline of the sense faculties' own ability to engage their objects, manifesting as graying hair, wrinkled skin, diminished vitality, frequent illness, a stooped spine, labored breathing, a bent frame, dependence on a cane, dulled memory and judgment, and the general breakdown of every faculty's own former function. The lecture illustrates this teaching with a story from the Ekottara Āgama: an elderly, ailing layman once approached the Buddha seeking guidance, and the Buddha told him simply that old age inevitably brings illness — birth, aging, sickness, and death are unavoidable for everyone — but that a genuine practitioner can learn to suffer in body while remaining untroubled in mind. Not fully understanding this teaching, the layman sought out Śāriputra, who explained more fully: the body, like feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, is impermanent and constantly breaking down regardless, this being simply how the four elements naturally behave — but so long as one does not cling to this same impermanent body and mind as a genuine, fixed self or as something belonging to oneself, no suffering actually follows from witnessing its own inevitable decline, exactly as clouds must eventually become rain, one transient form simply becoming another. A mind that has genuinely realized selflessness, the lecture concludes, need not age or suffer even while the body it inhabits visibly does, since suffering was never a necessary consequence of aging itself, only of clinging to what was never truly one's own to begin with.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Buddha's own deceptively simple teaching to the elderly layman — that the body inevitably suffers while the mind need not — as one of this entire treatise's most practically valuable insights, precisely because it requires no advanced meditative attainment to begin applying, only a genuine, sustained willingness to stop identifying the aggregates' own inevitable decline with "myself" or "my own." He notes that Śāriputra's own fuller explanation grounds this teaching directly in the selflessness doctrine this treatise has returned to repeatedly throughout its analysis of the aggregates — the body's aging is simply the four elements behaving exactly as they always do, and it is only the added layer of self-clinging, not the physical process itself, that actually generates suffering. Having explained aging in its three forms, he notes, the treatise turns to abiding, the temporary persistence any phenomenon shows before it too eventually passes away.

8. Section: Abiding and Impermanence — Momentary Persistence, and Three Ways Everything Eventually Falls Apart

The treatise turns to abiding, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the very same moment of arising already discussed, since abiding simply names conditioned phenomena's own brief persistence immediately following that moment. It names three kinds: momentary abiding, the instant-by-instant persistence belonging to any already-arisen phenomenon; continuous abiding, the ongoing sustaining of a being's own five aggregates through nourishment, or the physical world's own persistence across the long stretch of time before a cosmic age finally begins to decline; and abiding through established convention, meaning the laws, systems, and institutions different peoples and regions establish to secure order and stability for their own communities. The treatise then turns to impermanence itself, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the stage in which a phenomenon, having already arisen, subsequently breaks apart and ceases. It names three kinds: impermanence through outright destruction, the plain cessation any conditioned phenomenon undergoes the instant after it arises; impermanence through transformation, the alteration by which a once-pleasing state turns into a displeasing one, whether through the body's own many internal changes or the physical world's own many external ones; and impermanence through separation, the loss of wealth, status, or the people one loves, each of these three together exhausting the ways in which everything that arises must, sooner or later, also come apart.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sequence — arising, abiding, aging, and now impermanence — as together tracing the same four-phase cycle any conditioned phenomenon inevitably passes through, each phase provisionally established as its own distinct factor purely for the sake of careful analysis, even though, taken together, they describe nothing more than a single continuous, ever-changing process. He notes that impermanence through separation — losing wealth, status, or loved ones — connects this abstract philosophical category directly back to lived human experience, the very same losses that provoke genuine grief being simply this treatise's own carefully named third variety of a universal law nothing whatsoever escapes. Having completed this four-phase cycle of arising, abiding, aging, and impermanence, he notes, the treatise turns to a rather different kind of mind-dissociated factor — names, phrases, and syllables themselves, the building blocks of language through which all of this analysis has been expressed in the first place.

9. Section: Names, Phrases, and Syllables — And Why a Raised Eyebrow Can Teach the Dharma Too

The treatise turns to names, phrases, and syllables together, asking upon what basis a name-group is established, and answering: upon whatever provisional linguistic convention allows a word to designate something. It names three kinds: provisional names, such as designating a body as "I" or "a being," or designating some object as "a vase" or "a robe," none of these labels corresponding to any independently real substance; substantive names, such as designating the actual sense faculties and their objects — eye, ear, form, sound — categories genuinely grounded in dependently arisen phenomena; and names either commonly understood or not commonly understood by ordinary people, some conventions being widely shared and others obscure. Phrases and syllables, the treatise explains, follow this same threefold pattern exactly, each likewise divisible into provisional, substantive, and more-or-less commonly understood forms, differing only in scale — a phrase being a complete statement rather than a single word, and a syllable being the individual sound or written character phrases and names are themselves built from. The treatise adds a further distinction: phrases divide into declarative phrases, which simply state some quality, and explanatory phrases, which elaborate on that same quality's own meaning, while syllables divide into those grasped through sound and those grasped through written form. The lecture notes that this entire threefold linguistic system is itself only provisionally grounded in whichever sense object a given world's beings happen to perceive most keenly — in this present world, where sight, hearing, and thought are sharpest, names and phrases are built primarily from visible form, sound, and mental images, but in other buddha-lands, beings with especially keen smell, taste, or touch might just as validly build their own entire language from fragrance, flavor, or texture instead, since ultimately any of the six sense objects, skillfully employed, can serve as a vehicle for teaching — a raised eyebrow, a smile while holding up a flower, a sigh, a cough, or simply a remembered image can all convey the Dharma every bit as effectively as spoken words or written characters, teaching never being strictly confined to language in the first place.

Having explained the linguistic building blocks through which every other teaching in this treatise has itself been expressed, he notes, the treatise turns to the tenth mind-dissociated factor — ordinary-being status itself, the provisional label marking anyone who has not yet directly realized the supramundane path.

10. Section: Ordinary-Being Status Across Three Realms, and Continuity's Forward and Reverse Flow

The treatise turns to ordinary-being status, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the stage in which every supramundane, noble quality remains as yet unrealized. It names three kinds, corresponding to the three realms: ordinary-being status bound to the desire realm, to the form realm, and to the formless realm respectively — since a being still bound by any one of the three realms' own afflictions, whichever realm it happens to inhabit, remains, by that very fact, an ordinary being rather than a noble one. The treatise then turns to continuity, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the uninterrupted succession of cause and effect. It names three kinds: momentary continuity, the ceaseless succession of arising and ceasing within a single mental stream; continuity through rebirth, an ordinary being's own uninterrupted passage back and forth through the three realms, life after life; and continuity through defilement and purification, the ongoing succession of defiled and pure states of mind alternating within a single being's own experience — the lecture connecting this last kind directly to the twelve links of dependent origination, forward continuity following the defiled direction from ignorance through formations all the way to aging and death, exactly as this condition existing gives rise to that one, and reverse continuity following the purifying direction instead, ignorance's own cessation bringing the cessation of formations, all the way to the cessation of aging and death, exactly as this condition ceasing brings that one's own cessation as well.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats continuity's own forward-and-reverse structure, mapped directly onto dependent origination's own twelve links, as this treatise's own reminder that the very same causal chain binding a being to saṃsāra can, once properly understood and reversed, become the exact mechanism of that being's own liberation — nothing extra needs to be added to the process, only the same links traced backward, each cause's own cessation bringing its effect's cessation in turn. Having explained ordinary-being status and continuity, he notes, the treatise turns to a further pair of factors — determinate distinction, ensuring cause and effect never blur into one another, and correspondence, the fitting match between a cause and its own proper effect.

11. Section: Cause Never Blurs With Effect — Determinate Distinction and the Beginning of Correspondence

The treatise turns to determinate distinction, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the fact that each phenomenon possesses its own distinct character, never blurring into confusion with any other. It names three kinds: distinction of characteristic, meaning that cause and effect always retain their own separate, non-interchangeable identities — a wholesome cause always yielding a pleasant result, an unwholesome cause always yielding a painful one, the two never actually merging into one another, exactly as the truths of suffering and origin, describing saṃsāra's own forward flow, remain forever distinct from the truths of cessation and the path, describing liberation's own reverse flow, or as the twelve sense-fields each retain their own separate identity, or as the three kinds of feeling, or the varying lifespans and dimensions of different realms of rebirth, never actually collapsing into one another despite their shared status as conditioned phenomena; distinction of cause, meaning that wholesome and unwholesome karma remain forever distinct in their own causal power, wholesome karma alone capable of yielding a favorable rebirth, unwholesome karma alone capable of yielding an unfavorable one; and distinction of effect, meaning that painful and pleasant results themselves remain forever distinct, painful results always accompanied by painful sensation, pleasant results always accompanied by pleasant sensation, cause and effect corresponding reliably rather than randomly. The treatise then opens a further factor, correspondence, asking upon what basis it is established — the fitting match ensuring a given cause reliably brings about its own proper effect rather than some other, unrelated one.

Having established why cause and effect never actually merge, he notes, the treatise turns to correspondence itself — the equally important guarantee that a cause, once distinct from its effect, still actually connects to that effect in a reliable, fitting way rather than producing results at random.

12. Section: Correspondence's Three Faces, and Three Speeds — From a Single Thought to a Buddha's Own Reach

The treatise closes correspondence, explaining that it is established upon the fitting match between cause and effect, itself of three kinds: correspondence through combination, meaning simply that wholesome causes reliably yield pleasant results and unwholesome causes reliably yield painful ones; correspondence through skillful means, meaning the fitting relationship between preparatory practice — reciting scripture, prostrating, cultivating the foundations of mindfulness — and the deeper realization such practice is meant to prepare the way for; and correspondence through conformity with principle, meaning wisdom's own fitting alignment with the four classical modes of reasoning a phenomenon can be properly understood through. The treatise then turns to speed, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the swift, unceasing flow that both form and mind display without exception. It names three kinds: the speed of conditioned phenomena themselves, referring to the traditional teaching that a single finger-snap already contains six hundred arisings and ceasings, every conditioned thing changing far too quickly for ordinary perception to track; the speed of embodied beings, referring to how swiftly humans, elephants, horses, birds, and especially yakṣas and gods can move from place to place; and the speed of supernatural power, referring to the tradition's own account of a being capable of reaching the highest heaven in the time it takes to bend and extend an arm.

13. Section: Sequence From Sunrise to Sunset, and Time Itself — Relative Even Among the Heavens

The treatise turns to sequence, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the orderly succession any given process of conditioned arising displays. It names three kinds: momentary sequence, the strict, uninterrupted order in which each successive mental moment follows the one before it; the sequence of bodily activity, meaning the customary order of a day's own routine — for a layperson, waking, washing, dressing, working, bathing, and resting in turn; for a monastic, arranging one's robes, entering the village for alms, eating properly, washing the bowl and feet, retiring to a quiet cell to recite scripture and reflect, sitting in meditation through the day, resting during the deepest hours of night, and rising again before dawn to resume walking meditation and further reflection; and the sequence by which the physical universe itself was originally arranged at the very start of a cosmic age. The treatise then turns to time, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the uninterrupted succession of cause and effect itself, time being nothing more than a provisional label placed on this same succession — what has already arisen and ceased is called the past, what has not yet arisen is called the future, and what has arisen but not yet ceased is called the present. It names three kinds accordingly: past, future, and present, the lecture noting that time itself, far from being any fixed, independent measure, is entirely relative — a single day and night for humans lasts twenty-four hours, yet the very same single day and night for the Heaven of the Four Great Kings equals fifty human years, and for the Heaven of the Thirty-Three equals a full hundred, proof that "time" names nothing more than this same relative succession of cause and effect, provisionally measured differently depending on where one happens to stand within it.

14. Section: Place in Three Directions, and Number Built From Nothing but Comparison

The treatise turns to place, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the position occupied by whichever internal or external forms are actually being considered. It names three kinds: above, below, and to the side — the lecture noting that "to the side" here loosely covers every intermediate direction as well, east, west, south, north, and the points between, however finely such directions might otherwise be subdivided. The treatise then turns to number, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon whatever measure allows a phenomenon's own quantity to be expressed and understood. It names three kinds: the singular, the dual, and the plural. The lecture explains that number itself arises entirely through relative comparison among phenomena — one thing simply being taller than another, one day simply hotter than the previous one, one distance simply farther than a shorter one — this same comparative relationship being what actually generates the entire counting system, from the simplest single digits through the traditional "lower," "middle," and "higher" systems of enumeration, culminating in India's own ten traditional terms for numbers beyond ordinary reckoning altogether, among them the immeasurable, the boundless, the incomparable, the uncountable, and the inconceivable — vast quantities named specifically to indicate they exceed what any ordinary counting system could actually express.

15. Section: Union and Disunion Close the List — Twenty-Four Factors That Are Neither Form Nor Mind, Fully Explained

The treatise turns to union, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering: upon the stage in which every necessary supporting condition for a given undertaking is complete, without a single one lacking. It names three kinds: union through gathering, meaning simply people or conditions assembling together in one place, as students gathering to study this very treatise; union through shared purpose, meaning multiple minds and mental factors converging on the very same object, as an entire class attending to the same teaching together; and union through completion, meaning an undertaking reaching its own final, successful conclusion, as a house, once built, stands finished. The treatise then turns to disunion, asking upon what basis it is established, and answering simply: the exact reverse of union — wherever a necessary supporting condition is lacking, disunion is established instead, itself likewise of three kinds, mirroring union's own three in reverse: failure to gather, failure to share a common object, and failure to reach completion. The lecture notes the practical stakes of this final pair directly: a household that achieves genuine union flourishes, while one riven by disunion falls apart, and a monastic community that achieves genuine union sustains the teaching itself for the benefit of all, while one riven by disunion allows the genuine teaching to fade and corrupted teachings to flourish in its place. With this, the treatise concludes its entire survey of the twenty-four mind-dissociated formations — from attainment and the two mindless meditative states through the life faculty, species-similarity, arising, aging, abiding, impermanence, the linguistic units of names, phrases, and syllables, ordinary-being status, continuity, determinate distinction, correspondence, speed, sequence, time, place, and number, concluding now with union and disunion — bringing the second of four topics within this stage of analysis to its complete close, and the treatise turns to its third topic: an extensive, twenty-nine-angle examination distinguishing exactly which of the five aggregates possess which properties.

16. Section: Twenty-Nine Angles Begin — Which Aggregate Has Form, and Which Portion Can Actually Be Seen

17. Section: Resistance, Contamination, and Affliction's Four Genuine Faults

The treatise continues through its twenty-nine angles. Resistance means the capacity of coarse matter to occupy space and mutually obstruct other matter — free of the three kinds of subtlety already discussed elsewhere — and applies to only a portion of form aggregate, specifically the ten physical sense-fields, excluding the intermediate being and matter produced through meditative mastery, both too subtle to obstruct anything. Contamination means being followed by affliction's own latent heaviness, not yet countered, and arising from affliction in the first place — nearly the whole of the five aggregates qualifies, apart from what has already been purified. The treatise elaborates contamination's own meaning further: wherever affliction is present, four specific faults follow — the fault of restlessness, since affliction's own defining character is an inability to remain still, agitating body and mind alike; the fault of internal and external alteration, since affliction distorts both one's own inner state and one's perception of the outer world, each feeding the other in an escalating spiral; the fault of generating unwholesome conduct, since affliction directly provokes killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech; and the fault of securing a future rebirth, since every arising affliction is immediately stored as a seed destined to ripen into some future result. The lecture urges a direct, honest self-examination here: of the countless thoughts arising across a single day, how many are genuinely wholesome, and how many merely circle around sensory indulgence — a private audit, it suggests, that already reveals which realm of rebirth a person is quietly preparing for.

19. Section: Worldliness, the Three Realms — And Why One Single Instant Already Contains Past, Present, and Future

The treatise turns to worldliness, explaining that it means simply serving as the basis for conceptual proliferation — a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, since it is precisely the contaminated aggregates that give rise to every kind of discursive elaboration a being generates. Confinement to a realm means being subsumed under the desire, form, or formless realm as "the world" in this same sense — again a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, wherever a being's own experience is actually colored by whichever realm's conceptual proliferation currently governs it. The treatise then turns to time itself: the past means whatever cause and effect have already been fully experienced — all five aggregates qualify; the future means whatever cause and effect have not yet been experienced — all five aggregates likewise qualify; and the present means whatever cause has already been experienced while its own effect has not yet been — again all five aggregates qualify. The lecture draws out the striking implication here: since the present aggregates are simultaneously the ripened effect of past causes and the freshly planted cause of future effects, a single present moment already contains all three times at once, past, present, and future collapsed into this same instant — which is precisely why, the lecture explains, this entire meticulous sorting of the aggregates across time ultimately serves selflessness itself: neither the past self, nor the present self, nor the future self is ever truly found, each merely a temporary configuration of causes and conditions, so that the whole of this treatise's exhaustive analysis amounts, in the end, to one extended contemplation of no-self.

21. Section: Confinement to a Realm, Explained Two Ways — By Where It Arises, and By Whether Pliancy Accompanies It

The treatise offers two distinct explanations of what confines the aggregates to a particular realm. According to the first, confinement to the desire realm means being born there and not yet possessing the meditative antidote capable of transcending it, or having already attained and then emerged from such an antidote — the aggregates, across past, future, and present, manifesting under these conditions all belong to the desire realm. Master Cháng Bǎi treats the second explanation's own simpler criterion — whether pliancy-accompanied concentration is actually present — as a practically useful shortcut for the same conclusion the first, more elaborate explanation reaches by tracing a being's own history of attainment and emergence: wherever genuine meditative pliancy is absent, the mind remains desire-realm territory regardless of outward circumstance, and wherever it is present, the mind has already, in that moment, transcended into form-realm or subtler territory. Having explained confinement to a realm through both approaches, he notes, the treatise turns to moral quality itself, likewise explained through two distinct approaches — the familiar threefold division into wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral.

22. Section: Wholesome, Unwholesome, Neutral — Explained Twice, Once by Result and Once by Fault

The treatise offers two explanations of moral quality as well. According to the first, wholesomeness means whatever secures a pleasant future result, together with whatever uncontaminated virtue permanently counters and severs affliction's own suffering — a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, spanning both contaminated and uncontaminated wholesome states alike. Unwholesomeness means whatever secures a painful future result and gives rise to unwholesome conduct — again a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, wherever root or secondary affliction is actually present. And neutrality means simply whatever is neither of these — a portion of all five aggregates qualifies, including karma-ripened neutral states, meditatively produced neutral states, and the neutral qualities inherent in external objects and in ordinary bodily growth. According to the second explanation, wholesomeness means freedom from fault together with whatever counters fault and accords with genuine virtue — the first half of this definition describing uncontaminated wholesomeness specifically, the second half describing contaminated wholesomeness, which still carries the capacity to counter fault and give rise to further uncontaminated virtue; unwholesomeness, by direct contrast, means the opposite of this; and neutrality means simply whatever is neither wholesome nor unwholesome by either measure.

23. Section: Neither Learner Nor Beyond, Severed by Insight or Cultivation — Closing the Twenty-Nine Angles

The treatise completes its twenty-nine-angle analysis with its final properties. Being neither a learner nor beyond learning means simply falling outside both of these categories — every ordinary, unenlightened being's own contaminated aggregates qualify, together with whatever neutral or obscured qualities even a noble one's own continuity still contains beyond the specifically liberating qualities already counted as learning or its completion. Being severed by insight means whatever the direct realization achieved at the first stage of awakening is capable of cutting — roughly the hundred and twelve conceptually generated afflictions tied to wrong view, doubt, and their companions. Being severed by cultivation means whatever the sustained cultivation following that same initial realization is capable of cutting — the sixteen innate afflictions still remaining across the three realms. And being beyond all severance entirely means whatever has already been permanently countered and eliminated for good — an arhat's own aggregates, once every defilement has been exhausted, leaving only wholesome and neutral seeds behind, qualify as beyond all further severance. The treatise then confirms that this same twenty-nine-angle analysis, developed in such detail for form aggregate, applies equally, by direct contrast, to the four formless aggregates as well — wherever form aggregate was shown to possess some property, the formless aggregates are understood to lack it, and vice versa. With this, the treatise concludes its third topic within this stage of analysis — this exhaustive inventory of exactly which aggregates possess which of twenty-nine distinct properties — and turns to its fourth and final topic: how the very meaning of "aggregate" itself, and of "form" and "name" specifically, came to be established in the first place.

24. Section: What "Aggregate" Actually Means — And Why a Noble One Feels Bodily Pain Without Mental Suffering

The treatise turns to its fourth and final topic, establishing what the word "aggregate" itself actually means, asking what constitutes aggregation, and answering with four distinct senses. The first, designating a collective entity, names how a great variety of individual phenomena — every kind of matter from the eye through mind-object form, for instance — are gathered together under a single collective label, "form aggregate," itself nothing more than a convenient designation for the whole collection rather than an additional, separately real thing. The second, mutual intermixing, names how the members of any given aggregate never actually occur in isolation, but always arise together, interwoven — the four elements always intermixed within any physical cluster, and the four formless aggregates always co-arising within any single moment of mind, nothing ever standing entirely alone. The third, a single unified category, names how an entire, highly varied range of related phenomena gets summarized under one simple heading — the eleven kinds of matter simply called "form," the many varieties of feeling simply called "feeling," and so through perception, formations, and the eight consciousnesses. And the fourth, increase and decrease, names how every aggregate, being conditioned, inevitably grows or diminishes over time — a body gaining or losing weight, a mind's wholesome qualities waxing or waning. The lecture illustrates this fourfold analysis with the Buddha's own teaching to his son Rāhula in the Saṃyukta Āgama: contemplate every form — past, future, present, internal, external, coarse, subtle, inferior, excellent, distant, or near — as neither self, nor different from self, nor mutually contained within self, and apply this same eleven-angle contemplation equally to feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, since it is precisely nostalgic attachment to the past aggregates, hopeful longing for future ones, clinging to the present ones, and craving toward the internal, the external, the coarse, the subtle, the inferior, the excellent, the distant, and the near alike that keeps a being bound to suffering — eleven distinct doorways through which the very same underlying craving operates, requiring eleven distinct angles of contemplation to fully see through. The lecture closes with a related teaching from the same collection: an unlearned ordinary being, struck by pain, suffers doubly, in both body and mind, wailing and despairing, while a well-taught noble disciple, struck by the very same physical pain, suffers only in body, the mind itself remaining free of aversion — not because the noble one somehow avoids all sensation, but because craving, aversion, and delusion no longer accompany whatever sensation does arise, leaving only the bare, unavoidable bodily fact without the additional, avoidable mental anguish an unenlightened being invariably layers on top of it.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the teaching to Rāhula as this entire twenty-nine-angle analysis's own hidden purpose finally made explicit: every one of those properties — past and future, internal and external, coarse and subtle — was never simply an academic exercise in classification, but eleven distinct doorways through which craving specifically operates, each requiring its own deliberate contemplation before genuine selflessness can take hold across the board. He treats the teaching on bodily versus mental pain as this treatise's own most practically applicable insight into what liberation actually feels like moment to moment — not an absence of sensation, which remains as real for a noble one as for anyone else, but a mind that has genuinely stopped adding craving, aversion, and delusion on top of whatever sensation simply arises, the difference between suffering doubly and suffering only once. Having explained aggregation's own fourfold meaning so richly, he notes, the treatise turns to a more focused question: why form specifically is called "form" at all.

25. Section: Why Form Is Called "Form" — Growth, and Two Kinds of Obstruction

The treatise asks why form aggregate specifically earns the name "form," answering with two meanings: growth in various locations, and obstruction. Growth names how form is nourished and expands over time, not merely external crops sown and cultivated in soil, but the body's own internal growth as well, the elements sustaining and expanding the very faculties and their objects through food and other nourishment, this same growth then producing further growth in turn. Obstruction names how form displays a changeable, resistant character, whether a body's own complexion visibly shifting with anger, joy, fear, or injury, or external matter like grass and trees visibly damaged by wind and frost. The treatise elaborates this same obstruction further into two more specific senses: alteration through direct contact, meaning that the five sense faculties and their four physical objects can be struck and damaged by a hand, foot, or stick; and distinction through occupied position, meaning that matter, possessing genuine volume, always occupies its own distinct location in space, mutually obstructing whatever else might otherwise occupy that same space.

26. Section: Why the Four Formless Aggregates Are Collectively Called "Name" — And Why the Mind Is Basically a Pile of Labels

The treatise asks why feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness together earn the collective title "name," answering with two reasons: they incline toward and engage whatever object they encounter, and they depend on conventional designation to actually distinguish and make sense of that same object. Every act of cognition, the treatise explains, ultimately depends on naming — a being cannot engage any object at all without first grasping it through some conventional label, feeling, perceiving, and constructing responses only in relation to whatever has already been named and thereby singled out from everything else. The lecture draws out a striking implication here: since all four formless aggregates depend this thoroughly on naming to function, the mind itself, examined honestly, turns out to be little more than an elaborate structure built from names — nearly every act of thinking, remembering, or planning proceeds by manipulating labels rather than engaging reality directly, and a name, the lecture stresses, is nothing more than a name, carrying no genuine substance of its own; the mind that manipulates these same names is itself merely a dependently arisen process, without any fixed, independent self behind it — a reflection that, sustained carefully, can lead directly toward suchness itself, the quiescent, unconditioned reality words alone can never actually reach.

Having explained why form is called "form" and the four formless aggregates are called "name," he notes, the treatise turns to a further, related question — why the aggregates are said to be "subsumed" under their own essential nature specifically, rather than under something else entirely.

27. Section: Each Aggregate Belongs to Its Own Nature Alone — And Opens the Meaning of Correspondence

The treatise asks to what the aggregates actually belong, and for what purpose the very notion of "subsumption" is established in the first place, answering that each aggregate belongs to its own essential nature, never to some other aggregate's nature instead — form aggregate's own subsumption is form aggregate itself, and so for each of the remaining four — and that this notion of subsumption exists specifically so that a practitioner can come to comprehensively understand each aggregate's own distinct category, thereby breaking through the false appearance of the aggregates as some single, unified, aggregated whole and opening the way toward the liberating insight of the emptiness gate. The treatise then turns to its final question within this stage of analysis: to what do all phenomena actually correspond, and for what purpose is correspondence itself established? The lecture notes a fivefold classical account of correspondence worth keeping in mind here: correspondence with another nature rather than one's own; correspondence, among such other natures, specifically with what does not contradict one's own; correspondence, among what does not contradict, specifically between matching grades of intensity — soft with soft, medium with medium, superior with superior, never across mismatched grades; correspondence occurring at the very same time rather than different times; and correspondence occurring within the very same realm rather than different realms — five successively narrower conditions together defining what makes any two phenomena genuinely capable of corresponding to one another at all.

28. Section: Anāthapiṇḍika's Deathbed Tears of Joy — Correspondence Closes the Aggregates' Entire Analysis

The treatise closes its final question within this stage of analysis: to what do phenomena actually correspond? It answers that correspondence occurs with another nature, never with one's own — mind and its mental factors operating together, distinct yet joined, is what correspondence actually names. This same notion is established, the treatise explains, so that a practitioner can fully understand that mind's own essential nature is inherently pure, merely appearing defiled or undefiled depending entirely on which mental factors happen to accompany it at a given moment, growing more or less pure as those factors shift — mind itself being simply the bare capacity for awareness, taking on the coloring of whatever craving, aversion, or their absence happens to correspond with it, never itself the fixed, permanent "self" doing the feeling, perceiving, or willing. The lecture illustrates this liberating insight with the celebrated account of the householder Anāthapiṇḍika's own final days: after decades of devoted service to the Buddha, dying and confined to his bed, he sent for spiritual guidance, and the Buddha dispatched Śāriputra, who brought Ānanda along to recite supporting verses. Finding the householder in pain, Śāriputra first guided him to recall his own long record of generosity and devotion, lifting his spirits — yet noting that comfort alone was not enough, he had Ānanda press further: are your hands and feet truly you? No, the householder answered. Then no more are form, feeling, perception, formations, or consciousness truly you, Ānanda continued — the fear now overtaking him was simply the conviction that "self" was about to vanish, that everything he owned and loved was about to be lost forever; yet this same body was never his to begin with, borrowed only temporarily through the coming together of causes and conditions, and it was time to set it down. Hearing this, Anāthapiṇḍika wept — not from grief, he explained, but from overwhelming joy, having spent his whole life absorbed in meritorious deeds without ever finding the time to hear so liberating a teaching until this very last moment, and he asked only one further favor: that the Buddha be persuaded to teach this same liberating truth to lay householders while they still had time to practice it, rather than reserving it only for the dying. Ānanda promised to relay the request, and shortly after they departed, Anāthapiṇḍika passed away in peace.

29. Section: Refuting "Only Mind Is Real" — Why Feeling, Perception, and Formations Cannot Be Mere Illusions

The treatise turns to refuting a rival view held by certain renunciants and brahmins, who claim that within the name-aggregates, mind alone is genuinely real, while feeling, perception, and formations possess no reality of their own. The treatise rejects this outright, explaining first that it renders the very fivefold structure of the aggregates incoherent — scripture consistently speaks of exactly five aggregates, never four or six, precisely because each aggregate possesses its own distinct essential nature; if mind alone were real and the rest were not, this fivefold structure could never actually be established at all. Anticipating the objection that feeling, perception, and formations might instead be explained as mere positional phases of mind itself — mind first, then feeling, then perception, then formations, arising in sequence rather than simultaneously — the treatise identifies four separate faults in this same alternative theory. First, whether or not these supposed phases actually display distinct functions leads to failure either way: if they do display genuinely distinct functions, then their very distinctness in function demands separately real substances behind each function, contradicting the claim that only mind is real; and if they display no distinct function at all, then positing separate "phases" in the first place accomplishes nothing, an empty exercise in relabeling.

Having identified this first fault in the positional-phase theory, he notes, the treatise turns to three further faults, each attacking the same underlying claim from a different angle — comparing it unfavorably to the six consciousnesses' own genuine basis for differentiation, to matter's own capacity for gradual transformation, and to the plain fact that a single moment's cause admits no room for sequential unfolding at all.

30. Section: Milk Curdling Into Yogurt — Why Mind Cannot Gradually Become Feeling, Perception, or Will

The treatise names three further faults in the theory that feeling, perception, and formations are mere positional phases of mind. The second fault points out that this theory cannot even appeal to the precedent of the six sense-consciousnesses, which do genuinely differ according to their own distinct supporting faculties and objects — eye-consciousness depending on the eye and visible form, ear-consciousness on the ear and sound, and so on — since feeling, perception, and formations, unlike the six consciousnesses, all arise simultaneously from the very same faculty and object at once, offering no comparable basis for treating them as sequential phases rather than as genuinely distinct, co-arising realities. The third fault points out that only material things, possessing genuine physical substance, can undergo the kind of gradual transformation the positional-phase theory would require — milk gradually curdling into yogurt, and yogurt gradually transforming further into clarified butter, precisely because matter persists through successive moments and can therefore change by degrees; mind, by contrast, being formless and lasting only a single instant before ceasing entirely, has no comparable window of persistence within which to gradually "become" feeling, then perception, then formations in turn — the lecture illustrating this with a small story about a woman who left milk with a friend for safekeeping, only to return days later demanding her milk back when she found it had curdled into yogurt instead, her friend insisting, reasonably enough, that the yogurt simply was what the milk had become — a transformation only possible because milk, unlike momentary mind, actually persists long enough to change. And the fourth fault points out that the very cause generating mind in the first place shows no internal differentiation whatsoever — mind arises simply through faculty and object meeting, a single, undifferentiated causal condition, so that no basis exists for supposing this same undifferentiated cause somehow generates a sequence of internally different "positions" within a single arising moment, when in that same single instant no differentiated cause can actually be found to explain why one moment would count as feeling, the next as perception, and the next as formations.

With this refutation now thoroughly established, the treatise's investigation into the aggregates reaches its true and final close, and turns at last to the elements — the second of the six domains of skillful understanding awaiting examination.

31. Section: A Lamp and Its Light — Scripture Itself Proves Mind and Mental Factors Co-Arise

The treatise concludes its refutation with a third line of argument: the "mind alone is real" position directly contradicts the Buddha's own recorded teaching. Scripture repeatedly states that craving, aversion, and delusion afflict and defile the mind, preventing liberation — language that only makes sense if these afflictions are themselves genuinely real phenomena existing alongside mind, rather than mind's own mere positional phases, since afflictions could hardly "afflict" a mind they were simply identical with. The treatise presses this same point against the fallback position that consciousness simply comes first, with mental factors following in sequence — this fails for the very same reason already established: no differentiated cause can be found within a single arising moment to justify treating what follows as somehow distinct from what precedes it. The treatise then cites scripture directly: three sense faculties, object, and consciousness meeting together give rise to contact, and simultaneously with that very same contact arise feeling, perception, and volition as well; other scriptures state plainly that these phenomena are constantly, inseparably conjoined, never to be analytically separated into something and its own later derivative. The Buddha himself, the treatise notes, illustrated this same inseparability through the simile of a lamp and its own light — light does not follow the lamp as some later, secondary phase, but shines the very instant the lamp is lit, the two utterly inseparable from their first appearance onward, exactly as mind and its mental factors arise together, never one first and the other second.

32. Section: Milk, Yogurt, and Clarified Butter — And a Sick Monk Healed by Realizing There Was Never a Self to Lose

The treatise addresses a second potentially troubling scripture, the Three Similes Discourse, where the Buddha describes milk transforming into yogurt and yogurt into clarified butter to illustrate three kinds of embodiment: in one place, beings take the coarse four elements themselves to be a self, as in the desire realm; in another, a mind-generated body arises, as in the form realm, where beings are born through mental transformation rather than through parents; and in another, formless perception itself gives rise to existence, as in the formless realm. The treatise asks pointedly: does this teaching really mean that desire-realm beings possess only the elements, form-realm beings only mind, and formless-realm beings only perception, with nothing else present at all? Of course not — each realm's inhabitants possess a complete set of aggregates just as any other realm's do; the Buddha simply highlighted whichever aspect most distinctively characterizes that particular realm, without thereby denying that mental factors accompany mind everywhere, in every realm. Every teaching, the treatise concludes, necessarily has this same kind of hidden dimension, since no single formulation can ever capture the whole of reality at once — emphasizing one feature inevitably leaves others temporarily unstated, not thereby denied. Having refuted the rival view from every angle, the treatise confirms its own conclusion in verse: the aggregates' fivefold structure would fail, the positional-phase theory carries four distinct faults, and the position contradicts scripture directly — mind and its mental factors are established, beyond doubt, as mutually corresponding realities. The lecture closes this section with the moving story of the ailing monk Assaji, whom the Buddha visited when gravely ill; asked how he felt, Assaji confessed regret — not over any broken precept, but because his illness now prevented him from re-entering the deep meditative absorption he had once relied on, leaving him anxious and bereft. The Buddha asked him directly: is this body you? No. Is there some other you apart from it? No. Do you dwell within it? No. Does it belong to you? No — and if form is truly without self in this way, the Buddha continued, so too are feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness; the genuine point of renunciation was never securing meditative absorption, but permanently severing craving, aversion, and delusion, and this task remains entirely open to Assaji regardless of whether he can still enter deep concentration. 33. Section: A New Beginning — The Eighteen Elements, and What Actually Constitutes the Eye-Element

The treatise formally opens its second major topic: the skillful understanding of the elements — the eighteen elements comprising the six sense faculties, their six corresponding objects, and the six consciousnesses arising from them, "element" here meaning cause, since the storehouse consciousness contains the seed of every phenomenon, and each element sustains its own distinctive character and its own causal relationship to its effect. The treatise opens with the eye-element specifically, asking what actually constitutes it, and answering: whatever seed capable of generating the eye faculty has not yet been permanently severed, or, even where such generation has already been permanently severed, whatever remains sustained by the life faculty still continuing — this second case describing an arhat who has already secured nirvāṇa with residue remaining, no future rebirth's eye faculty yet to come, yet whose present eye still functions for as long as this current life continues. Asked next how many of the eighteen elements are genuinely real and how many merely provisional, the treatise answers seventeen real, or alternatively twelve, depending on which of two counting methods is applied.

34. Section: A Young Monk's Joyful Death — The Four-Cornered Logic of Eye and Eye-Element

The treatise explains why seventeen of the eighteen elements count as real while only one, the mental faculty, counts as merely provisional: the six consciousnesses, the very instant one has just ceased, are collectively labeled "the mental faculty" purely as a convenient way of naming whatever immediately precedes and gives rise to the next moment of consciousness — no separate mental faculty exists apart from this same momentary cessation of the six consciousnesses themselves. Alternatively, twelve elements may be counted as real and six as provisional instead, if the six consciousnesses themselves are treated as merely the mental faculty's own expanded, sixfold unfolding, with no separate reality of their own apart from that same underlying faculty — either counting method being simply a different, equally valid way of drawing the same conventional boundary. The treatise then turns to a detailed, four-cornered analysis of the relationship between the eye faculty in its present, active sense and the eye-element in its broader, seed-based sense: some possess an eye without possessing eye-element, as an arhat's very last moment of eye-consciousness immediately before final nirvāṇa, since no further seed remains to generate any future eye; some possess eye-element without possessing an eye, as a being not yet born with a functioning eye, or one whose eye has already been destroyed, or an ordinary being reborn in the formless realm, where the underlying seed persists in the storehouse consciousness even though no physical eye exists to manifest it; some possess both, covering every ordinary case besides these; and some possess neither, as an arhat whose eye has already been destroyed with no seed remaining, or one already reborn formless and already liberated, or one who has already entered final nirvāṇa entirely. The lecture illustrates the first of these four cases with a moving story: a young monk, gravely ill soon after ordination, confessed to the visiting Buddha that he regretted his monastic path, not from any broken precept, but from fear of the further rebirth his own impending death would bring, having died before securing the noble path. The Buddha guided him through a simple chain of questions — does the eye give rise to eye-consciousness? Yes. Does eye-consciousness, in turn, give rise to feeling? Yes. Without an eye, would eye-consciousness arise? No. Without eye-consciousness, would such feeling arise? No — and concluded: then why still cling to the very faculties whose absence would end this same chain of craving and feeling entirely? Hearing this, the young monk died at once, joyfully, his features growing radiant and at peace even as his illness had left him haggard — and the Buddha told his anxious companions there was no need to prepare for any future rebirth at all, since this same teaching had brought the dying monk to arhatship in that very last moment, entering final nirvāṇa directly, his own eye at that instant becoming eye without eye-element, no further eye ever to follow.

38. Section: Which Elements Span All Three Realms, Which Are Grasped by Life, and Their Scriptural Synonyms

The treatise completes its account of realm-confinement: no element belongs exclusively to both the form and formless realms while excluding the desire realm, but three elements — the mental faculty, mental consciousness, and the phenomena-element — extend across all three realms without exception. 39. Section: Six Meanings of "Element" — And Why Even Nirvāṇa and Space Earn the Name

The treatise opens its second major division within this analysis: the meaning of the term "element" itself. Asked what this meaning actually is, the treatise answers with six senses. Cause means simply serving as the basis other phenomena depend upon, as earth, water, fire, and wind serve as the basis for derived matter. Seed means the capacity to directly generate further phenomena bearing a different character from oneself, as form aggregate generates its own effects, and so through consciousness. Inherent nature means the timeless, beginninglessly established reality underlying all phenomena — emptiness itself, the true nature abiding beneath every conditioned thing, from which every specific phenomenon in turn arises. Potential for realization means the capacity, latent within the storehouse consciousness since beginningless time, to eventually attain nirvāṇa — the very seed of the three vehicles' own liberation, present in some beings and absent in others. Subtlety means whatever remains not yet ripened into its own visible result, still latent as potential rather than yet manifest. And sustaining means the capacity to support and maintain some further result, as derived matter is sustained by the elements that produce it. The treatise then turns to a further question: why nirvāṇa and space themselves are also called "elements," despite being unconditioned, when this same sixfold analysis was developed specifically for conditioned phenomena. It answers that both qualify through this same sixth sense, sustaining: nirvāṇa sustains the permanent non-arising of suffering, and space sustains the very possibility of a body's own limbs and eyes moving and functioning at all — both, in their own distinct way, actively supporting something else, and thereby earning the name "element" even without being generated causes or seeds themselves.

40. Section: Why Eighteen Specifically, and Six Further Ways of Slicing the Same Elements

The treatise confirms why nirvāṇa and space earn the name "element": nirvāṇa sustains the permanent non-arising of suffering, granting the practitioner who attains it a cool, unshakeable freedom no longer subject to any of the three realms' own painful results, while space sustains the very possibility of the eye perceiving form and the body moving from place to place, neither function possible without room enough for them to occur. The treatise then asks what purpose establishing these eighteen elements specifically actually serves, answering that it reveals two things: the causal relationship linking each element to what depends on it, and the actual functional relationship between a sense faculty, its object, and the resulting experience of pleasure or pain — of the full six general meanings already explained, only two, seed and sustaining, are actually what ground the eighteen elements' own specific establishment here. 41. Section: Does the Eye Really See? Ultimate Truth Says No, Conventional Truth Says Yes

The treatise closes its second major division with a pointed question: when it is said that the eye sees form, or that mind understands phenomena, does this mean the sense faculties themselves are what actually see or understand, or does it mean their corresponding consciousnesses are? Yet in the conventional sense, the treatise continues, it remains entirely proper to speak of the eye as "seeing" and the other faculties similarly, precisely because the faculties themselves represent the single most prominent, most readily identifiable factor among everything actually involved in the process — ordinary people naturally and reasonably designate whichever element stands out most clearly as the agent of an action, even though, examined closely, no single agent was ever really there at all.

42. Section: Sequence at Last — Why the Eye Is Named First, and the Buddha's Three Ways of Teaching

The treatise closes its second division by clarifying why ordinary language still properly says "the eye sees": whenever the sense faculties are intact, their corresponding consciousnesses reliably arise, and even where a particular faculty happens to be damaged, the underlying capacity for consciousness to arise again, given the right conditions, is never actually destroyed — a damaged eye, once repaired, can see again precisely because the seed for eye-consciousness was never truly lost, only its physical support temporarily impaired. The deepest truth here, the treatise stresses, is simply that seeing itself occurs, with no additional, separately findable "seer" standing behind that seeing at all. The treatise then opens its third and final division within this extensive analysis: the sequence in which the eighteen elements are conventionally listed, offering two explanatory schemes. The first, a threefold scheme, orders the eighteen according to what depends on what: the six sense faculties as what consciousness depends upon, the six objects as what consciousness takes as its concern, and the six consciousnesses themselves as what jointly depends on both faculty and object together. The second, a sixfold scheme, offers six distinct reasons for the specific order in which the faculties and objects are listed. The first reason follows the sheer scope of each faculty's own activity: the eye perceives the widest range of objects, hence it is named first, followed by the ear, nose, tongue, and body in decreasing order of their own perceptual range. The second reason follows the natural sequence of ordinary social life: people first see one another with their eyes, then greet each other with words heard by the ear, then share a meal savored by nose and tongue together, and finally, as evening falls, settle into soft bedding felt by the body — a sequence that, quite naturally, orders the five faculties eye, ear, nose and tongue together, and body last. And the third reason follows the varying degree of pleasure each object provides, ordinary beings' own preferences among the five senses further reinforcing this same conventional sequence. The lecture pauses here to note the Buddha's own three distinct modes of teaching: speaking entirely from his own realized wisdom, comprehensible only to another buddha; speaking entirely according to his audience's own capacity, comprehensible even to the simplest being; and speaking a blend of both, agreeing with ordinary people's own conventional views wherever those views are sound, and departing from them only where genuine correction is needed — this same threefold approach, the lecture suggests, underlying why the elements' own conventional sequence sometimes tracks ordinary social custom so closely.

43. Section: Cosmetics, Daily Routine, and the Widening Reach of Each Faculty — Reasons Four Through Six

The fifth reason looks to the ordinary rhythm of a working life: people first occupy themselves with bodily and verbal action, whether wholesome or unwholesome, in pursuit of life's necessities; only then do they eat their meals; and only once fed and satisfied do they turn to sensory indulgence — a sequence of livelihood, nourishment, and pleasure that likewise orders color and sound before smell and taste, and smell and taste before touch. 44. Section: Two Hard Questions — Why Form-Realm Beings Still Have a Nose and Tongue, and Whether Thought Persists Above the Second Absorption

Having established the eighteen elements' own definition, extent, and sequence, the treatise now addresses objections. The first asks: since beings born into the form realm have already become free of desire for smell and taste as objects, why do they still possess the nose and tongue elements at all? The treatise gives two reasons. First, having a nose and tongue simply makes the body appear complete and dignified — a form-realm being with only eyes, ears, and a body, but no nose or mouth, would hardly look presentable, so the nose and tongue elements remain even though their corresponding consciousnesses go unused. Second, more technically, form-realm beings have become free of desire only for the objects smell and taste themselves, not for the physical sense faculties as such — desire for the faculties, as opposed to their objects, persists a while longer, and this is why the nose and tongue elements themselves still arise. The second hard question concerns the second absorption and above, traditionally described as free of both coarse examination and fine reflection (尋伺): if the five sense-consciousnesses (which do involve coarse examination when accompanied by mind-consciousness) must be active for a being to perceive anything at all, how can the second absorption and above be called free of examination and reflection? And if those sense-consciousnesses are not active there, how could form-realm beings above the first absorption still perceive their own realm's objects through their intact physical faculties at all? The treatise answers that seeds for examination-and-reflection-accompanied consciousness continue to accompany the very absorption that is itself free of examination and reflection; emerging from that absorption, such consciousness can indeed become active again, and once active, it in turn continues to be accompanied by seeds for the examination-free absorption — so this level of absorption is never absolutely, exclusively free of examination and reflection, but merely predominantly so, admitting occasional exceptions upon emerging from deep stillness.

45. Section: Two Eyes, One Seeing — Closing the Hard Questions with the Lamp-Flame Analogy

Three further objections close this discussion. The first asks why the eye, ear, and nose each have two parts while the tongue and body have only one — answered first by simple bodily symmetry and dignity, and, as other treatises add, because paired faculties sharpen perception itself: two eyes yield depth perception where one could not, and two ears together locate a sound's direction. The second, more technical objection asks whether a single consciousness arises from the two parts of a paired faculty, or two separate consciousnesses — answered firmly that only a single consciousness ever arises, because whichever of the two parts happens to be unobstructed and undamaged is the one through which clear cognition occurs, while damage or obstruction in either part alone yields unclear cognition without at all implying two separate consciousnesses; consciousness itself, moreover, being formless, occupies no spatial location the way physical form does, so it cannot literally be split into left-part and right-part the way the paired faculty itself can. Four historical schools' worries about this very point are surveyed — whether two simultaneous consciousnesses arise, whether one consciousness shifts from one part to the other, whether one consciousness splits in half, or whether one consciousness somehow spans both — each alternative shown to lead to an absurd consequence, leaving only the treatise's own account intact regardless of how far apart the two eyes of some cosmically enormous being might be. The third and final objection concerns the eye faculty and eye-consciousness themselves: if they stand in a cause-and-effect relationship, how can they exist simultaneously, since a seed must ordinarily precede the sprout it produces? The treatise answers that the eye faculty is not eye-consciousness's generating cause in the way a seed generates a sprout, but merely its supporting cause — a distinction it illustrates with the simile of a lamp's flame and the light that flame gives off: the two arise together, inseparably, the light utterly dependent on the flame yet never temporally preceding it, exactly as eye-consciousness depends on, yet coexists with, the eye faculty that supports it. The same reasoning is then extended, without further elaboration needed, across ear, nose, tongue, and body and their own respective consciousnesses.

46. Section: Which Elements Definitely Arise in Which Realm — Desire, Form, and Formless

The treatise's fourth and final objection-topic turns to a wholly different question: across the three realms of existence, which of the eighteen elements definitely arise, and which only sometimes arise? In the desire realm, the eye, ear, nose, and tongue elements, together with their corresponding consciousnesses, do not definitely arise — some beings are born blind, deaf, or otherwise lacking a given faculty from birth. The body element, by contrast, definitely arises for every desire-realm being without exception, as do body-consciousness, mind, mind's object (the dharma-element), mind-consciousness, and all five external objects (color, sound, scent, taste, and touch) — ten elements definite, eight indefinite. In the form realm, by striking contrast, the eye element definitely arises for every being born or matured there, since form-realm beings are all spontaneously born complete, never gradually developing through a womb-stage the way desire-realm beings do, and so never suffer the accidents of birth that leave a faculty missing; the same holds for ear, nose, tongue, and body, and for eye-, ear-, and body-consciousness. Only scent, taste, and their corresponding nose- and tongue-consciousness fail to arise there at all, since form-realm beings no longer take solid food — fourteen of the eighteen elements definitely arise, the remaining four never arising. In the formless realm, having entirely transcended form, only mind, mind's object, and mind-consciousness definitely arise; all fifteen form-related elements fail to arise at all, apart from the rare exception of a being with mastery over meditative absorption manifesting form through that very mastery.

47. Section: Rāhula's Patient Path to Arhatship, and the Opening Definition of "Sense-Field"

The treatise closes its skillful understanding of the elements simply by noting that, exactly as with the aggregates, a summary verse gathering all its key points could likewise be composed here, though the treatise itself does not bother spelling it out again, having already demonstrated the method — and the same holds for the sense-fields' own summary verse still to come. Before turning to the sense-fields themselves, the lecture recalls a story from the Saṃyukta Āgama: the young monk Rāhula once asked the Buddha to teach him something he could take to a quiet place and contemplate single-mindedly toward realizing the noble path. The Buddha, sensing that Rāhula's liberating wisdom had not yet matured enough to bear a higher teaching, asked instead whether he had ever taught others the five aggregates of clinging — Rāhula said he had not, so the Buddha told him to go teach others exactly that, which he dutifully did. Returning again to ask for the path, the Buddha again found him not yet ready, and asked whether he had taught others the six sense-fields — again not yet, so Rāhula went and taught that too. Returning a third time, the Buddha found him still not ready, and asked whether he had taught dependent origination — again not yet, so Rāhula taught that as well. Only after all three of these — the aggregates, the sense-fields, dependent origination — had been taught to others, and Rāhula, sent to sit alone and reflect on all he had learned and taught, came to realize for himself that every one of these teachings flows toward, tends toward, and ultimately abides in nirvāṇa, did the Buddha judge his liberating wisdom finally ripe enough to receive the teaching on impermanence itself — hearing which, Rāhula went to a quiet place, practiced calm and insight with full dedication, and attained arhatship. The lecture offers this story as its own reminder of why these very six kinds of skillful understanding are worth studying so carefully: their object is itself pure, their study brings joy, dispels wrongdoing, cultivates goodness, and opens the way to genuine understanding, exactly as it did for Rāhula himself. The sense-fields' own analysis then opens with the same method used throughout: first defining precisely what the eye sense-field is — that faculty which has already been attained and not abandoned, and which continues without interruption as a not-yet-ceased phenomenon, still capable of giving rise to its consciousness — with the same definition extended, without further elaboration needed, to the remaining five sense-fields of ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind.

48. Section: Sense-Field Versus Field-of-Contact, and the Four Ways an Eye May or May Not Be a "Sense-Field"

The treatise next distinguishes "sense-field" from a closely related term, "field-of-contact." A sense-field, as just defined, is simply that faculty which, having been attained, remains uninterrupted and capable of generating consciousness. A field-of-contact is instead defined through its relationship to the mental factor of contact: any of the six sense-fields that operates together with contact, or that continuously gives rise to and accords with the various feelings of pleasure, pain, and neutrality that contact brings about. The treatise then applies a fourfold logical analysis to the relationship between "eye" and "sense-field" themselves, asking whether every eye is a sense-field, and whether everything called a sense-field is an eye. First, there can be an eye that is not a sense-field: an eye that has been attained and not yet lost, yet which is, in that very moment, about to cease without further continuation — as with an arhat's very last eye just before entering final nirvāṇa without remainder, its capacity to give rise to consciousness effectively spent, even though the physical eye itself has not yet actually perished. Second, there can be a sense-field that is not an eye: any of the remaining five sense-fields — ear, nose, tongue, body, or mind — each abiding in its own sense-field nature without being the eye at all. Third, there can be something both an eye and a sense-field together: an eye that has been attained, not abandoned, and which continues uninterrupted rather than being on the verge of ceasing — the ordinary, functioning case. And fourth, there can be something neither eye nor sense-field: an eye never attained at all, or once attained but subsequently lost or destroyed, together with any of the other five faculties failing, in that moment, to abide in its own sense-field nature.

49. Section: Six Meanings of "Sense-Field," and the Three Conditions It Exists to Reveal

Continuing the fourfold logic, the treatise next asks the reverse question: is every sense-field a field-of-contact, and is every field-of-contact a sense-field? The answer is asymmetric — every field-of-contact is necessarily a sense-field, but not every sense-field is a field-of-contact, since a faculty can continue its own uninterrupted continuum without actually meeting an object in the way that generates contact. This is illustrated with two striking cases: form-realm beings' own nose and tongue faculties, which continue to exist yet, since that realm has no solid food and hence no scent or taste objects to encounter, never actually engage in contact at all; and, more strikingly still, the six faculties of beings born into the mindless heaven, where consciousness itself never becomes active throughout that entire lifespan, so that even though all six faculties remain physically intact, none of them ever functions as a genuine sense-field capable of generating consciousness, let alone contact. The treatise then asks a deeper question: what is the very meaning of the term "sense-field," and what purpose does establishing this category serve? Six overlapping meanings are given — a sense-field is that gateway through which mind and its mental factors themselves grow and develop; that condition upon which they depend; the preparatory means by which they arise, though not their generating cause; that which, in combination with its object, gives rise to contact; that upon which mind and its mental factors constantly rely; and that dwelling-place in which mind and its mental factors abide while making use of their respective objects. Ultimately, the treatise explains, the entire category of "sense-field" exists to reveal three kinds of condition recognized elsewhere in this treatise's own analysis of causation: the immediately-preceding condition, the object-condition, and the dominant condition — the inner six sense-fields serving as dominant condition, the outer six as object-condition, and mind's own immediately preceding moment as the immediately-preceding condition, together giving rise to each of the six consciousnesses and their accompanying mental factors.

50. Section: The Sense-Fields Complete — Meditative Attainments Folded In, and the Same Dependency as the Elements

The treatise's remaining three topics close out the skillful understanding of the sense-fields entirely. First, an extensive analysis paralleling the elements' own — how many sense-fields have form and how not, and by what reasoning their conventional sequence is taught — is simply left to be inferred by analogy with the elements' own already-completed treatment, rather than spelled out afresh. Second, the treatise turns to what the twelve sense-fields subsume: the Buddha's own teaching on the eight forms of meditative mastery and the ten meditative totalities, both taught in detail elsewhere in this very treatise; the four formless meditative bases beginning with the sphere of infinite space; and, more simply, the twofold grouping of the mindless meditative attainment together with the state of neither-perception-nor-non-perception — all of these, however variously scripture may name them, are shown to fall entirely within the range of the twelve sense-fields, since each depends on mind, which is itself the mind sense-field, and each takes an object, which is itself the phenomena sense-field, so that nothing named in any of these lists ever actually falls outside this twelvefold framework. Third and finally, the sense-fields' own relationship of dependency is said to parallel exactly what was already explained for the elements — form-realm beings' nose and tongue sense-fields existing chiefly for bodily dignity, and so forth — so that discussion, too, need not be repeated. With this, the skillful understanding of the twelve sense-fields stands complete, the treatise having now finished two of the six kinds of skillful understanding named at this chapter's very opening — the aggregates and the elements — and a third, the sense-fields, brought here to its own close in turn.

51. Section: A Fourth Skillful Understanding Opens — What Dependent Origination Actually Says About the Self

Before opening the treatise's fourth kind of skillful understanding, the lecture pauses to explain dependent origination itself: the principle that phenomena arise dependent on conditions, that their impermanent arising and passing is never random but always follows a fixed pattern given a fixed set of conditions, a principle so fundamental that it holds true whether or not any buddha ever appears in the world to discover it — it is simply how things are, the abiding nature of phenomena, the ever-standing character of the world itself. Its classic formula is this: because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises — nothing whatsoever exists in some absolute, independent way, everything existing only relative to something else. The actual chain of phenomena produced through such conditions — ignorance conditioning formations, formations conditioning consciousness, consciousness conditioning name-and-form, and onward through the six sense-fields, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, existence, birth, and finally old age and death, that whole great mass of suffering — is called "dependently arisen phenomena," dependent origination itself being the underlying principle and dependently arisen phenomena being its concrete manifestation, the Buddha's own greatness lying simply in having discovered, verified, and revealed this principle to living beings; to see dependent origination, it is said, is to see the Dharma, and to see the Dharma is to see the Buddha. The treatise now formally opens this fourth topic by asking what, precisely, counts as a "dependently arisen phenomenon": one that has no ruling self behind it, no agent who acts, no recipient who receives its results, no real independent function of its own, and no freedom or autonomy whatsoever — arising strictly from causes, relying on a gathering of conditions, originally nonexistent, then existent, and, once existent, ultimately dispersing again. This entire twelvefold chain, the lecture stresses, describes only the aggregates, sense-fields, and elements in their own perpetual flux — examined carefully, no truly existing "being," no self such as the ones we habitually cling to as "I" or "you," can ever actually be found within it at all.

52. Section: Cause-Aspect and Effect-Aspect, Real and Provisional Among the Twelve Links

The treatise next distinguishes two aspects within this same twelvefold chain: considered as cause, it is called dependent origination itself; considered as effect, it is called dependently arisen phenomena. Because ignorance's own underlying latent tendency remains unbroken and continues to exist, ignorance's own active manifestation comes to exist; because that active manifestation of ignorance arises, the formations that depend on it become active in turn — the unbroken seed serving as cause is what "dependent origination" names, while the seed's resulting manifestation is what "dependently arisen phenomena" names, and precisely the same reasoning is then extended, link by link, through the whole remaining chain from formations through old age and death. The treatise then distinguishes which of the twelve links are ultimately real and which merely provisional: existence, birth, and old-age-and-death are provisional, since "existence" simply names the combined potential of formations, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-fields, contact, and feeling, already established as seeds, while "birth" and "old age and death" simply name shifting phases within the manifestation of that same combination — mere labels for a process, not separately real entities in their own right. The remaining nine links, from ignorance through feeling, together with craving and grasping, are by contrast genuinely real. The treatise closes this opening topic with a fivefold framework for organizing the entire twelvefold chain by its function in producing suffering: the basis for what draws suffering into being, the basis for what actually generates suffering, the drawing-cause itself, the generating-cause itself, and the arising of suffering proper — the first of these, the basis for the drawing-cause, is explained through name-and-form's own conditioning of the six sense-fields, which becomes the object and support for ignorance's own foolish clinging to an inner self within those very six faculties, exactly the confused self-view that constitutes ignorance in the first place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the treatise's own related discussion elsewhere of "drawing cause" and "generating cause" to unpack this fivefold framework further: ignorance and formations are the drawing cause, drawing into being the resulting consciousness, name-and-form, six sense-fields, contact, and feeling that follow; craving, grasping, and existence are then the generating cause, actually generating the next life's birth and old-age-and-death — the whole cycle repeating itself with no single, findable self steering any part of it. He treats the real-versus-provisional distinction among the twelve links as a useful reminder that even within a framework devoted to refuting a fixed self, some of the framework's own terms are themselves only provisional labels rather than separately existing things — "birth" and "old age and death," in particular, naming phases of an already-established process rather than pointing to anything newly real in their own right.

53. Section: Completing the Fivefold Suffering-Cause Framework — Why Birth and Old Age Deserve the Name "Suffering"

The treatise completes its fivefold framework for organizing the twelve links. The basis for the generating-cause of suffering is explained through contact conditioning feeling: an unlearned, ordinary being, upon contact with an object, experiences some feeling — pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral — and, relying on that very feeling, gives rise to craving for external objects; craving in turn conditions grasping, and grasping conditions existence, so that contact conditioning feeling itself becomes the shared basis for craving, grasping, and existence, all three serving as generating-causes of future suffering. The drawing-cause of suffering proper is then simply named as the seven links running from ignorance conditioning formations through contact conditioning feeling — consciousness, in the present life, having been permeated by wholesome, unwholesome, or unshakeable meditative karma, its resulting seeds drawing forth a future body's consciousness and the rest, and hence future birth, old age, and death. The generating-cause of suffering proper is named as feeling conditioning craving, craving conditioning grasping, and grasping conditioning existence — "existence" here simply naming karmic seeds, already drawn forth, now further nourished and taken hold of by affliction, on the verge of manifesting as a future life. And the very arising of suffering itself is named simply as existence conditioning birth, and birth conditioning old age and death. The treatise closes this topic by explaining precisely why birth and old age-and-death deserve to be called "suffering" at all: the very same seeds of consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-fields, contact, and feeling that were earlier called the drawing-cause of suffering, once they actually manifest as a concrete result, receive the new names "birth" and "old age and death" — and, having now yielded their result, are for the first time properly called "suffering," since a cause, however inevitably it leads to suffering, is not itself yet suffering until its result has actually ripened. With this the treatise's analysis of dependent origination by way of all twelve links closes, and it turns next to examining the same twelvefold chain through its three specifically afflictive links alone: ignorance, craving, and grasping.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing explanation of why birth and old-age-death alone earn the name "suffering" as a subtle but important point: the seven links from ignorance through feeling are already the cause of suffering, already inevitable in their result, yet are not themselves called "suffering" until that result actually ripens — cause and effect kept scrupulously distinct even while their connection is treated as certain. He notes that this fivefold framework, having now organized the entire twelve-link chain around the single practical question of how suffering is drawn into being and then actually generated, gives a meditator a genuinely useful map for practice: contact-conditioning-feeling named twice, as the shared hinge on which both the drawing-cause and the generating-cause of suffering both turn — precisely where, he notes, mindfulness of feeling has always been taught as the most direct point of intervention in the entire chain.

54. Section: What Ignorance Actually Is — Refuting the View That It Is Merely "Absent Wisdom"

Having examined dependent origination through all twelve links together, the treatise turns to examine it through its three specifically afflictive links — ignorance, craving, and grasping — beginning with ignorance itself. The treatise first states plainly: ignorance, that which genuine wisdom is meant to counter, is its own distinct mental factor, whose essential nature is to obscure and conceal; it is neither the mere absence of wisdom nor a distorted form of wisdom, contrary to a rival position holding that "ignorance" simply names the lack of clear insight, or perhaps a wrong kind of insight, rather than any positive factor of its own. The treatise marshals several arguments against this rival view. First, if ignorance were nothing more than the mere absence of wisdom, it could never be described, as scripture in fact describes it, as coming in mild, middling, and intense grades, since a mere absence has no positive qualities capable of admitting degrees at all — beings in the formless realm possessing only a mild grade, form-realm beings a middling grade, and desire-realm beings the most intense grade. Second, on that same rival view, ignorance could never be distinguished into latent seed and active manifestation either, since a mere absence remains identically "absent" at all times, admitting no such distinction. Third, since ordinary beings' minds are said, on that rival view, to be perpetually devoid of undefiled wisdom at every single moment, every one of their minds — wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral alike — would have to count as afflicted without exception, collapsing the very distinction between wholesome, unwholesome, and neutral mental states that the treatise elsewhere relies on throughout. Fourth, a mere absence of something is neither a conditioned nor an unconditioned phenomenon in its own right, and so could never properly be called either defiled or pure — yet ignorance is unmistakably treated throughout the teachings as a genuinely defiled factor, which a bare absence could never be.

Nor could latent seed and active manifestation be distinguished for it, since a phenomenon without positive nature has an identical character at all times, admitting no distinction between manifestation and latent binding. Also, since an ordinary being's mind — wholesome, defiled, or neutral — is at all times devoid of the light of undefiled wisdom, if this mere absence were ignorance, every mind whatsoever would become defiled. Also, a phenomenon without positive nature is neither conditioned nor unconditioned; being subsumed under neither, it can be neither defiled nor pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this refutation as a model of careful philosophical method: rather than simply asserting that ignorance is a real, positive mental factor, the treatise works through four separate consequences that would follow if the rival "mere absence" view were true — no possible grades, no possible seed/manifestation distinction, universal defilement, and no possible defiled/pure distinction — each one plainly contradicting what is otherwise accepted, and each therefore counting as evidence against the rival view in turn. He notes the citation, from a related sūtra, of ignorance's three grades mapped directly onto the three realms — mildest in the formless realm, most intense in the desire realm — as a vivid way of making an abstract philosophical point concrete: even liberation from coarse sensory craving, as in the higher realms, does not mean liberation from ignorance itself, only a lessening of its intensity, with even arhats, the lecture notes elsewhere, still carrying a subtle trace of it in the form of cognitive obscuration until buddhahood alone finally leaves it behind entirely.

55. Section: Six Faults Close the First Refutation, and a New Rival View — Is Ignorance Simply "Wrong Wisdom"?

Two final faults close the refutation of "ignorance is merely absent wisdom." Fifth, if a mind's continuum were, on that rival view, permanently identical with the mere absence of undefiled wisdom at all times, undefiled wisdom itself could never arise at all within that continuum — an absurd consequence, since undefiled wisdom plainly does arise once genuine insight develops. Sixth, that same rival view could never account for ignorance actually being extinguished, since extinguishing requires something with a genuine, positive nature capable of ceasing — a mere absence, having nothing positive to it in the first place, has nothing that could ever be said to cease, yet Buddhist teaching everywhere describes the extinguishing of ignorance as the very gateway to liberation. With all six faults now assembled — no possible three grades, no seed/manifestation distinction, universal defilement, no defiled/pure distinction, undefiled wisdom's impossible arising, and ignorance's impossible cessation — the treatise concludes that ignorance must indeed be its own distinct, genuinely existing mental factor, not a mere absence of something else. The treatise then turns to a second rival view: perhaps ignorance simply is "wrong wisdom" — a distorted form of cognition rather than any lack of cognition. Before refuting this view, the treatise first presses it for greater precision, posing three possible readings in turn: does "wrong wisdom" mean every kind of wisdom except the undefiled wisdom possessed by those still in training toward liberation; does it mean specifically the defiled wisdom bound up with the five wrong views; or does it mean any wisdom whatsoever that happens to be associated with some affliction? Taking up the first reading, the treatise raises an immediate objection: if every non-undefiled kind of cognition in an ordinary being's mind-stream counted as "wrong wisdom" and hence as ignorance, then no ordinary being's mind could ever be wholesome or neutral — every moment of thought would have to be defiled without exception, which plainly contradicts the everyday fact that ordinary beings do experience wholesome and neutral mental states, not only defiled ones.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sixth and final fault — that a mere absence has nothing capable of ceasing — as the refutation's own clearest capstone: the entire path to liberation is described everywhere in terms of extinguishing ignorance, and a position that cannot even make sense of that basic description has already disqualified itself. He notes that the treatise's move from refuting "ignorance is absent wisdom" to next examining "ignorance is wrong wisdom" reflects a real historical debate, not a merely hypothetical one — different schools of Abhidharma analysis genuinely held each of these positions, and the treatise works through each with the same careful, question-first method throughout. He flags the three-way clarifying question posed here as itself worth remembering, since each of the three readings will receive its own distinct refutation in what follows, rather than being dismissed together as a single package.

56. Section: Why Wrong Views and Ignorance Are Not the Same, and Why Afflicted Wisdom Cannot Be Ignorance Either

The treatise takes up the second reading of "wrong wisdom" — that it means specifically the defiled, wrongly-grasping wisdom bound up with the five wrong views: the view of a self within the aggregates, the extreme view of permanence or annihilation, wrong view proper, view-attachment, and attachment to wrong precepts and observances. Against equating this defiled view-wisdom with ignorance itself, the treatise first distinguishes their characteristic activity: failing to truly comprehend the real nature of phenomena is, precisely, ignorance itself; but wrongly clinging to some fixed characteristic of those phenomena, on the basis of that very failure to comprehend, is instead what constitutes "view" — the two operate together, ignorance always underlying the various wrong views, yet remaining distinguishable from them, as when self-view arises through ignorance's own power by clinging to the aggregates as "I" and "mine," each of the other wrong views similarly clinging wrongly to its own particular object, all of them, note, still never separate from ignorance's own underlying confusion, even while being their own distinct phenomenon. Because ignorance and the wrong views have genuinely distinct characteristic activities, calling these five defiled views themselves "ignorance" does not hold up. A further objection presses the same point from a different angle: were view and ignorance truly identical, the Buddha would never have listed them as two separate latent tendencies among the seven fundamental latent tendencies, nor would scripture, anywhere at all, ever apply the term "ignorance" directly to any of the wrong views. Turning to the third reading — that "wrong wisdom" means wisdom associated with any affliction whatsoever — the treatise raises three more objections: first, since the five wrong views are themselves said to have wisdom as their essential nature, and two distinct instances of wisdom could never coexist and correspond within a single moment of mind, this reading would make the wrong views permanently unable to ever correspond with ignorance at all, which is plainly false, since view and ignorance are everywhere said to arise together; second, if greed and the other afflictions could, through their own force, turn an associated wisdom into ignorance itself, then greed's own force ought to be capable of producing ignorance directly, when in fact the relationship runs the other way — ignorance itself leads and gives rise to greed and the rest, not the reverse; and third, wisdom associated with any other affliction is called defiled precisely because of that association, not because its own essential nature is somehow ignorance itself — an essential nature that is not itself the nature of confusion cannot properly become "ignorance" simply by keeping bad company.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the ignorance/view distinction drawn here as one of this fascicle's more psychologically precise moments: ignorance is simply not-knowing, a kind of blindness, while view is the further, active step of confidently grasping some wrong idea on the basis of that blindness — related, inseparable in practice, yet analytically distinct, much as failing to see a rope in the dark is different from actively believing it to be a snake. He treats the third reading's rebuttal — that ignorance leads affliction, not the other way around — as restating a point this fascicle has emphasized before: ignorance stands at the very root of the whole afflictive process, itself never derived from any other affliction, however entangled the two may become once affliction is already underway.

57. Section: The Poisoned Spring, Ignorance Confirmed as Its Own Reality, and What "Formations" Actually Means

Two final points close the third objection against equating afflicted wisdom with ignorance: wisdom associated with any other affliction becomes defiled only through that very association, its own essential nature remaining wisdom, so it cannot be forced into the nature of confusion merely by keeping bad company; and this holds regardless of which affliction it happens to be associated with. With all three rival readings of "wrong wisdom" now refuted, the treatise concludes decisively that ignorance is its own distinct mental factor, genuinely associated with mind, not reducible to any absence or any distortion of some other faculty. The lecture pauses here to offer a memorable parable from the Āgamas illustrating exactly why craving and grasping behave the way they do despite everyone already knowing better: a thirsty traveler comes upon a cool, inviting spring by the roadside; another traveler warns him the water is poisoned, but, too thirsty to care, he drinks anyway, thinking only to satisfy his thirst first and worry about consequences later — and dies from the poison just as warned. The Buddha compares this directly to ordinary beings caught in birth and death: told repeatedly that the five sensory pleasures are poisoned, they crave them anyway, thinking only to indulge first and deal with consequences later, and so remain bound to the wheel of birth and death precisely as the thirsty traveler was bound to his own poisoning. With ignorance's own nature settled, the treatise turns next to the second afflictive link, formations, citing the Buddha's own threefold division: bodily formation, verbal formation, and mental formation. Bodily formation is defined two ways — first as the very breath itself, the wind that enters and leaves the body, since breath, by its nature of subtle movement, is what actually sets the body into motion in the first place; and second as bodily action itself, since a confused being first stirs the breath's own motion before ever acting on that stirring by committing a defiled bodily deed. The same reasoning extends to the other two: since breath gives rise to bodily action, it is called bodily formation; coarse and fine mental examination, being what actually makes speech possible, is called verbal formation; and feeling and perception, which invariably accompany the mental act of intention, are called mental formation — together, these three are simply named body, speech, and mind formation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the poisoned-spring parable as this entire discussion's own emotional payload after so many pages of dense technical refutation: everyone already "knows" sensory craving leads to suffering, exactly as the thirsty traveler already knew the water was poisoned, yet the pull of immediate thirst overrides that knowledge every time — precisely the gap between knowing and actually being free that the whole twelve-link chain exists to close. He notes this parable is how many of the Buddha's own disciples in the Āgamas are described as finally breaking through to realization, sitting with this exact image until craving's own irrational logic became undeniably clear to them. He treats the definition of bodily formation as breath itself as a small but practically useful teaching in its own right: breath, being the very thing that sets the body moving in the first place, is why breath awareness has always served as such a natural entry point for meditation on the body and its formations.

58. Section: Birth Belongs to Whatever Realm It Falls Into, and Sorting the Twelve Links by Karma and Affliction

The treatise briefly addresses the birth link: whatever realm or meditative stage a being is reborn into, the existence link that precedes that birth is understood to belong to that very realm or stage — rebirth in the desire realm carries desire-realm existence, rebirth in the form realm carries form-realm existence, and so forth, existence being simply the karmic seeds, already nourished by craving and grasping, standing closest to the birth they are about to produce. The treatise then turns to a new topic: how the twelve links are subsumed under the three broader categories of affliction earlier established for this whole discussion — karmic action, affliction itself, and suffering. Two of the twelve links, formations and existence, are subsumed under karmic action, since both are fundamentally activities of body, speech, or mind, whether currently being enacted or already accumulated as seeds awaiting fruition. Three links — ignorance, craving, and grasping — are subsumed under affliction itself, these being the twelvefold chain's own three specifically afflictive members already examined at length across the preceding several sections. The remaining seven links — consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-fields, contact, feeling, birth, and old age-and-death — fall, by elimination, under the category of suffering, being simply the resulting experience that karmic action, driven by affliction, inevitably produces. This threefold sorting gives the entire twelve-link chain a compact summary: affliction produces karmic action, karmic action produces suffering, and suffering itself, through craving and grasping, produces further affliction all over again — the very engine of birth and death's own endless repetition.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold sorting — affliction, karmic action, suffering — as the single most useful summary of the entire twelve-link teaching offered anywhere in this fascicle: rather than memorizing twelve separate links in sequence, a student can instead see the whole cycle as simply affliction producing action, action producing suffering, and suffering, through renewed craving and grasping, producing affliction all over again, an endlessly self-perpetuating loop with no beginning point and no external cause driving it from outside. He notes that this same threefold framework will likely recur as the treatise continues sorting the twelve links by other criteria in what follows, and recommends holding onto it as a stable reference point for whatever more detailed classification comes next.

59. Section: Drawing-Cause Versus Generating-Cause, and Two Rounds of Cause and Effect Across Twelve Links

The remaining seven links — consciousness through old age-and-death — are confirmed to fall under the category of suffering, giving a simple summary verse: three links belong to affliction, two to karmic action, and seven together make up the singular path of suffering that beings tread lifetime after lifetime. The treatise then offers a more detailed classification of the same three categories. Within karmic action, formation specifically serves as the drawing-cause, while existence specifically serves as the generating-cause. Within affliction, ignorance alone is what actually triggers the drawing-cause, while craving and grasping together are what actually trigger the generating-cause — ignorance drawing forth the seeds of a future result, craving and grasping then nourishing those seeds until they actually generate that result. Within the suffering category itself, a further distinction applies: birth and old age-and-death are provisionally established labels for a future result not yet arrived, while the other five links — consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense-fields, contact, and feeling — do double duty, serving simultaneously as the seed-cause of a future suffering-result not yet ripened, and, in their present active manifestation, as this very life's own already-ripened suffering-result, arising from causes sown in a previous life. The treatise closes this topic with a final cause-and-effect sorting cutting across all these categories: the five present-result links together with the two future-result links are together called the "result" side of dependent origination, while the remaining five links — ignorance, formations, craving, grasping, and existence — are called the "cause" side, this whole framework mapping neatly onto the classic two-lifetime, single-round model of causality long taught in this tradition, where a drawing cause and its drawn result belong to one span of cause-and-effect, and a generating cause and its generated result belong to the next.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this section's layered classifications as building toward a single coherent picture rather than piling up distinctions for their own sake: the same twelve links get sorted first by broad category — affliction, action, suffering — then by function within each category — drawing versus generating — then by temporal role within the suffering category itself, each pass revealing a different facet of the same underlying process. He highlights the closing two-lifetime model as this fascicle's own way of reconciling what can otherwise look like competing accounts of the twelve links: rather than twelve separate moments strung out over many lives, the whole chain maps onto just two connected life-spans, a drawing cause and its result in one, a generating cause and its result carrying into the next — a framework he notes recurs throughout this tradition's broader treatment of karma and rebirth.

60. Section: The Five Things Ignorance Generates, Starting with Doubt About Who We Really Are

The treatise now turns to an extensive examination of ignorance itself, beginning by naming, in brief, five things that not-knowing generates as their cause: doubt, craving, misplaced faith, wrong views, and overestimation. Doubt arises simply because not understanding the true nature of things leaves room for uncertainty about them. Craving arises because not knowing that all conditioned, defiled phenomena are illusory and insubstantial leaves an opening for all manner of attachment to take root. Misplaced faith arises when confidence is placed in something outside genuine teaching altogether — believing, say, in some heterodox doctrine having nothing to do with liberation. Wrong views arise because not-knowing gives rise to mistaken doctrines such as eternalism or annihilationism, or a distorted view of causality — every wrong view ultimately tracing back to some underlying ignorance. And overestimation arises when confused self-assessment leads someone to claim an attainment they have not actually reached — mistaking, for instance, the first meditative absorption for the first stage of sagehood, not through complete absence of any practice at all, but through a basic confusion about what has and has not actually been accomplished. The treatise then examines the first of these five in detail: doubt. Ignorance concerning the prior period, the later period, or both together is what actually generates doubt — doubts of the kind classically described in scripture as spanning all three times: did I exist in the past, or did I not? The treatise clarifies this terminology precisely — the past is called the "prior period," the future the "later period," and the present, which stands as the later period relative to the past and as the prior period relative to the future, is accordingly called "both the prior and later period" together. Doubt about the past accordingly arises from not-knowing about the prior period; doubt about the future arises from not-knowing about the later period; and doubt directed inward, at oneself — whose body is this, who am I really, where has this being come from, and where will it go once it perishes here — arises from not-knowing about both periods together, since such doubt spans past origin and future destination in a single breath.

If one doubts the past, this doubt should be understood as generated by not-knowing the prior period. If one doubts the future, this doubt should be understood as generated by not-knowing the later period. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lingers on the existential doubts named here — who am I, where did I come from, where am I going — as questions every reflective person eventually asks, and treats the treatise's own diagnosis as genuinely striking: these are not questions awaiting some further factual answer, but questions that only arise at all because of a prior, more basic not-knowing about how a self is falsely constructed onto the aggregates in the first place. He cross-references the twenty forms of self-view catalogued elsewhere in this tradition's compendium literature — form is the self, the self possesses form, form belongs to the self, the self dwells within form, and so on for feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness in turn — as the fuller technical background against which this section's own brief mention of "whose is this, who am I" should be read. He also flags overestimation as worth remembering precisely because it is not describing a fraud or a liar, but a sincere practitioner sincerely confused about their own progress — a caution, he suggests, worth taking personally by any serious meditator, not only by scholars analyzing the chain of dependent origination from a safe philosophical distance.

61. Section: How Ignorance Breeds Craving, and a Modern Story of Trading One's Eyes for Thirty Years of Fortune-Telling

The treatise turns to ignorance's second generated effect, craving. Not knowing one's own inner body, not knowing the outer world of sights, sounds, and the rest, and not knowing other beings at all generates, respectively, inner craving, outer craving, and inner-outer craving, together with craving for future existence, craving bound up with delight in what is already possessed, and craving that eagerly seeks after what is not yet possessed — inner craving being simply self-attachment born of not seeing through the body's selflessness, outer craving being attachment to sensory objects born of not seeing through their illusory, insubstantial nature, and inner-outer craving being attachment to other beings, as when affection or resentment fastens onto family and associates without recognizing that no fixed "other person" can ultimately be found there either. Every one of these cravings, the treatise stresses, traces back to the same root: failing to see that all conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a dewdrop, a flash of lightning, and so falsely clinging to both a self and a solid world of objects. Ignorance's third generated effect is misplaced faith — not knowing karma, not knowing its ripened results, and not knowing the connection between the two leads beings, through their own confusion about their own actions, to place devout trust and refuge in deities entirely outside the proper object of genuine faith, seeking wealth or heavenly rebirth from spirits who have themselves never severed a single affliction, possessing at most some minor supernatural power, willing to help when it suits them and just as willing to withdraw that help — or worse — when it does not. The lecture illustrates this with a story from modern Taiwan: a fortune-teller in Kaohsiung struck a bargain, thirty years earlier, with a spirit that agreed to make his predictions unfailingly accurate for exactly thirty years, in exchange for his eyes at the end of that term; he accepted, reasoning he would have made more than enough money by then, and indeed his fortune-telling proved remarkably accurate and lucrative for three decades — until the appointed day arrived, the spirit took his eyes exactly as promised, and he was left both blind and permanently unable to read fortunes again. Precisely this kind of not knowing karma and its results, the lecture notes, leads so many people, even today, into exactly this sort of bargain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fortune-teller story as a genuinely contemporary illustration of an ancient warning: the spirit in question was never claimed to be enlightened or even particularly benevolent, only mildly powerful and entirely self-interested, exactly the kind of being the treatise warns against placing ultimate trust in — help offered on a timer, and just as easily withdrawn, or worse, once that timer runs out. He connects the sixfold classification of craving here — inner, outer, inner-outer, future-existence, delight-bound, and eagerly-seeking — back to the treatise's own much earlier, more technical discussion of the same six kinds of craving, this passage simply tracing each one back to its own specific variety of not-knowing as root cause. He notes the recurring theme across this entire section: whether the object is one's own body, the external world, other people, or the workings of karma itself, in every single case the same underlying not-knowing is what actually generates the resulting affliction — nothing here is arbitrary or separately caused.

62. Section: Sixty-Two Wrong Views, and the First Kind of Overestimation — Starving, Burning, or Leaping to Reach Heaven

Ignorance's fourth generated effect is wrong views: not properly understanding the Three Jewels, nor the four noble truths, generates self-view, and, based on self-view, the full sixty-two wrong views catalogued elsewhere in the canon — views that go on to assert, among other things, that giving yields no merit, that there is no such thing as love bearing fruit, that no action produces any corresponding result, that honoring one's parents brings no benefit whatsoever, and so on through every variety of nihilistic and eternalistic error, all traceable to this same root failure to properly understand the Three Jewels and the four truths, and to take refuge in them, rather than simply resting content with whatever visions or experiences happen to arise in meditative stillness and drawing wild, self-serving conclusions from them. Ignorance's fifth and final generated effect is overestimation, which the treatise divides into two kinds. The first concerns mistaken methods for attaining heavenly rebirth: not knowing the true causes of things, nor which resulting phenomena are themselves wholesome or unwholesome, leads beings to develop entirely mistaken notions about what actually leads to a fortunate rebirth, and to act with great confidence on those mistaken notions — starving oneself to death, throwing oneself into fire, or leaping from a high cliff, imagining these self-destructive acts to be genuine methods for reaching heaven, simply because the true relationship between wholesome and unwholesome causes and their pleasant or unpleasant results is not properly understood. The lecture notes how natural such errors can feel without the guidance of genuine teaching — inherited customs of animal sacrifice for good fortune, or a widespread assumption that a good person becomes, after death, a protective ghost cared for by descendants — none of these being any less confused, in their own way, than the more extreme ascetic practices of self-starvation or self-immolation that some traditions in India pursued in the mistaken belief that this life's suffering, once exhausted through such deliberate torment, would guarantee heavenly rebirth in the next.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the self-starvation, self-immolation, and cliff-leaping examples not as exotic historical curiosities but as extreme versions of an error still common today in milder form: whenever anyone assumes that suffering endured now automatically banks credit toward a better outcome later, without any actual understanding of which causes truly produce which results, the same basic confusion is at work, however far short of these extremes the actual behavior falls. He notes that the treatise's harshest judgment falls not on the sincerity of these seekers — many clearly believed, with real devotion, that they were doing something spiritually meaningful — but on the sheer mismatch between their chosen method and any genuine causal path to their stated goal, precisely the gap that not-knowing karma and its results always opens up. He flags this first kind of overestimation, concerning the path to heaven, as the milder of the two the treatise is about to distinguish — a second, subtler kind, concerning the very fruits of the contemplative path itself, follows next.

63. Section: Venerable Sudarśana's Rainmaking Pride — Mistaking Meditative Power for Arhatship

The treatise's second kind of overestimation concerns something far subtler than mistaken paths to heaven: mistaking a genuine spiritual attainment for one it is not. Not truly understanding what actually occurs when the six faculties meet their six objects — that all such experience is conditioned, impermanent, without any real substance or self, illusory rather than solid — while nonetheless already having achieved considerable meditative stillness, easily leads to prematurely believing one has attained the fruit of a genuine contemplative — reaching a certain depth of absorption and mistaking it for full liberation from every affliction, when the underlying seeds of affliction have merely been suppressed for a time by concentration's own force, not actually uprooted at their source. The lecture illustrates this with a well-known story from the biography of King Aśoka: a monk in Kashmir named Sudarśana had attained the four mundane meditative absorptions and the five worldly super-knowledges, and, being able to reliably bring rain during droughts, came to believe himself an arhat. Wishing to correct this mistaken confidence, the venerable Upagupta caused a twelve-year drought to strike the kingdom of Mathurā; when the terrified populace begged him for rain, he told them he himself could not bring it, but that a monk named Sudarśana in Kashmir was known for exactly this power. Sudarśana, invited by messengers, flew to Mathurā by his own meditative power and brought the rain the moment the grateful people asked him to, whereupon he received such lavish offerings that he found himself thinking, with quiet pride, that even Upagupta's own following could not match what he had just received — and then, catching himself in that very thought, immediately reasoned that a true arhat could never feel such conceit, and so realized, to his own shock, that he could not actually be an arhat after all. He went at once to Upagupta seeking proper instruction; Upagupta rebuked him sharply for defying the Buddha's own prohibition against monks performing rainmaking displays, and for the pride that had followed from it, at which Sudarśana repented wholeheartedly — and, receiving genuine instruction at last, actually attained arhatship.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Venerable Sudarśana's story as this entire fivefold discussion's most memorable cautionary tale, precisely because he was not a fraud at all — his meditative powers were genuinely real, his rainmaking genuinely effective — yet even so, a single unguarded moment of pride was enough to reveal that his own self-assessment had been mistaken all along. He highlights Sudarśana's own catching of himself — reasoning, upon noticing his pride, that a true arhat simply could not feel this way, and so concluding correctly that he was not one — as itself a small model of exactly the discernment this entire passage is trying to teach, turned inward on oneself rather than merely outward toward others' claims. He connects this story to a further citation from a related sūtra on worldly versus transcendent wisdom: the very same practice, whether the four foundations of mindfulness or the six perfections, becomes worldly wisdom the moment it is grasped at and turned into something to claim as one's own accomplishment, and only becomes genuinely transcendent wisdom when practiced without any such grasping or sense of personal attainment at all — the deepest reason, he suggests, why overestimation can arise even in someone as accomplished as Sudarśana clearly was.

64. Section: Four More Downstream Harms, from Denying All Cause to Wasted Effort on the Path

The treatise completes its account of the five defilements' own downstream consequences. Craving's own defilement draws forth all the great suffering of future birth, old age, sickness, and the rest — an inevitable consequence of clinging to one's own life and its objects. Misplaced faith's own defilement, rooted in devotion misdirected toward an improper object, leads to claiming either that life arises from no cause whatsoever, or that some unequal cause — a creator god, or similar — is life's true and proper cause, thereby denying any real efficacy to human effort altogether, whether for good or for ill, as though heaven alone decided everyone's fate regardless of what anyone actually does. Views' own defilement leads a person, confident that their own thinking is supreme, to act on whatever impulse arises without restraint, freely committing every kind of unwholesome deed, and thereby draws forth the suffering of the miserable realms to come — freedom in the act of creating the cause, but considerably less freedom once its result arrives. And overestimation's own defilement causes a person's sincere effort to yield either a result entirely different from the one intended, or no result at all: someone genuinely trying to reach heaven through starvation or self-immolation instead falls into the miserable realms, an utterly different result from the one sought, while someone genuinely practicing the four foundations of mindfulness, yet doing so with an underlying grasping after attainment, achieves at most a mundane meditative state, all sincere effort toward genuine liberation entirely wasted. With all five defilements and their downstream harms now fully accounted for, the treatise notes that everything else belonging to the skillful understanding of dependent origination has already been extensively explained elsewhere in this very treatise's earlier discussion of the stages associated with examination and reflection, and will not be repeated here — any remaining aspects of this topic appear again only much later, in this treatise's own compendium of textual explanations, for students wishing to pursue the subject in still greater depth.
