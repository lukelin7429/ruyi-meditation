The Embrace of the Factors of Enlightenment Begins

Fascicle 97 opens the embrace of the factors of enlightenment (菩提分法擇攝), the fourth and final of sūtra matters' four great divisions, following directly upon the dependent-origination, food, truths, and elements section closed entirely in fascicle 96. Its own general summary verse names nine great topics across eleven and a half particular verses: the four foundations of mindfulness, the four right efforts, the four bases of supernormal power, the five faculties, the five powers, the seven factors of enlightenment, the eightfold path, mindfulness of breathing, the trainee and the adept beyond training, and the four confirmed purities. This fascicle opens the four foundations of mindfulness's own first particular verse, distinguishing the true renunciant, the true path, and the true finality that only the inner dharma possesses — the four causes and conditions by which the noble eightfold path and its ultimate fruit exist within the Buddha's teaching alone, and nowhere among the outside paths.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 97 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: The Embrace of the Factors of Enlightenment Begins — Concluding What Came Before, Introducing What Follows

Thus has been explained the embrace of dependent origination, food, the truths, and the elements. The embrace of the factors of enlightenment, I shall now explain.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the embrace of the factors of enlightenment (菩提分法擇攝), the fourth and final of sūtra matters' four great divisions (己四), decisively explaining the Saṃyukta Āgama's teaching on the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, beginning with concluding what came before, introducing what follows (結前生後) — the fascicles' own account of 攝事分 (spanning fascicles 85 through 100) divides sūtra matters into four embraces by subject: the aggregates' embrace (行擇攝, fascicles 85–88, 178 sūtras), the sense-bases' embrace (處擇攝, fascicles 89–92, 285 sūtras), the dependent-origination, food, truths, and elements' embrace (緣起食諦界擇攝, fascicles 93–96, 296 sūtras, now closed entirely), and the factors of enlightenment's embrace (菩提分法擇攝, fascicles 97–98, 405 sūtras) — the largest of the four, now opening. This fascicle is the first of its two, hence its subtitle "the fourth, part one" (第四之一).

Relying on the dominant power of cultivating the four foundations of mindfulness, by four causes and conditions, one should know that the inner dharma has the path of the renunciant, and has finality; the outside dharmas definitely have neither the path of the renunciant, nor finality. 5. Section: Explaining Each Mark Individually Begins — Briefly Explaining All of It: The Four Causes and Conditions

What are called the four causes and conditions? First, relying on four places, one attains the four confirmed wisdoms; second, one is liberated from four kinds of external secondary affliction; third, the inner dharma's disciples and the outside paths' disciples belong to different classes; fourth, the inner dharma's great teacher and the outside paths' teachers belong to different classes.

Chinese Source

云何名為四種因緣？一者、依止四處，得四證智故；二者、解脫四種外隨煩惱故；三者、內法弟子與外道弟子不同品類故；四者、內法大師與外道師不同品類故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are called the four causes and conditions? First, relying on four places, one attains the four confirmed wisdoms; second, one is liberated from four kinds of external secondary affliction; third, the inner dharma's disciples and the outside paths' disciples belong to different classes; fourth, the inner dharma's great teacher and the outside paths' teachers belong to different classes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining each mark individually (別釋相), the second of the renunciant's matters, beginning with briefly explaining all of it (略釋一切), a fourfold structure, beginning with the four causes and conditions (四種因緣) — first, the four confirmed wisdoms arise relying on four places: the place of respite where the three fetters are permanently severed, the place of power where one never falls back, the place fixed toward the enlightenment-destined class, and the place of at most seven more rebirths; second, freedom from four kinds of external affliction — in this life, in future lives, from mutual contradiction, and from being unable to teach or awaken others to the noble truths; third, unlike outside-path disciples, who fall through hearing into mere attachment, through thinking into defiled clinging, and through cultivation into further clinging, settling into either the extreme of eternalism or the extreme of nihilism, hoarded and guarded over a long night of habituation, the inner dharma's disciples walk the middle way, free of both extremes; fourth, outside-path teachers, though claiming to teach the severance and full knowledge of every clinging, are unable to actually establish the true severing and full knowledge of clinging — only the inner dharma possesses both the path of the renunciant and its finality. Each of these four causes will be unfolded in fuller detail in what follows.

8. Section: Listing

First, the renunciant of the supreme path; second, the renunciant of the expounded path; third, the renunciant who lives by the path; fourth, the renunciant who defiles the path.

Chinese Source

一者、勝道沙門，二者、論道沙門，三者、命道沙門，四者、汙道沙門。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, the renunciant of the supreme path; second, the renunciant of the expounded path; third, the renunciant who lives by the path; fourth, the renunciant who defiles the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens listing (列), the third — the renunciant of the supreme path has realized the ultimate truth itself; the renunciant of the expounded path teaches and expounds the noble path without necessarily having fully realized it; the renunciant who lives by the path has devoted their entire life to the eightfold path's cultivation, whether or not they teach it to others; and the renunciant who defiles the path, though present within the Buddhist community, cultivates precepts, concentration, and wisdom in a corrupted, mistaken way.

9. Section: Pointing to Fuller Explanation, Closing "The Inner Dharma's Renunciant" Entirely

These four renunciants, whether briefly or extensively explained, are as already distinguished in their marks in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Chinese Source

是四沙門若略若廣，如聲聞地已辯其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

These four renunciants, whether briefly or extensively explained, are as already distinguished in their marks in the Śrāvaka Ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 披尋記 and the Śrāvaka Ground

With this, pointing to fuller explanation (指) closes entirely, closing the inner dharma's renunciant (內法沙門4: 徵/標/列/指) entirely. Per the 披尋記, citing the Śrāvaka Ground: the well-gone ones (buddhas and arhats) are the renunciants of the supreme path; those who teach the true dharma are the renunciants of the expounded path; those who cultivate wholesome conduct are the renunciants who live by the path; and those who practice wrongly are the renunciants who defile the path — with fuller detail given elsewhere: trainees and adepts beyond training, having destroyed every affliction severed by seeing and cultivation with the uncontaminated path, are renunciants of the supreme path; in the absence of a buddha or bodhisattvas diligently cultivating for enlightenment's sake, śrāvaka elders who preserve the three canonical baskets, sustaining the conventional dharma and discipline and keeping the wheel of the true dharma turning without interruption, are renunciants of the expounded path; ordinary beings whose nature has grown well-tamed, who diligently cultivate right conduct for their own benefit, possessing shame and remorse, delighting in genuine study, and capable of attaining what is not yet attained, touching what is not yet touched, and realizing what is not yet realized — such beings, possessing the warmth of the preparatory stage capable of nurturing the life-root of noble wisdom, are called renunciants who live by the path, since their path is alive, not dead and unmoving; and those who break the precepts, commit much evil, and falsely claim to practice the holy life without actually doing so — having destroyed the very root of the path from the start, powerless and incapable, not a vessel for the path's arising, unable to attain it even when a genuine teacher and a genuine realizer of the path stand right before them — are renunciants who defile the path. The World-Honored One is said to have declared, of these same four kinds, that they alone exist, and that outside the śramaṇas' and brāhmaṇas' own teachings, no such renunciant can be found at all.

13. Section: Distinguishing Its Absence Within Outside Doctrines, Closing "Clarifying Its Establishment," "Explaining," and "The Inner Dharma's Path" Entirely

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With this, distinguishing its absence within outside doctrines (簡外法無) closes entirely, closing clarifying its establishment (明施設2: 唯內法有/簡外法無) entirely, closing explaining (釋2: 出體性/明施設) entirely, closing the inner dharma's path (內法道2: 徵/釋) entirely — since outside paths never establish the genuine eightfold path in the first place, they lack even the possibility of a "defiled" version of it, let alone the supreme, expounded, or living kinds of renunciant that depend on the path's own prior existence.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

16. Section: Explaining Begins — The Four Supporting Places

The four places are: first, the place of respite where the three fetters are permanently severed; second, the place of power belonging to the dharma of never falling back; third, the place fixed toward the class destined for enlightenment; fourth, the place of conduct bound to at most seven more rebirths.

Chinese Source

謂四處者，一、三結永斷蘇息處，二、無退墮法勢力處，三、定趣菩提種類處，四、極七反有隨行處。

Ru-Yi Translation

The four places are: first, the place of respite where the three fetters are permanently severed; second, the place of power belonging to the dharma of never falling back; third, the place fixed toward the class destined for enlightenment; fourth, the place of conduct bound to at most seven more rebirths.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing 瑜伽論記

This opens explaining (釋), the second, beginning with the four supporting places (四依處) — the stream-enterer who, at the fifteenth moment of the path of seeing, permanently severs the view of a real body, clinging to rites and vows, and doubt, reaches respite (per the 瑜伽論記, this corresponds to the truth of cessation); their noble path now has power enough never to regress back into an ordinary being; they are fixed, never falling to the evil destinies, toward the fruit of exhaustion and non-arising wisdom; and their remaining rebirths are capped at seven, cycling between the human and heavenly realms before arhatship is reached — four marks of merit belonging to the stream-enterer's own severance of affliction.

20. Section: Explaining Begins — Distinguishing the Afflictions: The First External Secondary Affliction, Present-Dharma

One should know that, here, the various outside paths, lacking the foundations of mindfulness, lose their mindfulness and dwell without right knowledge. Experiencing feelings — whether pleasant, painful, or neither — they give rise to defilement toward the pleasant, aversion toward the painful, and delusion toward the neither. This is called the first external secondary affliction, in the present dharma.

Chinese Source

當知此中，諸外道類闕念住故，其念忘失，不正知住，領納諸受或樂、或苦、或非苦樂，於樂起染，於苦起恚，於非苦樂發起愚癡。如是名為第一現法外隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

One should know that, here, the various outside paths, lacking the foundations of mindfulness, lose their mindfulness and dwell without right knowledge. Experiencing feelings — whether pleasant, painful, or neither — they give rise to defilement toward the pleasant, aversion toward the painful, and delusion toward the neither. This is called the first external secondary affliction, in the present dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens explaining (釋), the third, beginning with distinguishing the afflictions (辨煩惱), a fourfold structure, beginning with the first external secondary affliction, present-dharma (第一現法外隨煩惱) — without the foundations of mindfulness (contemplating the body's impurity, feeling's suffering, mind's impermanence, and dharmas' selflessness), mindfulness lapses and every feeling provokes craving, aversion, or delusion in turn.

21. Section: The Second External Secondary Affliction, Later-Dharma

Because of such defilement, aversion, and delusion, taking feeling as condition, craving for future existence arises; taking craving as condition, every kind of clinging arises; because of craving and clinging, taking clinging as condition, existence is brought about; extensively said, up to the accumulation and growth of the great mass of pure suffering. This is called the second external secondary affliction, in a later dharma.

Chinese Source

彼由如是染恚癡故，以受為緣，生後有愛；以愛為緣，發生諸取；有愛、取故，以取為緣，成辦於有；廣說乃至純大苦聚積集增長。如是名為第二後法外隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because of such defilement, aversion, and delusion, taking feeling as condition, craving for future existence arises; taking craving as condition, every kind of clinging arises; because of craving and clinging, taking clinging as condition, existence is brought about; extensively said, up to the accumulation and growth of the great mass of pure suffering. This is called the second external secondary affliction, in a later dharma.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the second external secondary affliction, later-dharma (第二後法外隨煩惱), the second — feeling's affliction, left unresolved, drives the entire causal chain of dependent origination onward through craving, clinging, and existence, into future birth, aging, and death's whole mass of suffering.

22. Section: The Third External Secondary Affliction

Further, the various outside paths, taking the view of a real body as their root, hold every kind of distinct view and doctrine — since their intentions differ from one another, they turn, in succession, into mutual contradiction. This is called the third external secondary affliction.

Chinese Source

又諸外道，薩迦耶見以為根本種種見趣，意各別故，彼此展轉互相違戾。是名第三外隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the various outside paths, taking the view of a real body as their root, hold every kind of distinct view and doctrine — since their intentions differ from one another, they turn, in succession, into mutual contradiction. This is called the third external secondary affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens the third external secondary affliction (第三外隨煩惱), the third — every outside doctrine, rooted in the same view of a real self, still generates the sixty-two speculative views and endless mutual disputation, since these views were never pursued for truth's own sake but only to defeat a rival.

23. Section: The Fourth External Secondary Affliction, Closing "Distinguishing the Afflictions" Entirely

Further, the various outside paths, throughout the entire scope of the four noble truths, are not even able to establish their own teaching, much less awaken to them. Therefore, although they may gain some degree of dominance in wisdom within their own school and lineage, they are, in truth, without knowledge, fallen into the domain of ignorance. This is called the fourth external secondary affliction.

Chinese Source

又諸外道，遍於一切四聖諦中，尚無有能施設其教，況當覺悟。是故彼於自師宗智雖得增上，而實無知，墮無明趣。是名第四外隨煩惱。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, the various outside paths, throughout the entire scope of the four noble truths, are not even able to establish their own teaching, much less awaken to them. Therefore, although they may gain some degree of dominance in wisdom within their own school and lineage, they are, in truth, without knowledge, fallen into the domain of ignorance. This opens explaining (申二釋2), beginning with refuting what is wrong (酉一遮非) — one should not speak or teach amid noisy disturbance, nor blurt out unconsidered words in haste, nor address listeners who are hungry, thirsty, exhausted, or ill, nor address listeners whose posture or bearing signals disrespect for the teaching (the Prātimokṣa Sūtra details such improper postures — teaching one whose head is covered, or wrapped, or who remains mounted, among others). The Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discussion frames these as three right times: when the listener is willing to hear, when the listener's decorum is proper, and when the listener's mind is free of agitation or defilement — such as one rushing to finish some other task, one serving another, or one engaged in killing or enmity.

95. Section: Measured Speech

Ru-Yi Translation

What is measured speech? It means that one's phrasing is complete and well-rounded; one speaks exactly as much as is truly needed, no more and no less; and one does not speak confused words devoid of meaning. Such kinds as these are called measured speech.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

This opens measured speech (未三應量語), the third — well-rounded phrasing that says exactly what the point requires, neither too much (exhausting the listener) nor too little (leaving the point unclear), and free of irrelevant, disordered digression.

98. Section: Being Inwardly Free of Excessive Pride at the Time of Finality Begins — Named Self-Knowledge

Toward wholesome dharmas such as faith that one does not actually possess, one does not give rise to excessive pride, claiming to possess them oneself. Toward what is slight, one does not exaggerate it into something vast. Only toward what is truly possessed, up to the full extent it is possessed, does one truly know it and declare oneself to possess it — this is therefore called self-knowledge.

Chinese Source

於己所無信等善法，不起上慢，謂為自有。於其狹小，亦不增益以為廣大。唯於實有乃至所有，如實了知自稱言有，故名自知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Toward wholesome dharmas such as faith that one does not actually possess, one does not give rise to excessive pride, claiming to possess them oneself. Toward what is slight, one does not exaggerate it into something vast. Only toward what is truly possessed, up to the full extent it is possessed, does one truly know it and declare oneself to possess it — this is therefore called self-knowledge.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi, citing the 瑜伽論記 and 披尋記

This opens being inwardly free of excessive pride at the time of finality (辰二於究竟時內離上慢2), the sixth of delight's seven dharmas, beginning with named self-knowledge (巳一名自知) — one neither claims wholesome qualities (faith, precepts, learning, generosity, wisdom, and the rest) one does not possess, nor inflates a small attainment into a great one, but honestly acknowledges exactly what has, and has not, actually been achieved. The 瑜伽論記 numbers this the sixth of the seven dharmas to be known (knowing the dharma, its meaning, the time, the measure, the assembly, oneself, and high from low); the 披尋記 explains that self-knowledge is verified through three marks — by way of its basis, by way of its own nature, and by way of its season — detailed further in the Śrāvaka Ground's decisive discussion.
