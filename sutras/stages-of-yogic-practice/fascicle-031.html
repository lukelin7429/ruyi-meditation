Fascicle 31 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十一 · The Third Yogic Process Continues

Dependent Origination, the Elements, and the Breath

Fascicle 31 continues directly from fascicle 30, completing the third of the third yogic process's five loci — one-pointedness of mind — with the remaining three of five objects for purifying conduct: contemplating dependent origination as the antidote to delusion, elemental analysis as the antidote to pride, and breath-counting as the antidote to scattered thought. It then opens the fourth locus, purifying the obstructions to concentration.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: fascicle 31 complete Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 31 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Dependent Origination Contemplation Opens — No Creator, No Fixed Self, No First Cause

Fascicle 31 opens exactly where fascicle 30 closed, moving to the third of the five objects for purifying conduct: contemplating dependent origination, the antidote to delusion. Its opening teaching is a direct refutation of every rival account of how the world and living beings come to be.

Chinese Source

云何勤修緣起觀者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依緣性緣起增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正了知如是如是諸法生故，彼彼法生；如是如是諸法滅故，彼彼法滅。此中都無自在、作者、生者、化者，能造諸法；亦無自性、士夫、中間能轉變者，轉變諸法。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates dependent-origination contemplation investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on conditioned dependent origination, one is able to correctly know that because such-and-such phenomena arise, such-and-such other phenomena arise; and because such-and-such phenomena cease, such-and-such other phenomena cease. Herein there is no sovereign deity, no maker, no producer, no transformer able to fashion phenomena; nor is there any inherent nature, cosmic person, or intermediate power able to transform phenomena.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames dependent-origination contemplation as the antidote for delusion in its specifically philosophical form — not mere confusion, but active wrong view about how life and the world actually arise. He reads the fourfold denial here as a direct refutation of rival Indian schools' "unequal causation" theories: no all-powerful deity (refuting the claim that Śiva creates all beings, itself begging the question of who created Śiva); no fixed "maker," "producer," or "transformer" standing apart from the process itself; no unknowable "primal nature" (refuting Sāṃkhya's puruṣa-prakṛti dualism); no eternal cosmic "person" residing within the five aggregates as their unchanging owner; and no Great Brahmā, seated in the "intermediate" meditative state between the first and second dhyānas, shaping the universe from outside it while itself somehow arising from nothing at all. Against all of these, dependent origination offers a single formula — "because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises" — cited from the *Sūtra on Dependent Origination*, tracing the twelve links from ignorance through aging-and-death, and its reverse: extinguish ignorance, and the entire chain of suffering unravels link by link. Cháng Bǎi stresses that life, on this account, is never authored by anyone — it is simply conditions arranging and rearranging themselves, moment by moment, with no fixed owner anywhere in the process.

2. Investigating Substance: The Twelve Links, and Mind Itself as Empty of Fixed Existence

Chinese Source

復審思擇十二有支若內若外，而起勝解，是名尋思諸緣起事。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns the twelve links, both inner and outer, giving rise to a vivid understanding of them — this is called investigating the substance of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "inner" as the six sense faculties and six consciousnesses, "outer" as the six sense objects — contact between them, like sparks struck from rubbed wood, giving rise to consciousness's ceaseless activity, none of it fixed or substantial. He draws on the classical elephant-and-pool simile from the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*: no one sees the elephant itself bathing, only the muddied water and the footprints left behind, from which the elephant's passage is inferred — just so, mind itself can never be directly grasped as a fixed thing, only inferred from its effects (a spoken word, an angry gesture), the way fire cannot be found already hiding inside unstruck wood, yet reliably appears once the friction begins and vanishes again once it stops. He cites the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*'s teaching that consciousness is "merely a name, without any real substance behind the name," and Kumārajīva's rendering of the *Sūtra on Non-Action*, which even denies that "ordinary being" and "sage" are truths beyond convention. Cháng Bǎi's point: investigating dependent origination's substance means directly confronting that neither the world of objects nor the mind observing them holds up as something fixed and truly existing — both are simply the twelve links in motion, nothing more.

3. Section: Particular and Universal Characteristics — Impermanence, Suffering, Emptiness

The treatise now turns from what dependent origination's links are (substance) to their defining features, both individually (particular characteristic) and as a set (universal characteristic) — arriving at the three marks shared by everything conditioned.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：如是一切緣生諸行，無不皆是本無今有，有已散滅，是故前後皆是無常。皆有生老病死法故，其性是苦。不自在故，中間士夫不可得故，性空、無我。是名尋思緣起共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: all these conditioned formations, without exception, did not exist before and now exist, and having existed, will disperse and cease — hence, in every respect, they are impermanent. Because they all carry birth, old age, sickness, and death, their nature is suffering. Because they are not self-governing, and no intermediate cosmic person can be found within them, their nature is empty and without self. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes the particular characteristics of each of the twelve links are simply the detailed analysis already given in fascicles 9 and 10 (ignorance's nineteen kinds, the eight forms of consciousness, and so on), cross-referenced rather than repeated. The universal characteristic then draws three conclusions from that same material: since everything dependently arisen appeared where nothing existed before and will disperse again, it is impermanent through and through; since it carries birth, aging, sickness, and death, its nature is suffering (the *Compendium of Determinations*' verse: "phenomena cannot produce another, nor can they produce themselves — through the gathering of many conditions they arise, not as something old made newly present," and its counterpart on cessation, "phenomena cannot extinguish another, nor extinguish themselves — through conditions they arise, and having arisen, naturally cease"); and since nothing within the process is self-governing, and no eternal cosmic intermediary (as certain rival schools claimed) can be found running it from within, its nature is empty of any real self. Cháng Bǎi calls this the treatise's most direct route from "everything is conditioned" to "everything is empty" — the same three marks appear throughout Buddhist literature, but here derived specifically from dependent origination's own structure rather than asserted independently.

4. Category and Time: Delusion versus Clarity, and Impermanence Across the Three Times

Chinese Source

復審思擇：我若於彼無常、苦、空、無我諸行如實道理發生迷惑，便為顛倒，黑品所攝，廣說如前。若不迷惑，便無顛倒，白品所攝，廣說如前。是名尋思諸緣起品。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: if I become confused regarding the true principle that conditioned formations are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, this is a distortion, belonging to the dark category, as extensively explained before. If I am not confused, this is free of distortion, belonging to the bright category, as extensively explained before. This is called investigating the category of dependent origination.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：於過去世所得自體無正常性，如是已住；於現在世所得自體無正常性，如是今住；於未來世所得自體無正常性，如是當住。是名尋思諸緣起時。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: the self-existence obtained in past lives had no fixed, constant nature — it simply abided as such. The self-existence of the present life has no fixed, constant nature — it simply abides as such now. The self-existence to be obtained in future lives will have no fixed, constant nature — it will simply abide as such. This is called investigating the time of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats category here simply as whether one has actually absorbed the preceding teaching — confusion about impermanence, suffering, and no-self is the "dark" failure mode already detailed at length under contemplation of impurity, while clarity is its "bright" mirror. On time, he offers the film-reel image: a lifetime looks like a single continuous, stable thing the way a film looks like continuous motion, but both are really a rapid succession of discrete, momentary frames — the sense of stability is an illusion of speed, not a genuine persistence. Past, present, and future selves are all, without exception, this same flickering, momentary process; none of the three carries any more fixed reality than the others.

5. Section: Principle — No Fixed Actor, Two Causes, Two Fruits, and Dependent Origination's Close

The final and richest component, principle, works dependent origination through mutual dependence (both ultimate and conventional truth, and the causal mechanics of karma and rebirth), then briefly through function, valid proof, and natural law — closing the treatise's third purifying-conduct object.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：唯有諸業及異熟果，其中主宰都不可得，所謂作者及與受者，唯有於法假想建立。謂於無明緣行乃至生緣老死中，發起假想，施設言論，說為作者及與受者：有如是名、如是種、如是性、如是飲食、如是領受若苦若樂、如是長壽、如是久住、如是極於壽量邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: there is only karma and its ripened fruit, with no sovereign master to be found among them — no "actor," no "one who receives" — such things are only provisionally established with respect to phenomena. That is, within ignorance conditioning volitional formations, on through birth conditioning aging-and-death, one gives rise to provisional conceptions and sets up expressions, speaking of an "actor" and "one who receives": having such-and-such a name, such-and-such a lineage, such-and-such a family, such-and-such food, such-and-such experience of pleasure or pain, such-and-such a lifespan, such-and-such a duration, such-and-such a final limit to that lifespan.

Chinese Source

又於此中有二種果及二種因。二種果者：一、自體果，二、受用境界果。二種因者：一、牽引因，二、生起因。……由此二因增上力故，便為三苦之所隨逐，招集一切純大苦蘊。如是名依觀待道理，尋思緣起所有道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, herein there are two kinds of fruit and two kinds of cause. The two fruits: first, the fruit of one's own being; second, the fruit of experienced circumstances. The two causes: first, the impelling cause; second, the generating cause. … Through the empowering force of these two causes, one is pursued by the three kinds of suffering, gathering the entire mass of suffering. This is called investigating the principles of dependent origination by way of the principle of mutual dependence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi separates two levels at work in this passage. At the ultimate level, there is no real "actor" accumulating karma and no real "receiver" experiencing its ripened fruit — searching the five aggregates for such a fixed party turns up nothing, the way searching a body for pleasure "in here" or "out there" finds it nowhere at all. Yet at the conventional level, karma and its fruit remain entirely real and binding: a name, a lineage, a family, a lifespan, and everything else that makes up an ordinary biography are all provisionally, but functionally, real — quoting the *Compendium of Determinations*' verse that once the body dissolves, its "name-body" (mental factors) dissolves with it, yet karma made is still karma received in a future life, since actor and receiver are strictly "neither the same nor different": not the same, because the aggregates that acted and the aggregates that later receive the fruit are genuinely distinct moments; not different, because the later fruit could never have arisen without the earlier act as its cause. He then details the causal mechanics: the "two fruits" are one's own resulting existence (body and mind themselves, the ripened result of past karma) and the pleasant or unpleasant experiences that existence then encounters; the "two causes" are the impelling cause (ignorance and formations, planting the seed for a future rebirth, elaborated across seven links through feeling) and the generating cause (craving, grasping, and becoming, which actually moisten that seed into ripening — Cháng Bǎi tells the vivid folk tale of a man's spirit, distracted by an old lover's beckoning en route to a routine errand in the underworld, tumbling straight through rebirth as a piglet and then a snake before being violently retrieved, as a cautionary illustration of how quickly craving redirects a wandering mind toward its next birth). He closes briefly on the remaining three principles — function (sustained cultivation of this contemplation reliably severs delusion), valid proof (confirmed by the Buddha's own teaching, direct inner experience, and inference alike), and natural law (this causal relationship simply holds, not open to further debate) — closing dependent-origination contemplation, the third of the five purifying-conduct objects, in full.

6. Section: Elemental Analysis Opens — the Antidote to Pride

The fourth of the five purifying-conduct objects now opens: analyzing the body into its six constituent elements, the antidote to excessive pride and self-importance.

Chinese Source

云何勤修界差別觀者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依界差別增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正解了一切界義。謂種性義及種子義、因義、性義，是其界義。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates elemental-analysis contemplation investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on the differentiation of elements, one is able to correctly understand the meaning of "element" in every sense. That is, the meaning of lineage, the meaning of seed, the meaning of cause, and the meaning of nature — these are the meanings of "element."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the six elements this contemplation dissects the body into — earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness, the same six the classical texts say a being enters the womb dependent upon — and explains why this particular practice targets pride specifically: breaking the body down into six impersonal, lawfully arising constituents leaves no room for the sense of a single, superior "I" that pride depends on. He glosses "element" itself through four overlapping senses drawn from Buddhist technical vocabulary: lineage (a category or type, as in the ten dharma-realms of hell-being through buddha, each with its own causal lineage), seed (a causal potential capable of producing its own effect), cause, and nature — all four converging on the same point: whatever is called an "element" is defined entirely by its causal role, never by being some fixed, independent thing in itself.

7. The Six Elements' Substance and Their Particular and Universal Characteristics

Chinese Source

又正尋思地等六界內外差別，發起勝解，如是名為尋思界事。又正尋思：地為堅相，乃至風為輕動相，識為了別相，空界為虛空相、遍滿色相、無障礙相。是名尋思諸界自相。又正尋思：此一切界，以要言之，皆是無常乃至無我。是名尋思諸界共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates the inner and outer distinction among earth and the other six elements, giving rise to a vivid understanding — this is called investigating the substance of the elements. One further rightly investigates: earth has the mark of solidity … wind has the mark of light movement, consciousness has the mark of discernment, and the space element has the mark of empty space, pervading form, and being unobstructed. This is called investigating the particular characteristics of the elements. One further rightly investigates: all these elements, in brief, are impermanent, and so on through being without self. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of the elements.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recommends the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*'s detailed method for actually carrying out this analysis: beginning each morning with the hair about to be shaved, asking directly whether it is "self" — it was absent in the womb, its loss causes no grief, burning or discarding it causes no pain, so it is neither self nor belonging to self — and then applying the identical question, part by part, to teeth (an infant has none and is still a being; their eventual loss still leaves life possible), to limbs (accident or injury can remove them without erasing the person), working systematically through the whole body's solid matter (earth), then its fluids (water — is blood, replaceable by transfusion, "self"?), then its warmth (fire) and its motion (wind), then the space within it (its cavities and passages), and finally consciousness itself, both one's own and — even more obviously ungraspable — another's. Once the elements' particular characteristics have each been directly investigated this way, their shared universal characteristic follows immediately: every one of them, without exception, is impermanent, and therefore suffering, and therefore empty of any real self within it.

8. Section: Category, Time, and Principle — Elemental Analysis Closes

The final three components close elemental analysis: category exposes pride as the direct result of mistaking a mere aggregate for a real self; time traces the six elements through conception itself; and principle roots the entire practice in the classical teaching of entry into the womb.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：於一合相界差別性不了知者，由界差別所合成身，發起高慢，便為顛倒，黑品所攝，廣說如前。與上相違，便無顛倒，白品所攝，廣說如前。如是名為尋思界品。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: if one fails to understand the differentiated nature of the elements within what appears as a single unified mass, and, on account of the body formed by this combination of elements, gives rise to arrogance, this is a distortion, belonging to the dark category, as extensively explained before. The reverse of this is free of distortion, belonging to the bright category, as extensively explained before. This is called investigating the category of the elements.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：去來今世六界為緣，得入母胎。如是名為尋思界時。又正尋思：如草木等眾緣和合，圍繞虛空，數名為舍。如是六界為所依故，筋骨血肉眾緣和合，圍繞虛空，假想、等想施設言論，數名為身。復由宿世諸業煩惱及自種子以為因緣。如是名依觀待道理尋思諸界差別道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: in past, present, and future lives alike, the six elements serve as conditions for entry into the womb. This is called investigating the time of the elements. One further rightly investigates: just as grass, wood, and other conditions combine to enclose empty space and are provisionally called "a dwelling," so too, depending on these six elements, sinew, bone, blood, and flesh combine to enclose empty space, and through provisional, conventional conception one sets up the expression and provisionally calls it "a body." Further, this depends on karma and affliction accumulated across past lives, together with one's own seeds, as causes and conditions. This is called investigating the principles of the elements by way of the principle of mutual dependence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the "single unified mass" here the same way he earlier treated a car — a whole made only of assembled parts, mistaken for one real thing — and quotes the classical maxim "with no self within, I have no basis for pride; with no person without, no one else can wrong me" as the direct antidote arrogance calls for. On time, he cross-references the "Stage of Mind"'s teaching that conception requires three conditions present together — the mother's fertility, an intermediate-state being's presence, and its arising desire — and that these correspond exactly to the six elements: an unobstructed womb corresponds to the space element; unobstructed reproductive substances correspond to earth, water, fire, and wind; and the ālaya-consciousness's actual union with the fertilized substance corresponds to the consciousness element. A third condition, freedom from karmic obstruction, requires the same beings to actually share karma binding them as parent and child — explaining, he notes, why grievances about one's birth family are ultimately misplaced, since the relationship itself is jointly authored karma, not an accident inflicted by one party on the other. On principle, he extends the classical house-and-space analogy — a "house" is nothing but wood and space provisionally named — directly to the body itself, built of the same six elements enclosing the same kind of empty space, and provisionally called "a body" by the identical convention. He closes briefly through the remaining three principles (sustained practice reliably severs pride; the Buddha's teaching, direct meditative experience, and inference together confirm it; and the causal link between this practice and pride's cessation is simply a fixed law of nature), closing elemental analysis — the fourth of the five purifying-conduct objects — in full.

9. Section: Breath-Counting Contemplation Opens — the Fifth and Final Purifying-Conduct Object

The fifth and final object for purifying conduct now opens: mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath, the antidote for a scattered, discursive mind.

Chinese Source

云何勤修阿那波那念者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依入出息念增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正了知於入出息所緣境界，繫心了達，無忘明記，是阿那波那念義。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath, one is able to correctly know the object that is the in-breath and out-breath, binding the mind to it and comprehending it, without forgetting, held clearly in mind. This is the meaning of mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：入息、出息在內可得；繫屬身故，外處攝故，內外差別。如是名為尋思其事。又正尋思：入息有二，出息有二。若風入內，名為入息；若風出外，名為出息。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: the in-breath and out-breath can be found within the body — bound to the body, they belong to the inner sphere; yet they are also included within the outer sphere, hence there is an inner and outer distinction. This is called investigating their substance. One further rightly investigates: there are two kinds of in-breath and two kinds of out-breath. When the air enters within, this is called the in-breath; when the air exits without, this is called the out-breath.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies breath-counting as the classical remedy for a mind too restless and discursive to settle on any of the previous four objects, and notes its wide textual support — the *Great Ānāpāna-smṛti Sūtra*, the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*, the *Sūtra on the Samādhi of Sitting Meditation*, and the āgamas all treat it at length. On substance, he explains the breath's dual inner-outer status: as air actually moving within the body it counts as an inner matter tied to the body, yet since wind itself is one of the four elements shared with the entire outer physical world, it belongs to the outer sphere as well — the breath is, in this sense, the one meditation object that most directly bridges inner and outer. On its particular characteristic, he flags that "two kinds" of in-breath and out-breath will be detailed next — the breath proper and a subtler "interval" breath — cross-referencing the fuller treatment already given for breath-counting in fascicle 25's discussion of objects of meditation more generally.

10. Section: Breath-Counting's Remaining Components — All Five Purifying-Conduct Objects Now Complete

The remaining four components close breath-counting contemplation, and with it, all five objects for purifying conduct — the entirety of the antidotes for craving, hatred, delusion, pride, and scattered thought.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：入息滅已有出息生，出息滅已有入息生，入出息轉繫屬命根及有識身；此入出息及所依止皆是無常。是名尋思諸息共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: when the in-breath ceases, the out-breath arises; when the out-breath ceases, the in-breath arises. The turning of the in-breath and out-breath is bound to the life-faculty and to the body possessing consciousness; this in-breath and out-breath, and what they depend upon, are all impermanent. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of the breaths.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：此中都無持入息者、持出息者，入息、出息繫屬於彼；唯於從因從緣所生諸行，發起假想，施設言論，說有能持入出息者。如是名依觀待道理尋思其理……是名勤修阿那波那念者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: herein there is no one who controls the in-breath, no one who controls the out-breath — the in-breath and out-breath are simply bound to that body. It is only with respect to conditioned formations, arisen from causes and conditions, that one gives rise to a provisional conception and sets up an expression, speaking of "one who controls the in-breath and out-breath." This is called investigating the principle of the breaths by way of the principle of mutual dependence … This is called the insight, with objects differentiated by the six things, of one who diligently cultivates mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the breath offers an unusually direct route to impermanence, since it is ceaselessly changing in real time — never fixed as purely inhaling or purely exhaling, sometimes long, sometimes short, sometimes urgent, sometimes calm — and, being tied directly to the life-faculty and to consciousness's presence in the body, it makes impermanence, and through it suffering and no-self, unusually easy to actually witness rather than merely infer. On category, an untrained mind attending to the breath still gets pulled away by the eight kinds of unwholesome discursive thought (desire, ill will, harm, homeland, relatives, powerful patrons, contempt, and denial of death's nearness) — the dark category — while a settled mind free of these distractions belongs to the bright. On principle, no one actually "controls" the breath in any ultimate sense; it is simply conditioned process, with "controller" only a conventional label laid over it. He closes by naming the great virtue of breath-counting specifically: since calm-abiding and insight can be practiced on the very same object — count the breath to build stillness, then investigate the same breath through the six things to build insight — it uniquely demonstrates how the two are meant to work as a single unified practice, not two separate pursuits. With this, all five objects for purifying conduct — impurity, loving-kindness, dependent origination, elemental analysis, and breath-counting — are complete.

11. How Calm-Abiding and Insight Reinforce Each Other

Chinese Source

如是依止淨行所緣，尋思六事差別觀已，數數於內令心寂靜，數數復於如所尋思，以勝觀行審諦伺察。彼由奢摩他為依止故，令毗鉢舍那速得清淨；復由毗鉢舍那為依止故，令奢摩他增長廣大。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, relying on the objects for purifying conduct, having investigated the six-thing differentiated contemplation, one repeatedly settles the mind inwardly into stillness, and then repeatedly, with respect to what has been investigated, carefully and truthfully examines it further through superior insight practice. Relying on calm-abiding, insight swiftly becomes purified; and relying, in turn, on insight, calm-abiding grows and expands.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this brief passage as the payoff of the entire six-things structure just completed across all five objects: calm-abiding and insight are never rivals but mutual causes, alternating in a single sustained practice — stillness clarifies what investigation later examines, and investigation, once complete, feeds back to deepen the very stillness it depended on. He cross-references this same relationship to the four kinds of wisdom-conduct (correct discernment and utmost discernment corresponding to substance and characteristic; category, time, and principle folding into utmost discernment; meaning cutting across both), to the three gates of insight (mere-mark, investigation, and examination — shallow to deep), and to the three kinds of realization (verbal-meaning realization, realization of a thing's full extent, and realization of things as they truly are) — all, he stresses, different lenses on the identical underlying practice, not separate techniques to be learned one after another.

12. Section: Skillful-Means and Purifying-Affliction Objects Deferred — the Fourth Category of Meditation Objects Named

Before moving on, the treatise names the larger four-part framework its five purifying-conduct objects belong to, and explicitly defers two of the four categories — skillful-means objects and purifying-affliction objects — to later fascicles.

Chinese Source

若依止善巧所緣及淨惑所緣，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那，於其自處我後當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for investigating insight with objects differentiated by the six things by relying on skillful-means objects or purifying-affliction objects, I shall explain these later, in their own proper place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the complete fourfold framework of meditation objects in Yogācāra practice: pervasive objects (the four found throughout every practice — discerning image, non-discerning image, the full extent of a thing's nature, and the accomplished result — corresponding respectively to insight, calm-abiding, the twin "full extent" and "true nature" characteristics, and the purified object plus purified basis that mark genuine accomplishment); objects for purifying conduct (the five just completed, the Tiantai tradition's five-methods-for-stilling-the-mind); skillful-means objects (mastery of the aggregates, sense-fields, elements, dependent origination, and what is possible or impossible — sometimes expanded to seven by adding the faculties and the four truths, detailed at length in the "Determinations" section, fascicles 53–57); and purifying-affliction objects (the mundane path's comparative six-aspect method and the supramundane path's direct four-truths method, promised for fascicles 33 and 34). Cháng Bǎi notes plainly that skillful-means objects never receive their own dedicated six-things treatment anywhere in the text as it survives, while purifying-affliction objects are taken up later exactly as promised — a reminder that the treatise's own scope, not the translator's choice, sets these boundaries.

13. Nine Kinds of Preparatory Practice, Bright and Dark

Chinese Source

復次，此中有九種白品所攝加行。與此相違，當知即是九種黑品所攝加行。云何名為白品所攝九種加行？一、相應加行，二、串習加行，三、不緩加行，四、無倒加行，五、應時加行，六、解了加行，七、無厭足加行，八、不捨軛加行，九、正加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, herein there are nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the bright category. The reverse of these should be known as the nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the dark category. What are the nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the bright category? First, fitting practice. Second, habituated practice. Third, unhurried practice. Fourth, undistorted practice. Fifth, timely practice. Sixth, understanding practice. Seventh, insatiable practice. Eighth, unabandoning practice. Ninth, correct practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews all nine before their individual detail follows: fitting practice matches the meditation object to the practitioner's actual dominant affliction (contemplating impurity for one dominated by craving, and so on through the other four objects); habituated practice reflects genuine repeated familiarity across the seven kinds of mental engagement, from learning the object's mark through the fruit of effort's culmination; unhurried practice sustains unbroken, earnest effort without slackening; undistorted practice proceeds without error; timely practice knows precisely when to apply stillness, investigation, energizing, or equanimizing; understanding practice grasps the nature, object, cause, and timing of each of those four with enough clarity to enter and exit concentration freely; insatiable practice refuses to settle for whatever level has already been reached, always reaching for what is more excellent still; unabandoning practice never neglects precepts or the other provisions of the path even while absorbed in concentration; and correct practice — the ninth, to be detailed next — brings the whole set to completion. He notes each of the nine has an exact dark-category mirror, named simply by reversal, spelling out nine distinct ways preparatory practice can go wrong just as precisely as it names nine ways it can go right.

14. Section: What the Nine Practices Accomplish — Speed to Attainment, Bright and Dark

Before detailing each of the nine bright-category practices individually, the treatise states their overall effect — and its exact mirror image for the nine dark-category failures.

Chinese Source

由此九種白品所攝加行故，能令其心速疾得定，令三摩地轉更昇進。又由此故，於所應往地及隨所應得，速疾能往、能得，無有稽遲。黑品所攝九種加行，不能令心速疾得定，不令三摩地轉更昇進。又由此故，於所應往地及隨所應得，極大稽遲，不能速疾往趣、獲得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through these nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the bright category, the mind swiftly attains concentration, and samādhi continues to advance further still. Further, through these, one swiftly reaches and attains whatever stage and whatever level are to be reached, without delay. The nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the dark category do not enable the mind to swiftly attain concentration, nor do they enable samādhi to advance further. Further, through these, one is greatly delayed in reaching whatever stage and whatever level are to be reached, unable to swiftly arrive at or attain them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "attaining concentration" first as reaching near-attainment concentration (the threshold just below the first dhyāna proper), and "advancing further" as passing from there into the first dhyāna's actual root absorption and on through the second, third, and fourth — each stage bringing progressively fuller freedom from the afflictions specific to meditative attainment (subtle craving, wrong view, pride, and doubt). "Whatever stage" covers the entire hierarchy above the first dhyāna, up through the peak of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and ultimately the noble path itself. He frames the whole nine-item list, bright and dark alike, as a diagnostic: a practitioner stuck at the same level for years should look here first, checking one by one whether fitting, habituation, urgency, freedom from error, timing, understanding, insatiability, unbroken discipline, and correct alternation of stillness and insight are each actually present — since any single one done in its dark, reversed form is often enough to explain years of stalled progress.

15. Fitting Practice: Matching the Object to One's Own Temperament

Chinese Source

云何名為相應加行？謂若貪行者，應於不淨安住其心；若瞋行者，應於慈愍安住其心；若癡行者，應於緣起安住其心；若憍慢行者，應於界差別安住其心；若尋思行者，應於阿那波那念安住其心；若等分行者，或薄塵行者，應隨所樂攀緣一境安住其心；勤修加行。如是名為相應加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is fitting practice? That is, one dominated by craving should settle the mind on impurity; one dominated by hatred should settle the mind on loving-kindness; one dominated by delusion should settle the mind on dependent origination; one dominated by pride should settle the mind on elemental analysis; one dominated by discursive thought should settle the mind on mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath; and one whose afflictions are evenly balanced, or lightly present, should settle the mind on whichever object he prefers, diligently cultivating the practice. This is called fitting practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this first item as simply the practical payoff of everything just covered across the five purifying-conduct objects: choosing the wrong antidote for one's own dominant affliction guarantees poor results no matter how diligently one otherwise practices. He notes practitioners sometimes discover their true fit only after years with the "wrong" object — one long-time breath-counter, for instance, finally settling quickly once switching to elemental analysis — and stresses that self-honesty about one's own actual temperament, not simply whichever practice is most popular or most familiar, is what "fitting" really requires.

16. Habituated Practice: Distinguishing the Seasoned Practitioner from the True Beginner

Chinese Source

云何名為串習加行？謂於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，已曾數習乃至少分，非於一切皆初修業。所以者何？初修業者，雖於相應所緣境界勤修加行，而有諸蓋數數現行，身心麤重。由是因緣，不能令心速疾得定。如是名為串習加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is habituated practice? That is, one who has already repeatedly practiced calm-abiding and insight, at least to some degree — not one who is, in every respect, a complete beginner. Why is this? Because a complete beginner, even while diligently applying effort to a fitting object, still has the hindrances repeatedly manifesting, and body and mind remain coarse and heavy. For this reason, the mind cannot swiftly attain concentration. This is called habituated practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi pins down exactly where the line falls: a true beginner has, at most, only reached the first of the seven kinds of mental engagement (learning the object's mark), still mixed with ordinary hearing- and reflection-based understanding, while a "habituated" practitioner has already moved through confident understanding at least partway toward the effort's culmination. He notes this isn't a moral failing in the beginner — even choosing a perfectly fitting object, a genuine newcomer will still find the hindrances surfacing again and again, body aching, mind restless, simply because the requisite quantity and quality of practice hasn't yet accumulated, whether in this life or carried over from a previous one. A seasoned practitioner, by contrast, settles almost immediately, hindrance-free, precisely because that accumulated familiarity is already in place.

17. Section: Unhurried Practice — Not Delighting in Busywork, Not Delighting in Crowds, Urgent Resolve

The third practice, sustaining effort without slackening, unfolds into three concrete behaviors: finishing necessary duties without lingering in them, keeping social contact brief and purposeful, and refusing to postpone genuine effort to some imagined later life.

Chinese Source

云何名為不緩加行？謂無間方便、殷重方便勤修觀行。若從定出，或為乞食，或為恭敬承事師長，或為看病，或為隨順修和敬業，或為所餘如是等類諸所作事；而心於彼所作事業，不全隨順、不全趣向、不全臨入，唯有速疾令事究竟，還復精勤宴坐寂靜，修諸觀行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is unhurried practice? That is, cultivating meditative practice with uninterrupted, earnest effort. If, having risen from concentration, one must beg for alms, attend respectfully upon a teacher, tend the sick, act in accordance with the six kinds of harmony, or carry out other such necessary tasks — the mind, with respect to these tasks, does not fully yield to them, does not fully turn toward them, does not fully enter into them; one merely brings the task swiftly to completion and then returns once more to diligent, still sitting, cultivating meditative practice.

Chinese Source

若有苾芻、苾芻尼、鄔波索迦、剎帝利、婆羅門等種種異眾共相會遇，雖久雜處，現相語儀，而不相續安立言論，唯樂遠離，勤修觀行。又能如是勇猛精進：謂我於今定當趣證所應證得，不應慢緩。

Ru-Yi Translation

If monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen, warrior-caste persons, brahmins, or other various different assemblies happen to meet, though remaining together for some time, exchanging courteous greetings, one does not continue on with extended conversation, but delights only in seclusion, diligently cultivating meditative practice. Further, one is thereby able to remain courageously diligent in this way: "I must, at this very time, resolutely reach what is to be realized — I should not be slack or delay."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the necessary-duties passage as mapping directly onto the three kinds of wisdom already established: fully engaging with a task the way one engages with hearing-based study, fully turning toward it the way one turns toward reflection, and fully entering it the way one enters cultivation — none of which the unhurried practitioner allows a mere errand to claim, precisely because his heart remains oriented toward the meditation seat even mid-task. On social contact, he notes the treatise's own model reply is simply the ordinary courtesy of asking after someone's health and well-being, nothing more elaborate — contrasted with the Mahāyāna's broader teaching (citing the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s "Chapter on Pure Conduct," which supplies a specific contemplation for literally every daily encounter, from crossing a bridge to waking up) that every moment, not only seated meditation, can be turned into practice. He treats the concluding resolve — refusing the comfortable thought that boundless future lives leave plenty of time to practice later — as the emotional core of unhurried practice: without a felt sense of urgency, sustained effort simply doesn't happen, whatever techniques one otherwise knows.

18. The Causes of Sudden Death, and Resolving to Make the Most of Whatever Time Remains

Chinese Source

我有多種橫死因緣，所謂身中或風、或熱、或痰發動；或所飲食不正銷化，住在身中成宿食病；或為於外蛇蝎、蚰蜓、百足等類諸惡毒蟲之所蜇螫；或復為人非人等類之所驚恐；因斯夭沒。於如是等諸橫死處恆常思惟，修無常想，住不放逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

I have many possible causes of sudden death: perhaps wind, heat, or phlegm disorders arising within the body; or food improperly digested, remaining in the body and becoming a lodged ailment; or being stung or bitten by outer venomous creatures such as snakes, scorpions, centipedes, or the like; or being terrified by humans or non-humans — and dying prematurely through any of these. Constantly reflecting on such causes of sudden death, one cultivates the perception of impermanence, and abides in non-heedlessness.

Chinese Source

由住如是不放逸故，恆自思惟：我之壽命儻得更經七日、六日、五日……乃至存活經爾所時，於佛聖教精勤作意修習瑜伽，齊爾所時，於佛聖教，我當決定多有所作。如是名為不緩加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Abiding in such non-heedlessness, one constantly reflects: "Should my life extend for seven more days, or six, or five … for however much time I have left to live, I must diligently direct my attention to cultivating yogic practice within the Buddha's noble teaching; for that entire span of time, within the Buddha's noble teaching, I am resolved to accomplish a great deal." This is called unhurried practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this passage as the treatise's own antidote to the "denial of death's nearness" discursive thought already flagged as an obstacle earlier in the fascicle: rather than vaguely acknowledging mortality, the practitioner is instructed to concretely enumerate specific, mundane causes of sudden death — an internal imbalance, food poisoning, a venomous bite, a fright — precisely because the abstract fact of impermanence rarely moves anyone, while a vivid, particular scenario does. The counting-down exercise that follows (seven days, then six, then down to a single in-breath or out-breath) is meant to be actually felt, not merely recited, so that whatever time remains — a lifetime or a single hour — gets treated as valuable and not to be wasted. Cháng Bǎi calls this the emotional engine behind unhurried practice: a genuine sense that this may be the only opportunity, which alone reliably overcomes the comfortable postponement so common among practitioners with an otherwise stable, uneventful life.

19. Undistorted Practice: Learning Correctly, Without Pride or Private Opinion

Chinese Source

云何名為無倒加行？謂如善達修瑜伽行諸瑜伽師之所開悟，即如是學，於法、於義不顛倒取，無有我慢，亦不安住自所見取，無邪僻執，於尊教誨終不輕毀。如是名為無倒加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is undistorted practice? That is, exactly as one is instructed by teachers well versed in yogic practice, one learns accordingly — grasping neither the teaching nor its meaning in a distorted way, without arrogance, not settling into one's own private views, free of deviant clinging, and never disparaging the instruction of one's venerable teachers. This is called undistorted practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats undistorted practice as guarding against two opposite failure modes at once: adding one's own arrogant embellishment to what a teacher has actually taught, and stubbornly clinging to a private interpretation once formed rather than remaining correctable. He recommends sustained, patient, repeated study of the treatise itself as the practical remedy — cross-referencing earlier and later passages against each other until understanding deepens gradually — and notes that impatience with exactly this kind of unglamorous repetition is, in his experience, the single most common reason students never actually correct their own distortions.

20. Section: Timely Practice Opens — Stillness, Insight, Energizing, and Equanimizing

The fifth practice, timely practice, requires knowing exactly when each of four distinct techniques is called for — stillness, insight, energizing, and equanimizing — and understanding each one's own nature, object, and proper occasion.

Chinese Source

云何名為應時加行？謂於時時間修習止相，於時時間修習觀相，於時時間修習舉相，於時時間修習捨相。又能如實了知其止、止相、止時；了知其觀、觀相、觀時；了知其舉、舉相、舉時；了知其捨、捨相、捨時。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is timely practice? That is, at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of stillness; at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of insight; at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of energizing; and at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of equanimizing. Further, one correctly and truly knows stillness, its mark, and its proper time; knows insight, its mark, and its proper time; knows energizing, its mark, and its proper time; and knows equanimizing, its mark, and its proper time.

Chinese Source

云何為止？謂九相心住。能令其心無相、無分別、寂靜、極寂靜、等住寂止、純一無雜，故名為止。所緣相者，謂奢摩他品所知事同分影像，是名所緣相。由此所緣，令心寂靜。因緣相者，謂依奢摩他所熏習心，為令後時奢摩他定皆清淨故，修習瑜伽毗鉢舍那所有加行，是名因緣相。云何止時？謂心掉舉時，或恐掉舉時，是修止時。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is stillness? It is the nine stages of mental abiding, which cause the mind to become free of marks, free of discursive thought, tranquil, thoroughly tranquil, evenly abiding at rest, and singly unified without admixture — hence it is called stillness. Its object-mark is the image of the same type as the object of knowledge belonging to the calm-abiding type; through this object, the mind becomes tranquil. Its causal mark is that, relying on the mind trained by calm-abiding, in order that calm-abiding's concentration may become ever more purified in the future, one cultivates the entire practice of insight — this is its causal mark. What is the proper time for stillness? When the mind is agitated, or on the verge of becoming agitated, this is the time to cultivate stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "stillness" here as nothing other than the nine stages of mental abiding already detailed in fascicle 30, now revisited from the angle of when to actually apply them: the moment agitation surfaces, or is even sensed approaching, is precisely the signal to return to the object and let it settle. He treats the object-mark and causal-mark distinction as capturing two different roles insight can play relative to stillness: as an object in its own right (the mental image matching what the senses first perceived) and, more subtly, as a supporting cause — sustained investigation into an affliction's danger, done in advance, makes the mind far less likely to be disturbed by that same affliction once returning to still sitting. He gives the classical example: a practitioner prone to lust who first spends time deliberately reflecting on the body's impurity (an act of insight) will then find the mind considerably steadier when later returning to still concentration, precisely because the groundwork of investigation was already done — insight and stillness reinforcing each other exactly as promised earlier in the fascicle.

21. Section: Insight, Energizing, and Equanimizing — Timely Practice Completes

The remaining three techniques close timely practice: insight investigates what stillness has settled; energizing lifts a sinking mind through six inspiring recollections; and equanimizing lets a mind already free of both dullness and agitation simply rest.

Chinese Source

云何為觀？謂四行、三門、六事差別所緣觀行。所緣相者，謂毗鉢舍那品所知事同分影像。由此所緣，令慧觀察。因緣相者，謂依毗鉢舍那所熏習心，為令後時毗鉢舍那皆清淨故，修習內心奢摩他定所有加行。云何觀時？謂心沈沒時，或恐沈沒時，是修觀時。又依奢摩他所熏習心，先應於彼所知事境如實覺了，故於爾時是修觀時。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is insight? It is the fourfold wisdom-conduct, the three gates, and the contemplation with objects differentiated by the six things. Its object-mark is the image of the same type as the object of knowledge belonging to the insight type; through this object, wisdom investigates. Its causal mark is that, relying on the mind trained by insight, in order that insight may become ever more purified in the future, one cultivates the entire practice of inward calm-abiding concentration. What is the proper time for insight? When the mind is sinking, or on the verge of sinking, this is the time to cultivate insight. Further, relying on the mind trained by calm-abiding, one should first correctly understand the object of knowledge as it truly is — this too is the time to cultivate insight.

Chinese Source

云何為舉？謂由隨取一種淨妙所緣境界，顯示、勸導、慶慰其心。云何舉時？謂心沈下時，或恐沈下時，是修舉時。云何為捨？謂於所緣心無染汙、心平等性……令心隨與任運作用。云何捨時？謂於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那品，所有掉舉心已解脫，是修捨時。如是名為應時加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is energizing? It is taking up whichever pure, excellent object inspires the mind, displaying it, urging oneself on with it, and consoling the mind through it. What is the proper time for energizing? When the mind is sinking low, or on the verge of sinking low, this is the time to cultivate energizing. What is equanimizing? It is a mind, with respect to its object, free of defilement, evenly balanced … letting the mind proceed spontaneously in its function. What is the proper time for equanimizing? When the mind, whether in calm-abiding or insight, has already been freed of all agitation, this is the time to cultivate equanimizing. This is called timely practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes insight is called for at two distinct moments — when the mind is sinking toward dullness (investigation naturally rouses it) and when stillness has already settled the object clearly enough that its meaning, substance, and other features can now actually be examined. Energizing's tool is the traditional six recollections — of the Buddha's perfected qualities, the Dharma's power to remove affliction, the Saṅgha's embodiment of the path, one's own pure precepts, one's own generosity freely given, and the heavens attainable through a virtuous life — any one of which, genuinely recalled, lifts a flagging mind the way encouragement revives a tired climber partway up a mountain. Cháng Bǎi distinguishes energizing's target (a mind gone slack or discouraged) from insight's target (a mind gone dull and heavy), and equanimizing's occasion — a mind already free of restlessness in either stillness or insight — as the point where effort itself should finally relax, letting the practice simply continue on its own, the mature "upper equanimity" that appears at the ninth of the nine mental abidings. With all four techniques — stillness, insight, energizing, and equanimizing — now fully specified, timely practice, the fifth of the nine preparatory practices, is complete.

22. Understanding Practice: Freedom to Enter, Abide In, and Rise From Concentration

Chinese Source

云何名為解了加行？謂於如是所說諸相善取、善了。善取了已，欲入定時即便能入，欲住定時即便能住，欲起定時即便能起。或時棄捨諸三摩地所行影像，作意思惟諸不定地所有本性所緣境界。如是名為解了加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is understanding practice? That is, skillfully grasping and skillfully comprehending all the marks just described. Having skillfully grasped and comprehended them, when one wishes to enter concentration, one is able to enter it; when one wishes to abide in concentration, one is able to abide in it; when one wishes to rise from concentration, one is able to rise from it. At times, one sets aside the image belonging to samādhi's own activity and directs attention instead to the original object belonging to the non-concentrated state. This is called understanding practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies two distinct things a practitioner must understand: first, the entire preceding apparatus (stillness, insight, energizing, equanimizing — their nature, marks, and timing) well enough to enter, remain in, and exit concentration entirely at will, rather than being at the mercy of whatever state happens to arise; and second, that when the meditative image itself has grown unclear — a corpse's remembered appearance fading, say — the correct response is not to strain harder in sitting but to rise, return to the original object in ordinary waking perception (visiting the actual site, or observing an actual death, exactly as the Buddha is said to have encouraged monks to do), and only then resume. He tells a story of a meditator whose sitting produced persistent headaches, unresolved by one teacher's advice but eventually cured by a second teacher's simple instruction to lie directly on bare earth — illustrating that even physical complications arising from practice fall within what "understanding" a genuine teacher needs to address. He closes noting that witnessing old age, sickness, and death firsthand — traditionally encouraged rather than avoided — is itself a form of this same understanding practice, since it is exactly the kind of direct, un-conceptualized encounter with a meditation object's original form that abstract reflection alone cannot substitute for.

23. Section: Insatiable Practice Opens — Never Settling for "Enough"

The seventh practice guards against the complacency of premature satisfaction, insisting a practitioner always press toward what is more excellent still.

Chinese Source

云何名為無厭足加行？謂於善法無有厭足，修斷無廢，於展轉上展轉勝處，多住希求。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is insatiable practice? That is, never being satisfied with wholesome states, never abandoning the cultivation of what is to be severed, and continually dwelling in the aspiration for ever higher, ever more excellent states.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties insatiable practice directly back to fascicle 30's teaching on nurturing the provisions for concentration — the same warning against "feeling one has already done enough" that opened the entire locus of seclusion now returns here, applied specifically to concentration itself. A practitioner who reaches near-attainment concentration and stops there, content, never reaches the first dhyāna; one who reaches the first dhyāna and stops there never reaches the second — insatiability is simply the sustained aspiration that keeps this progression from stalling at any intermediate plateau, however genuinely pleasant that plateau might feel.

24. Insatiable Practice Continued: Never Retreating Midway

Chinese Source

不唯獲得少小靜定，便於中路而生退屈。於餘所作，常有進求。如是名為無厭足加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

One does not, merely upon attaining some slight measure of stillness, grow discouraged and retreat midway. Toward whatever else remains to be accomplished, one constantly presses forward in pursuit. This is called insatiable practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "some slight measure of stillness" specifically as near-attainment concentration, the threshold just below the first dhyāna proper — genuine samādhi, but still far from complete: not yet the root absorption itself, let alone the second, third, or fourth dhyānas beyond it, and not yet free of the subtle craving, wrong view, pride, doubt, and delusion that meditative attainment itself can generate. Stopping there, satisfied, forecloses everything still ahead; insatiable practice is simply the refusal to let that first taste of stillness masquerade as the destination.

25. Section: Unabandoning Practice — Precepts as the Yoke That Keeps the Mind from Scattering

The eighth practice returns to where fascicle 30 began: the provisions of precepts, sense-restraint, and moderation, here named the "yoke" that must never be set down even while deeply absorbed in concentration.

Chinese Source

云何名為不捨軛加行？謂於一切所受學處，無穿、無闕；雖見少年顏容端正可愛母邑，而不取相、不取隨好；於食平等；勤修覺悟；少事、少業、少諸散亂；於久所作、久所說等，能自隨憶，令他隨憶。如是等法，說名不捨軛加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is unabandoning practice? That is, with respect to every precept one has undertaken, neither breaking nor lacking any of them; though seeing a young woman of attractive, pleasing appearance, not grasping at her general appearance nor at her alluring details; being even-handed regarding food; diligently cultivating wakefulness; having few affairs, little activity, and little distraction; and being able, oneself, to recall — and to help others recall — what was done and said long ago. Such practices as these are called unabandoning practice.

Chinese Source

由此諸法，能正隨順心一境性，不捨其軛，令心不散；不令其心馳流外境；不令其心內不調柔。如是名為不捨軛加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through these various practices, one rightly accords with one-pointedness of mind, never setting down this yoke, keeping the mind from scattering — not letting the mind race outward toward external objects, and not letting the mind remain inwardly unyielding. This is called unabandoning practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "yoke" as a deliberately chosen image: an ox or horse yoked to a cart is thereby made governable, and precepts function identically, harnessing a mind that would otherwise wander freely. He notes this entire list — intact precepts, restraint even in the presence of attractive people (grasping neither the overall impression nor its alluring particulars), moderation in eating, sustained wakefulness practice, minimal worldly entanglement, and honest accountability for one's own past conduct — is simply the fourteen-item twofold provisions from fascicle 30 returning one final time, now framed specifically as what must never be abandoned even once concentration is well underway. His point is pointed: advanced meditative attainment is no license to relax ethical discipline; the same yoke that got a practitioner into deep stillness is what keeps that stillness from collapsing back into scattered mind.

26. Section: Correct Practice Opens — Five Ways of Properly Dispelling the Object, and the Beginner's Method

The ninth and final preparatory practice, correct practice, brings the whole set to completion: alternating between generating vivid understanding of an object and then correctly dispelling it, cycling insight and stillness together — detailed here through five specific methods of "dispelling," two of which matter most for an actual beginner.

Chinese Source

云何名正加行？謂於所緣數起勝解，數正除遣，是名正加行。如有勤修不淨觀者，數正除遣於諸不淨作意思惟諸不淨相。其正除遣，復有五種：一、內攝其心故；二、不念作意故；三、於餘作意故；四、對治作意故；五、無相界作意故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is correct practice? That is, repeatedly generating vivid understanding with respect to the object, and repeatedly, correctly dispelling it — this is called correct practice. For instance, one who diligently cultivates contemplation of impurity repeatedly and correctly dispels, and directs attention to, various marks of impurity. This correct dispelling is, moreover, of five kinds: first, by gathering the mind inward; second, by non-attentive engagement; third, by engagement with a different object; fourth, by engagement as an antidote; fifth, by engagement with the markless realm.

Chinese Source

雖遍安立一切所緣正除遣相總有五種，然此義中，正意唯取內攝其心、不念作意。初修業者始修業時，最初全不於所緣境繫縛其心……唯作是念：我心云何得無散亂、無相、無分別、寂靜、極寂靜、無轉無動、無所希望、離諸作用、於內適悅？

Ru-Yi Translation

Although the marks of correct dispelling, applied across every object, are five in total, the primary intent here takes up only the first two — gathering the mind inward and non-attentive engagement. When a beginner first takes up practice, at the very start he cannot bind the mind to the object at all … he can only reflect thus: "How may my mind become free of distraction, free of marks, free of discursive thought, tranquil, thoroughly tranquil, unmoving and unwavering, free of expectation, free of all effortful activity, and inwardly at ease?"

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the full five methods of "dispelling" an object once vivid understanding of it has served its purpose: gathering the mind inward (returning from insight's investigation back to the nine mental abidings' stillness); non-attentive engagement (at the very start of practice, simply turning away from all distracting marks rather than engaging any object at all); engaging a different object (moving on, say, from the discoloration stage of a corpse to the bloating stage, once the first has been sufficiently absorbed); engaging as an antidote (deliberately taking up whichever of the five purifying-conduct objects specifically counters one's own dominant affliction); and engaging the markless realm (turning attention to emptiness itself, the *Prajñāpāramitā*'s teaching that all phenomena are, at bottom, without any real, fixed mark at all). Of these five, he stresses, only the first two matter for an actual beginner, who has no refined object yet to dispel in the first place — such a practitioner's whole task is simply, repeatedly, to draw a habitually distracted mind inward and let it settle, without yet attempting the subtler four remaining methods reserved for more advanced stages.

27. Section: Gathering the Mind Inward, and the Painter's Apprentice

The treatise now details the first of correct practice's two beginner-relevant methods, gathering the mind inward, and illustrates the entire generate-and-dispel cycle with a vivid analogy: an apprentice painter who must destroy and redraw the same figure again and again before ever becoming a master.

Chinese Source

若於所緣唯數勝解，不數除遣；即不令彼所有勝解後後明淨究竟而轉，不能往趣乃至現觀所知境事。由數勝解、數除遣故，後後勝解展轉明淨究竟而轉，亦能往趣乃至現觀所知境事。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, with respect to the object, one only repeatedly generates vivid understanding without repeatedly dispelling it, then one's successive understandings never become progressively clearer and more refined, and one is unable to proceed all the way to directly witnessing the object of knowledge. But through repeatedly generating vivid understanding and repeatedly dispelling it, each successive understanding becomes progressively clearer and more refined, and one is thereby able to proceed all the way to directly witnessing the object of knowledge.

Chinese Source

譬如世間畫師弟子，初習畫業，先從師所受所學樣。諦觀諦觀，作彼形相；作已作已，尋即除毀；既除毀已，尋復更作。如如除毀，數數更作，如是如是後後形相轉明、轉淨究竟顯現……若不數除所作形相，即於其上數數重畫，便於形相永無明淨究竟顯期。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like a painter's apprentice in the world: newly taking up the craft of painting, he first receives from his teacher a model to study. Gazing at it carefully, again and again, he renders its form; having rendered it, he then promptly destroys it; having destroyed it, he promptly renders it once more. As he destroys it again and again, and renders it again and again, each successive form becomes ever clearer, ever more refined, until it is perfectly displayed … But if he does not repeatedly destroy what he has rendered, and instead simply paints over it again and again on the same surface, the form will never have any hope of becoming clear and refined to completion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the painter's apprentice as the treatise's own clearest illustration of why "dispelling" is not mere forgetting but the very mechanism of refinement: each redrawn attempt is not wasted repetition but genuine progress, since the willingness to destroy an imperfect version is exactly what allows the next attempt to improve on it. A student who never tears up a flawed first draft, instead layering correction upon correction on the same sheet, only produces an increasingly muddled mess — precisely, Cháng Bǎi notes, what happens to a meditator who keeps adding new vivid understanding to an old, undispelled mental image without ever clearing it away first. The lesson generalizes beyond any single object: sustained practice over "much time," not a single breakthrough sitting, is what a community eventually comes to recognize as mastery.

28. Section: Correct Practice Closes — Fixed and Flexible Objects, Coarse and Subtle Images, and All Nine Practices Complete

The treatise closes correct practice by noting that dispelling an object never obligates returning to that same object, that this holds whether the object is small, vast, or boundless, and that objects with form yield coarser images than formless ones — completing all nine bright-category preparatory practices.

Chinese Source

若於此境起勝解已，定於此境復正除遣；非於此境正除遣已，定於此境復起勝解。於狹小境起勝解已，即於狹小而正除遣。廣大、無量，當知亦爾。於狹小境正除遣已，或於狹小復起勝解，或於廣大復起勝解，或於無量復起勝解。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one has generated vivid understanding with respect to a given object, one must then correctly dispel that same object — but having correctly dispelled a given object, one is not thereby bound to generate vivid understanding of that same object again. Having generated vivid understanding of a small object, one correctly dispels that same small object — and the same holds for a vast or a boundless object. Having correctly dispelled a small object, one may generate vivid understanding again of a small object, or of a vast object, or of a boundless object.

Chinese Source

若諸色法所有相貌影像顯現，當知是麤，變化相似。諸無色法假名為先，如所領受增上力故，影像顯現。如是一切名正加行。如是九種白品加行，於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，當知隨順。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whatever image appears bearing the marks of a form-possessing phenomenon should be known as coarse, its transformations closely resembling their original. Formless phenomena, preceded by provisional naming, appear as images through the empowering force of what has been apprehended of them. All of this is called correct practice. These nine kinds of bright-category preparatory practice should be known as conducive to both calm-abiding and insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses the asymmetry the treatise insists on: generating understanding of an object obligates its later dispelling, but dispelling never obligates returning to that identical object — a practitioner is free to move to a smaller, larger, or more expansive version of the same theme, or to an entirely different one, once the prior image has been cleared. He distinguishes form-based objects (a corpse's stages of decay) as yielding relatively coarse mental images, closely tracking what the physical senses originally perceived, from formless objects (feelings, or reflections like no-self itself) as yielding subtler images built entirely from language and concept rather than direct sensory resemblance — no corpse can be pointed to for "suffering" or "impermanence" the way one can be pointed to for "impurity." With this, correct practice — and with it, all nine kinds of bright-category preparatory practice, from fitting practice through correct practice — stand complete, each shown conducive to both calm-abiding and insight alike.

29. Section: Locus Three, One-Pointedness of Mind, Closes — Eighteen Practices in Total

The treatise names the dark-category mirror of all nine bright practices in a single stroke, then closes the entire third locus — one-pointedness of mind — with a final tally.

Chinese Source

與是相違九種加行，於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，當知違逆。如是黑品、白品差別建立加行，有十八種。如是名為心一境性。

Ru-Yi Translation

The nine kinds of preparatory practice that are the reverse of these should be known as obstructing both calm-abiding and insight. Thus, the preparatory practices established as differentiated into the dark and bright categories number eighteen in total. This is called one-pointedness of mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the dark-category nine as needing no separate elaboration, since each is simply the bright practice's precise negation — an unfitting object, a true beginner's inevitable hindrances mistaken for failure, chronic slackness, distorted understanding clung to without correction, mistimed technique, confusion about how to enter and exit concentration, premature satisfaction, abandoned discipline, and one-sided practice that never alternates stillness with insight. With this tally of eighteen — nine bright, nine dark — the entire third locus of establishing yogic practice, one-pointedness of mind, closes in full: its own four-part definition of samādhi, the nine stages of mental abiding, the four kinds of wisdom-conduct, all five objects for purifying conduct, and finally these eighteen preparatory practices, together constituting everything the treatise has to say about bringing the mind to genuine one-pointedness.

30. Section: The Fourth Locus Opens — Purifying the Obstructions

With one-pointedness of mind complete, the treatise turns to its fourth locus: identifying and removing the obstructions that stand in the way of genuine concentration, approached through four systematic steps.

Chinese Source

云何淨障？謂即如是正修加行諸瑜伽師，由四因緣，能令其心淨除諸障。何等為四？一、遍知自性故；二、遍知因緣故；三、遍知過患故；四、修習對治故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purifying the obstructions? That is, a teacher of yogic practice correctly cultivating this preparatory practice is able to purify the mind of its obstructions through four causes and conditions. What are the four? First, thoroughly knowing their own-nature. Second, thoroughly knowing their causes and conditions. Third, thoroughly knowing their dangers. Fourth, cultivating their antidotes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames this fourfold method as a general diagnostic template applicable to any obstruction whatsoever: first identify precisely what the obstruction actually is, distinct from anything else; then trace what specifically causes it to arise; then understand concretely what damage it does if left unaddressed; and only then apply the actual remedy — skipping any of the first three steps, he notes, tends to produce remedies that don't actually fit the real problem. This same four-step structure will now be applied to each of four specific obstructions in turn.

31. The Four Obstructions' Own-Nature: Timidity, Being Covered Over, Discursive Thought, Self-Conceit

Chinese Source

云何遍知諸障自性？謂能遍知障有四種：一、怯弱障，二、蓋覆障，三、尋思障，四、自舉障。怯弱障者，謂於出離及於遠離勤修行時，所有染汙思慕、不樂、希望、憂惱。蓋覆障者，謂貪欲等五蓋。尋思障者，謂欲尋思等染汙尋思。自舉障者，謂於少分下劣智見安隱住中，而自高舉，謂我能得，餘則不爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is thoroughly knowing the obstructions' own-nature? That is, being able to thoroughly know that there are four kinds of obstruction: first, the obstruction of timidity; second, the obstruction of being covered over; third, the obstruction of discursive thought; fourth, the obstruction of self-conceit. The obstruction of timidity is the defiled longing, discontent, craving, and distress that arise while diligently cultivating renunciation and seclusion. The obstruction of being covered over is the five hindrances, beginning with craving. The obstruction of discursive thought is defiled discursive thought, beginning with the thought of desire. The obstruction of self-conceit is, while abiding at ease in some slight, inferior degree of understanding, becoming self-important, thinking: "I alone am capable of this — others are not."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes the four precisely: timidity is an emotional resistance to renunciation and seclusion themselves — missing former company, disliking quiet solitude, craving worldly pleasures still, and growing distressed when they are unobtainable or lost — a failure of heart rather than a failure of technique. Being covered over is simply the five hindrances already detailed in fascicle 30 (craving, ill will, dullness-and-drowsiness, restlessness-and-worry, and doubt), now named specifically as an obstruction to concentration's clarity. Discursive thought is the eight kinds of unwholesome mental wandering — desire, ill will, harm, homeland, relatives, denial of death's nearness, powerful patrons, and contempt — already familiar from the nine mental abidings' account of taming and pacifying. Self-conceit, the subtlest of the four, is pride arising precisely from genuine but still minor attainment (near-attainment concentration, say), illustrated by Cháng Bǎi with the example of a practitioner born to a distinguished family who takes his ordination itself as proof of superiority over "lesser" monks — a self-regard that can survive and even feed on real spiritual progress, making it especially dangerous precisely because it doesn't feel like failure from the inside.

32. Section: The Six Causes of Timidity

Having named the four obstructions, the treatise now traces the specific causes behind the first — timidity — beginning with six concrete conditions that produce it.

Chinese Source

謂能遍知初怯弱障，有六因緣：一、由先業增上力故，或由疾病所擾惱故，其身羸劣；二、太過加行；三、不修加行；四、初修加行；五、煩惱熾盛；六、於遠離猶未串習。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thoroughly knowing the first, the obstruction of timidity, there are six causes and conditions: first, through the empowering force of past karma, or through affliction by illness, the body becomes weak and frail; second, excessive exertion; third, failing to practice at all; fourth, being a genuine beginner; fifth, the afflictions burning intensely; sixth, not yet being habituated to seclusion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads these six as spanning both bodily and mental causes: a frail body, whether from karmic inheritance or present illness, simply cannot sustain sitting practice regardless of resolve; excessive exertion — pushing too hard without the balance of the middle way — paradoxically produces the same discouragement as not practicing at all; being a true beginner (not yet past the mark-learning stage of mental engagement) predictably brings the hindrances forward again and again, easily mistaken for personal failure rather than a normal stage; intensely burning affliction can simply overpower whatever resolve a practitioner brings to the cushion; and a mind never habituated to actual seclusion will resist and recoil from it out of sheer unfamiliarity, the way any unpracticed skill feels awkward and unwelcome before it feels natural. Cháng Bǎi's point in cataloging these causes so specifically is practical: timidity is not a single monolithic problem but at least six distinct ones, each calling for its own particular remedy rather than a single generic fix.

33. Section: The Causes of the Remaining Three Obstructions — Unwise Attention to Each of the Five Hindrances

Where timidity has six distinct causes, being covered over, discursive thought, and self-conceit share a single root cause: unwise attention, repeatedly habituated. The treatise specifies exactly what that unwise attention looks like for each of the five hindrances in turn.

Chinese Source

遍知蓋覆、尋思、自舉障因緣者，謂於隨順蓋覆、尋思，及自舉障處所法中，非理作意多分串習，是名蓋覆、尋思、自舉障之因緣。若不作意思惟不淨，而於淨相作意思惟，是名此中非理作意。若不作意思惟慈愍，而於瞋相作意思惟……若不作意思惟明相，而於闇相作意思惟……若不作意思惟奢摩他相，而於親屬、國土、不死……諸惡尋思作意思惟……若不作意思惟緣性緣起，而於三世諸行計我、我所，不如理想作意思惟，是名此中非理作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for thoroughly knowing the causes of being covered over, discursive thought, and self-conceit: it is habitually engaging, in large part, in unwise attention within matters conducive to being covered over, to discursive thought, and to self-conceit — this is called the cause of being covered over, discursive thought, and self-conceit. Not directing attention to impurity, but instead directing attention to attractive appearance — this is the unwise attention meant here. Not directing attention to loving-kindness, but instead directing attention to a hateful aspect … Not directing attention to the mark of brightness, but instead directing attention to a dark aspect … Not directing attention to the mark of calm-abiding, but instead directing attention, with various unwholesome discursive thoughts, to relatives, homeland, denial of death's nearness … Not directing attention to conditioned dependent origination, but instead conceiving, in an unwise manner, a self and what belongs to a self within the conditioned formations of the three times — this is the unwise attention meant here.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps this list directly onto the five hindrances and their remedies already established: craving grows precisely because attention keeps returning to a person's attractive surface rather than to the body's underlying impurity; ill will grows because attention dwells on the offense rather than on loving-kindness; dullness and drowsiness grow because attention settles into literal or figurative darkness rather than deliberately cultivating a mental image of brightness; restlessness and worry grow because attention wanders to family, homeland, and nostalgic memory rather than staying with the calm-abiding object; and doubt grows because, lacking any real grasp of dependent origination, attention keeps reconstructing a fixed self and its possessions out of what are actually just momentary conditioned formations. In every single case, Cháng Bǎi stresses, the affliction is not something that simply happens to a practitioner — it is actively fed by exactly where attention is habitually pointed, which means the affliction's cause and its cure turn out to be the very same lever, aimed in opposite directions.

34. Section: The Dangers of the Four Obstructions — Failing to Attain, and Falling to the Lower Realms

Left unaddressed, these four obstructions carry real consequences: the four dhyānas remain forever unattained, or, once attained, are lost again — and the corrupted states of mind they produce can lead, at life's end, to rebirth in the lower realms.

Chinese Source

云何遍知諸障過患？謂遍了知此障有故，於其四種未證不證，已證退失。敗壞瑜伽所有加行，有染汙住、有苦惱住，自毀、毀他，身壞命終生諸惡趣。是名遍知諸障過患。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is thoroughly knowing the dangers of the obstructions? That is, thoroughly knowing that, because these obstructions exist, one fails to attain the four dhyānas, or, having attained them, loses them again. Yogic practice and its entire preparatory effort are ruined; one abides in a defiled state, abides in a state of distress, harms oneself and harms others, and, upon the body's destruction and life's end, is born into the lower realms. This is called thoroughly knowing the dangers of the obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks "abiding in a defiled state" as failing to sever five specific corrupting states tied to desire and unwholesome action — joy driven by desire, sorrow driven by desire, joy driven by unwholesome action, sorrow driven by unwholesome action, and a fifth, more insidious "unwholesome-driven equanimity": simply permitting unwholesome thoughts to arise unchecked without either enjoying or resisting them, whether through passive indulgence, idle plotting of wrongdoing never quite acted on, habituated moral numbness, or flat indifference to one's own arising greed, hatred, and delusion. He treats this fifth state as the most dangerous precisely because it produces neither the guilt nor the disturbance that might otherwise prompt correction — it feels like nothing at all, which is exactly what lets it become permanent. "Abiding in a state of distress" is simply the flip side: without severing these corrupting states, neither genuine joy nor genuine ease nor concentration itself can ever actually take root, leaving the practitioner stuck indefinitely short of any real attainment.

35. Section: Antidotes — the Six Recollections for Timidity, and Their Specific Applications

The treatise now supplies the actual remedies: a general antidote for timidity (the six recollections), specific antidotes matched to timidity's six individual causes, and finally the single governing antidote for the remaining three obstructions.

Chinese Source

謂諸怯弱，總用隨念以為對治。由隨念作意慶悅其心，令諸怯弱已生除遣，未生不生。其身羸劣、太過加行、初修加行，用於精進平等通達以為對治。不修加行，用恭敬聽聞、勤加請問以為對治。煩惱熾盛，用不淨等所緣加行以為對治。若未串習，即用如是思擇方便以為對治……應正思擇，於其遠離捨不喜樂，修習喜樂。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for timidity in general, the six recollections serve, altogether, as its antidote: through the mental engagement of recollection, one delights and gladdens the mind, causing timidity already arisen to be dispelled and timidity not yet arisen not to arise. Bodily frailty, excessive exertion, and being a genuine beginner are countered through even, thoroughly understood diligence. Failing to practice at all is countered through reverent listening and diligently asking questions. Intensely burning affliction is countered through the fitting purifying-conduct object. Not yet being habituated is countered through this very deliberation … one should rightly discern, and set aside discontent with seclusion, cultivating delight in it instead.

Chinese Source

餘蓋覆等非理作意，用彼相違如理作意以為對治。應知是名修習對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

The unwise attention behind being covered over, discursive thought, and self-conceit is countered through wise attention, its exact reverse. This should be known as cultivating the antidote.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the six recollections (of the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, one's own precepts, one's own generosity, and the heavens) return here from their earlier role energizing a sinking mind, now applied specifically against timidity's emotional resistance to renunciation itself. He then matches remedy to cause with precision: physical frailty, excessive strain, and inexperience are treated together through a single balanced, well-informed diligence — neither so gentle it accomplishes nothing nor so forceful it breaks the practitioner, and grounded in genuine understanding of the whole preceding apparatus of stillness, insight, energizing, and equanimizing; simple non-practice is treated through the plain remedy of actually listening and asking, since the obstruction there is ignorance of the practice's own value; intense affliction is treated by returning to whichever of the five purifying-conduct objects actually fits the dominant affliction; and unfamiliarity with seclusion is treated through a specific piece of reasoning — reminding oneself that today's reluctance, left unaddressed, guarantees tomorrow's identical reluctance, so that cultivated delight, however initially forced, is the only way out of the cycle, the way sustained practice eventually makes a difficult discipline feel natural. For the remaining three obstructions, the remedy is stated with maximum economy: since their cause is unwise attention, their antidote is simply its precise opposite, wise attention, applied to whichever of the five hindrances or eight discursive thoughts has actually arisen — completing all four steps (own-nature, causes, dangers, antidotes) for all four obstructions, and with them, the fourth locus, purifying the obstructions, in full.

36. Section: The Five Loci Recapped — Locus Four Closes

The treatise closes purifying the obstructions with a full recap of all five loci that have structured fascicles 30 and 31 together, before turning to overview material on the sheer scope of calm-abiding and insight.

Chinese Source

又遍了知諸障自性，是能障礙、是能染汙、是黑品攝、是應遠離……由是因緣，能令其心淨除諸障。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, thoroughly knowing the obstructions' own-nature — that they obstruct, that they defile, that they belong to the dark category, and that they are to be abandoned … through these causes and conditions, the mind is able to be purified of the obstructions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi uses this closing passage to step back and recap the full five-locus structure spanning fascicles 30 and 31 together: safeguarding the provisions for concentration (the fourteen-item twofold provisions, precepts foremost among them); seclusion (place, deportment, body, and mind, each brought to full perfection); one-pointedness of mind (samādhi's own definition, the nine mental abidings for calm-abiding, the four wisdom-conducts for insight, all five purifying-conduct objects, and eighteen preparatory practices); purifying the obstructions (the four obstructions of timidity, being covered over, discursive thought, and self-conceit, each worked through own-nature, cause, danger, and antidote, just completed); and the fifth and final locus, cultivating mental engagement, still to come — where, Cháng Bǎi notes, a practitioner must reach at least the attained mental engagement of near-attainment concentration for genuine yogic practice to be said to have truly begun.

37. Section: Stillness and Insight Mutually Reinforce, Culminating in Transformation of the Basis

With locus 4 closed, the treatise steps back to explain, in overview, how calm-abiding and insight build on each other in an accelerating cycle — ultimately purifying the very basis of the mind itself.

Chinese Source

悟入道理正修行者，如如毗鉢舍那串習清淨增上力故，增長廣大；如是如是能生身心所有輕安奢摩他品，當知亦得增長廣大。如如身心獲得輕安，如是如是於其所緣心一境性轉復增長；如如於緣心一境性轉復增長，如是如是轉復獲得身心輕安。

Ru-Yi Translation

For one who correctly practices, having entered into the principle: to whatever degree insight, through habituated purification, becomes empowered and expands, to that same degree the calm-abiding that generates all bodily and mental pliancy should also be known to expand. To whatever degree body and mind attain pliancy, to that degree one-pointedness of mind, with respect to its object, further increases; and to whatever degree one-pointedness of mind further increases, to that degree bodily and mental pliancy is further attained.

Chinese Source

心一境性、身心輕安，如是二法展轉相依，展轉相屬。身心輕安、心一境性，如是二法若得轉依，方乃究竟。得轉依故，於所知事現量智生。

Ru-Yi Translation

One-pointedness of mind and bodily-mental pliancy — these two states depend upon each other, mutually bound together. Bodily-mental pliancy and one-pointedness of mind — only once these two states achieve transformation of the basis do they reach their culmination. Because the basis is transformed, direct, unmediated wisdom regarding the object of knowledge arises.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi describes an accelerating feedback loop: purer insight generates greater bodily-mental pliancy (calm-abiding's own fruit), which in turn deepens one-pointedness of mind, which in turn generates still more pliancy — each strengthening the other until, at some threshold, something more fundamental than incremental improvement occurs: "transformation of the basis," the actual conversion of the ālaya-consciousness's afflictive seeds into pure ones. He explains this technical term precisely: sustained cultivation of stillness, energizing, and equanimizing eventually causes every coarse, heavy affliction to be extinguished at the root, so that the very basis a practitioner depends upon (the aggregates, and ultimately the ālaya-consciousness itself) becomes purified — and once purified, direct, non-conceptual wisdom regarding reality simply arises on its own, no longer mediated by any residual grasping. This, Cháng Bǎi notes, is the technical account of what it means to genuinely realize emptiness through sustained stillness-and-insight practice, not a single dramatic breakthrough but the compounding result of exactly the reinforcing cycle just described.

38. Section: How to Know a Purifying-Conduct Object Has Been Perfected — Three Signs, Illustrated Through Impurity

The treatise now answers a natural practical question: how does a practitioner actually know when contemplation of impurity — or any of the other four purifying-conduct objects — has been genuinely perfected, rather than merely practiced?

Chinese Source

問：齊何當言究竟獲得不淨觀耶？答：以要言之，修觀行者於不淨觀正加行中，親近、修習、多修習故。若行、若住雖有種種境界現前，雖復觀察所有眾相，而住自性，不由加行，多分不淨行相顯現，非諸淨相。由於不淨善修習故，於能隨順貪欲纏處法，心不趣入、心不愛樂、心不信解，安住於捨，深生厭逆。當於爾時，修觀行者應自了知：我今已得不淨觀，我今已得所修果。齊此名為於不淨觀已得究竟。與此相違，當知名為未得究竟。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: To what point should one say contemplation of impurity has been genuinely perfected? Answer: In brief, through drawing near to, cultivating, and extensively cultivating correct practice within contemplation of impurity. Whether walking or standing, though various objects present themselves, and though one observes their every feature, one abides in that very nature — without effort, images of impurity predominantly appear, not images of attractiveness. Because contemplation of impurity has been well cultivated, with respect to states conducive to craving, the mind does not enter into them, does not delight in them, does not place confidence in them, but abides in equanimity, deeply and thoroughly repelled. At that time, the practitioner should know for himself: "I have now attained contemplation of impurity; I have now attained the fruit of this practice." To this point is contemplation of impurity said to be genuinely perfected. The reverse of this should be known as not yet perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distills this into three concrete signs applicable to any of the five objects: first, the corresponding image now arises spontaneously and predominantly, in ordinary activity as much as in seated practice, without any deliberate effort to summon it; second, the specific affliction the object was meant to counter — here, craving — simply no longer takes hold when its normal trigger appears, the mind neither drawn in, delighted, nor persuaded by an attractive surface, resting instead in settled, thoroughgoing aversion; and third, the practitioner directly knows this for himself, with no need for external confirmation. He retells the Upagupta story once more here for precisely this teaching: a disciple believing himself already free of desire, sent out to beg, found himself charmed by a smiling woman's beauty — and only in that very moment of attraction recognized his contemplation was not yet perfected, whereupon he immediately visualized her teeth as a bare skull and, desire extinguished on the spot, realized the fruit he had wrongly believed already his. Cháng Bǎi's point: perfection is tested precisely in the moment of genuine temptation, not in the calm of the meditation seat where no real object is present to challenge it.

39. The Same Three Signs Applied to Loving-Kindness, Dependent Origination, Elemental Analysis, and Breath-Counting

Chinese Source

如不淨觀，如是慈愍、緣性緣起、界差別、阿那波那念，當知亦爾。於中差別者，謂多分慈心行相顯現，非瞋恚相……多分無常、苦、空、無我行相顯現，非彼常、樂、身見俱行愚癡行相……多分種種界性、非一界性身聚差別相想顯現，非身聚想……多分內寂靜想、奢摩他想顯現，非戲論想。

Ru-Yi Translation

As with contemplation of impurity, so too with loving-kindness, dependent-origination contemplation, elemental analysis, and mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath — the same should be known to hold. The difference lies only in this: for loving-kindness, images of a loving mind predominantly appear, not images of hatred … for dependent-origination contemplation, images of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self predominantly appear, not the delusory images bound up with permanence, pleasure, and the view of a fixed self … for elemental analysis, images of the body as a composite of differentiated elements, not a single unified mass, predominantly appear, not the image of a solid mass … for mindfulness of the breath, images of inner tranquility and calm-abiding predominantly appear, not images of idle discursive thought.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi confirms each of the remaining four objects follows the identical three-sign test, only the specific content differing: loving-kindness perfected shows itself as spontaneous warmth even toward provocation, with hatred's normal trigger simply failing to ignite anger; dependent-origination contemplation perfected shows itself as spontaneous impermanence-awareness, with delusion's characteristic distortions — treating the impermanent as permanent, treating suffering as pleasure, clinging to a fixed self — no longer arising; elemental analysis perfected shows itself as spontaneously seeing any body, one's own or another's, as a composite of differentiated elements rather than a single solid "thing," with the pride that depends on that solidity correspondingly absent; and breath-counting perfected shows itself as a mind that spontaneously settles into calm-abiding, wandering discursive thought's normal foothold no longer available. Each, in other words, is measured the same way: what appears spontaneously without effort, what affliction no longer takes hold, and whether the practitioner directly knows the difference in himself.

40. Section: Defining the Dual Operation of Calm-Abiding and Insight

The treatise now answers its deepest technical question yet: precisely what must occur for calm-abiding and insight to be said to operate together, "in tandem," rather than merely alternating one after the other.

Chinese Source

問：齊何當言奢摩他、毗鉢舍那二種和合平等俱轉，由此說名雙運轉道？答：若有獲得九相心住中第九相心住，謂三摩呬多。彼用如是圓滿三摩地為所依止，於法觀中修增上慧。

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: To what point should one say that calm-abiding and insight operate together in equal union, whereby this is called the dual-operation path? Answer: When one has attained the ninth of the nine stages of mental abiding, namely equipoise. Relying on such perfected samādhi as one's basis, one cultivates superior wisdom within contemplation of phenomena.

Chinese Source

彼於爾時，由法觀故，任運轉道，無功用轉，不由加行，毗鉢舍那清淨鮮白，隨奢摩他調柔攝受，如奢摩他道攝受而轉。齊此名為奢摩他、毗鉢舍那二種和合平等俱轉。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, through contemplating phenomena, the path proceeds spontaneously, functioning without effort, not depending on application; insight becomes pure and radiant, gathered in and made pliant by calm-abiding, proceeding just as calm-abiding's own path is gathered in. To this point is it called calm-abiding and insight operating together in equal union.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the crucial prerequisite: equipoise, the ninth and final stage of mental abiding, must first be reached — the point where stillness itself has become fully effortless — before insight practiced within that stillness can genuinely be called "dual operation" rather than simply alternating exercises. He unpacks the resulting state through its own defining marks: contemplation proceeds "spontaneously," the object of insight appearing without needing to be deliberately summoned; it proceeds "without effort," no longer requiring strain to sustain; and insight itself becomes "pure and radiant" — unclouded by the ten distracting marks, the eight discursive thoughts, or the five hindrances — while remaining perfectly gathered and pliant, exactly as calm-abiding's own tranquility is gathered and pliant. Cháng Bǎi notes this technical threshold corresponds, in the Hearer's path, to the attainment of stream-entry, and in the bodhisattva path, to the first ground — a genuinely advanced accomplishment, not something reached casually. With this precise definition of dual operation given, the treatise's account of how calm-abiding and insight ultimately unite into a single path is complete.

41. Section: A Summary Verse Recaps One-Pointedness of Mind and Purifying the Obstructions

Before opening the fifth and final locus, the treatise pauses to recap loci three and four in three compact four-line verses — reviewing the three gates and six things of insight, the nine preparatory practices, and the four steps for purifying obstructions.

Chinese Source

中嗢柁南曰：相尋思伺察　隨行有三門　義事相品時　理六事差別。初相應加行　次串習無緩　無顛倒應時　解了無厭足　不棄捨善軛　最後正加行　是九應當知　有二品差別。知自性因緣　見彼諸過患　正修習對治　令障得清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

The middle summary verse says: Mark, investigation, examination — these are followed as the three gates; meaning, substance, characteristic, category, time, and principle — these are the differentiated six things. First, fitting practice; next, habituated and unhurried practice; undistorted and timely practice; understanding and insatiable practice; not abandoning the good yoke; and last, correct practice — these nine should be known, each with its bright and dark distinction. Knowing their own-nature and causes, seeing their dangers, correctly cultivating their antidotes — thus are the obstructions purified.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes this triad of verses is offered purely as a memory aid, condensing everything just covered across fascicles 30 and 31 into a form meant to be memorized and recited: the three gates and six things structuring insight's practice, all nine preparatory practices in their fixed order, and the four-step method (own-nature, cause, danger, antidote) for purifying any obstruction whatsoever. He notes nothing new is introduced here — the verse's value is purely mnemonic, letting a practitioner hold the entire preceding structure in mind at a glance.

42. Section: The Fifth and Final Locus Opens — Cultivating Mental Engagement

The treatise now opens the fifth and last of the five loci for establishing yogic practice: four specific kinds of mental engagement a beginner must cultivate from the very outset.

Chinese Source

云何修作意？謂初修業者始修業時，於如是所安立普遍相中，由一境性及淨諸障，離邪加行，學正加行。彼應最初作如是念：我今為證心一境性及斷喜樂，當勤修習四種作意。何等為四？一、調練心作意，二、滋潤心作意，三、生輕安作意，四、淨智見作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is cultivating mental engagement? That is, when a beginner first takes up practice, within the universal marks thus established, through one-pointedness of mind and purifying the obstructions, he abandons wrong practice and learns correct practice. He should, from the very outset, reflect thus: "I now, in order to realize one-pointedness of mind and to sever attachment to worldly joy, should diligently cultivate four kinds of mental engagement." What are the four? First, the mental engagement of taming the mind. Second, the mental engagement of moistening the mind. Third, the mental engagement of generating pliancy. Fourth, the mental engagement of purifying wisdom and insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "severing attachment to worldly joy" as the resolve that must precede all four engagements: recognizing that both worldly decline and worldly flourishing alike are, in the end, equally unsatisfying and impermanent, so that neither is worth clinging to — an outlook that must be genuinely felt, not merely intellectually granted, before the four engagements can do their work. He previews all four before their individual detail: taming the mind generates revulsion toward whatever is genuinely loathsome; moistening the mind generates delight toward whatever is genuinely worth esteeming; generating pliancy alternates between the two, settling the mind into an inwardly still, markless, non-discriminating state that dissolves the body and mind's underlying heaviness; and purifying wisdom and insight, relying on that same inward stillness, cultivates superior wisdom through repeated contemplation of phenomena. Together, he notes, these four span the entire arc from a genuine beginner's very first sitting through the cultivation of actual insight — the last locus completing what the first four loci made possible.

43. The Four Engagements Defined: Taming, Moistening, Generating Pliancy, and Purifying Insight

Chinese Source

云何調練心作意？謂由此作意，於可厭患法令心厭離，是名調練心作意。云何滋潤心作意？謂由此作意，於可欣尚法令心欣樂，是名滋潤心作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mental engagement of taming the mind? That is, through this engagement, one causes the mind to become revulsed toward loathsome things — this is called the mental engagement of taming the mind. What is the mental engagement of moistening the mind? That is, through this engagement, one causes the mind to delight in esteemed things — this is called the mental engagement of moistening the mind.

Chinese Source

云何生輕安作意？謂由此作意，於時時間，於可厭法令心厭離；於時時間，於可欣法令心欣樂已；安住內寂靜、無相、無分別中，一境念轉。由是因緣，對治一切身心麤重，能令一切身心適悅，生起一切身心輕安。云何淨智見作意？謂由此作意，於時時間，即用如是內心寂靜為所依止，由內靜心數數加行，於法觀中修增上慧，是名淨智見作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the mental engagement of generating pliancy? That is, through this engagement, at fitting times one causes the mind to become revulsed toward loathsome things, and at fitting times one causes the mind to delight in esteemed things; having done so, one abides in inward stillness, free of marks, free of discursive thought, with mindfulness continuing on a single object. Through this, one counters all bodily and mental heaviness, brings all body and mind to ease, and gives rise to complete bodily and mental pliancy. What is the mental engagement of purifying wisdom and insight? That is, through this engagement, at fitting times, relying precisely on that same inward stillness, through repeated inward application one cultivates superior wisdom within contemplation of phenomena — this is called the mental engagement of purifying wisdom and insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these four as building directly on each other: taming and moistening work as a matched pair, alternately cultivating disgust toward what should be abandoned and delight toward what should be pursued — a beginner needs both, not just one, since revulsion alone without any positive counterweight tends toward grim aversion, while delight alone without revulsion tends toward complacent attachment. Generating pliancy then combines both in alternation, settling the mind into the same markless, non-discriminating stillness already detailed as calm-abiding's own signature state, directly countering the body and mind's underlying "heaviness" (the residual incapacity that makes sustained practice feel effortful) with genuine ease. Purifying wisdom and insight, the fourth and final engagement, is where that same inward stillness finally becomes the platform for cultivating actual wisdom — matching, Cháng Bǎi notes, precisely the same stillness-then-insight sequence structuring the entire treatise since fascicle 30's opening definition of samādhi.

44. Four Objects of Loathing, Three Objects of Esteem

Chinese Source

何等名為可厭患處？略有四種可厭患處：謂自衰損及他衰損，現在會遇正現前時，如理作意數思惟故，成可厭處。若自興盛及他興盛，過去盡滅離變壞時，如理作意數思惟故，成可厭處。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is called a loathsome object? There are, in brief, four kinds of loathsome object: one's own decline and another's decline, when actually encountered and directly present, become, through repeated sound-reasoning attention, a loathsome object; and one's own flourishing and another's flourishing, when they have entirely passed away, ceased, departed, or degraded, become, through repeated sound-reasoning attention, a loathsome object.

Chinese Source

略有三種可欣尚處：一者、三寶；二者、學處清淨、尸羅清淨；三者、於自所證差別，深生信解，心無怯弱。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are, in brief, three kinds of esteemed object: first, the Three Jewels; second, purity of the precepts one has undertaken; third, deep confidence and understanding, free of timidity, regarding the various things one has personally attained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the loathsome objects deliberately span both self and other, and both decline and flourishing — decline (in wealth, health, lifespan, or loved ones) is loathsome because it is painful in the moment, while flourishing is loathsome not while it lasts but the instant it is seen to have already ended, decayed, or been lost, which reveals its true impermanent nature. Reflecting on both one's own and others' cases prevents the exercise from curdling into pure self-pity or, worse, quiet satisfaction at others' misfortune — the point is recognizing conditioned existence's instability wherever it appears, not brooding on personal grievance. The three esteemed objects then supply the positive counterweight: the Three Jewels, one's own precept purity, and — perhaps most psychologically important — genuine, unafraid confidence that one's own already-attained progress, however modest, is real and capable of growing further.

45. Section: The Triple Gem as Object of Esteem

The treatise details the first esteemed object at length: grateful recollection of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, and the hope of a genuinely good death that recollection inspires.

Chinese Source

謂作是念：我今善得如是大利，謂蒙如來應正等覺為我大師。我今善得如是大利，謂善說法毗奈耶中，我得出家。我今善得如是大利，謂我與諸具戒、具德、忍辱、柔和、成賢善法同梵行者共為法侶。我今當得賢善命終、賢善殞歿，當得賢善趣於後世。

Ru-Yi Translation

One reflects thus: "I have now truly gained such great benefit — that the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly Enlightened One, is my great teacher. I have now truly gained such great benefit — that I have been able to go forth within the well-taught Dharma and discipline. I have now truly gained such great benefit — that I have, as fellow companions in the path, those of pure precepts, virtue, patience, and gentleness, who accord with the wholesome Dharma." "I shall attain a good death, a good passing, and shall attain a good destination in the life to come."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats each of the three reflections as a distinct, deliberately savored gratitude: having the Buddha himself as teacher, rarer than anything in ordinary life; having actually gone forth within a correctly transmitted teaching and discipline, rather than merely hearing of one; and having genuine companions on the path — patient, gentle, virtuous — to walk alongside rather than walking alone. He clarifies "a good death" through its technical range across the four stages of realization: a stream-enterer needing at most seven more rounds of rebirth before final liberation, a once-returner needing only one more, a non-returner never again returning to the desire realm, and an arhat facing no further rebirth at all — each, in its own degree, a death faced without the confusion or dread that grips an ordinary being who has done nothing to prepare for it. Cháng Bǎi reads this whole passage as showing gratitude itself functioning as a genuine object of meditation, not mere sentiment — deliberately recollected, it directly counters the discouragement timidity depends on.

46. Precept Purity and Confidence in One's Own Attainment

Chinese Source

謂作是念：我今善得如是大利。謂於如來應正等覺大師、善說法毗奈耶、善修正行聲聞眾中，我得與彼同梵行者，同戒、同學、同修慈仁身語意業、同其所見、同所受用……謂無悔為先，發生歡喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

One reflects thus: "I have now truly gained such great benefit — that, within the community of the Tathāgata, the Worthy One, the Perfectly Enlightened One, the well-taught Dharma and discipline, and those who rightly practice correct conduct, I share the same precepts, the same training, the same cultivation of loving-kindness in body, speech, and mind, the same views, and the same use of resources with my fellow practitioners of the pure life." … That is, freedom from regret comes first, and from it, joy arises.

Chinese Source

謂作是念：我今有力、有所堪能，尸羅清淨，堪為法器。得與如是同梵行者同清淨戒，得與有智正至善士同其所見，我有堪能精勤修習如是正行。於現法中，能得未得、能觸未觸、能證未證。由是令心生大歡喜。

Ru-Yi Translation

One reflects thus: "I now have strength, I have capability; my precepts are pure, and I am fit to be a vessel for the Dharma. I am able to share pure precepts with such fellow practitioners of the pure life, and to share the same views with wise, correctly arrived, virtuous persons. I am capable of diligently cultivating such correct conduct — and in this very life, able to attain what is not yet attained, to touch what is not yet touched, to realize what is not yet realized." Through this, great joy arises in the mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the six marks of genuine spiritual friendship named here — sharing precepts, training, kindly conduct in body, speech, and mind, views, and material resources — as exactly the classical six kinds of harmony, and notes that recollecting one's own intact precepts produces joy through a very direct mechanism: freedom from regret. A broken precept nags at the conscience and unsettles concentration; a genuinely clean conscience, by contrast, is itself a source of steady contentment requiring no further justification. On confidence in one's own attainment, Cháng Bǎi stresses this is not arrogance but its precise opposite — a plain, sober trust that one is, in fact, a fit vessel for practice, capable of real progress, which alone overcomes the self-doubt that timidity depends on; every accomplished practitioner, he notes, was once a beginner who had to first believe progress was possible before it actually happened.

Fascicle 31 Complete — the Goldsmith's Craft

Fascicle 31 carried the third yogic process from where fascicle 30 left it — completing all five objects for purifying conduct, the eighteen preparatory practices, and the four obstructions' full treatment — before opening the fifth and final locus, cultivating mental engagement, and closing on a vivid image: the practitioner's mind, refined through taming, moistening, generating pliancy, and purifying insight, becomes as workable as gold or silver in a skilled smith's hands, ready to be shaped into whatever the practitioner genuinely aspires to.

Chinese Source

譬如黠慧鍛金銀師，或彼弟子，於時時間燒鍊金銀，令其棄捨一切垢穢；於時時間投清冷水，令於彼彼莊嚴具業，有所堪任，調柔隨順……隨其所樂種種義中，如所信解，皆能成辦。

Ru-Yi Translation

It is like a skilled goldsmith or silversmith, or his apprentice: from time to time smelting the gold or silver, causing it to shed all its impurities; from time to time plunging it into cool water, so that it becomes fit for crafting into whatever ornament is desired, pliant and workable … whatever object one aspires to, according to one's own confident understanding, one is able to accomplish it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the goldsmith's alternating fire and cool water as a direct parallel to taming (the mind's impurities burned away through revulsion toward what is loathsome) and moistening (the mind made receptive through delight in what is esteemed) — repeated cycles, not a single treatment, gradually rendering what was once rigid and unworkable genuinely pliant. Once that pliancy is reached, generating pliancy and purifying wisdom and insight — the third and fourth engagements — let the practitioner shape that workable mind into whichever specific object of practice is genuinely aspired to, whether one of the five purifying-conduct objects, the skillful-means objects, or the purifying-affliction objects still to come. With this image, fascicle 31's account of cultivating mental engagement reaches a natural resting point, though not yet its full close: engagements three and four have been defined and their combined function shown, but not yet given the same extended treatment engagements one and two just received. The fifth locus, cultivating mental engagement, therefore continues into fascicle 32, alongside whatever of the skillful-means and purifying-affliction objects still remain to complete the third yogic process's account of establishing yogic practice.
