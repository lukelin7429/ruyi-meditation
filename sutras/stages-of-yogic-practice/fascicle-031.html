Fascicle 31 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十一 · The Third Yogic Process Continues

Dependent Origination, the Elements, and the Breath

Fascicle 31 continues directly from fascicle 30, completing the third of the third yogic process's five loci — one-pointedness of mind — with the remaining three of five objects for purifying conduct: contemplating dependent origination as the antidote to delusion, elemental analysis as the antidote to pride, and breath-counting as the antidote to scattered thought. It then opens the fourth locus, purifying the obstructions to concentration.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 31 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Dependent Origination Contemplation Opens — No Creator, No Fixed Self, No First Cause

Fascicle 31 opens exactly where fascicle 30 closed, moving to the third of the five objects for purifying conduct: contemplating dependent origination, the antidote to delusion. Its opening teaching is a direct refutation of every rival account of how the world and living beings come to be.

Chinese Source

云何勤修緣起觀者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依緣性緣起增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正了知如是如是諸法生故，彼彼法生；如是如是諸法滅故，彼彼法滅。此中都無自在、作者、生者、化者，能造諸法；亦無自性、士夫、中間能轉變者，轉變諸法。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates dependent-origination contemplation investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on conditioned dependent origination, one is able to correctly know that because such-and-such phenomena arise, such-and-such other phenomena arise; and because such-and-such phenomena cease, such-and-such other phenomena cease. Herein there is no sovereign deity, no maker, no producer, no transformer able to fashion phenomena; nor is there any inherent nature, cosmic person, or intermediate power able to transform phenomena.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames dependent-origination contemplation as the antidote for delusion in its specifically philosophical form — not mere confusion, but active wrong view about how life and the world actually arise. He reads the fourfold denial here as a direct refutation of rival Indian schools' "unequal causation" theories: no all-powerful deity (refuting the claim that Śiva creates all beings, itself begging the question of who created Śiva); no fixed "maker," "producer," or "transformer" standing apart from the process itself; no unknowable "primal nature" (refuting Sāṃkhya's puruṣa-prakṛti dualism); no eternal cosmic "person" residing within the five aggregates as their unchanging owner; and no Great Brahmā, seated in the "intermediate" meditative state between the first and second dhyānas, shaping the universe from outside it while itself somehow arising from nothing at all. Against all of these, dependent origination offers a single formula — "because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises" — cited from the *Sūtra on Dependent Origination*, tracing the twelve links from ignorance through aging-and-death, and its reverse: extinguish ignorance, and the entire chain of suffering unravels link by link. Cháng Bǎi stresses that life, on this account, is never authored by anyone — it is simply conditions arranging and rearranging themselves, moment by moment, with no fixed owner anywhere in the process.

2. Investigating Substance: The Twelve Links, and Mind Itself as Empty of Fixed Existence

Chinese Source

復審思擇十二有支若內若外，而起勝解，是名尋思諸緣起事。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns the twelve links, both inner and outer, giving rise to a vivid understanding of them — this is called investigating the substance of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "inner" as the six sense faculties and six consciousnesses, "outer" as the six sense objects — contact between them, like sparks struck from rubbed wood, giving rise to consciousness's ceaseless activity, none of it fixed or substantial. He draws on the classical elephant-and-pool simile from the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*: no one sees the elephant itself bathing, only the muddied water and the footprints left behind, from which the elephant's passage is inferred — just so, mind itself can never be directly grasped as a fixed thing, only inferred from its effects (a spoken word, an angry gesture), the way fire cannot be found already hiding inside unstruck wood, yet reliably appears once the friction begins and vanishes again once it stops. He cites the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*'s teaching that consciousness is "merely a name, without any real substance behind the name," and Kumārajīva's rendering of the *Sūtra on Non-Action*, which even denies that "ordinary being" and "sage" are truths beyond convention. Cháng Bǎi's point: investigating dependent origination's substance means directly confronting that neither the world of objects nor the mind observing them holds up as something fixed and truly existing — both are simply the twelve links in motion, nothing more.

3. Milestone: Particular and Universal Characteristics — Impermanence, Suffering, Emptiness

The treatise now turns from what dependent origination's links are (substance) to their defining features, both individually (particular characteristic) and as a set (universal characteristic) — arriving at the three marks shared by everything conditioned.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：如是一切緣生諸行，無不皆是本無今有，有已散滅，是故前後皆是無常。皆有生老病死法故，其性是苦。不自在故，中間士夫不可得故，性空、無我。是名尋思緣起共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: all these conditioned formations, without exception, did not exist before and now exist, and having existed, will disperse and cease — hence, in every respect, they are impermanent. Because they all carry birth, old age, sickness, and death, their nature is suffering. Because they are not self-governing, and no intermediate cosmic person can be found within them, their nature is empty and without self. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes the particular characteristics of each of the twelve links are simply the detailed analysis already given in fascicles 9 and 10 (ignorance's nineteen kinds, the eight forms of consciousness, and so on), cross-referenced rather than repeated. The universal characteristic then draws three conclusions from that same material: since everything dependently arisen appeared where nothing existed before and will disperse again, it is impermanent through and through; since it carries birth, aging, sickness, and death, its nature is suffering (the *Compendium of Determinations*' verse: "phenomena cannot produce another, nor can they produce themselves — through the gathering of many conditions they arise, not as something old made newly present," and its counterpart on cessation, "phenomena cannot extinguish another, nor extinguish themselves — through conditions they arise, and having arisen, naturally cease"); and since nothing within the process is self-governing, and no eternal cosmic intermediary (as certain rival schools claimed) can be found running it from within, its nature is empty of any real self. Cháng Bǎi calls this the treatise's most direct route from "everything is conditioned" to "everything is empty" — the same three marks appear throughout Buddhist literature, but here derived specifically from dependent origination's own structure rather than asserted independently.

4. Category and Time: Delusion versus Clarity, and Impermanence Across the Three Times

Chinese Source

復審思擇：我若於彼無常、苦、空、無我諸行如實道理發生迷惑，便為顛倒，黑品所攝，廣說如前。若不迷惑，便無顛倒，白品所攝，廣說如前。是名尋思諸緣起品。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: if I become confused regarding the true principle that conditioned formations are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, this is a distortion, belonging to the dark category, as extensively explained before. If I am not confused, this is free of distortion, belonging to the bright category, as extensively explained before. This is called investigating the category of dependent origination.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：於過去世所得自體無正常性，如是已住；於現在世所得自體無正常性，如是今住；於未來世所得自體無正常性，如是當住。是名尋思諸緣起時。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: the self-existence obtained in past lives had no fixed, constant nature — it simply abided as such. The self-existence of the present life has no fixed, constant nature — it simply abides as such now. The self-existence to be obtained in future lives will have no fixed, constant nature — it will simply abide as such. This is called investigating the time of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats category here simply as whether one has actually absorbed the preceding teaching — confusion about impermanence, suffering, and no-self is the "dark" failure mode already detailed at length under contemplation of impurity, while clarity is its "bright" mirror. On time, he offers the film-reel image: a lifetime looks like a single continuous, stable thing the way a film looks like continuous motion, but both are really a rapid succession of discrete, momentary frames — the sense of stability is an illusion of speed, not a genuine persistence. Past, present, and future selves are all, without exception, this same flickering, momentary process; none of the three carries any more fixed reality than the others.

5. Milestone: Principle — No Fixed Actor, Two Causes, Two Fruits, and Dependent Origination's Close

The final and richest component, principle, works dependent origination through mutual dependence (both ultimate and conventional truth, and the causal mechanics of karma and rebirth), then briefly through function, valid proof, and natural law — closing the treatise's third purifying-conduct object.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：唯有諸業及異熟果，其中主宰都不可得，所謂作者及與受者，唯有於法假想建立。謂於無明緣行乃至生緣老死中，發起假想，施設言論，說為作者及與受者：有如是名、如是種、如是性、如是飲食、如是領受若苦若樂、如是長壽、如是久住、如是極於壽量邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: there is only karma and its ripened fruit, with no sovereign master to be found among them — no "actor," no "one who receives" — such things are only provisionally established with respect to phenomena. That is, within ignorance conditioning volitional formations, on through birth conditioning aging-and-death, one gives rise to provisional conceptions and sets up expressions, speaking of an "actor" and "one who receives": having such-and-such a name, such-and-such a lineage, such-and-such a family, such-and-such food, such-and-such experience of pleasure or pain, such-and-such a lifespan, such-and-such a duration, such-and-such a final limit to that lifespan.

Chinese Source

又於此中有二種果及二種因。二種果者：一、自體果，二、受用境界果。二種因者：一、牽引因，二、生起因。……由此二因增上力故，便為三苦之所隨逐，招集一切純大苦蘊。如是名依觀待道理，尋思緣起所有道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, herein there are two kinds of fruit and two kinds of cause. The two fruits: first, the fruit of one's own being; second, the fruit of experienced circumstances. The two causes: first, the impelling cause; second, the generating cause. … Through the empowering force of these two causes, one is pursued by the three kinds of suffering, gathering the entire mass of suffering. This is called investigating the principles of dependent origination by way of the principle of mutual dependence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi separates two levels at work in this passage. At the ultimate level, there is no real "actor" accumulating karma and no real "receiver" experiencing its ripened fruit — searching the five aggregates for such a fixed party turns up nothing, the way searching a body for pleasure "in here" or "out there" finds it nowhere at all. Yet at the conventional level, karma and its fruit remain entirely real and binding: a name, a lineage, a family, a lifespan, and everything else that makes up an ordinary biography are all provisionally, but functionally, real — quoting the *Compendium of Determinations*' verse that once the body dissolves, its "name-body" (mental factors) dissolves with it, yet karma made is still karma received in a future life, since actor and receiver are strictly "neither the same nor different": not the same, because the aggregates that acted and the aggregates that later receive the fruit are genuinely distinct moments; not different, because the later fruit could never have arisen without the earlier act as its cause. He then details the causal mechanics: the "two fruits" are one's own resulting existence (body and mind themselves, the ripened result of past karma) and the pleasant or unpleasant experiences that existence then encounters; the "two causes" are the impelling cause (ignorance and formations, planting the seed for a future rebirth, elaborated across seven links through feeling) and the generating cause (craving, grasping, and becoming, which actually moisten that seed into ripening — Cháng Bǎi tells the vivid folk tale of a man's spirit, distracted by an old lover's beckoning en route to a routine errand in the underworld, tumbling straight through rebirth as a piglet and then a snake before being violently retrieved, as a cautionary illustration of how quickly craving redirects a wandering mind toward its next birth). He closes briefly on the remaining three principles — function (sustained cultivation of this contemplation reliably severs delusion), valid proof (confirmed by the Buddha's own teaching, direct inner experience, and inference alike), and natural law (this causal relationship simply holds, not open to further debate) — closing dependent-origination contemplation, the third of the five purifying-conduct objects, in full.

6. Milestone: Elemental Analysis Opens — the Antidote to Pride

The fourth of the five purifying-conduct objects now opens: analyzing the body into its six constituent elements, the antidote to excessive pride and self-importance.

Chinese Source

云何勤修界差別觀者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依界差別增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正解了一切界義。謂種性義及種子義、因義、性義，是其界義。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates elemental-analysis contemplation investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on the differentiation of elements, one is able to correctly understand the meaning of "element" in every sense. That is, the meaning of lineage, the meaning of seed, the meaning of cause, and the meaning of nature — these are the meanings of "element."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the six elements this contemplation dissects the body into — earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness, the same six the classical texts say a being enters the womb dependent upon — and explains why this particular practice targets pride specifically: breaking the body down into six impersonal, lawfully arising constituents leaves no room for the sense of a single, superior "I" that pride depends on. He glosses "element" itself through four overlapping senses drawn from Buddhist technical vocabulary: lineage (a category or type, as in the ten dharma-realms of hell-being through buddha, each with its own causal lineage), seed (a causal potential capable of producing its own effect), cause, and nature — all four converging on the same point: whatever is called an "element" is defined entirely by its causal role, never by being some fixed, independent thing in itself.
