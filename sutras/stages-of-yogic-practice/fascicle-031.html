Fascicle 31 · 瑜伽師地論卷第三十一 · The Third Yogic Process Continues

Dependent Origination, the Elements, and the Breath

Fascicle 31 continues directly from fascicle 30, completing the third of the third yogic process's five loci — one-pointedness of mind — with the remaining three of five objects for purifying conduct: contemplating dependent origination as the antidote to delusion, elemental analysis as the antidote to pride, and breath-counting as the antidote to scattered thought. It then opens the fourth locus, purifying the obstructions to concentration.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: translation in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 31 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Dependent Origination Contemplation Opens — No Creator, No Fixed Self, No First Cause

Fascicle 31 opens exactly where fascicle 30 closed, moving to the third of the five objects for purifying conduct: contemplating dependent origination, the antidote to delusion. Its opening teaching is a direct refutation of every rival account of how the world and living beings come to be.

Chinese Source

云何勤修緣起觀者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依緣性緣起增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正了知如是如是諸法生故，彼彼法生；如是如是諸法滅故，彼彼法滅。此中都無自在、作者、生者、化者，能造諸法；亦無自性、士夫、中間能轉變者，轉變諸法。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates dependent-origination contemplation investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on conditioned dependent origination, one is able to correctly know that because such-and-such phenomena arise, such-and-such other phenomena arise; and because such-and-such phenomena cease, such-and-such other phenomena cease. Herein there is no sovereign deity, no maker, no producer, no transformer able to fashion phenomena; nor is there any inherent nature, cosmic person, or intermediate power able to transform phenomena.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames dependent-origination contemplation as the antidote for delusion in its specifically philosophical form — not mere confusion, but active wrong view about how life and the world actually arise. He reads the fourfold denial here as a direct refutation of rival Indian schools' "unequal causation" theories: no all-powerful deity (refuting the claim that Śiva creates all beings, itself begging the question of who created Śiva); no fixed "maker," "producer," or "transformer" standing apart from the process itself; no unknowable "primal nature" (refuting Sāṃkhya's puruṣa-prakṛti dualism); no eternal cosmic "person" residing within the five aggregates as their unchanging owner; and no Great Brahmā, seated in the "intermediate" meditative state between the first and second dhyānas, shaping the universe from outside it while itself somehow arising from nothing at all. Against all of these, dependent origination offers a single formula — "because this exists, that exists; because this arises, that arises" — cited from the *Sūtra on Dependent Origination*, tracing the twelve links from ignorance through aging-and-death, and its reverse: extinguish ignorance, and the entire chain of suffering unravels link by link. Cháng Bǎi stresses that life, on this account, is never authored by anyone — it is simply conditions arranging and rearranging themselves, moment by moment, with no fixed owner anywhere in the process.

2. Investigating Substance: The Twelve Links, and Mind Itself as Empty of Fixed Existence

Chinese Source

復審思擇十二有支若內若外，而起勝解，是名尋思諸緣起事。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns the twelve links, both inner and outer, giving rise to a vivid understanding of them — this is called investigating the substance of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "inner" as the six sense faculties and six consciousnesses, "outer" as the six sense objects — contact between them, like sparks struck from rubbed wood, giving rise to consciousness's ceaseless activity, none of it fixed or substantial. He draws on the classical elephant-and-pool simile from the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*: no one sees the elephant itself bathing, only the muddied water and the footprints left behind, from which the elephant's passage is inferred — just so, mind itself can never be directly grasped as a fixed thing, only inferred from its effects (a spoken word, an angry gesture), the way fire cannot be found already hiding inside unstruck wood, yet reliably appears once the friction begins and vanishes again once it stops. He cites the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*'s teaching that consciousness is "merely a name, without any real substance behind the name," and Kumārajīva's rendering of the *Sūtra on Non-Action*, which even denies that "ordinary being" and "sage" are truths beyond convention. Cháng Bǎi's point: investigating dependent origination's substance means directly confronting that neither the world of objects nor the mind observing them holds up as something fixed and truly existing — both are simply the twelve links in motion, nothing more.

3. Milestone: Particular and Universal Characteristics — Impermanence, Suffering, Emptiness

The treatise now turns from what dependent origination's links are (substance) to their defining features, both individually (particular characteristic) and as a set (universal characteristic) — arriving at the three marks shared by everything conditioned.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：如是一切緣生諸行，無不皆是本無今有，有已散滅，是故前後皆是無常。皆有生老病死法故，其性是苦。不自在故，中間士夫不可得故，性空、無我。是名尋思緣起共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: all these conditioned formations, without exception, did not exist before and now exist, and having existed, will disperse and cease — hence, in every respect, they are impermanent. Because they all carry birth, old age, sickness, and death, their nature is suffering. Because they are not self-governing, and no intermediate cosmic person can be found within them, their nature is empty and without self. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first notes the particular characteristics of each of the twelve links are simply the detailed analysis already given in fascicles 9 and 10 (ignorance's nineteen kinds, the eight forms of consciousness, and so on), cross-referenced rather than repeated. The universal characteristic then draws three conclusions from that same material: since everything dependently arisen appeared where nothing existed before and will disperse again, it is impermanent through and through; since it carries birth, aging, sickness, and death, its nature is suffering (the *Compendium of Determinations*' verse: "phenomena cannot produce another, nor can they produce themselves — through the gathering of many conditions they arise, not as something old made newly present," and its counterpart on cessation, "phenomena cannot extinguish another, nor extinguish themselves — through conditions they arise, and having arisen, naturally cease"); and since nothing within the process is self-governing, and no eternal cosmic intermediary (as certain rival schools claimed) can be found running it from within, its nature is empty of any real self. Cháng Bǎi calls this the treatise's most direct route from "everything is conditioned" to "everything is empty" — the same three marks appear throughout Buddhist literature, but here derived specifically from dependent origination's own structure rather than asserted independently.

4. Category and Time: Delusion versus Clarity, and Impermanence Across the Three Times

Chinese Source

復審思擇：我若於彼無常、苦、空、無我諸行如實道理發生迷惑，便為顛倒，黑品所攝，廣說如前。若不迷惑，便無顛倒，白品所攝，廣說如前。是名尋思諸緣起品。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: if I become confused regarding the true principle that conditioned formations are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, this is a distortion, belonging to the dark category, as extensively explained before. If I am not confused, this is free of distortion, belonging to the bright category, as extensively explained before. This is called investigating the category of dependent origination.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：於過去世所得自體無正常性，如是已住；於現在世所得自體無正常性，如是今住；於未來世所得自體無正常性，如是當住。是名尋思諸緣起時。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: the self-existence obtained in past lives had no fixed, constant nature — it simply abided as such. The self-existence of the present life has no fixed, constant nature — it simply abides as such now. The self-existence to be obtained in future lives will have no fixed, constant nature — it will simply abide as such. This is called investigating the time of dependent origination.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats category here simply as whether one has actually absorbed the preceding teaching — confusion about impermanence, suffering, and no-self is the "dark" failure mode already detailed at length under contemplation of impurity, while clarity is its "bright" mirror. On time, he offers the film-reel image: a lifetime looks like a single continuous, stable thing the way a film looks like continuous motion, but both are really a rapid succession of discrete, momentary frames — the sense of stability is an illusion of speed, not a genuine persistence. Past, present, and future selves are all, without exception, this same flickering, momentary process; none of the three carries any more fixed reality than the others.

5. Milestone: Principle — No Fixed Actor, Two Causes, Two Fruits, and Dependent Origination's Close

The final and richest component, principle, works dependent origination through mutual dependence (both ultimate and conventional truth, and the causal mechanics of karma and rebirth), then briefly through function, valid proof, and natural law — closing the treatise's third purifying-conduct object.

Chinese Source

復審思擇：唯有諸業及異熟果，其中主宰都不可得，所謂作者及與受者，唯有於法假想建立。謂於無明緣行乃至生緣老死中，發起假想，施設言論，說為作者及與受者：有如是名、如是種、如是性、如是飲食、如是領受若苦若樂、如是長壽、如是久住、如是極於壽量邊際。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further carefully discerns: there is only karma and its ripened fruit, with no sovereign master to be found among them — no "actor," no "one who receives" — such things are only provisionally established with respect to phenomena. That is, within ignorance conditioning volitional formations, on through birth conditioning aging-and-death, one gives rise to provisional conceptions and sets up expressions, speaking of an "actor" and "one who receives": having such-and-such a name, such-and-such a lineage, such-and-such a family, such-and-such food, such-and-such experience of pleasure or pain, such-and-such a lifespan, such-and-such a duration, such-and-such a final limit to that lifespan.

Chinese Source

又於此中有二種果及二種因。二種果者：一、自體果，二、受用境界果。二種因者：一、牽引因，二、生起因。……由此二因增上力故，便為三苦之所隨逐，招集一切純大苦蘊。如是名依觀待道理，尋思緣起所有道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, herein there are two kinds of fruit and two kinds of cause. The two fruits: first, the fruit of one's own being; second, the fruit of experienced circumstances. The two causes: first, the impelling cause; second, the generating cause. … Through the empowering force of these two causes, one is pursued by the three kinds of suffering, gathering the entire mass of suffering. This is called investigating the principles of dependent origination by way of the principle of mutual dependence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi separates two levels at work in this passage. At the ultimate level, there is no real "actor" accumulating karma and no real "receiver" experiencing its ripened fruit — searching the five aggregates for such a fixed party turns up nothing, the way searching a body for pleasure "in here" or "out there" finds it nowhere at all. Yet at the conventional level, karma and its fruit remain entirely real and binding: a name, a lineage, a family, a lifespan, and everything else that makes up an ordinary biography are all provisionally, but functionally, real — quoting the *Compendium of Determinations*' verse that once the body dissolves, its "name-body" (mental factors) dissolves with it, yet karma made is still karma received in a future life, since actor and receiver are strictly "neither the same nor different": not the same, because the aggregates that acted and the aggregates that later receive the fruit are genuinely distinct moments; not different, because the later fruit could never have arisen without the earlier act as its cause. He then details the causal mechanics: the "two fruits" are one's own resulting existence (body and mind themselves, the ripened result of past karma) and the pleasant or unpleasant experiences that existence then encounters; the "two causes" are the impelling cause (ignorance and formations, planting the seed for a future rebirth, elaborated across seven links through feeling) and the generating cause (craving, grasping, and becoming, which actually moisten that seed into ripening — Cháng Bǎi tells the vivid folk tale of a man's spirit, distracted by an old lover's beckoning en route to a routine errand in the underworld, tumbling straight through rebirth as a piglet and then a snake before being violently retrieved, as a cautionary illustration of how quickly craving redirects a wandering mind toward its next birth). He closes briefly on the remaining three principles — function (sustained cultivation of this contemplation reliably severs delusion), valid proof (confirmed by the Buddha's own teaching, direct inner experience, and inference alike), and natural law (this causal relationship simply holds, not open to further debate) — closing dependent-origination contemplation, the third of the five purifying-conduct objects, in full.

6. Milestone: Elemental Analysis Opens — the Antidote to Pride

The fourth of the five purifying-conduct objects now opens: analyzing the body into its six constituent elements, the antidote to excessive pride and self-importance.

Chinese Source

云何勤修界差別觀者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依界差別增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正解了一切界義。謂種性義及種子義、因義、性義，是其界義。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates elemental-analysis contemplation investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on the differentiation of elements, one is able to correctly understand the meaning of "element" in every sense. That is, the meaning of lineage, the meaning of seed, the meaning of cause, and the meaning of nature — these are the meanings of "element."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the six elements this contemplation dissects the body into — earth, water, fire, wind, space, and consciousness, the same six the classical texts say a being enters the womb dependent upon — and explains why this particular practice targets pride specifically: breaking the body down into six impersonal, lawfully arising constituents leaves no room for the sense of a single, superior "I" that pride depends on. He glosses "element" itself through four overlapping senses drawn from Buddhist technical vocabulary: lineage (a category or type, as in the ten dharma-realms of hell-being through buddha, each with its own causal lineage), seed (a causal potential capable of producing its own effect), cause, and nature — all four converging on the same point: whatever is called an "element" is defined entirely by its causal role, never by being some fixed, independent thing in itself.

7. The Six Elements' Substance and Their Particular and Universal Characteristics

Chinese Source

又正尋思地等六界內外差別，發起勝解，如是名為尋思界事。又正尋思：地為堅相，乃至風為輕動相，識為了別相，空界為虛空相、遍滿色相、無障礙相。是名尋思諸界自相。又正尋思：此一切界，以要言之，皆是無常乃至無我。是名尋思諸界共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates the inner and outer distinction among earth and the other six elements, giving rise to a vivid understanding — this is called investigating the substance of the elements. One further rightly investigates: earth has the mark of solidity … wind has the mark of light movement, consciousness has the mark of discernment, and the space element has the mark of empty space, pervading form, and being unobstructed. This is called investigating the particular characteristics of the elements. One further rightly investigates: all these elements, in brief, are impermanent, and so on through being without self. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of the elements.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi recommends the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*'s detailed method for actually carrying out this analysis: beginning each morning with the hair about to be shaved, asking directly whether it is "self" — it was absent in the womb, its loss causes no grief, burning or discarding it causes no pain, so it is neither self nor belonging to self — and then applying the identical question, part by part, to teeth (an infant has none and is still a being; their eventual loss still leaves life possible), to limbs (accident or injury can remove them without erasing the person), working systematically through the whole body's solid matter (earth), then its fluids (water — is blood, replaceable by transfusion, "self"?), then its warmth (fire) and its motion (wind), then the space within it (its cavities and passages), and finally consciousness itself, both one's own and — even more obviously ungraspable — another's. Once the elements' particular characteristics have each been directly investigated this way, their shared universal characteristic follows immediately: every one of them, without exception, is impermanent, and therefore suffering, and therefore empty of any real self within it.

8. Milestone: Category, Time, and Principle — Elemental Analysis Closes

The final three components close elemental analysis: category exposes pride as the direct result of mistaking a mere aggregate for a real self; time traces the six elements through conception itself; and principle roots the entire practice in the classical teaching of entry into the womb.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：於一合相界差別性不了知者，由界差別所合成身，發起高慢，便為顛倒，黑品所攝，廣說如前。與上相違，便無顛倒，白品所攝，廣說如前。如是名為尋思界品。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: if one fails to understand the differentiated nature of the elements within what appears as a single unified mass, and, on account of the body formed by this combination of elements, gives rise to arrogance, this is a distortion, belonging to the dark category, as extensively explained before. The reverse of this is free of distortion, belonging to the bright category, as extensively explained before. This is called investigating the category of the elements.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：去來今世六界為緣，得入母胎。如是名為尋思界時。又正尋思：如草木等眾緣和合，圍繞虛空，數名為舍。如是六界為所依故，筋骨血肉眾緣和合，圍繞虛空，假想、等想施設言論，數名為身。復由宿世諸業煩惱及自種子以為因緣。如是名依觀待道理尋思諸界差別道理。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: in past, present, and future lives alike, the six elements serve as conditions for entry into the womb. This is called investigating the time of the elements. One further rightly investigates: just as grass, wood, and other conditions combine to enclose empty space and are provisionally called "a dwelling," so too, depending on these six elements, sinew, bone, blood, and flesh combine to enclose empty space, and through provisional, conventional conception one sets up the expression and provisionally calls it "a body." Further, this depends on karma and affliction accumulated across past lives, together with one's own seeds, as causes and conditions. This is called investigating the principles of the elements by way of the principle of mutual dependence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the "single unified mass" here the same way he earlier treated a car — a whole made only of assembled parts, mistaken for one real thing — and quotes the classical maxim "with no self within, I have no basis for pride; with no person without, no one else can wrong me" as the direct antidote arrogance calls for. On time, he cross-references the "Stage of Mind"'s teaching that conception requires three conditions present together — the mother's fertility, an intermediate-state being's presence, and its arising desire — and that these correspond exactly to the six elements: an unobstructed womb corresponds to the space element; unobstructed reproductive substances correspond to earth, water, fire, and wind; and the ālaya-consciousness's actual union with the fertilized substance corresponds to the consciousness element. A third condition, freedom from karmic obstruction, requires the same beings to actually share karma binding them as parent and child — explaining, he notes, why grievances about one's birth family are ultimately misplaced, since the relationship itself is jointly authored karma, not an accident inflicted by one party on the other. On principle, he extends the classical house-and-space analogy — a "house" is nothing but wood and space provisionally named — directly to the body itself, built of the same six elements enclosing the same kind of empty space, and provisionally called "a body" by the identical convention. He closes briefly through the remaining three principles (sustained practice reliably severs pride; the Buddha's teaching, direct meditative experience, and inference together confirm it; and the causal link between this practice and pride's cessation is simply a fixed law of nature), closing elemental analysis — the fourth of the five purifying-conduct objects — in full.

9. Milestone: Breath-Counting Contemplation Opens — the Fifth and Final Purifying-Conduct Object

The fifth and final object for purifying conduct now opens: mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath, the antidote for a scattered, discursive mind.

Chinese Source

云何勤修阿那波那念者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那？謂依入出息念增上正法聽聞受持增上力故，能正了知於入出息所緣境界，繫心了達，無忘明記，是阿那波那念義。

Ru-Yi Translation

How does one who diligently cultivates mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath investigate insight with objects differentiated by the six things? That is, by the empowering force of having heard and upheld the excellent teaching on mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath, one is able to correctly know the object that is the in-breath and out-breath, binding the mind to it and comprehending it, without forgetting, held clearly in mind. This is the meaning of mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：入息、出息在內可得；繫屬身故，外處攝故，內外差別。如是名為尋思其事。又正尋思：入息有二，出息有二。若風入內，名為入息；若風出外，名為出息。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: the in-breath and out-breath can be found within the body — bound to the body, they belong to the inner sphere; yet they are also included within the outer sphere, hence there is an inner and outer distinction. This is called investigating their substance. One further rightly investigates: there are two kinds of in-breath and two kinds of out-breath. When the air enters within, this is called the in-breath; when the air exits without, this is called the out-breath.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies breath-counting as the classical remedy for a mind too restless and discursive to settle on any of the previous four objects, and notes its wide textual support — the *Great Ānāpāna-smṛti Sūtra*, the *Sūtra on the Stages of Practice*, the *Sūtra on the Samādhi of Sitting Meditation*, and the āgamas all treat it at length. On substance, he explains the breath's dual inner-outer status: as air actually moving within the body it counts as an inner matter tied to the body, yet since wind itself is one of the four elements shared with the entire outer physical world, it belongs to the outer sphere as well — the breath is, in this sense, the one meditation object that most directly bridges inner and outer. On its particular characteristic, he flags that "two kinds" of in-breath and out-breath will be detailed next — the breath proper and a subtler "interval" breath — cross-referencing the fuller treatment already given for breath-counting in fascicle 25's discussion of objects of meditation more generally.

10. Milestone: Breath-Counting's Remaining Components — All Five Purifying-Conduct Objects Now Complete

The remaining four components close breath-counting contemplation, and with it, all five objects for purifying conduct — the entirety of the antidotes for craving, hatred, delusion, pride, and scattered thought.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：入息滅已有出息生，出息滅已有入息生，入出息轉繫屬命根及有識身；此入出息及所依止皆是無常。是名尋思諸息共相。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: when the in-breath ceases, the out-breath arises; when the out-breath ceases, the in-breath arises. The turning of the in-breath and out-breath is bound to the life-faculty and to the body possessing consciousness; this in-breath and out-breath, and what they depend upon, are all impermanent. This is called investigating the universal characteristic of the breaths.

Chinese Source

又正尋思：此中都無持入息者、持出息者，入息、出息繫屬於彼；唯於從因從緣所生諸行，發起假想，施設言論，說有能持入出息者。如是名依觀待道理尋思其理……是名勤修阿那波那念者，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那。

Ru-Yi Translation

One further rightly investigates: herein there is no one who controls the in-breath, no one who controls the out-breath — the in-breath and out-breath are simply bound to that body. It is only with respect to conditioned formations, arisen from causes and conditions, that one gives rise to a provisional conception and sets up an expression, speaking of "one who controls the in-breath and out-breath." This is called investigating the principle of the breaths by way of the principle of mutual dependence … This is called the insight, with objects differentiated by the six things, of one who diligently cultivates mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes the breath offers an unusually direct route to impermanence, since it is ceaselessly changing in real time — never fixed as purely inhaling or purely exhaling, sometimes long, sometimes short, sometimes urgent, sometimes calm — and, being tied directly to the life-faculty and to consciousness's presence in the body, it makes impermanence, and through it suffering and no-self, unusually easy to actually witness rather than merely infer. On category, an untrained mind attending to the breath still gets pulled away by the eight kinds of unwholesome discursive thought (desire, ill will, harm, homeland, relatives, powerful patrons, contempt, and denial of death's nearness) — the dark category — while a settled mind free of these distractions belongs to the bright. On principle, no one actually "controls" the breath in any ultimate sense; it is simply conditioned process, with "controller" only a conventional label laid over it. He closes by naming the great virtue of breath-counting specifically: since calm-abiding and insight can be practiced on the very same object — count the breath to build stillness, then investigate the same breath through the six things to build insight — it uniquely demonstrates how the two are meant to work as a single unified practice, not two separate pursuits. With this, all five objects for purifying conduct — impurity, loving-kindness, dependent origination, elemental analysis, and breath-counting — are complete.

11. How Calm-Abiding and Insight Reinforce Each Other

Chinese Source

如是依止淨行所緣，尋思六事差別觀已，數數於內令心寂靜，數數復於如所尋思，以勝觀行審諦伺察。彼由奢摩他為依止故，令毗鉢舍那速得清淨；復由毗鉢舍那為依止故，令奢摩他增長廣大。

Ru-Yi Translation

Thus, relying on the objects for purifying conduct, having investigated the six-thing differentiated contemplation, one repeatedly settles the mind inwardly into stillness, and then repeatedly, with respect to what has been investigated, carefully and truthfully examines it further through superior insight practice. Relying on calm-abiding, insight swiftly becomes purified; and relying, in turn, on insight, calm-abiding grows and expands.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this brief passage as the payoff of the entire six-things structure just completed across all five objects: calm-abiding and insight are never rivals but mutual causes, alternating in a single sustained practice — stillness clarifies what investigation later examines, and investigation, once complete, feeds back to deepen the very stillness it depended on. He cross-references this same relationship to the four kinds of wisdom-conduct (correct discernment and utmost discernment corresponding to substance and characteristic; category, time, and principle folding into utmost discernment; meaning cutting across both), to the three gates of insight (mere-mark, investigation, and examination — shallow to deep), and to the three kinds of realization (verbal-meaning realization, realization of a thing's full extent, and realization of things as they truly are) — all, he stresses, different lenses on the identical underlying practice, not separate techniques to be learned one after another.

12. Milestone: Skillful-Means and Purifying-Affliction Objects Deferred — the Fourth Category of Meditation Objects Named

Before moving on, the treatise names the larger four-part framework its five purifying-conduct objects belong to, and explicitly defers two of the four categories — skillful-means objects and purifying-affliction objects — to later fascicles.

Chinese Source

若依止善巧所緣及淨惑所緣，尋思六事差別所緣毗鉢舍那，於其自處我後當說。

Ru-Yi Translation

As for investigating insight with objects differentiated by the six things by relying on skillful-means objects or purifying-affliction objects, I shall explain these later, in their own proper place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi maps the complete fourfold framework of meditation objects in Yogācāra practice: pervasive objects (the four found throughout every practice — discerning image, non-discerning image, the full extent of a thing's nature, and the accomplished result — corresponding respectively to insight, calm-abiding, the twin "full extent" and "true nature" characteristics, and the purified object plus purified basis that mark genuine accomplishment); objects for purifying conduct (the five just completed, the Tiantai tradition's five-methods-for-stilling-the-mind); skillful-means objects (mastery of the aggregates, sense-fields, elements, dependent origination, and what is possible or impossible — sometimes expanded to seven by adding the faculties and the four truths, detailed at length in the "Determinations" section, fascicles 53–57); and purifying-affliction objects (the mundane path's comparative six-aspect method and the supramundane path's direct four-truths method, promised for fascicles 33 and 34). Cháng Bǎi notes plainly that skillful-means objects never receive their own dedicated six-things treatment anywhere in the text as it survives, while purifying-affliction objects are taken up later exactly as promised — a reminder that the treatise's own scope, not the translator's choice, sets these boundaries.

13. Nine Kinds of Preparatory Practice, Bright and Dark

Chinese Source

復次，此中有九種白品所攝加行。與此相違，當知即是九種黑品所攝加行。云何名為白品所攝九種加行？一、相應加行，二、串習加行，三、不緩加行，四、無倒加行，五、應時加行，六、解了加行，七、無厭足加行，八、不捨軛加行，九、正加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, herein there are nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the bright category. The reverse of these should be known as the nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the dark category. What are the nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the bright category? First, fitting practice. Second, habituated practice. Third, unhurried practice. Fourth, undistorted practice. Fifth, timely practice. Sixth, understanding practice. Seventh, insatiable practice. Eighth, unabandoning practice. Ninth, correct practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews all nine before their individual detail follows: fitting practice matches the meditation object to the practitioner's actual dominant affliction (contemplating impurity for one dominated by craving, and so on through the other four objects); habituated practice reflects genuine repeated familiarity across the seven kinds of mental engagement, from learning the object's mark through the fruit of effort's culmination; unhurried practice sustains unbroken, earnest effort without slackening; undistorted practice proceeds without error; timely practice knows precisely when to apply stillness, investigation, energizing, or equanimizing; understanding practice grasps the nature, object, cause, and timing of each of those four with enough clarity to enter and exit concentration freely; insatiable practice refuses to settle for whatever level has already been reached, always reaching for what is more excellent still; unabandoning practice never neglects precepts or the other provisions of the path even while absorbed in concentration; and correct practice — the ninth, to be detailed next — brings the whole set to completion. He notes each of the nine has an exact dark-category mirror, named simply by reversal, spelling out nine distinct ways preparatory practice can go wrong just as precisely as it names nine ways it can go right.

14. Milestone: What the Nine Practices Accomplish — Speed to Attainment, Bright and Dark

Before detailing each of the nine bright-category practices individually, the treatise states their overall effect — and its exact mirror image for the nine dark-category failures.

Chinese Source

由此九種白品所攝加行故，能令其心速疾得定，令三摩地轉更昇進。又由此故，於所應往地及隨所應得，速疾能往、能得，無有稽遲。黑品所攝九種加行，不能令心速疾得定，不令三摩地轉更昇進。又由此故，於所應往地及隨所應得，極大稽遲，不能速疾往趣、獲得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through these nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the bright category, the mind swiftly attains concentration, and samādhi continues to advance further still. Further, through these, one swiftly reaches and attains whatever stage and whatever level are to be reached, without delay. The nine kinds of preparatory practice belonging to the dark category do not enable the mind to swiftly attain concentration, nor do they enable samādhi to advance further. Further, through these, one is greatly delayed in reaching whatever stage and whatever level are to be reached, unable to swiftly arrive at or attain them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "attaining concentration" first as reaching near-attainment concentration (the threshold just below the first dhyāna proper), and "advancing further" as passing from there into the first dhyāna's actual root absorption and on through the second, third, and fourth — each stage bringing progressively fuller freedom from the afflictions specific to meditative attainment (subtle craving, wrong view, pride, and doubt). "Whatever stage" covers the entire hierarchy above the first dhyāna, up through the peak of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and ultimately the noble path itself. He frames the whole nine-item list, bright and dark alike, as a diagnostic: a practitioner stuck at the same level for years should look here first, checking one by one whether fitting, habituation, urgency, freedom from error, timing, understanding, insatiability, unbroken discipline, and correct alternation of stillness and insight are each actually present — since any single one done in its dark, reversed form is often enough to explain years of stalled progress.

15. Fitting Practice: Matching the Object to One's Own Temperament

Chinese Source

云何名為相應加行？謂若貪行者，應於不淨安住其心；若瞋行者，應於慈愍安住其心；若癡行者，應於緣起安住其心；若憍慢行者，應於界差別安住其心；若尋思行者，應於阿那波那念安住其心；若等分行者，或薄塵行者，應隨所樂攀緣一境安住其心；勤修加行。如是名為相應加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is fitting practice? That is, one dominated by craving should settle the mind on impurity; one dominated by hatred should settle the mind on loving-kindness; one dominated by delusion should settle the mind on dependent origination; one dominated by pride should settle the mind on elemental analysis; one dominated by discursive thought should settle the mind on mindfulness of the in-breath and out-breath; and one whose afflictions are evenly balanced, or lightly present, should settle the mind on whichever object he prefers, diligently cultivating the practice. This is called fitting practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this first item as simply the practical payoff of everything just covered across the five purifying-conduct objects: choosing the wrong antidote for one's own dominant affliction guarantees poor results no matter how diligently one otherwise practices. He notes practitioners sometimes discover their true fit only after years with the "wrong" object — one long-time breath-counter, for instance, finally settling quickly once switching to elemental analysis — and stresses that self-honesty about one's own actual temperament, not simply whichever practice is most popular or most familiar, is what "fitting" really requires.

16. Habituated Practice: Distinguishing the Seasoned Practitioner from the True Beginner

Chinese Source

云何名為串習加行？謂於奢摩他、毗鉢舍那，已曾數習乃至少分，非於一切皆初修業。所以者何？初修業者，雖於相應所緣境界勤修加行，而有諸蓋數數現行，身心麤重。由是因緣，不能令心速疾得定。如是名為串習加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is habituated practice? That is, one who has already repeatedly practiced calm-abiding and insight, at least to some degree — not one who is, in every respect, a complete beginner. Why is this? Because a complete beginner, even while diligently applying effort to a fitting object, still has the hindrances repeatedly manifesting, and body and mind remain coarse and heavy. For this reason, the mind cannot swiftly attain concentration. This is called habituated practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi pins down exactly where the line falls: a true beginner has, at most, only reached the first of the seven kinds of mental engagement (learning the object's mark), still mixed with ordinary hearing- and reflection-based understanding, while a "habituated" practitioner has already moved through confident understanding at least partway toward the effort's culmination. He notes this isn't a moral failing in the beginner — even choosing a perfectly fitting object, a genuine newcomer will still find the hindrances surfacing again and again, body aching, mind restless, simply because the requisite quantity and quality of practice hasn't yet accumulated, whether in this life or carried over from a previous one. A seasoned practitioner, by contrast, settles almost immediately, hindrance-free, precisely because that accumulated familiarity is already in place.

17. Milestone: Unhurried Practice — Not Delighting in Busywork, Not Delighting in Crowds, Urgent Resolve

The third practice, sustaining effort without slackening, unfolds into three concrete behaviors: finishing necessary duties without lingering in them, keeping social contact brief and purposeful, and refusing to postpone genuine effort to some imagined later life.

Chinese Source

云何名為不緩加行？謂無間方便、殷重方便勤修觀行。若從定出，或為乞食，或為恭敬承事師長，或為看病，或為隨順修和敬業，或為所餘如是等類諸所作事；而心於彼所作事業，不全隨順、不全趣向、不全臨入，唯有速疾令事究竟，還復精勤宴坐寂靜，修諸觀行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is unhurried practice? That is, cultivating meditative practice with uninterrupted, earnest effort. If, having risen from concentration, one must beg for alms, attend respectfully upon a teacher, tend the sick, act in accordance with the six kinds of harmony, or carry out other such necessary tasks — the mind, with respect to these tasks, does not fully yield to them, does not fully turn toward them, does not fully enter into them; one merely brings the task swiftly to completion and then returns once more to diligent, still sitting, cultivating meditative practice.

Chinese Source

若有苾芻、苾芻尼、鄔波索迦、剎帝利、婆羅門等種種異眾共相會遇，雖久雜處，現相語儀，而不相續安立言論，唯樂遠離，勤修觀行。又能如是勇猛精進：謂我於今定當趣證所應證得，不應慢緩。

Ru-Yi Translation

If monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen, warrior-caste persons, brahmins, or other various different assemblies happen to meet, though remaining together for some time, exchanging courteous greetings, one does not continue on with extended conversation, but delights only in seclusion, diligently cultivating meditative practice. Further, one is thereby able to remain courageously diligent in this way: "I must, at this very time, resolutely reach what is to be realized — I should not be slack or delay."

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the necessary-duties passage as mapping directly onto the three kinds of wisdom already established: fully engaging with a task the way one engages with hearing-based study, fully turning toward it the way one turns toward reflection, and fully entering it the way one enters cultivation — none of which the unhurried practitioner allows a mere errand to claim, precisely because his heart remains oriented toward the meditation seat even mid-task. On social contact, he notes the treatise's own model reply is simply the ordinary courtesy of asking after someone's health and well-being, nothing more elaborate — contrasted with the Mahāyāna's broader teaching (citing the *Avataṃsaka Sūtra*'s "Chapter on Pure Conduct," which supplies a specific contemplation for literally every daily encounter, from crossing a bridge to waking up) that every moment, not only seated meditation, can be turned into practice. He treats the concluding resolve — refusing the comfortable thought that boundless future lives leave plenty of time to practice later — as the emotional core of unhurried practice: without a felt sense of urgency, sustained effort simply doesn't happen, whatever techniques one otherwise knows.

18. The Causes of Sudden Death, and Resolving to Make the Most of Whatever Time Remains

Chinese Source

我有多種橫死因緣，所謂身中或風、或熱、或痰發動；或所飲食不正銷化，住在身中成宿食病；或為於外蛇蝎、蚰蜓、百足等類諸惡毒蟲之所蜇螫；或復為人非人等類之所驚恐；因斯夭沒。於如是等諸橫死處恆常思惟，修無常想，住不放逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

I have many possible causes of sudden death: perhaps wind, heat, or phlegm disorders arising within the body; or food improperly digested, remaining in the body and becoming a lodged ailment; or being stung or bitten by outer venomous creatures such as snakes, scorpions, centipedes, or the like; or being terrified by humans or non-humans — and dying prematurely through any of these. Constantly reflecting on such causes of sudden death, one cultivates the perception of impermanence, and abides in non-heedlessness.

Chinese Source

由住如是不放逸故，恆自思惟：我之壽命儻得更經七日、六日、五日……乃至存活經爾所時，於佛聖教精勤作意修習瑜伽，齊爾所時，於佛聖教，我當決定多有所作。如是名為不緩加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Abiding in such non-heedlessness, one constantly reflects: "Should my life extend for seven more days, or six, or five … for however much time I have left to live, I must diligently direct my attention to cultivating yogic practice within the Buddha's noble teaching; for that entire span of time, within the Buddha's noble teaching, I am resolved to accomplish a great deal." This is called unhurried practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this passage as the treatise's own antidote to the "denial of death's nearness" discursive thought already flagged as an obstacle earlier in the fascicle: rather than vaguely acknowledging mortality, the practitioner is instructed to concretely enumerate specific, mundane causes of sudden death — an internal imbalance, food poisoning, a venomous bite, a fright — precisely because the abstract fact of impermanence rarely moves anyone, while a vivid, particular scenario does. The counting-down exercise that follows (seven days, then six, then down to a single in-breath or out-breath) is meant to be actually felt, not merely recited, so that whatever time remains — a lifetime or a single hour — gets treated as valuable and not to be wasted. Cháng Bǎi calls this the emotional engine behind unhurried practice: a genuine sense that this may be the only opportunity, which alone reliably overcomes the comfortable postponement so common among practitioners with an otherwise stable, uneventful life.

19. Undistorted Practice: Learning Correctly, Without Pride or Private Opinion

Chinese Source

云何名為無倒加行？謂如善達修瑜伽行諸瑜伽師之所開悟，即如是學，於法、於義不顛倒取，無有我慢，亦不安住自所見取，無邪僻執，於尊教誨終不輕毀。如是名為無倒加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is undistorted practice? That is, exactly as one is instructed by teachers well versed in yogic practice, one learns accordingly — grasping neither the teaching nor its meaning in a distorted way, without arrogance, not settling into one's own private views, free of deviant clinging, and never disparaging the instruction of one's venerable teachers. This is called undistorted practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats undistorted practice as guarding against two opposite failure modes at once: adding one's own arrogant embellishment to what a teacher has actually taught, and stubbornly clinging to a private interpretation once formed rather than remaining correctable. He recommends sustained, patient, repeated study of the treatise itself as the practical remedy — cross-referencing earlier and later passages against each other until understanding deepens gradually — and notes that impatience with exactly this kind of unglamorous repetition is, in his experience, the single most common reason students never actually correct their own distortions.

20. Milestone: Timely Practice Opens — Stillness, Insight, Energizing, and Equanimizing

The fifth practice, timely practice, requires knowing exactly when each of four distinct techniques is called for — stillness, insight, energizing, and equanimizing — and understanding each one's own nature, object, and proper occasion.

Chinese Source

云何名為應時加行？謂於時時間修習止相，於時時間修習觀相，於時時間修習舉相，於時時間修習捨相。又能如實了知其止、止相、止時；了知其觀、觀相、觀時；了知其舉、舉相、舉時；了知其捨、捨相、捨時。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is timely practice? That is, at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of stillness; at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of insight; at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of energizing; and at the fitting time, one cultivates the mark of equanimizing. Further, one correctly and truly knows stillness, its mark, and its proper time; knows insight, its mark, and its proper time; knows energizing, its mark, and its proper time; and knows equanimizing, its mark, and its proper time.

Chinese Source

云何為止？謂九相心住。能令其心無相、無分別、寂靜、極寂靜、等住寂止、純一無雜，故名為止。所緣相者，謂奢摩他品所知事同分影像，是名所緣相。由此所緣，令心寂靜。因緣相者，謂依奢摩他所熏習心，為令後時奢摩他定皆清淨故，修習瑜伽毗鉢舍那所有加行，是名因緣相。云何止時？謂心掉舉時，或恐掉舉時，是修止時。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is stillness? It is the nine stages of mental abiding, which cause the mind to become free of marks, free of discursive thought, tranquil, thoroughly tranquil, evenly abiding at rest, and singly unified without admixture — hence it is called stillness. Its object-mark is the image of the same type as the object of knowledge belonging to the calm-abiding type; through this object, the mind becomes tranquil. Its causal mark is that, relying on the mind trained by calm-abiding, in order that calm-abiding's concentration may become ever more purified in the future, one cultivates the entire practice of insight — this is its causal mark. What is the proper time for stillness? When the mind is agitated, or on the verge of becoming agitated, this is the time to cultivate stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "stillness" here as nothing other than the nine stages of mental abiding already detailed in fascicle 30, now revisited from the angle of when to actually apply them: the moment agitation surfaces, or is even sensed approaching, is precisely the signal to return to the object and let it settle. He treats the object-mark and causal-mark distinction as capturing two different roles insight can play relative to stillness: as an object in its own right (the mental image matching what the senses first perceived) and, more subtly, as a supporting cause — sustained investigation into an affliction's danger, done in advance, makes the mind far less likely to be disturbed by that same affliction once returning to still sitting. He gives the classical example: a practitioner prone to lust who first spends time deliberately reflecting on the body's impurity (an act of insight) will then find the mind considerably steadier when later returning to still concentration, precisely because the groundwork of investigation was already done — insight and stillness reinforcing each other exactly as promised earlier in the fascicle.
