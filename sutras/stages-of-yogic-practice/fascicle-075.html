Fascicle 75 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十五 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Three Clusters of Discipline, Six States of Mind

This is the fourth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. The Determination proceeds chapter by chapter through the topics of the Bodhisattva Ground itself; the eighth of these chapters, on giving, closed at the end of the previous fascicle, and this one takes up the ninth, the Chapter on Moral Discipline. It opens with the three clusters that make up a bodhisattva's moral discipline — the cluster of restraint, the cluster that gathers wholesome practice, and the cluster that brings benefit to beings — and then turns to six states of mind a bodhisattva must watch for while training in the cluster that gathers wholesome practice, since a single careless state of mind can turn an act of practice into a breach.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 75 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Three Clusters of a Bodhisattva's Discipline, and Six States of Mind to Watch in the Cluster That Gathers Wholesome Practice

This is the fourth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground has three parts: first, concluding what came before and introducing what follows; second, the determination proceeding chapter by chapter, in fifteen sections; the first eight of these have already been given, and the ninth, the Chapter on Giving and the Rest, determines the six perfections — of which giving was already given in the previous fascicle, and moral discipline is determined from here. Further, it should be known that a bodhisattva's discipline is, in brief, three clusters. The first, the cluster of the discipline of restraint, is such as the Blessed One briefly set out as the character of restraint for the beings he trained who followed the śrāvaka path — this should be known as exactly this cluster. What is the cluster of the discipline that gathers wholesome practice? When bodhisattvas diligently cultivate the discipline that gathers wholesome practice, they should, in brief, carefully observe six states of mind. What are the six? Contemptuous disregard; a mind joined with laziness; a mind under some hidden cover; a mind wearied by exertion; a mind that follows illness; and a mind that follows an obstruction. What is contemptuous disregard? If, with regard to wholesome things, a bodhisattva has a light and careless mind, a mind without any firm conviction, and a mind that looks down on and slights them, this is called contemptuous disregard.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the treatise passes so quickly over the first of the three clusters, the discipline of restraint: it is simply the śrāvaka's own code of individual liberation, the precepts already fully set out for the seven assemblies of monks, nuns, novices, and lay disciples, and a bodhisattva takes up exactly that code without needing it restated. What is new, and what the treatise dwells on instead, is the second cluster, the discipline that gathers wholesome practice — everything a bodhisattva actively cultivates rather than merely refrains from — where the precept is kept or broken by the state of mind behind an action as much as by the action itself. He notes that this is why bodhisattva discipline is reckoned stricter than the śrāvaka's own: a stirring of contempt or laziness toward practicing what is good can itself constitute a breach, well before any outward deed is done.
