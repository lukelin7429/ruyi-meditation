Fascicle 75 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十五 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Three Clusters of Discipline, Six States of Mind

This is the fourth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. The Determination proceeds chapter by chapter through the topics of the Bodhisattva Ground itself; the eighth of these chapters, on giving, closed at the end of the previous fascicle, and this one takes up the ninth, the Chapter on Moral Discipline. It opens with the three clusters that make up a bodhisattva's moral discipline — the cluster of restraint, the cluster that gathers wholesome practice, and the cluster that brings benefit to beings — and then turns to six states of mind a bodhisattva must watch for while training in the cluster that gathers wholesome practice, since a single careless state of mind can turn an act of practice into a breach.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 75 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Three Clusters of a Bodhisattva's Discipline, and Six States of Mind to Watch in the Cluster That Gathers Wholesome Practice

This is the fourth part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground has three parts: first, concluding what came before and introducing what follows; second, the determination proceeding chapter by chapter, in fifteen sections; the first eight of these have already been given, and the ninth, the Chapter on Giving and the Rest, determines the six perfections — of which giving was already given in the previous fascicle, and moral discipline is determined from here. Further, it should be known that a bodhisattva's discipline is, in brief, three clusters. The first, the cluster of the discipline of restraint, is such as the Blessed One briefly set out as the character of restraint for the beings he trained who followed the śrāvaka path — this should be known as exactly this cluster. What is the cluster of the discipline that gathers wholesome practice? When bodhisattvas diligently cultivate the discipline that gathers wholesome practice, they should, in brief, carefully observe six states of mind. What are the six? Contemptuous disregard; a mind joined with laziness; a mind under some hidden cover; a mind wearied by exertion; a mind that follows illness; and a mind that follows an obstruction. What is contemptuous disregard? If, with regard to wholesome things, a bodhisattva has a light and careless mind, a mind without any firm conviction, and a mind that looks down on and slights them, this is called contemptuous disregard.

Chinese Source

攝決擇分中菩薩地之四。復次，當知菩薩毗奈耶略有三聚。初律儀戒毗奈耶聚，如薄伽梵為諸聲聞所化有情略說毗奈耶相，當知即此毗奈耶聚。云何攝善法戒毗奈耶聚？謂諸菩薩，於攝善法戒勤修習時，略於六心應善觀察。何等為六？一、輕懱心，二、懈怠俱行心，三、有覆蔽心，四、勤勞倦心，五、病隨行心，六、障隨行心。若諸菩薩，於善法中所有輕心、無勝解心及陵懱心，名輕懱心。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground, part four. Further, it should be known that a bodhisattva's discipline is, in brief, three clusters. The first, the cluster of the discipline of restraint, is such as the Blessed One briefly set out as the character of restraint for the beings he trained who followed the śrāvaka path — this should be known as exactly this cluster. What is the cluster of the discipline that gathers wholesome practice? When bodhisattvas diligently cultivate this discipline, they should, in brief, carefully observe six states of mind. What are the six? Contemptuous disregard; a mind joined with laziness; a mind under some hidden cover; a mind wearied by exertion; a mind that follows illness; and a mind that follows an obstruction. What is contemptuous disregard? If, with regard to wholesome things, a bodhisattva has a light and careless mind, a mind without any firm conviction, and a mind that looks down on and slights them, this is called contemptuous disregard.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the treatise passes so quickly over the first of the three clusters, the discipline of restraint: it is simply the śrāvaka's own code of individual liberation, the precepts already fully set out for the seven assemblies of monks, nuns, novices, and lay disciples, and a bodhisattva takes up exactly that code without needing it restated. What is new, and what the treatise dwells on instead, is the second cluster, the discipline that gathers wholesome practice — everything a bodhisattva actively cultivates rather than merely refrains from — where the precept is kept or broken by the state of mind behind an action as much as by the action itself. He notes that this is why bodhisattva discipline is reckoned stricter than the śrāvaka's own: a stirring of contempt or laziness toward practicing what is good can itself constitute a breach, well before any outward deed is done.

2. Section: A Mind Joined with Laziness, a Mind Under Cover, and a Mind Wearied by Its Own Exertion

What is a mind joined with laziness? If a mind is entangled by idleness, by intoxicated pride, or by heedlessness, this is called a mind joined with laziness. What is a mind under some hidden cover? If any one of the hindrances, such as craving, has become active, or if a mind is entangled by the root afflictions or by the secondary afflictions, this is called a mind under cover. And what is a mind wearied by exertion? If, while abiding in bold and enhanced diligence, the body grows weary and the mind grows tired, so that this weariness obscures the mind, this is called a mind wearied by exertion.

Chinese Source

若有懶惰、憍醉、放逸所纏繞心，名懈怠俱行心。若貪欲等隨有一蓋，或諸煩惱及隨煩惱所纏繞心，名有覆蔽心。若住勇猛增上精進，身疲、心倦映蔽其心，名勤勞倦心。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a mind joined with laziness? If a mind is entangled by idleness, by intoxicated pride, or by heedlessness, this is called a mind joined with laziness. What is a mind under some hidden cover? If any one of the hindrances, such as craving, has become active, or if a mind is entangled by the root afflictions or the secondary afflictions, this is called a mind under cover. And what is a mind wearied by exertion? If, while abiding in bold and enhanced diligence, the body grows weary and the mind grows tired, so that this weariness obscures the mind, this is called a mind wearied by exertion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi separates the three strands inside a mind joined with laziness: idleness that keeps postponing today's practice to tomorrow, or gives up on oneself as incapable of ever succeeding; intoxicated pride that grows complacent over some advantage — health, youth, lineage, beauty, wealth, or learning; and sheer heedlessness that lets defiled habits run unchecked. A mind under cover, he notes, is the classic five hindrances or any of the root and secondary afflictions taking hold so completely that shame, awareness of fault, and the wish to get free of it all disappear together. And on a mind wearied by exertion he tells the story of the monk Śroṇa Koṭikarṇa, who drove himself so hard in solitary practice that he nearly gave up entirely, until the Buddha compared the mind to a lute string — too taut or too slack, neither produces music, and only the string tuned to neither extreme sounds true — after which Śroṇa found the middle way of effort and went on to realize arhatship. Laziness is too little exertion and this weariness is too much; neither is genuine diligence.

3. Section: A Mind Following Illness, and a Mind Following an Obstruction

What is a mind that follows illness? If various illnesses harm and trouble the mind so that it has no strength and cannot bear to practice, this is called a mind that follows illness. And what is a mind that follows an obstruction? If an obstruction such as delight in idle talk keeps pursuing the mind, this is called a mind that follows an obstruction.

Chinese Source

若有諸病損惱其心，無有力能，不堪修行，名病隨行心。若有喜樂談論等障隨逐其心，名障隨行心。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a mind that follows illness? If various illnesses harm and trouble the mind so that it has no strength and cannot bear to practice, this is called a mind that follows illness. And what is a mind that follows an obstruction? If an obstruction such as delight in idle talk keeps pursuing the mind, this is called a mind that follows an obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on a meditation master's classical account of the illnesses that can afflict a practitioner in sitting — imbalance among the body's internal currents, bad posture or a lapse of attention that lets sickness in, a mind that goes hunting for supernormal knowledge and thereby invites a spirit's interference, a mind fixed on gain and offerings that invites a similar deception, ignorance of proper breathing, the ripening of past unwholesome karma, thinking pressed too hard until the mind exhausts itself, disturbance by outside noise met with irritation rather than equanimity, and an imbalance between calming and insight themselves — and notes that the point of the list is not diagnosis for its own sake but the reminder that a body and mind without capacity cannot sustain wholesome practice, whatever the cause. On "obstruction" he specifies, from an earlier fascicle's own list, the eight obstructions to seclusion — being burdened with heavy affairs, attachment to one's undertakings, attachment to talk, attachment to sleep, attachment to noisy company, frequent gatherings, attachment to mixing socially, and attachment to idle discourse — plus the inner hindrance of scarce resources and outward hindrance of bad company or an unsuitable place, all of which keep the mind from the seclusion practice requires.

4. Section: Watching for the Six States of Mind, and Which Ones Carry Fault

Amid these six states of mind, a bodhisattva should rightly observe: among these six states of mind, is any one of them presently active in me, or is none of them? With regard to the first three states of mind, a bodhisattva should never let them arise at all; and if they have already arisen, he should not tolerate them. If he tolerates them and does not cast them off, he is at fault throughout, in every instance. When a mind wearied by exertion is present, and because of it one sets aside the means of taking up what is wholesome — if this is only to give the body and mind brief rest from their weariness and fatigue, meaning to continue cultivating the wholesome extensively afterward, it should be known that there is no fault in this.

Chinese Source

菩薩於此六種心中，應正觀察：我於如是六種心中，為有隨一現前行耶？為無有耶？於前三心，菩薩一向不應生起；設已生起，不應忍受。若有忍受而不棄捨，遍於一切皆名有罪。勤勞倦心現在前時，由此心故，捨善方便。若為暫息身心疲惱，當於善法多修習者，當知無罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Amid these six states of mind, a bodhisattva should rightly observe: among these six, is any one of them presently active in me, or is none of them? With regard to the first three, a bodhisattva should never let them arise at all; and if they have already arisen, he should not tolerate them. If he tolerates them and does not cast them off, he is at fault throughout, in every instance. When a mind wearied by exertion is present, and because of it one sets aside the means of taking up what is wholesome — if this is only to give the body and mind brief rest, meaning to continue cultivating the wholesome extensively afterward, it should be known that there is no fault in this.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi ties this verdict to a later fascicle's own account of why faults are committed at all — through ignorance, through heedlessness, through the afflictions running strong, or through contempt — and its ruling that faults from ignorance or heedlessness are undefiled, of a lesser grade, while faults from the afflictions running strong or from contempt are defiled. On that reading, the mind under cover answers to affliction running strong and contemptuous disregard answers to contempt itself, which is why both, along with the laziness lying between them, are never to be tolerated even briefly; a mind wearied by exertion is treated differently because it is not itself an affliction but an honest exhaustion, and stepping back from practice for a short rest with every intention of resuming is a sensible response to a real limit, not a lapse of discipline. He offers the retreat schedule's customary day of rest after an intensive session as the ordinary example: the body and mind are genuinely spent, and a brief pause that restores the capacity to keep practicing carries no fault at all.

5. Section: When Weariness and Distraction Carry Fault, and a Summary of the Six States of Mind

If, because of a mind wearied by exertion, one abandons wholesome practice entirely, thinking: what use is there in my diligently cultivating these wholesome things, when it only leaves me abiding now in this suffering — if one thinks in this way, it should be known that there is fault. When a mind that follows illness is present, a bodhisattva has no mastery over it, and cannot cultivate the effort toward wholesome practice as he wishes; though he bears with this, there is no fault. When a mind that follows an obstruction is present, if one does not fall into it as one might wish, or if one observes that great benefit lies within it, then though one bears with it, there is no fault. But if one enters into it exactly as one wishes, or observes that no benefit lies within it, or only a little, and bears with it regardless, it should be known that there is fault. So then: of these six states of mind, if the first three have already arisen and are tolerated, this is fault throughout, without exception. A mind that follows illness, even when tolerated, is entirely without fault. And for the remaining two, a mind wearied by exertion and a mind that follows an obstruction, when they have arisen and are tolerated, there may be fault, or there may be none.

Chinese Source

若於一切畢竟捨離，謂我何用精勤修習如是善法，令我現在安住此苦。若如是者，當知有罪。病隨行心現在前時，菩薩於此無有自在，不隨所欲修善加行，雖復忍受而無有罪。障隨行心現在前時，若不隨欲墮在其中，或觀此中有大義利，雖復忍受而無有罪。若隨所欲故入其中，或觀是中無有義利，或少義利而故忍受，當知有罪。如是六心，前三生已而忍受者，一向有罪；病隨行心雖復忍受，一向無罪；餘之二心，若生起已而忍受者，或是有罪、或是無罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, because of a mind wearied by exertion, one abandons wholesome practice entirely, thinking there is no use in this diligence that only leaves one suffering — if one thinks this way, there is fault. When a mind that follows illness is present, one has no mastery over it and cannot cultivate wholesome effort as wished; though one bears with it, there is no fault. When a mind that follows an obstruction is present, if one does not fall into it as one might wish, or observes great benefit within it, then though one bears with it, there is no fault; but if one enters it exactly as wished, or observes little or no benefit and bears with it regardless, there is fault. So: of these six states of mind, if the first three have arisen and are tolerated, this is fault throughout; a mind that follows illness, even tolerated, is entirely without fault; and for the remaining two, there may be fault, or there may be none.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi gives the example the treatise itself keeps in mind for a blameless obstruction: a bodhisattva who joins in idle songs or worldly talk with an old person, not out of self-indulgence but because it draws that person toward the Dharma, is using pleasure as a hook to lead someone in — the classical pattern of first drawing beings in through what delights them, then guiding them onward to the path. What tips such an engagement into fault is drifting further into it than the purpose requires, or engaging where there was never any real benefit to begin with. He also cites the treatise's own parallel case, the rule on declining an invitation: refusing out of arrogance, resentment, or irritation is a defiling violation, refusing out of laziness or forgetfulness is a non-defiling one, while refusing because of illness, exhaustion, a deranged mind, distance, danger on the road, or in order to guide the host away from something unwholesome carries no violation at all — the same logic of intention and circumstance that governs all six states of mind here.

6. Section: Six Places to Watch in the Discipline That Benefits Beings — Wealth Waning and Wealth Flourishing

If bodhisattvas, while diligently cultivating the discipline that acts for the benefit of beings, should rightly observe six places that this practice gathers up. These are: self, other, wealth waning, wealth flourishing, the teaching waning, and the teaching flourishing — these are called the six places. What is meant by wealth waning? It means that food, clothing, and the rest, not yet obtained, remain unobtained, or, once obtained, are broken off and lost. And the contrary of this should be known as wealth flourishing.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於作有情利益戒中勤修習時，當正觀察六處攝行。所謂自、他、財衰、財盛、法衰、法盛，是名六處。言財衰者，謂衣食等未得不得，得已斷壞。與此相違，當知財盛。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas, while diligently cultivating the discipline that acts for the benefit of beings, should rightly observe six places that this practice gathers up: self, other, wealth waning, wealth flourishing, the teaching waning, and the teaching flourishing — these are called the six places. What is wealth waning? Food, clothing, and the rest, not yet obtained, remain unobtained, or, once obtained, are broken off and lost. And the contrary of this should be known as wealth flourishing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames these six places as the working checklist behind a question every practitioner eventually faces: how to balance cultivating merit against cultivating wisdom, and one's own good against another's. Self and other ask whose situation is actually affected by an act meant to help; wealth waning and flourishing ask whether that act leaves either party's material welfare worse or better off; and the teaching waning and flourishing, taken up next, ask the same question of each party's progress in discipline, concentration, and wisdom. He notes that the whole point of naming both waning and flourishing side by side, for wealth and for the teaching alike, is that a bodhisattva must weigh both outcomes together rather than assuming that any act done in the name of benefiting beings is automatically beneficial.

7. Section: What the Teaching Waning Means — Hearing Blocked, Reflection Blocked, and Practice Lost

What is meant by the teaching waning? It means going beyond what one should be learning, so that the subtle and wondrous verses of the Tathāgata's own teaching, belonging to the ultimate and never heard before, cannot be heard at all; and just as what was not heard before cannot be heard, so what was not reflected upon before cannot be reflected upon either — there being an obstruction to hearing and an obstruction to reflection; and even where hearing and reflection are managed, they are shortly afterward forgotten. And with regard to the wholesome states accomplished through cultivation not yet realized, one fails to realize them; or, having realized them, one falls back from them again.

Chinese Source

言法衰者，謂越所學，於先未聞勝義所攝如來所說微妙法句不得聽聞。如不聽聞先所未聞，如是於先所未思惟不得思惟，有聽聞障、有思惟障，設得聞思，尋復忘失。於所未證修所成善而未能證，設證還退。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is the teaching waning? Going beyond what one should be learning, so that the subtle and wondrous verses of the Tathāgata's teaching, belonging to the ultimate and never heard before, cannot be heard; and just as what was not heard before cannot be heard, so what was not reflected upon before cannot be reflected upon — an obstruction to hearing and an obstruction to reflection; and even where hearing and reflection are managed, they are shortly forgotten. And with regard to the wholesome states accomplished through cultivation not yet realized, one fails to realize them; or, having realized them, one falls back from them again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi sorts this into four distinct losses that can follow from over-committing to benefiting others at the expense of one's own training: the obstruction to hearing, which shuts a bodhisattva off from the profound teaching he has not yet encountered; the obstruction to reflection, which prevents him from working through what he has heard toward genuine conviction; forgetting, where hearing and reflection happen but do not stick; and falling back, where a cultivated wholesome state, even one already realized, slips away again for lack of continued practice. He notes the ordering is deliberate — hearing before reflection, reflection before cultivation — since each stage depends on the one before it, and a bodhisattva who neglects his own learning in the name of serving others risks losing ground at every one of these four points at once.

8. Section: The Teaching Flourishing, and Two Things a Bodhisattva Should Never Do

The contrary of this should be known as the teaching flourishing. Among these, if a bodhisattva's action brings about his own teaching waning while another's wealth flourishes, this should not be done. And as with bringing about another's wealth flourishing, so it is with bringing about another's teaching flourishing — this too should not be done, if it comes at the cost of one's own teaching waning. The sense of this is: going beyond what is included in the training, or acting in a way that accords with going beyond it, or falling back from what one has already realized in the teaching — all this should be known as the teaching waning.

Chinese Source

與此相違，當知法盛。此中菩薩作自法衰，令他財盛，此不應為。如令財盛，法盛亦爾。此中義者，越學所攝及能隨順越學所攝，或於證法退失所攝，當知法衰。

Ru-Yi Translation

The contrary of this should be known as the teaching flourishing. Among these, if a bodhisattva's action brings about his own teaching waning while another's wealth flourishes, this should not be done. And as with bringing about another's wealth flourishing, so with another's teaching flourishing — this too should not be done. The sense of this is: going beyond what is included in the training, or acting in a way that accords with going beyond it, or falling back from what one has already realized — all this should be known as the teaching waning.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out why letting one's own teaching wane is never justified, whatever it buys for someone else: a bodhisattva without discipline, concentration, and wisdom has no genuine eye for the teaching at all, and cannot actually tell true practice from false — so an attempt to bring another to flourish in the teaching while one's own training collapses risks nothing less than the blind leading the blind. He recalls the classical image behind this: the first five perfections are like a person walking blind, and wisdom is the eye that must lead them, so that helping others in the teaching without first keeping one's own eye open is not genuine benefit to either side. He also glosses "going beyond the training" from an earlier fascicle's own account of purely self-benefiting and purely other-benefiting conduct: clinging to wealth or teaching out of stinginess, craving a tainted existence, gain, others' service, or the pleasure of concentration on the one side; teaching or practicing giving and discipline under wrong views, denying cause and effect, or breaking the precepts to teach on the other — and notes that heedlessness which merely lets such lapses slide, without directly committing them, counts as acting in accordance with going beyond the training even when it is not the transgression itself.

9. Section: Eight Combinations of Self and Other, and Closing the Three Clusters of Discipline

Further, if bodhisattvas bring about their own wealth waning while another's wealth flourishes: if this flourishing does not lead to the teaching waning, this should be done; if it leads to the teaching waning, this should not be done. And as with bringing about another's wealth flourishing, so with another's teaching flourishing. Further, if bodhisattvas bring about both their own wealth flourishing and another's wealth flourishing, this should be done. And as with bringing about wealth flourishing, so with the teaching flourishing. Further, if bodhisattvas bring about their own teaching flourishing and another's wealth flourishing, this should be done. And as with bringing about wealth flourishing, so with the teaching flourishing. With regard to matters of this kind: if one does not practice them, this is fault; if one rightly practices them, this is without fault. So far this has set out, in brief, the three clusters that comprise a bodhisattva's discipline; a bodhisattva should constantly direct his attention to reflecting on them and training in them.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩作自財衰，令他財盛。若此財盛不引法衰，此則應為；若引法衰，此不應為。如令財盛，法盛亦爾。又諸菩薩作自財盛，令他財盛，此則應為。如令財盛，法盛亦爾。又諸菩薩作自法盛，令他財盛，此則應為。如令財盛，法盛亦爾。於如是事，若不修行，名為有罪；若正修行，是名無罪。如是且說菩薩所受三種律儀略毗奈耶，菩薩於中常應作意思惟修學。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, if bodhisattvas bring about their own wealth waning while another's wealth flourishes: if this flourishing does not lead to the teaching waning, this should be done; if it does, this should not be done. And as with wealth flourishing, so with the teaching flourishing. Further, if bodhisattvas bring about both their own wealth flourishing and another's wealth flourishing, this should be done — and as with wealth flourishing, so with the teaching flourishing. Further, if bodhisattvas bring about their own teaching flourishing and another's wealth flourishing, this should be done — and as with wealth flourishing, so with the teaching flourishing. With regard to matters of this kind: if one does not practice them, this is fault; if one rightly practices them, this is without fault. So far this has set out, in brief, the three clusters that comprise a bodhisattva's discipline; a bodhisattva should constantly direct his attention to reflecting on them and training in them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays the whole matrix out in one table so the pattern is visible at a glance: never act if it brings one's own teaching to wane, whether the other party's wealth or teaching flourishes as a result; act conditionally if it only costs one's own wealth, checking whether the other's gain also drags one's own teaching down; and always act when one's own wealth or teaching flourishes together with the other's, in any combination. Eight cases in all — two forbidden outright, two conditional, four always permitted — and the rule underneath every one of them is the same: a bodhisattva's own teaching, his discipline, concentration, and wisdom, is the one thing never to be traded away, since without it he has nothing genuine left to offer anyone else. With this the treatise closes its separate account of the three clusters — restraint, gathering wholesome practice, and benefiting beings — and turns next to summing them up as a whole.

10. Section: Why the Discipline of Restraint Is Foundational, and Why Motivation Decides Whether Discipline Is Truly Kept

If, among these three received clusters of a bodhisattva's discipline, any one is missing, it should be known that this is not to guard the discipline at all; one should say that the bodhisattva's discipline is unguarded, not that it is guarded. These three kinds of discipline are held together and made to cohere by the discipline of restraint. If one diligently guards this one, one is thereby able to diligently guard the other two as well; and if one is unable to guard this one, one is unable to guard the other two either. Therefore, whoever breaks the discipline of restraint is said to have broken the whole of a bodhisattva's discipline. If someone takes up the bodhisattva precepts only so that others will know he has done so, or only in conformity with others, or only because another persuaded him to — and not because an enhancing intention arose of itself, wishing to bring benefit and ease to beings, not because reflection and examination produced a pure confidence of his own, not because he dwelt in compassion toward beings and delighted in the wholesome — then it should be said that this is not a genuine guarding at all, nor a complete cultivation of the wholesome, and he will not attain the excellent fruit that discipline brings. But the contrary of this should be known as a genuine guarding, one that does attain that excellent fruit.

Chinese Source

若有於此三種所受菩薩戒中，隨有所闕，當知非護，當言不護菩薩律儀，不當言護。此三種戒，由律儀戒之所攝持，令其和合。若能於此精勤守護，亦能精勤守護餘二。若有於此不能守護，亦於餘二不能守護。是故若有毀律儀戒，名毀一切菩薩律儀。若有為令他了知故，隨順他故，由他勸導受菩薩戒，非自所起增上意樂、隨觀隨察自生淨信、於諸有情住憐愍心、愛樂善法受菩薩戒，當言此非真實防護，亦非圓滿修習善法，亦不能得彼果勝利。與此相違，當知乃名真實防護，亦能獲得彼果勝利。

Ru-Yi Translation

If, among these three received clusters, any one is missing, this is not to guard the discipline at all. These three are held together by the discipline of restraint: guard this one diligently and one is able to guard the other two; fail to guard this one and one is unable to guard the other two either. Therefore, whoever breaks the discipline of restraint is said to have broken the whole of a bodhisattva's discipline. If someone takes up the bodhisattva precepts only so others will know it, only in conformity with others, or only because persuaded by another — and not from an enhancing intention arisen of itself, a pure confidence of his own reflection, dwelling in compassion toward beings, and delight in the wholesome — this is not a genuine guarding, nor a complete cultivation of the wholesome, and he will not attain its excellent fruit. The contrary of this should be known as a genuine guarding that does attain that fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the claim that breaking restraint breaks all three clusters as the load-bearing point of this whole passage: restraint is not one discipline among three equals but the foundation the other two rest on, so a bodhisattva who cannot keep the precepts of restraint has, in effect, already lost his hold on gathering wholesome practice and benefiting beings as well, whatever he still appears to be doing. He underlines that the treatise then goes further still — even someone outwardly keeping all three perfectly may not be truly guarding the discipline at all, if the motive behind taking it up was only to be seen, to conform, or because someone else pushed for it. What actually constitutes guarding the precepts, on this reading, was never simply the outward act of receiving or observing them, but the intention behind it — genuine compassion and a self-generated confidence, without which the ceremony changes nothing real.

11. Section: What Carries the Precepts Into Future Lives, Four Ways They Are Given Up, and How They Are Taken Up Again

Further, if one does not give up this discipline, it should be known that it continues to follow one into other lives as well, not belonging to one who has given it up. Further, the causes for giving it up are, in brief, four. First, resolutely giving rise to a mind entirely unlike the mind with which one received the precepts. Second, deliberately giving voice, before some person of sound and discerning mind, to words renouncing the precepts. Third, breaking, wholly or in part, any of the four defeats. And fourth, breaking, wholly or in part, through a state of mind overtaken by the strongest grade of affliction, any of the four practices that accord with those four defeats. By these causes, it should be known that the bodhisattva precepts have been given up. And if one afterward regains a pure mind for receiving them, one should take them up again.

Chinese Source

復次，若有不捨如是律儀，當知餘生亦得隨轉，非彼捨者。又捨因緣略有四種。一者、決定發起受心不同分心；二者、若於有所識別大丈夫前，故意發起棄捨語言；三者、總別毀犯四種他所勝法；四者、若以增上品纏，總別毀犯隨順四種他所勝法。由此因緣，當知棄捨菩薩律儀。若有還得清淨受心，復應還受。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, if one does not give up this discipline, it continues to follow one into other lives as well, not belonging to one who has given it up. Further, the causes for giving it up are, in brief, four. First, resolutely giving rise to a mind entirely unlike the mind with which one received the precepts. Second, deliberately giving voice, before some person of sound and discerning mind, to words renouncing the precepts. Third, breaking, wholly or in part, any of the four defeats. And fourth, breaking, wholly or in part, through the strongest grade of affliction, any of the four practices that accord with those four defeats. By these causes, the bodhisattva precepts have been given up. And if one afterward regains a pure mind for receiving them, one should take them up again.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes these four causes by how deep the falling-away goes: the first, silently reversing the very conviction with which the precepts were taken — concluding that buddhahood is unreachable and beings too hard to save — gives them up the instant that thought settles, with no word needing to be spoken; the second is a lesser retreat, spoken aloud before a witness; and the third and fourth concern the four defeats themselves, which the Yogācāra bodhisattva precepts frame not as the śrāvaka's killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and lying, but as praising oneself while disparaging others, withholding wealth or teaching out of stinginess, meeting an apology with unrelenting anger, and slandering the true teaching — the first three broken by the act alone, the last four only when driven by the affliction's strongest grade, done repeatedly and without any shame. He notes the closing line's importance: unlike some codes that treat a broken vow as permanently unrecoverable, here even one who has given up the precepts may sincerely regain a pure mind and take them up again.

12. Section: Fault and No Fault in Giving Away Written Pages of the Teaching

If one withholds giving out of regard for wholesome practice, and not because stinginess obstructs one, there is likewise no fault in this. If there are palm-leaf pages already inscribed with the true teaching, and a being of infant-like understanding comes asking for them, then giving them to him should be known to be a fault; and urging another to give them is likewise a fault — unless one thinks: I now give this to him wishing to test whether this person can bear to receive and uphold the profound teaching, whether he can believe and understand it; giving with this thought is without fault. If one gives pages inscribed with a semblance of the true teaching, or with a rival doctrine — or pages not yet inscribed but intended for such a purpose — into the hands of a being who already believes and understands such things, or urges another to give them, this should be known to be a fault. A bodhisattva should only urge such a being to give up the rival treatise in his hands, or have him copy out the sacred teaching of the buddhas instead; or, if the bodhisattva himself wishes only to know that doctrine to be without solid substance, he may write it out for that purpose, but should not expound it to others.

Chinese Source

若顧善品，非慳貪障而不施者，亦無有罪。諸有葉紙已書正法，有嬰兒慧眾生來乞，若施與之，當知有罪；若勸他施，亦名有罪。除作是心：我今惠彼，欲試其人於甚深法堪受持不？能信解不？如是無罪。若以葉紙書似正法及外道論，或先已書，授彼信解眾生手中，或勸他與，當知有罪。菩薩唯應勸彼棄捨手中異論，或令書寫諸佛聖教，或自欲知彼不堅實，不應開示。

Ru-Yi Translation

If one withholds giving out of regard for wholesome practice, and not from stinginess, there is likewise no fault. If pages already inscribed with the true teaching are asked for by a being of infant-like understanding, giving them is a fault, and urging another to give them is likewise a fault — unless one gives wishing to test whether this person can bear to receive and believe the profound teaching, in which case there is no fault. If one gives pages inscribed with a semblance of the true teaching or a rival doctrine into the hands of one who already believes such things, or urges another to give them, this is a fault. A bodhisattva should only urge such a being to give up the rival treatise, or have him copy the sacred teaching instead; or, if the bodhisattva himself wishes only to know that doctrine to be without solid substance, he may write it for that purpose, but should not expound it to others.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that "infant-like understanding" describes not literal infancy but a being as unable to discern true teaching from false as an infant is unable to speak — such a person given scripture is as likely to disrespect it, leaving it in an unclean place, as to benefit from it, which is why the gift itself becomes a fault unless there is a real prospect of drawing the recipient toward faith. On rival treatises he notes the treatise permits a bodhisattva to study outside teachings, even copy them out, provided the purpose is either to help someone let go of them or to confirm for oneself, with a mind already secure in the Dharma, that they lack solid ground — but never to go on and teach them to others as if they were worth spreading, since what a bodhisattva owes the world is the genuine teaching and nothing else.

13. Section: Blank Pages Not Yet Inscribed — Asking the Purpose, and Reading the Intent

Or there may be palm-leaf pages not yet inscribed, and someone comes asking for them. At that time a bodhisattva should ask him: what do you intend to use this thing for? If he answers that he wishes to resell it to obtain food, then, if these pages were meant for inscribing the true teaching, one should not give them; if one has other wealth, one should give him its equivalent value instead, and if one has no such equivalent, one may withhold both the pages and the wealth, and there is still no fault. But if he answers that he seeks this thing in order to inscribe the true teaching, one should give him the pages, and further tell him: use them as you see fit. If he wishes to copy out some inferior text, withholding them is without fault — and as with an inferior text, so with a middling one. But if he wishes to copy out the most excellent scriptures, and one withholds them, this should be known to be a fault.

Chinese Source

或有葉紙猶未書寫，有來求乞，爾時菩薩應問彼言：汝今何用如是物為？彼若答言：我欲轉賣以充食用。若此葉紙為書正法，則不應與。有財物者，應施價直；若無價直，二俱不與，亦無有罪。彼若答言：我求此物為書正法。即以葉紙應施與之，仍告彼言：隨意受用。彼若欲書下劣典籍，不與無罪。如書下劣，書等亦爾。若欲書寫最勝經典，不施與者，當知有罪。

Ru-Yi Translation

Or there may be pages not yet inscribed, and someone comes asking for them. At that time a bodhisattva should ask him what he intends to use them for. If he answers that he wishes to resell them for food, then, if these pages were meant for the true teaching, one should not give them; if one has other wealth, one should give its equivalent value; and if one has no such equivalent, one may withhold both, without fault. But if he answers that he seeks them to inscribe the true teaching, one should give the pages and tell him to use them as he sees fit. If he wishes to copy an inferior text, withholding them is without fault, and the same holds for a middling text. But if he wishes to copy the most excellent scriptures, and one withholds them, this is a fault.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi lays out the commentary's threefold ranking of texts behind this rule — inferior, tied to desire and liable to stir up the afflictions; middling, ordinary worldly learning such as ethics or the arts and sciences; and supreme, the transcendent teaching itself — and notes the asymmetry the treatise builds in: withholding blank pages from someone who wants them for an inferior or middling text carries no fault at all, but withholding them from someone who wants them for the finest scriptures does. The whole passage, running from asking a stranger's purpose before parting with even blank paper to weighing the rank of what he means to copy, makes the same point running through this entire section — a bodhisattva's giving is never a reflex, but a decision made with the actual consequences of the gift in view.
