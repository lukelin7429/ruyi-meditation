The Mind That Was Never Truly Stained

Having completed a full ten-angle examination of form aggregate, the treatise now turns the same careful lens on the four formless aggregates — feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — opening with what it actually means to speak of "one mind," why mind's own nature is never itself defiled even when overrun by affliction, and how karma and affliction together bind a being to the wheel of birth and death.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 55 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: What "One Mind" Really Means — A Single Reverent Thought, and Countless Moments Mistaken for One

The treatise turns from form aggregate, now fully analyzed, to the four formless aggregates — feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — announcing that it will explain these four in the same detailed manner already applied to form. It opens with the first of ten angles examining these four aggregates together: establishing the character of "one mind." The treatise grounds this in two scriptural citations. The first has the Buddha himself declaring that if any being, in the Tathāgata's presence, generates even a single thought and speaks even a single utterance of reverence — "Well-Gone One! Great Teacher!" — he already praises that being as having accomplished a great deal of wholesome activity, how much more so one who acts in body and speech according to that same reverent intention; the lecture notes that even a single distracted thought entering a temple and uttering "homage to the Buddha" once is said, in the Lotus Sūtra, to plant the seed of eventual buddhahood, since a single sincere gesture toward the Tathāgata already sets something irreversible in motion. The second citation has the Buddha stating that through a single pure thought, one is destined for a fortunate rebirth. The treatise then explains what "one mind" actually means in both cases: it refers to a continuous succession of similar mental moments engaging the very same object one after another, this entire continuous stream being conventionally called "one mind" even though, strictly speaking, countless discrete moments of mind have actually occurred in succession — exactly as "one" utterance or "one" bodily action likewise refers, on close analysis, to a whole continuous sequence of closely resembling moments rather than to any single, truly indivisible instant.

Ru-Yi Translation

Having now analyzed form aggregate, I shall next explain the character to be established for the four formless aggregates it subsumes. As the character of one mind was established in the Basic Section, this is now clarified. As the World-Honored One said: if any being, in the presence of the Tathāgata, merely generates one thought and speaks one utterance — "Well-Gone One! Great Teacher!" — I say that being has already accomplished much among wholesome phenomena, how much more so one who acts with body and speech according to that same intention. And it is also said: through one pure thought, one is destined for a fortunate rebirth. Things of this kind should be understood, in this context, as a continuous succession subsumed under one mind, called "generating one mind" according to conventional expression. And according to this same conventional principle of continuity, one likewise speaks of generating one utterance or one bodily action.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening clarification as a quietly important corrective to how casually people speak of having "one thought" or performing "one action": what feels like a single mental event is always, on the treatise's own analysis, an unbroken succession of many closely resembling moments engaging the same object, "oneness" here being a convenient label for continuity rather than a claim about genuine indivisibility. He connects this directly to the Lotus Sūtra's famous teaching that even one distracted person's momentary bow toward a Buddha image plants an irreversible seed of future buddhahood — precisely because that "single" reverent moment is itself already a small continuous stream, sincere enough to leave a lasting trace in the storehouse consciousness. Having established what "one mind" conventionally means, he notes, the treatise turns to a related question: whether the discriminating mental consciousness and the five non-discriminating sense-consciousnesses that often accompany it actually take the same object at the same time.

2. Section: Why the Discriminating and Non-Discriminating Minds Always Share the Same Object

The treatise asks whether the discriminating mind — mental consciousness, capable of investigation and analysis — and the non-discriminating minds — the five sense-consciousnesses, incapable of such conceptual activity — should be said to take the very same present object, or different objects, answering firmly that they take the very same present object, for three reasons. First, because of utter clarity: when mental consciousness accompanies one of the five sense-consciousnesses, its own grasp of the present object's appearance becomes extremely clear and distinct, in a way it never achieves when merely recalling a past object on its own. Second, because attention itself is undivided: the same mental factor of attention that rouses the five sense-consciousnesses toward the present object rouses mental consciousness toward that identical object at the very same moment, without any difference between them. And third, because both depend on the same object for their own nourishment: both the discriminating and non-discriminating minds are sustained and strengthened by engaging the present object together, this shared dependence itself proving they share the very same object. The lecture notes the Explanation of the Underlying Meaning Sūtra's own confirmation of this same point: wherever an eye-consciousness arises, mental consciousness necessarily arises together with it; wherever eye- and ear-consciousness arise together, mental consciousness arises together with them as well; and so on however many of the five sense-consciousnesses happen to be active at once — only ever a single mental consciousness accompanying them all simultaneously, proving that whenever any of the five sense-consciousnesses arises, mental consciousness is always already present alongside it, sharing its very object.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: should the discriminating mind and the non-discriminating mind be said to take the same present object, or not? Answer: they should be said to take the same present object. Why? For three reasons: because of utter clarity, because of shared attention toward that object, and because both are nourished by the same dependence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this teaching as a useful piece of everyday introspection: the vivid clarity of seeing a color or hearing a sound right now, compared with the noticeably fuzzier quality of merely remembering a past color or sound, is itself direct evidence that mental consciousness is engaging the very same present object the relevant sense-consciousness is engaging, not working from some separate, private object of its own. Having settled that these two kinds of mind share their object, he notes, the treatise turns to a question of real practical and doctrinal weight: whether mind's own defilement, when it occurs, belongs to mind's own essential nature, or arises instead from something merely associated with mind — a question whose answer determines whether genuine liberation is even possible at all.

3. Section: Mind Is Never Itself Defiled — Only Ever Accompanied by What Defiles It

The treatise asks, when a defiled mind arises, whether this defilement belongs to mind's own essential nature, to what mind is merely associated with, or to the latent afflictive seeds that follow mind — answering that it belongs to association and to latent seeds, never to mind's own essential nature. This follows a fundamental principle already established earlier in the treatise: the World-Honored One himself taught that the nature of every mind is originally pure, since mind's own essential nature is never what generates the fault of impurity, unlike craving and the other afflictions, which are themselves impure through and through and generate every fault directly. The treatise then draws out the fault in the opposing view: if mind's own essential nature really were defiled, it would have to be irredeemably impure exactly like craving and its companions, an untenable consequence, since it would mean no amount of discipline, concentration, or wisdom could ever purify it, and no being could ever progress from ordinary status to sagehood — a result that would contradict the entire structure of cause and effect the Buddha's teaching depends upon. It is precisely because mind's own essential nature is never itself defiled that the treatise can say mind, the moment it arises, is essentially pure — the very afflictions that seem to overrun it being, in this light, nothing more than incidental, guest-like defilements passing through rather than any permanent stain on mind's own basic character.

Ru-Yi Translation

Question: when a defiled mind arises, should this be said to belong to its own essential nature, to association, or to latent seeds? Answer: it should be said to belong to association and to latent seeds, not to its own essential nature. If its own essential nature were defiled, it would have to be irredeemably impure like craving and the rest. If this were so, a great fault would follow. Because its own essential nature is not defiled, it is said that mind, when it arises, is essentially pure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this teaching on mind's original purity as one of the most quietly consequential doctrines in the entire treatise, since the whole possibility of spiritual practice depends on it: if affliction were simply what mind essentially is, no cultivation of discipline, concentration, or wisdom could ever succeed in removing it, and the entire path to awakening described throughout this treatise would collapse into incoherence. He notes that later commentators identified this teaching as substantially the same as an earlier tradition's own doctrine that mind has been pure since beginningless time regardless of whether it happens to be currently defiled or not — the point in both cases being that affliction is always a visitor, never a landlord. Having secured this crucial distinction between mind's own nature and what merely accompanies it, he notes, the treatise turns to unpacking exactly how affliction accompanies mind — beginning with the latent, dormant form of affliction the tradition calls "heaviness," and the noble ones' own unflinching way of directly contemplating suffering in even its most subtle form.

4. Section: Affliction Clings Through Association, and Its Dormant Seed Weighs the Whole Being Down

The treatise turns to how affliction actually accompanies mind, asking first what role the outwardly manifest afflictions themselves — craving, aversion, and the rest, actively arising again and again — play among the two causes of mental defilement, answering that they belong to association: manifest affliction defiles mind precisely by accompanying it directly. The treatise then asks what "latent seed" actually refers to, answering that it names the underlying heaviness and lack of ease belonging to the entire category of affliction — the dormant, seed-level residue that remains even when no affliction is currently manifesting outwardly, itself possessing five distinguishing marks: a heaviness that leaves body sluggish and mind joyless; a rigidity that makes character stubborn and difficult to train; an obstructive quality that blocks meditative concentration, the noble path, and liberation itself; a cowardice that leaves body and mind unable to shoulder any great responsibility; and an unfree, incapable quality that prevents body and mind from accomplishing genuine achievement in either mundane or supramundane pursuits. This same latent heaviness, the treatise explains, sustains all conditioned phenomena in a fundamentally unsatisfactory state, which is precisely why the noble ones, directly perceiving this pervasive suffering inherent in all conditioned existence, dwell in a settled contemplation of suffering even where ordinary beings notice nothing wrong at all — an ordinary person readily recognizes painful sensation as suffering, and even manages to notice, with some reflection, that pleasant sensation eventually gives way to suffering through its own decay, but almost never perceives the third and subtlest kind of suffering, the mere restless impermanence pervading even neutral experience, which only a noble one's penetrating insight actually uncovers. The treatise illustrates how such contemplation unfolds through the simile of a feverish, painful abscess: applying hot ash to it produces the suffering of pain itself; applying a cooling salve brings a moment's relief that inevitably fades again, illustrating the suffering of decay; and the abscess's own bare, ongoing existence, apart from either hot ash or cooling salve, constitutes the suffering of impermanence itself — precisely the condition of any body sustained by the three realms, as the earlier discussion of the aggregates already showed it to be.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the noble ones' capacity to perceive suffering even in neutral, apparently untroubled experience as the single clearest marker distinguishing awakened insight from ordinary perception — an unenlightened being readily grants that pain hurts and that pleasure fades, yet almost never notices the restless impermanence running beneath even a perfectly calm, contented moment, which is exactly what the noble ones' penetrating contemplation uncovers. He connects this to Zhū Zìqīng's own famous modern essay "Fleeting Days," whose lament that swallows return, willows green again, and peach blossoms bloom anew, yet a person's own days never return once passed, expresses in secular, literary language the very same insight this treatise draws out through the image of a feverish abscess — and to the verse the elder disciple Anuruddha is said to have spoken at the Buddha's own parinirvāṇa, likening all worldly merit, however vast, to the moon's reflection on water or the fragile trunk of a banana tree, inevitably destroyed by the wind of impermanence. Having established how deeply and subtly affliction's latent heaviness runs, he notes, the treatise turns to classifying defilement itself into three basic kinds, before examining exactly how karma and affliction together function as literal bonds holding a being captive to the cycle of birth and death.

5. Section: Three Kinds of Defilement, and the Five Ropes That Bound the Defeated Demon-King

The treatise names three kinds of defiling mental factor that together encompass every form of defilement whatsoever: defilement through karma, meaning unwholesome courses of action; defilement through feeling, meaning painful sensation itself; and defilement through affliction, meaning affliction as such. Of these three, the first two — karmic and feeling-based defilement — belong exclusively to the desire realm, since unwholesome courses of action occur only there, and painful sensation likewise belongs only to the desire realm, form-realm beings from the first absorption onward experiencing joy or pleasure, and beings from the fourth absorption upward, together with the entire formless realm, experiencing only equanimity; affliction-based defilement, by contrast, extends throughout all three realms without exception. The treatise then turns to how affliction and karma each function as literal bonds, asking first what constitutes the bond of affliction, and answering simply: every latent afflictive seed. The lecture illustrates this bond's own grip with a story from the scriptures: when the devas once defeated the asura king in battle, the Trāyastriṃśa devas bound him with five ropes and brought him before Śakra, lord of the gods; catching sight of the heavens' own splendor, the asura king found himself thinking how wonderful it would be to simply remain there rather than return to his own asura palace — and the very instant this thought of longing arose, all five ropes loosened at once, releasing him into the heavenly pleasures he had just begun to crave; yet the moment he thought instead of returning home, the ropes tightened once more, binding him ever more securely as his craving for celestial pleasure vanished. The Buddha, the lecture notes, drew the obvious moral from this account: the bondage of Māra is even more binding than these five ropes, since anyone who clings to the five aggregates as a genuine self has already fallen into Māra's own grip, the self-love and self-view arising from this clinging themselves constituting a great affliction, an abscess, a thorn — which is why the Buddha's noble disciples are taught to recognize selfhood itself as the true affliction, the true abscess, the true thorn, abandoning self-attachment and cultivating the contemplation of selflessness as the only genuine escape from Māra's own hold, transcending birth and death, and advancing toward nirvāṇa.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the asura king's own five ropes as an unusually vivid, almost comic illustration of a genuinely serious point: bondage in this system is never simply an external force imposed on a passive victim, but something craving itself actively tightens or loosens moment to moment, the asura king literally binding and freeing himself according to what he happened to want in each instant. He treats the Buddha's own extension of this story — that clinging to the five aggregates as "self" is itself Māra's true bondage, far more binding than any physical rope — as the whole point of the illustration, self-view being the one attachment underlying every other form of craving this treatise has spent so much time analyzing. Having explained affliction's own bond, he notes, the treatise turns next to karma's own bondage, distinguishing it into several distinct kinds beginning with an attachment to busy, worldly activity that leaves no room for genuine practice.

6. Section: The Novice Who Longed to Be a Dragon — Karma's Remaining Bonds and Their Six-or-Four-Fold Count

The treatise completes its account of karma's bondage with three further kinds. Karma obstructing three specific attainments also counts as bondage — obstructing the very intention to renounce worldly life, obstructing the joy that comes from attaining the meditative seclusion that prepares the noble path, and obstructing the direct realization of the noble path itself. Karma that brings about one of eight especially difficult ripened conditions likewise counts as bondage — birth in one of the three lower destinies, birth among the long-lived heavens or the northern continent of Uttarakuru, birth blind, deaf, or mute, birth with only worldly cleverness rather than genuine faith, or birth before or after a buddha's own appearance in the world — each condition making it effectively impossible to encounter the teaching even where it exists. And karma arising from a fundamentally misguided aspiration also counts as bondage — wishing, for instance, to be reborn as a nāga or garuḍa as the reward for one's own virtuous conduct. The lecture illustrates this last, most poignant kind of bondage with an old story of an arhat and his young novice attendant, who lived together in the mountains, the arhat regularly invited to a nāga king's own palace for meals. One day, washing his master's bowl, the novice noticed a few grains of unusually fragrant rice and grew suspicious about where his master had actually been eating; concealing himself beneath the arhat's meditation seat, he was carried along, unnoticed, into the nāga palace itself, where the nāga king and his retinue bowed first to the arhat and then, to the arhat's own surprise, to the novice as well. Afterward, the arhat warned his young attendant firmly: however magnificent the nāga's palace, jeweled ornaments, and retinue might appear, the nāga king remained, in the end, merely an animal, still subject to three hidden miseries — its cherished feast turning to toads the moment it touched the nāga's own tongue, its beautiful consorts becoming a pair of mating serpents the instant of union, and scales growing backward along its own spine, sand lodged beneath them causing constant, piercing pain — while the novice himself, though not yet enlightened, needed only to keep his precepts purely to be reborn, in due course, among the Heaven of the Thirty-Three gods, a rebirth exceeding the nāga palace's own splendor a hundredfold. The novice made no reply, but from that day on could think of nothing but the nāga's life, lost his appetite, and eventually died of his own consuming longing — reborn, as a direct result of that final wish, as the nāga king's own son, while the nāga king himself, upon his own death, was reborn as a human being. The treatise concludes this list by explaining that these four kinds of karmic bondage may be counted either as six, if the threefold obstruction to renunciation, meditative joy, and the path is separated into three, or as four, if that same threefold obstruction is instead counted as a single kind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the young novice's own fatal longing as one of this treatise's most quietly devastating cautionary tales: he was warned in exquisite, unambiguous detail exactly how much genuine suffering underlies the nāga's glittering existence, understood every word, and was still unable to stop wanting it anyway — proof, he suggests, that intellectual understanding alone rarely overcomes deeply rooted craving without sustained practice to back it up. He notes the story's own quiet irony as its final sting: the novice who longed upward toward apparent splendor ended up a nāga's child, while the nāga king who had merely lived out his own karma, without any comparable craving of his own, was reborn simply as a human being — misguided aspiration, in other words, actively working against the aspirant's own genuine interest. Having completed this detailed account of the four formless aggregates' relationship to affliction, karma, and bondage, he notes, the treatise turns to a sixth major topic within this same ten-angle analysis: the specific mental factors that invariably or only occasionally accompany every act of mind, beginning with the five universal factors present in absolutely every moment of consciousness whatsoever.

7. Section: Five Mental Factors in Every Single Moment of Mind — Attention, Contact, Feeling, Perception, and Volition

The treatise turns to the sixth topic within this analysis of the four formless aggregates: which mental factors accompany consciousness, and how. It asks first how many universal mental factors arise together with consciousness whenever any of the eight kinds of consciousness arises, answering five: attention, contact, feeling, perception, and volition — present, without exception, in every mental moment, wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral alike, throughout every realm and every occasion. The treatise then names five further mental factors, the strongest among many others that arise only in connection with a particular object rather than universally: aspiration, resolve, mindfulness, concentration, and wisdom. Turning to each universal factor's own defining character, the treatise explains attention as simply what rouses mind toward its object, the very trigger that initiates the whole act of cognition. Contact is defined as the meeting of the three — faculty, object, and consciousness — which alone makes it possible for mind to grasp its object at all, contact itself serving as the foundation feeling, perception, and volition all depend upon. Feeling is defined as this same threefold meeting giving rise to the reception of an object, the actual experience of pleasure, pain, or neutrality. Perception is defined as this same threefold meeting establishing a conceptual label for the object and grasping it through that borrowed, conventional label — the treatise noting that perception operates through two distinct modes: awakened perception, belonging to beings skilled in language such as gods and humans, capable of articulating and understanding conceptual meaning directly; and dormant, language-latent perception, belonging to beings not yet skilled in language, such as infants and animals, who still manage a rough grasp of their surroundings — an infant recognizing basic patterns before it can speak, a dog recognizing its own master and wagging its tail — even without fully articulate conceptual thought, since the seed of language still lies latent within them, quietly shaping whatever crude discrimination they are capable of. And volition is defined as this same threefold meeting causing mind to actively engage its object, either drawing toward what is pleasing or pulling away from what is displeasing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between awakened and dormant perception as a genuinely useful way of understanding how infants and animals clearly recognize their surroundings without possessing full language — the seed of conceptual labeling lies latent within them, shaping a rough, functional discrimination even before it ever blossoms into articulate speech, an insight he suggests any parent watching a preverbal child recognize a familiar face has already observed directly. He notes that all five universal factors together form the basic architecture of every single moment of experience without exception — attention rousing mind, contact bringing the three together, feeling receiving what is met, perception labeling it, and volition finally acting upon it — a chain worth tracing deliberately in one's own meditation practice. Having explained all five universal factors' own defining character, he notes, the treatise turns to the five strongest of the many non-universal, particular-object factors — beginning with aspiration, the wish that first draws mind toward a specific object rather than any object whatsoever.

8. Section: Five Further Factors — Aspiration, Resolve, Mindfulness, Concentration, and Wisdom's Threefold Discernment

The treatise turns to the five strongest particular-object mental factors, each arising only in connection with a specific kind of object rather than universally. Aspiration is defined as inclining toward and wishing for whatever particular object is at hand — wishing to unite with a desired object, or wishing to be free of an undesired one. Resolve is defined as inclining toward and firmly affirming a particular object — a settled, unshakeable conviction, whether the object affirmed happens to be blue rather than red, the four noble truths accepted without doubt, or, just as unshakeably, some mistaken view a person has simply made up their mind about; once resolve of this decisive kind has arisen, it is not easily dislodged, whether what it affirms happens to be true or false. Mindfulness is defined as inclining toward and clearly retaining a particular object — remembering, without forgetting, something previously experienced, though it does not arise at all with respect to something never previously encountered. Concentration is defined as inclining toward a particular object with sustained, careful reflection, serving as the very basis for one-pointedness of mind — repeatedly recollecting the same object without distraction, whether cultivating awareness of the breath or contemplating an image of impurity, this same sustained focus eventually yielding genuine concentration; concentration in this broader sense includes both the settled and the still-distracted mind, wherever sustained, repeated focus on a single object is present. And wisdom is defined as inclining toward a particular object with discernment of phenomena — sometimes reasonable, sometimes unreasonable, and sometimes neither — the capacity to distinguish wholesome from unwholesome, true from false, and thereby to sever doubt; it is precisely wisdom, among the six perfections, that is uniquely unshared with ordinary or non-Buddhist practitioners, since the other five perfections can, in some form, be practiced by anyone, while only wisdom's own guiding light allows the cultivation of all six together to actually overcome the twin obstructions of affliction and to what is knowable, and thereby to realize great awakening.

9. Section: What Each Mental Factor Actually Does — From Rousing the Mind to Judging Right and Wrong

The treatise turns from what each of the ten mental factors is to what each one actually does. Attention's function is to draw mind toward its object — without it, even an undamaged faculty facing a fully present object registers nothing at all, exactly as one may look straight at something and still not truly see it, or hear a sound and still not truly listen, until attention itself is roused. Contact's function is to serve as the basis for feeling, perception, and volition, none of which can arise without it. Feeling's function is to serve as the very condition craving depends upon — craving to unite with what is pleasing, or to separate from what is displeasing, always arising in dependence on some prior feeling, so that a being entirely free of feeling would no longer be troubled by pleasure or pain's own pull at all. Perception's function is to paint and narrate mind's own object, the Flower Ornament Sūtra's own image of mind as a skilled painter, producing every world through its own brushwork, aptly describing perception's role in coloring in and labeling whatever consciousness encounters — first the label is formed, then the words follow, so that even words that seem to burst out "without thinking" have, in truth, already passed through preparatory, decisive, and executing stages of intention so habitual and swift that they only feel instantaneous, since without some prior recognition of what is being spoken about, no speech could follow at all. And volition's function is to give rise to investigation, analysis, and the bodily and verbal actions that follow from them, volition's own defining nature being precisely this capacity to construct and carry an intention through to actual expression.

10. Section: Aspiration Fuels the Effort, Concentration Grounds the Wisdom, and Wisdom Judges What Is Pure

The treatise completes its account of the mental factors' functions with the five particular-object factors. Aspiration's function is to generate diligent effort — without some prior wish, no sustained exertion ever actually gets underway, exactly as wishing to understand the teaching is what first brings a student to sit down and study it, and what then keeps that same student pressing forward, undiscouraged, through the inevitable difficulties of daily practice; the lecture cites the classical description of a truly diligent person as one who faces any task without hesitation, holds firm resolve, never tires, and sees every undertaking through to genuine completion. Resolve's function is to affirm, one way or another, whatever merit, fault, or neither a given object possesses — a firm, decisive understanding that leaves no residual doubt about how to judge what has been encountered. Mindfulness's function is to retain what was long ago thought, done, or said — the lecture stressing the word "long" here specifically, since mindfulness applies only to what has actually already been experienced, this same steady retention proving indispensable to anyone hoping to sustain meditative concentration or to remain faithful to the teaching without lapsing into error. Concentration's function is to serve as the very basis for supramundane wisdom itself — genuine insight capable of cutting through affliction requires, at minimum, the access concentration bordering the first absorption as its foundation, mere intellectual reflection prior to that attainment counting only as an approximation of calming and insight rather than the real thing. And wisdom's function is to investigate, in accordance with reason, whether any given instance of speech or conduct in body, speech, and mind is defiled or pure — a discerning person capable of judging purity and defilement correctly cultivates goodness in body, speech, and mind alike, this same discernment itself admitting further, subtler gradations, since even the highest worldly wholesomeness remains, by comparison with uncontaminated wisdom, still a form of defilement, and even an arhat's own residual ignorance about what is merely knowable, though no longer an affliction, still counts as something a bodhisattva must eventually overcome — purity and defilement, in other words, being established relative to how deep one's own realization has actually gone, rather than as any single, fixed line.

11. Section: What Triggers Each Particular Factor, and Why Feeling Sometimes Belongs to the Eye and Sometimes to Thought

The treatise asks under what specific circumstances each of the five particular-object mental factors actually arises, answering in sequence: aspiration arises toward a beloved object, resolve toward a firmly settled object, mindfulness toward a habitually familiar object, and both concentration and wisdom toward an object under sustained observation — concentration and wisdom sharing this last condition, while the other three each have their own distinct trigger. The lecture stresses a crucial practical point buried in this sequence: the five universal factors arise the instant any object is merely encountered, still just a bare mental event with no committed direction yet, so that catching an unwholesome impulse at this earliest stage costs relatively little effort to interrupt — hence the traditional saying that one need not fear a thought's arising, only fear being slow to notice it — whereas once a particular-object factor like resolve or aspiration has taken hold, the mind has already committed to a direction that becomes far harder to redirect. The treatise then turns to a further question: since feeling, perception, and formations are named among the four formless aggregates, do they belong to the class of merely initial, unreflective mind, to searching mind, or to determining mind? The answer is that they belong to whichever class of mind they happen to accompany at a given moment — the very same feeling, perception, or volition counting as part of an initial sense-consciousness's own class when it arises alongside eye-consciousness's first bare contact with an object, or as part of mental consciousness's own class when it arises during that same consciousness's subsequent searching or determining activity — since only mental consciousness, never the five sense-consciousnesses, is capable of searching or determining in the first place.

12. Section: Egg Yolk and Egg White — Why Mind and Its Factors Never Operate Separately

The treatise raises a natural puzzle: scripture says the four formless aggregates always operate combined, never separately, and that none of these phenomena can genuinely be divided or analyzed apart from one another — yet if each of the four possesses its own distinct character, why should they be described as an undifferentiated combination at all? The treatise answers that only when many factors combine together can an object actually be experienced, received, and understood in full; lacking even one of the four, whatever task consciousness sets out to accomplish remains incomplete. The lecture illustrates this with the classical dialogue between King Milinda and the monk Nāgasena: asked why name and form can never occur independently of one another, Nāgasena replied that they are mutually dependent and co-arising, exactly as a hen's egg is never merely yolk or merely white, but always both together inseparably — name is never found without form, nor form without name, and it is precisely this same mutual dependence, endlessly repeating, that has kept beings circling through birth and death since beginningless time. The lecture adds a homelier illustration for the same principle: a bowl of corn soup requires corn, cream, water, carrots, celery, salt, and pepper simmered together — a taster might, with effort, discern each individual ingredient's own particular flavor, yet only their combination actually constitutes "corn soup" as such. Dependent origination itself, the lecture explains, works by this same logic of mutual reliance — "this existing, that exists; this arising, that arises" — each of the five aggregates possessing its own distinct nature, yet none of them ever actually functioning to receive and understand an object except in combined operation with the others, a principle that reveals every conditioned phenomenon's own impermanent, dependent, and selfless character the moment it is properly understood.

13. Section: Four Names for Mind and Its Factors — Having an Object, Being Associated, Having Activity, Having a Basis

The treatise asks how many different names distinguish mind and its associated mental factors, answering that there are countless such names, among which four stand out: having an object, being associated, having activity, and having a basis. Asked why the five sense faculties, which likewise engage objects, are not also called "having an object" the way mind and its factors are, the treatise explains that the sense faculties can continue to exist even apart from any object they might take, persisting in a kind of ongoing continuity regardless, whereas mind and its mental factors simply cannot arise at all without an object present — wherever they occur, some object of cognition is necessarily already occurring together with them, which is why this name belongs to them uniquely. Asked why mind and its factors are called "associated," the treatise gives four reasons: they share the very same object; they depend on the very same faculty; they arise in the very same instant; and they accomplish the very same task, whether recognizing something as blue or generating some wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral karma together. Asked why they are called "having activity," the treatise explains that upon a single object, countless distinct mental factors arise and operate together — attention rousing the mind, contact bringing the three together, feeling receiving, perception labeling, volition constructing — this entire simultaneous flurry of distinct activity earning the name. And asked why they are called "having a basis," the treatise explains that mind and its factors, as a single category, always depend on multiple supports at once in order to arise at all — a co-existing basis such as the eye faculty, an immediately preceding basis such as the previous moment of consciousness making way for the next, and a seed-basis such as the underlying potential stored in the storehouse consciousness — while conditioned phenomena generally, though none of them arise without some cause whatsoever, are not called "having a basis" in this specialized sense, since that term here refers specifically to this constant, threefold dependency unique to mind and its factors, form aggregate not depending on any faculty the way mind depends on these three supports.

14. Section: Clarity and Ignorance, Cause and Fruit — And a Detour Through the Platform Sūtra's Thirty-Six Pairs

The treatise examines several meaningful contrasts among mental states. Pleasant, painful, and neutral feeling stand in mutual contrast to one another, since although all three belong to the same broad category of feeling, they occupy genuinely different positions within it. Neutral feeling further stands in contrast to ignorance specifically, since neutral feeling accompanies every instance of feeling as its constant companion exactly as ignorance accompanies every affliction as its constant companion — both serving, each within its own domain, as the universal partner underlying every other member of their category. Clarity — wisdom capable of overcoming affliction — stands in contrast to ignorance as remedy against what it remedies. And clarity stands in contrast to nirvāṇa as cause against effect, since clarity is the very wisdom that severs affliction and thereby brings about nirvāṇa as its fruit. The lecture pauses here to recall the Sixth Patriarch's own famous teaching to his students on how to expound the dharma skillfully: whatever one-sided position a questioner takes, answer by pointing to its natural opposite, so that heaven is met with earth, light with dark, existence with non-existence, affliction with awakening, birth with cessation, and so through thirty-six such paired opposites in all, since a teacher who becomes trapped defending "existence" alone, or "non-existence" alone, has already lost the true teaching — genuine skill lies in leading a questioner to see that every phenomenon arises only in mutual dependence on its own contrast, exactly as long is only ever long relative to short, or heavy only ever heavy relative to light, so that once this endless web of relativity is seen through entirely, a practitioner is no longer trapped by either extreme, and awakens instead to reality as such, beyond both.

15. Section: The Tenth and Final Topic — Nine Angles on Wholesome, Unwholesome, and Neutral, and Six Grounds Where Goodness Arises

16. Section: Eleven Wholesome Factors — Faith, Shame, Modesty, and the Roots That Make Genuine Goodness Possible

The treatise names the eleven mental factors whose presence constitutes wholesomeness in its own essential nature: faith, moral shame, moral modesty, non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion, diligence, pliancy, non-negligence, equanimity, and non-harming. Faith is a settled, deep conviction in and aspiration toward what is real, virtuous, and capable — trust in the reality of things as they are, in the Three Jewels' own genuine excellence, and in the attainability of both worldly and supramundane virtue — its function being to overcome doubt and generate delight in what is good, the Flower Ornament Sūtra itself calling faith the very source of the path and the mother nourishing every wholesome quality, severing the net of doubt and opening the unsurpassed gate to nirvāṇa. Moral shame is reverence for what is genuinely worthy, arising from one's own inner sense of principle — the aspiration to become, through one's own effort, whatever admirable person one already reveres. Moral modesty is a related but distinct sensitivity to worldly disapproval, a reluctance to be censured or scorned by others, both moral shame and modesty together restraining unwholesome conduct before it occurs. Non-greed is simply freedom from attachment to any of the three realms' pleasures or their underlying causes; non-hatred is freedom from aversion toward suffering and its causes — the treatise noting that both arise together within any wholesome state of mind, one addressing attachment to pleasant conditions, the other addressing aversion toward painful ones, rather than only one or the other ever arising in isolation. Non-delusion is clear insight into the true nature of causes and conditions, dispelling the confusion that otherwise leaves a person unable to distinguish right from wrong. Diligence is courageous, sustained effort in cultivating virtue and abandoning fault, distinguished from mere busy activity by its exclusively wholesome direction. Pliancy is the genuine ease and lightness of body and mind that follows once meditative concentration has subdued their underlying heaviness, granting the capacity to sustain serious practice. Non-negligence is simply diligence and the three roots of non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion actively guarding against the resurgence of what has already been abandoned — the scriptures declaring that every wholesome quality whatsoever ultimately depends on this single root of non-negligence. Equanimity is that same fourfold combination bringing the mind to rest in an even, upright, effortless stillness, free of agitation. And non-harming is simply non-hatred directed specifically toward not causing injury to other beings — non-hatred itself granting happiness, non-harming specifically removing suffering, the two established as distinct in name precisely to highlight compassion's own two complementary aspects.

17. Section: The Rice Merchant's Riddle — How Faith Met the Moment of Firm Conviction

The treatise turns to matching each of the eleven wholesome factors against the six grounds already established, beginning with the observation that faith accompanies the moment of firm conviction — since without settled trust in something, no genuine conviction about it can ever take hold, precisely as someone who lacks confidence in the value of renunciation will never resolve to actually leave ordinary life behind. The lecture illustrates faith's own quiet power with a story told by the venerable teacher Miàojìng in his own commentary on the chapter devoted to Avalokiteśvara: a rice merchant along the coast of Zhejiang habitually burned incense morning and evening before an image of the bodhisattva, sincerely but without any sense that his devotion had ever produced a visible result — until one night the bodhisattva appeared to him in a dream, warning that danger was coming, and offering four cryptic lines to memorize as protection: do not stop your boat at a bridge; if oil spills, wipe it on your head; a dou of grain yields three sheng of rice; flies will perch on the writing-brush's tip. Puzzled but faithful, the merchant kept the verse in mind. Soon after, transporting rice by boat, he remembered the first line just as his crew wanted to rest beneath a bridge, insisted they push on instead, and watched the bridge collapse moments after they passed beneath it. Newly convinced the dream had been genuine, he returned home, only to have a jar of oil spill across the floor that same evening; recalling the second line, he wiped some on his own head before going to sleep — and woke to find his wife murdered, decapitated, in their bed. Accused by his own father-in-law, who reasoned that a Buddhist husband and a wife with no faith in the Buddha could never truly get along, the merchant was hauled before the magistrate, unable to account for the crime at all; just as the magistrate lifted his brush to record a guilty verdict, a swarm of flies settled directly on its tip, and the merchant, recalling the fourth line, spoke it aloud. Startled and intrigued, the magistrate pressed him for the third line as well, and reasoned it through: a dou of unhusked grain yields only three sheng of milled rice, leaving seven sheng of husk behind — seven, perhaps, being a person's name, and husk suggesting the character for a common surname sounding similarly; investigating further, the magistrate located a man matching this description, a certain Kāng the Seventh, who confessed under questioning: he had been having an affair with the merchant's wife and meant to kill her husband that night in his sleep, deliberately sparing whichever of the two heads he found oiled — since only women oiled their hair in that era — and so, finding the husband's head anointed, killed the wife instead by mistake. The innocent merchant was released, and, profoundly shaken by the whole experience, went on to renounce the world and take up monastic practice.

18. Section: Which Wholesome Factor Accompanies Which Moment — From Setting Aside Faults to Embracing Others

The treatise completes its matching of the eleven wholesome factors against the six grounds. At the moment unwholesome states are set aside, moral shame and moral modesty both arise, the one restraining conduct through self-regard, the other through regard for how others would judge it — the lecture citing the teaching that these two "bright factors" alone save beings from ruin, that a person entirely without them can hardly be called human at all, and that even someone who has already erred can still find purity again through the courage to acknowledge the fault honestly. At the moment of actually performing a wholesome action, non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion, and diligence all arise together, since affliction leaves the mind agitated and heavy, incapable of settling into any wholesome activity at all, while these four roots keep it clear and steady enough to act. At the moment of worldly purification, pliancy arises, born of the very access concentration that first subdues coarse affliction. At the moment of supramundane purification, non-negligence and equanimity both arise, since sustained diligence toward liberation is never guaranteed to succeed but is nonetheless the only path with any hope of success, and once the noble path is actually attained, the resulting freedom from greed, hatred, and delusion settles into the effortless stillness called equanimity. And at the moment of embracing other beings, non-harming arises, since this same embrace is itself subsumed under great compassion, and no one genuinely embracing another being's welfare could simultaneously wish them harm.

19. Section: Three Merely Conventional Virtues, and What Makes a Wholesome Root Soft, Medium, or Superior

The treatise asks how many of the eleven wholesome factors exist only conventionally, as mere positional labels for something more basic, and how many exist as genuinely distinct realities, answering that three — non-negligence, equanimity, and non-harming — exist only conventionally. Non-negligence and equanimity are simply positional variants of non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion, and diligence together: this same fourfold combination, insofar as it actively guards against affliction's resurgence, is called non-negligence, and insofar as it settles into an even, effortless stillness once affliction has actually been removed, is called equanimity — non-negligence naming the cause, equanimity naming its resulting fruit. Non-harming, similarly, is simply non-hatred considered specifically in its application to not injuring other beings, without any further, separate reality of its own — non-hatred itself granting happiness, non-harming specifically removing suffering, compassion's own two complementary faces built from this single underlying root. The treatise then turns to a further angle: what distinguishes a wholesome root of merely soft strength, from one of medium strength, or one of superior strength. A soft wholesome root belongs to a mind not yet settled in meditative concentration at all, or, even where concentration has been attained, capable only of countering affliction's own coarsest, most obvious forms. A medium wholesome root belongs to worldly meditative attainment capable of countering affliction's middling grade. And a superior wholesome root belongs specifically to supramundane, transcendent insight, or to a meditative attainment capable of countering even affliction's subtlest, most deeply hidden grade. The treatise then explains what actually elevates any of the eleven factors into this superior grade: sustained effort exerted over the whole of one's present life; repeated habituation across many lifetimes; an inherent tendency built up through beginningless habituation and now arising spontaneously; devotion directed toward an especially worthy field of merit, such as the Three Jewels or an accomplished practitioner of loving-kindness or cessation, whose sheer merit can yield a result within this very life when approached with sufficiently strong faith and resolve; and purification through the supramundane path itself, which severs affliction and strengthens the power of the path directly.

20. Section: Eight Ways a Wholesome Root Actually Gets Started, From Generosity to Embracing Other Beings

The treatise asks how many kinds of activity give rise to a wholesome root in the first place, answering eight, briefly. 21. Section: Eight Remedies, Countless Distinctions — Wholesomeness's Own Many Faces

The treatise explains how the eight bases already named actually counter defilement: whenever craving, aversion, or wrong view first stains the mind's own continuity, whether in this present life or a future one, that same activity — generosity countering stinginess, discipline countering misconduct, meditation countering aversion, and so on down the list — serves as its direct remedy, ensuring the mind no longer remains bound to the affliction it has just encountered; sustained practice of these eight activities allows wholesome roots to keep growing without limit. The treatise then turns to a further angle: what distinguishes one wholesome state from another. 23. Section: Fifteen Remedies, From Simple Aversion to Complete Severance — Wholesomeness's Ninth and Final Angle

The treatise names fifteen ways wholesome cultivation counters affliction, completing the ninth and final angle applied to the wholesome. Remedy through aversion belongs to the preparatory path of learning, reflection, and cultivation, generating genuine distaste for suffering and its origin. Remedy through severance belongs to the supramundane path's own uninterrupted path, directly cutting affliction at the moment of its own destruction. Remedy through sustaining belongs to that same path's liberating fruit, securing the resulting transformation and safeguarding what has just been severed. Remedy through distancing belongs to the mundane path of cultivation that follows, permanently preventing already-severed affliction from ever recurring. Remedy toward attachment to a desired destiny counters craving for the pleasant results of rebirth as a human or a god. Remedy toward aversion to an undesired destiny counters the aversion and confusion that generate the ten unwholesome actions leading to the three lower realms. Remedy toward the latent seed belongs to the supramundane path specifically, severing affliction at its very root. Remedy toward coarse, medium, and subtle affliction each belong to correspondingly soft, medium, and superior grades of the path — coarser affliction, though seemingly more disruptive, actually yields to a weaker remedy, while the subtlest, most deeply hidden affliction requires the most powerful remedy of all to dislodge. Remedy through settling counters distraction, pliancy arising once concentration takes hold. Remedy through admonition and confession counters affliction through the willingness to accept correction and openly acknowledge fault — a practice the lecture illustrates with the Buddha's own teaching to the monastic community: when one monk faults another, both sides must reflect honestly rather than let resentment compound resentment, the one at fault recognizing the wrongdoing and confessing it, the one giving correction recognizing that correction itself, carelessly repeated, breeds only further discord, both together thereby settling what would otherwise fester. Remedy through weakening counters affliction through sustained personal effort that saps its habitual strength. Remedy through suppression counters manifest affliction directly, as the five specific meditative antidotes address craving, hatred, and their companions one by one, though only temporarily holding them in check rather than uprooting them. And remedy through complete severance belongs to the supramundane path alone, permanently cutting off affliction's own seed and freeing the mind from its bondage entirely. 24. Section: Root Afflictions and Their Six Grounds — Where Craving, Aversion, and Pride Actually Take Hold

The treatise turns to the unwholesome, explaining that every defiling phenomenon manifests through two forms: root affliction and secondary affliction, and that it will address root affliction's own nine angles first, before turning to secondary affliction's parallel nine. Asked how many grounds give rise to root affliction, the treatise answers six. The first, ignorance paired with a pleasing, defiled object, gives rise to craving — delight and clinging toward whatever seems desirable. The second, ignorance paired with a displeasing, defiled object, gives rise to aversion. The third, improper attention paired with a defiled object generally, gives rise to delusion itself. The fourth, ignorance paired with comparing oneself against inferior, equal, or superior beings, against one's own or another's five aggregates taken as a real self, against confused judgments about attainments gained or not yet gained, and against confused judgments about genuine merit, gives rise to pride — the lecture unpacking this single ground into seven distinct kinds of pride: ordinary pride toward those inferior; excessive pride toward equals, imagining oneself superior; pride-upon-pride toward superiors, imagining oneself equal or even greater; modest pride toward superiors, imagining the gap smaller than it truly is; self-pride, clinging to one's own aggregates as a genuine self; presumptuous pride, mistaking a lesser attainment for a greater one not yet reached; and wrongful pride, mistaking actual faults for genuine merits. 25. Section: Six Root Afflictions Defined — And Why Ignorance Accompanies Every Single One

The treatise names the six root afflictions constituting the unwholesome in its own essential nature: craving, aversion, ignorance, pride, view, and doubt. Craving is a defiled attachment, whether arising through habitual conceptual reinforcement or spontaneously, toward an inwardly or outwardly pleasing object, cultivated through bad company, wrong teaching, improper attention, and the ordinary lapse of mindfulness. Aversion is this same defiled reactivity directed instead toward a displeasing object, arising through the identical causes. Ignorance is a defiled lack of understanding, again either habitually reinforced or spontaneous, toward whatever ought properly to be known. Pride is an inflated sense of superiority or inferiority toward oneself or others, built from the same causes. View subdivides into five distinct kinds: the view of a real self within the five clung-to aggregates; the extreme view of either permanent selfhood or utter annihilation; wrong view generally; the view that clings to one's own view as supreme; and the view that clings to mistaken precepts and observances as the true path to liberation — the first two of these five arising both through habitual reinforcement and spontaneously, the remaining three only through habitual conceptual reinforcement, never spontaneously. And doubt is a genuine, unresolved hesitation toward what ought properly to be known, the mind unable to settle one way or the other. The treatise then asks which of these afflictions accompanies which others, answering first that ignorance accompanies every single one of them without exception — wherever craving, aversion, pride, view, or doubt arises, ignorance is already present, underlying and enabling it, exactly as darkness underlies every specific shape a room's shadows might take.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats ignorance's universal presence within every other affliction as this section's single most important structural fact: craving, aversion, pride, wrong view, and doubt are each, in their own way, simply ignorance taking on a more specific shape, which is why the tradition consistently treats dispelling ignorance through wisdom, rather than merely suppressing each affliction's outward symptom one by one, as the only genuinely decisive remedy. He notes that view's own further subdivision into five distinct kinds — self-view, extreme views, wrong view generally, view-attachment, and precept-attachment — captures an important distinction between views arising spontaneously in any unenlightened mind and views that require deliberate conceptual reinforcement to take hold at all, the former proving considerably harder to dislodge since they need no teaching or argument to arise in the first place. Having named all six root afflictions and confirmed ignorance's universal presence among them, he notes, the treatise turns to unpacking exactly which further afflictions can co-occur with one another, beginning with craving's own relationship to the rest.

26. Section: Doubt Stands Alone, Craving and Aversion Never Meet, But Pride and Wrong View Travel Together

The treatise continues examining which afflictions co-occur, explaining that doubt is associated with none of the others at all, since doubt's own defining character is an unresolved hesitation, neither settled ignorance nor settled misapprehension of an object, and so shares nothing in common with the other five, which are each some form of mistaken certainty rather than genuine uncertainty. Craving and aversion, by contrast, are mutually exclusive — wherever one is present, the other cannot be, their basic natures running in opposite directions — yet either one may still combine with pride or with wrong view: craving toward some object may accompany an inflated sense of superiority regarding it, or a distorted, speculative view about it, and aversion behaves exactly the same way, someone convinced of their own view's correctness finding it especially easy to grow irritated with anyone who disagrees. Pride and view, for their part, tend to reinforce one another particularly closely, since the very same inflated self-regard that generates pride typically also drives the distorted, self-serving reasoning that generates wrong view — arrogance and mistaken conviction feeding each other in a single, mutually strengthening loop.

Having settled how the six root afflictions variously combine, he notes, the treatise turns to a further angle: whether each affliction exists as a genuinely distinct reality, or merely as a conventional label for some more basic underlying phenomenon.

27. Section: Wrong View Is Just Tainted Wisdom — And Why the Coarsest Affliction Is Actually the Weakest

The treatise asks how many of the six root afflictions exist merely conventionally and how many as genuinely distinct realities, answering that view alone is merely conventional, being simply a positional variant of wisdom itself — wisdom, capable of manifesting as wholesome, unwholesome, or neutral depending on what accompanies it, becomes "wrong view" specifically when tainted, distorting the very faculty that ought to yield genuine insight into a source of further suffering instead; the remaining five afflictions — craving, aversion, ignorance, pride, and doubt — each possess their own genuinely distinct reality as separate mental factors. The treatise then turns to what makes any of these afflictions soft, medium, or superior in grade, offering a classification that runs in the opposite direction from the wholesome: whichever affliction is severed last, being the subtlest and most deeply hidden, counts as soft; whichever is severed partway through counts as medium; and whichever is severed first, being coarsest and most obvious, counts as superior — coarse affliction, though seemingly more disruptive, actually proving easier to remove, while the subtlest residue takes the greatest effort to finally dislodge. The treatise then names six further factors that elevate any affliction to this superior grade: affliction generated through sexual desire tends strongly toward the superior grade; affliction reinforced through long habituation likewise tends superior; affliction arising once the faculties have fully matured, in someone past adolescence, tends superior, since a mature person's afflictions manifest with far greater force than a child's, however genuinely present even a child's affliction already is; affliction belonging to someone entirely without the potential for liberation tends superior, since no path of cultivation stands ready to weaken it; affliction wrongly directed at an object that actually deserves reverence — parents, teachers, or the Three Jewels — tends superior, precisely because it violates what ought to have called forth respect instead; and affliction that has already manifested as actual bodily or verbal conduct tends superior, visible unwholesome action generally outweighing affliction that remains merely internal.

28. Section: Ten Grounds for Craving, Ten Matching Names — From Clinging to the Aggregates to Longing for Non-Existence

The treatise turns to the sixth angle within this analysis of root affliction, asking from how many grounds affliction arises, and beginning with craving's own ten: clinging to the five aggregates themselves as a genuine self; attachment to one's own views; longing for a desirable object not yet obtained; refusal to release a desirable object already obtained; nostalgic longing for some pleasant object previously enjoyed but no longer present; attraction toward unwholesome conduct itself when it seems to bring some benefit; the distorted perception of maleness and femaleness that provokes desire before it would otherwise arise and intensifies it once present; affection toward friends and family, who share resources and warm feeling; delight in fine clothing, food, bedding, and other requisites; and craving for future existence, the hope for a better rebirth to come, or, at the opposite extreme, a nihilistic craving for utter non-existence, wishing simply to be annihilated once life's suffering becomes unbearable. 29. Section: Ten Grounds for Aversion, and the Nine Vexations That Surround Every Ordinary Life

The treatise turns to aversion's own ten grounds: one's own body and the aging, sickness, and other unwelcome conditions it inevitably brings; a beloved being who fails to act as one wishes, resentment so often growing precisely out of intimacy; a disliked being toward whom ill will is already present; a past enemy or a past friend; a future enemy or a future friend; a present enemy or a present friend; any object generally that proves displeasing; jealousy, an inability to bear another's good fortune, wishing every excellence belonged to oneself instead; habitual tendencies toward anger carried over from past lives, reasserting themselves again and again; and simple disagreement with another's opinion. The treatise then explains that ten corresponding forms of aversion arise from these same ten grounds, though not always in strict sequence, before drawing the first six grounds — one's own body, a beloved being, a disliked being, and each of these last two considered across past, future, and present — together into what the tradition calls the nine vexations, since the beloved-versus-disliked distinction, multiplied across three times, yields nine specific occasions for aversion toward other beings altogether. The lecture reflects on how inescapable this predicament actually is: human beings cannot live apart from others, surrounded constantly by exactly these nine categories of relationship, so that without sustained cultivation of loving-kindness, aversion arises again and again almost automatically — friendships turning to enmity over a single careless word or glance, and old enmities just as easily dissolving given the right conditions, since neither friend nor foe is ever a fixed, permanent category, a truth Tsongkhapa himself captured in verse: friend and foe alike shift moment to moment, nothing about them ever truly settled, so a practitioner should abandon false distinctions of love and hatred and instead diligently cultivate the equanimity that regards every being with the same compassionate regard.
