Fascicle 78 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十八 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Avalokiteśvara Asks: How Many Kinds of Purity Do the Ten Grounds Contain?

This is the seventh part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the Chapter Distinguishing Yoga now closed in the previous fascicle — Maitreya's twenty-six questions on how calm-abiding and insight, practiced as one indivisible path, carry a bodhisattva from an ordinary being's first resolve to unsurpassed enlightenment — the Chapter on Excellent Qualities' citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra turns to its sixth topic: the Chapter Distinguishing the Ground-Pāramitās. Where the previous chapter's "resolute practice" (勝解行) concerned a bodhisattva still on the grounds of ordinary conviction, this chapter's "true practice" (如實行) concerns the ten grounds themselves — Utmost Joy through Cloud of Dharma, and the Buddha ground besides. It opens with a new questioner, the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, asking how many kinds of purity these eleven grounds contain, and how many parts each is divided into — the Buddha's answer beginning with a fourfold purity (superior intention, precepts, mind, and wisdom) that maps onto the grounds in turn.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 78 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Avalokiteśvara's Question — How Many Kinds of Purity Do the Eleven Grounds Contain?

Further, in dependence on the vehicle provisionally established, distinguishing and explaining in full the true Mahāyāna practice, it should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra describes: the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara said to the Buddha: World-Honored One! As the Buddha has taught, bodhisattvas have ten grounds — namely Utmost Joy, Free from Defilement, Emitter of Light, Blazing with Wisdom, Utmost Difficulty to Conquer, Manifest Presence, Far-Reaching, Immovable, Excellent Wisdom, and Cloud of Dharma — and the Buddha ground is further spoken of as an eleventh. Of these grounds, how many kinds of purity do they contain? Into how many parts are they divided? The Buddha told the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara: Good sons! You should know that these grounds contain four kinds of purity, and are divided into eleven parts.

Chinese Source

復次，依乘假立，分別解說如實大乘，當知如解深密經中，觀自在菩薩白佛言：世尊！如佛所說菩薩十地，所謂極喜地、離垢地、發光地、焰慧地、極難勝地、現前地、遠行地、不動地、善慧地、法雲地，復說佛地為第十一。如是諸地，幾種清淨？幾分所攝？佛告觀自在菩薩曰：善男子！當知諸地，四種清淨，十一分攝。

Ru-Yi Translation

Further, in dependence on the vehicle provisionally established, distinguishing and explaining in full the true Mahāyāna practice, it should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra describes: the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara said to the Buddha: World-Honored One! As the Buddha has taught, bodhisattvas have ten grounds — namely Utmost Joy, Free from Defilement, Emitter of Light, Blazing with Wisdom, Utmost Difficulty to Conquer, Manifest Presence, Far-Reaching, Immovable, Excellent Wisdom, and Cloud of Dharma — and the Buddha ground is further spoken of as an eleventh. Of these grounds, how many kinds of purity do they contain? Into how many parts are they divided? The Buddha told the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara: Good sons! You should know that these grounds contain four kinds of purity, and are divided into eleven parts.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens fascicle 78 by placing it within the Decisive-Discussion chapter's larger structure: "the seventh part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground" now moves from rebutting nihilistic emptiness and citing the Saṃdhinirmocana on ultimate truth, mind, the three natures, and the practice of yoga (fascicles 75 through 77) to a new citation, the Chapter Distinguishing the Ground-Pāramitās, unpacking how the ten pāramitās map onto the ten grounds. He explains "vehicle" (乘) as a means of conveyance carrying beings from the shore of birth-and-death to the shore of nirvāṇa, and glosses pāramitā itself through four traditional renderings — reaching the far shore, a deed brought to its utmost completion without limit, and Master Yìnshùn's twofold gloss distinguishing pāramitā as "a task perfectly accomplished" (its static sense) from pāramitā as "the path and method by which that accomplishment is reached" (its dynamic sense), two readings of a single word rather than two different things. He introduces the new questioner at length: Avalokiteśvara, who appears for the first time in this treatise's citations, already attained buddhahood in a past inconceivable eon as the Tathāgata Right Dharma Brightness, and who, out of great compassion, turned back from that attainment to appear as a bodhisattva, tirelessly ferrying beings across both this present world and Amitābha's Pure Land, destined to attain buddhahood there once more in the future as the Tathāgata King of the Universally Radiant Merit Mountain and to succeed Amitābha in guiding that land's beings — presently manifesting as an eighth-ground bodhisattva, acting effortlessly and abiding in signlessness, compassion and wisdom perfectly joined within, the three karmic activities perfectly observed without. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the deliberate shift in questioner and register: where Maitreya's twenty-six questions concerned "resolute practice" (勝解行), the still-aspirational cultivation proper to a bodhisattva before the grounds, Avalokiteśvara's questions concern "true practice" (如實行), the actual, realized cultivation of a bodhisattva already standing on the grounds themselves — the first oriented mainly toward one's own inward cultivation, the second toward the skillful outward work of guiding others. The Buddha's opening answer names two frameworks that will structure everything to follow: a fourfold purity (superior intention, precepts, mind, and wisdom) spanning the eleven grounds, and an elevenfold division within which each single ground itself contains both a portion already perfected and a portion still to be perfected as one advances toward the next.

2. Section: The Fourfold Purity — Superior Intention, Precepts, Mind, and Wisdom

How do the four kinds of purity contain the grounds? Namely: the purity of superior intention contains the first ground; the purity of superior precepts contains the second ground; the purity of superior mind contains the third ground; and the purity of superior wisdom, becoming ever more excellent and sublime ground after subsequent ground, should be known to contain the fourth ground up through the Buddha ground.

Chinese Source

云何四種清淨能攝諸地？謂增上意樂清淨，攝於初地；增上戒清淨，攝第二地；增上心清淨，攝第三地；增上慧清淨，於後後地轉勝妙故，當知能攝從第四地乃至佛地。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do the four kinds of purity contain the grounds? Namely: the purity of superior intention contains the first ground; the purity of superior precepts contains the second ground; the purity of superior mind contains the third ground; and the purity of superior wisdom, becoming ever more excellent and sublime ground after subsequent ground, should be known to contain the fourth ground up through the Buddha ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "superior" (增上), citing the Śrāvaka-bhūmi (juan 28), as carrying the sense of both a destination aimed at and a supremacy attained — "superior" because it is what practice tends toward, and superior because what it tends toward is itself supreme. "Intention" (意樂, Skt. āśaya) he traces to a root sense of a resting-place or dwelling, hence also rendered aspiration or resolve — whatever the mind inclines toward wanting to do. Citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 37), he defines it as resting on a settled, unshakeable confidence — in one's own discerning reflection on the Dharma, in the Buddha's own well-taught teaching and discipline (which cannot be argued away), and in deep trust of what others have already realized — while Vasubandhu's commentary on the Mahāyānasaṃgraha reduces its essence to two components, aspiration (a wish to attain) and resolve (a settled conviction), toward the Three Jewels and toward benefiting self and other alike. "Purity" itself he defines simply as freedom from the afflictive and cognitive obstructions: intention purified enough to directly realize suchness, from the first ground up through the ninth, is called a bodhisattva's "pure intention," while intention purified to its utmost, on the tenth ground and the Buddha ground, is called "well-purified intention." Master Cháng Bǎi then surveys, at length, Master Woncheuk's tenfold gloss on what "superior intention" specifically names on the first ground — drawn from the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 48) and organized, via the Avataṃsaka-sūtra commentarial tradition, around the first ground's traditional ten great vows: reverence toward one's teachers without deceit (paired with the vow to make offerings to all buddhas); patient, gentle ease living among fellow practitioners (paired with the vow to protect the true Dharma); mastery over affliction and the works of Māra (paired with the vow to demonstrate the Buddha's own career in the world); clear insight into the faults of all conditioned activity (paired with the vow to embrace every wholesome practice); a clear vision of great nirvāṇa's own benefit (paired with the vow to bring all beings to maturity); tireless application to the excellent factors of awakening (paired with the vow to manifest in every world); delight in seclusion as the natural accompaniment of that application (paired with the vow to purify every buddha-land); indifference to worldly rank, gain, and honor however tainted (paired with the vow that all bodhisattvas share a single aim); turning away from the lesser vehicles toward the greater one alone (paired with the vow that no effort go to waste); and the wish to bring benefit to every single being (paired with the vow to swiftly realize enlightenment) — each of the ten, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, also perfecting a corresponding quality of directness and integrity of mind (an unbending, gentle, tempered, wholesome, tranquil, genuine, undivided, unresentful, resolute, or magnanimous directness) as it reaches its own full ripeness. He treats this dense tenfold correspondence as illustrative rather than exhaustive — a template, borrowed from the *Avataṃsaka*'s own account of the first ground, for how each of the eleven grounds' governing purity will unfold from here: superior intention perfecting the first ground alone, superior precepts (already treated at length in this treatise's own account of the bodhisattva's threefold discipline) perfecting the second, superior mind (concentration free of any further backsliding) perfecting the third, and superior wisdom — refining itself ever further, ground after ground, with no single new name required — perfecting every ground from the fourth all the way to buddhahood itself.

3. Section: The Eleven Parts — Utmost Joy, What Is Perfected and What Remains

Good sons! You should know that these four kinds of purity comprehensively contain all the grounds. How do the eleven parts contain the grounds? Namely: bodhisattvas who, while still on the ground of resolute practice, have through the ten practices of the Dharma most excellently cultivated the forbearance of resolute conviction, thereby surpass that ground and enter the bodhisattva's correct nature apart from birth. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to abide with correct awareness amid the current arising of subtle, inadvertent transgressions. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

善男子！當知如是四種清淨普攝諸地。云何十一種分能攝諸地？謂諸菩薩先於勝解行地，依十法行極善修習勝解忍故，超過彼地，證入菩薩正性離生。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能於微細毀犯誤現行中正知而住。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good sons! You should know that these four kinds of purity comprehensively contain all the grounds. How do the eleven parts contain the grounds? Namely: bodhisattvas who, while still on the ground of resolute practice, have through the ten practices of the Dharma most excellently cultivated the forbearance of resolute conviction, thereby surpass that ground and enter the bodhisattva's correct nature apart from birth. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to abide with correct awareness amid the current arising of subtle, inadvertent transgressions. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first closes the fourfold-purity account: though every ground from the fourth up through buddhahood technically possesses all four purities together, each of the first three grounds is named for whichever purity is most distinctively its own — intention for the first, precepts for the second, mind for the third — with each later ground inheriting every quality the earlier grounds already established, while the earlier grounds have not yet perfected what the later ones will add. He also cross-references the Decisive-Discussion chapter's own fourfold "abiding" scheme (juan 79) — utmost joy, superior precepts, superior mind, superior wisdom — mapping the same structure onto four accomplishments a bodhisattva achieves by relying on them in turn: never abandoning the great bodhi-resolve, steadily increasing one's own wealth and virtue toward the day of enlightenment, being able to return to the desire realm by the power of vow without being stained by its afflictions, and — through mastery of both what can and cannot be verbally established — swiftly realizing a buddha's own wisdom without waiting on any further stage or any other's instruction. The second framework, the elevenfold division, then examines each of the eleven grounds (the ten plus the Buddha ground) in turn through a fixed pattern: what portion is already fully perfected (which is to say, what qualified entry into this very ground) and what portion remains unperfected (which is to say, precisely what must still be cultivated to advance to the next). On the first ground, Utmost Joy, what is already perfected is the forbearance of resolute conviction won through the ten practices of engaging the Dharma cultivated during the stage of resolute practice — writing out the scriptures, making offerings to them, giving them to others, listening to them read, delighting in reading them oneself, silently receiving and retaining them, reciting them aloud, expounding them to others, reflecting on their meaning through the four principles of reasoning, and finally cultivating calm-abiding and insight upon them directly — the first eight belonging to the wisdom born of hearing, the ninth to the wisdom born of reflection, and the tenth to the wisdom born of cultivation; mastering all ten to the point of unshakeable conviction is what carries a bodhisattva past the stage of resolute practice into the correct nature apart from birth, whether "correct nature" is read as the very substance of the noble path or as nirvāṇa itself, and "apart from birth" as freedom from the discriminated afflictive and cognitive obstructions — what the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-sūtra* (juan 468) calls freedom from "attainment" itself, since ordinary clinging to gain breeds affliction while a mind with nothing to attain breeds none, and what the same sūtra (juan 484) calls freedom even from "Dharma-attachment," the subtle clinging to the very teachings one has used to counteract affliction, dissolved only when the wisdom realizing the emptiness of both self and dharmas actually manifests. What remains unperfected on this same first ground, however, is that a bodhisattva here still lacks the constant, correct awareness needed to avoid subtle, wholly inadvertent transgressions of the precepts — not deliberate wrongdoing, but a lapse of mindfulness, as when one unknowingly steps on an ant — and it is precisely this residual gap that the second ground exists to close.

4. Section: Free from Defilement — Precepts Perfected, Concentration and Dhāraṇī Still to Come

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain the mundane, fully perfected states of concentrated absorption and meditative attainment, nor the fully perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能得世間圓滿等持、等至，及圓滿聞持陀羅尼。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain the mundane, fully perfected states of concentrated absorption and meditative attainment, nor the fully perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent further cultivation of exactly the correct awareness the first ground still lacked is what perfects the second ground, Free from Defilement: a bodhisattva here genuinely never transgresses the precepts, whether deliberately or through an inadvertent lapse of mindfulness. What remains unperfected, though, is twofold: the mundane, fully stabilized states of concentrated absorption (samādhi, spanning everything from the threshold concentration up through the four dhyānas and four formless attainments) and meditative attainment (samāpatti, the deeper equipoise from the first dhyāna upward) are not yet secure against regression; and the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard — one of the four dhāraṇīs the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 45) describes, alongside dhāraṇī retaining meaning, retaining mantra, and retaining the very forbearance realized through the other three — is not yet fully perfected either. Master Cháng Bǎi explains dhāraṇī broadly as a kind of total retention: holding all dharmas within a single dharma, all words within a single word, all meanings within a single meaning, so that a bodhisattva who has attained it can hear teachings never before encountered and retain them across any span of time without forgetting, becoming capable of fearless, unhindered teaching precisely because nothing already learned is ever again lost. It is this same dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard — the ability to hold the Dharma's own words and letters in memory without limit — whose remaining incompleteness marks exactly what a bodhisattva must still perfect to advance from the second ground to the third.

5. Section: Emitter of Light — Dhāraṇī Perfected, Craving for Concentration Still Remains

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to dwell in extensive cultivation of the factors of awakening they have attained, and their minds are not yet able to relinquish craving for meditative attainments and craving for the Dharma. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能令隨所獲得菩提分法多修習住，心未能捨諸等至愛及與法愛。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to dwell in extensive cultivation of the factors of awakening they have attained, and their minds are not yet able to relinquish craving for meditative attainments and craving for the Dharma. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Further diligent cultivation perfects the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard, carrying a bodhisattva into the third ground, Emitter of Light — so named because the light of concentration and total retention now illuminates the Dharma one has heard and practiced, or, on another reading, because an inwardly purified mind is itself what emits this light, hence its alternate name, the abode of superior mind. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Sub-commentary*'s gloss on precisely what "fully perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard" means: a bodhisattva who has attained this power of mindfulness and wisdom can hear teachings never before encountered, unfamiliar in their very words, scattered across an immeasurable number of scriptures compiled in every order and every arrangement, spoken directly or indirectly, and retain every one of them across any span of time without forgetting — a single hearing yielding a thousand realizations, the very meaning of "total retention." What remains unperfected on this third ground, however, is twofold: a bodhisattva here is not yet able to dwell in extensive, sustained cultivation of the thirty-seven factors of awakening already attained (the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest), and the mind has not yet relinquished two subtle cravings — craving for the meditative attainments themselves, and craving even for the Dharma-dhāraṇī just perfected. It is precisely the relinquishing of these two lingering attachments, to concentration and to the Dharma one has mastered, that the fourth ground exists to accomplish.

6. Section: Blazing with Wisdom — The Factors of Awakening Perfected, the Truths Still Only Partly Seen

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the principle of the truths as it truly is; nor are they yet able to relinquish the intention that turns exclusively away from saṃsāra and toward nirvāṇa; nor are they yet able to cultivate the factors of awakening embraced by skillful means. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能於諸諦道理如實觀察，又未能於生死涅槃棄捨一向背趣作意，又未能修方便所攝菩提分法。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the principle of the truths as it truly is; nor are they yet able to relinquish the intention that turns exclusively away from saṃsāra and toward nirvāṇa; nor are they yet able to cultivate the factors of awakening embraced by skillful means. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent, extensive cultivation of the thirty-seven factors of awakening, together with the relinquishing of craving for concentration and for the Dharma, perfects the third ground's remaining portion and carries a bodhisattva into the fourth, Blazing with Wisdom — named because wisdom now burns like a flame vigorous and unbroken enough to consume the very firewood of affliction, illuminating the true teaching; its alternate name, the abode of superior wisdom joined with the factors of awakening, names the same thing. What remains unperfected here is threefold: a bodhisattva has not yet observed the four noble truths, or equally the ultimate and conventional truths, with full, penetrating clarity; has not yet relinquished the still-lingering intention that turns exclusively away from saṃsāra toward nirvāṇa; and has not yet cultivated the factors of awakening as embraced by skillful means — meaning not the factors themselves but the further expedient means that support and complete their cultivation. It is precisely these three gaps that the fifth ground exists to close.

7. Section: Utmost Difficulty to Conquer — Three Skillful Means, and the Buddha Who Bathed a Sick Monk

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the flow of saṃsāra as it truly is, and, because they still generate considerable weariness toward it, are not yet able to dwell extensively in the intention toward the signless. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能於生死流轉如實觀察，又由於彼多生厭故，未能多住無相作意。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the flow of saṃsāra as it truly is, and, because they still generate considerable weariness toward it, are not yet able to dwell extensively in the intention toward the signless. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent cultivation of the three remaining gaps carries a bodhisattva into the fifth ground, Utmost Difficulty to Conquer, whose difficulty and whose conquest Master Cháng Bǎi glosses through the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* (juan 3): mundane and supramundane wisdom are, by nature, opposed — the one bearing characteristics and grasping at attainment, the other signless and without attainment — yet a fifth-ground bodhisattva succeeds in harmonizing the two so that the mundane simply is the supramundane, affliction simply is bodhi, and saṃsāra simply is nirvāṇa, an achievement so difficult that conquering it earns the ground its name. The observation of the four noble truths, or equally the ultimate and conventional truths, is perfected by direct, unobstructed insight into their mutual non-opposition; the exclusive turning away from saṃsāra is overcome by directly seeing that saṃsāra simply is nirvāṇa; and the factors of awakening embraced by skillful means are perfected through what the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 36) names as the great expedient means for realizing a buddha's own wisdom — mastery of emptiness itself, the direct understanding that the imagined nature is empty while the dependent and perfected natures, apart from mere verbal designation, genuinely exist. Citing the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*, Master Cháng Bǎi details the three skillful means embraced here: the practice of never being satisfied with merit accumulated, illustrated by the Buddha's own famous conduct — despite being fully perfected in blessing and wisdom, the Buddha still personally threaded a needle for a blind elderly monk who could no longer sew his own robes, and personally bathed a gravely ill young monk unable to rise from his bed, telling the astonished Aniruddha that no one in the world seeks merit more tirelessly than the Tathāgata, who is never satisfied in giving, instruction, patience, teaching by word and by meaning, protecting beings, and seeking the unsurpassed true path; the practice of tireless diligence in seeking the wisdom of prajñāpāramitā that has nothing to attain, contrasted with an ordinary being's habitual grasping after gain; and the practice of tireless cultivation of great loving-kindness and compassion, so that concern for others' benefit never wearies. What remains unperfected on this fifth ground, however, is that a bodhisattva here still cannot observe the very flow of dependent arising through birth-and-death — ignorance conditioning volitional formations, and so on through aging-and-death — with complete clarity, and, still generating considerable weariness toward it, cannot yet dwell extensively in the signless intention that the sixth ground will bring to completion.

8. Section: Manifest Presence and Far-Reaching — Dependent Arising Observed, Signless Intention Made Constant and Unbroken

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to dwell in extensive cultivation of the signless intention without gap and without interruption. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected. In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to relinquish exertion of effort within the signless abiding, nor are they yet able to attain mastery with regard to characteristics. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent cultivation of the fifth ground's remaining gap — clear observation of saṃsāra's own flow, together with the weariness that blocked extensive dwelling in the signless — carries a bodhisattva into the sixth ground, Manifest Presence, so named because prajñā's own wisdom now stands directly present before the mind, no longer merely a matter for exclusive weariness but observed exactly as it is, dwelling extensively (though not yet perfectly without gap or interruption) in the signless intention. What remains unperfected here is precisely that residual gap and interruption: even a sixth-ground bodhisattva's signless contemplation is not yet entirely free of the least momentary lapse, nor entirely free of conventional, characteristic-bearing thought occasionally intruding upon it — a flaw the seventh ground, Far-Reaching, exists to correct, sitting as it does at the very boundary between effortful and effortless practice, the furthest reach a bodhisattva can attain while still requiring some exertion to sustain the signless without interruption. Even here, though, two things remain unperfected: a seventh-ground bodhisattva can dwell continuously in signless samādhi only through continued, deliberate exertion, not yet effortlessly; and mastery over characteristics themselves — the free, effortless functioning appropriate to benefiting both oneself and others amid conditioned appearances — is not yet complete. Both gaps together are exactly what the eighth ground will finally close.

9. Section: Immovable and Excellent Wisdom — Effortless Mastery, the Four Kinds of Unhindered Eloquence

Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected. In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain great mastery, amid the teaching of the Dharma, with regard to synonymous expressions, manifold characteristics, etymological explanation, and their every distinction and kind. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected. In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain the direct, personally realized experience of the fully perfected Dharma-body. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent cultivation of the seventh ground's two remaining gaps — effortless persistence in the signless, and mastery over characteristic-bearing appearances — carries a bodhisattva into the eighth ground, Immovable, so named because nothing, not even affliction itself, can any longer disturb an effortless activity no longer requiring exertion to sustain. What remains unperfected here concerns the four kinds of unhindered eloquence: mastery of synonymous expression (unhindered understanding of the Dharma's own words and terms), of manifold characteristics (unhindered understanding of the many meanings those words convey), of etymological explanation (unhindered ability to gloss and re-gloss a single term — suchness, true nature, the dharma-realm, reality, the limit of reality, the signless realm, the realm free of all discursive elaboration — through any number of different formulations), and of teaching itself in every possible arrangement (unhindered, spontaneous eloquence, saying whatever needs saying in whatever way it needs saying) — none of these four is yet fully mastered. It is precisely this fourfold eloquence that the ninth ground, Excellent Wisdom, brings to completion, so that a ninth-ground bodhisattva can teach the Dharma with total freedom in any form whatsoever; citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's parallel fourfold scheme (juan 43 and 45) — wisdom regarding the Dharma's synonyms, wisdom regarding its characteristics, wisdom regarding its verbal explanation, and wisdom regarding its manifold kinds — Master Cháng Bǎi confirms these are simply the same four unhindered forms of eloquence under a different name. What remains unperfected even on the ninth ground, however, is the direct, personally realized experience of the fully perfected Dharma-body itself — a completion reserved for the tenth ground alone.

10. Section: Cloud of Dharma and the Tathāgata Ground — All Eleven Parts Perfected

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain, with regard to every knowable object everywhere, the unattached, unobstructed wondrous wisdom and wondrous vision. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected. In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion is fully perfected; and because that portion is perfected, every one of the eleven parts is thereby fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能得遍於一切所知境界無著無礙妙智妙見。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。由是因緣，此分圓滿；此分滿故，於一切分皆得圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain, with regard to every knowable object everywhere, the unattached, unobstructed wondrous wisdom and wondrous vision. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected. In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent cultivation of the ninth ground's remaining gap carries a bodhisattva into the tenth ground, Cloud of Dharma: the Dharma-body, like a great cloud filling all of space, is now fully perfected — though Master Cháng Bǎi notes that even this ground's fullness still falls short of a buddha's own, whose perfected Dharma-body is called nothing less than fully "accomplished." What remains unperfected even here is the unattached, unobstructed wondrous knowledge and vision with respect to absolutely every knowable object: unattached in the sense of being free of the afflictive obstruction, unobstructed in the sense of being free of the cognitive obstruction, since a tenth-ground bodhisattva has not yet entirely severed the most subtle remainder of either. Only at the Buddha ground, the eleventh and final part, is this last gap closed: diligent cultivation brings the unattached, unobstructed wondrous wisdom and vision itself, together with omniscience, to full completion, and because this eleventh and highest part is perfected, every one of the preceding ten parts is thereby fully and simultaneously perfected as well — buddhahood being not one more portion added alongside the rest, but the single completion in which all eleven are gathered up together. With this, the elevenfold division brought to its close, Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own summary line still to come: these eleven parts, taken together, comprehensively contain the entire scope of all eleven grounds, closing the first of the eight sections making up this chapter's account of the ground-pāramitās — the section on what each of the eleven grounds contains.

11. Section: What Contains the Grounds Closes — Avalokiteśvara Asks Why Each Ground Bears Its Name

Good sons! You should know that these eleven parts comprehensively contain all the grounds. World-Honored One! For what reason is the first ground called Utmost Joy, and so on up to what reason is it called the Buddha ground? Good sons! Because one accomplishes great benefit, and attains an outward-transcending mind never attained before, giving rise to great joy — for this reason the first is called Utmost Joy.

Chinese Source

善男子！當知如是十一種分普攝諸地。世尊！何緣最初名極喜地？乃至何緣說名佛地？善男子！成就大義，得未曾得出世間心，生大歡喜，是故最初名極喜地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good sons! You should know that these eleven parts comprehensively contain all the grounds. World-Honored One! For what reason is the first ground called Utmost Joy, and so on up to what reason is it called the Buddha ground? Good sons! Because one accomplishes great benefit, and attains an outward-transcending mind never attained before, giving rise to great joy — for this reason the first is called Utmost Joy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With the eleven-part division closed, Master Cháng Bǎi notes this completes the first of eight sections making up the chapter's account of the ground-pāramitās — what each ground contains — and Avalokiteśvara now moves to the second section, the meaning behind each ground's name. "Great benefit" is glossed as the virtue of simultaneously accomplishing benefit for oneself and for others — an achievement ordinary beings find perpetually at odds, since pursuing one's own interest and pursuing another's typically pull in opposite directions, but which a first-ground bodhisattva, once joined with non-discriminating wisdom, is able to accomplish together at every moment; "an outward-transcending mind never attained before" names this very non-discriminating wisdom itself, free of the characteristic-bearing, discriminating quality that mundane wisdom always carries. Possessing both — the accomplishment of others' and one's own benefit together, and wisdom never before tasted — a first-ground bodhisattva generates such joy that whatever is done is done in accord with the Dharma, hence the name Utmost Joy. Master Cháng Bǎi surveys several parallel explanations from the *Sub-commentary* (juan 8) and elsewhere — the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s verse on a bodhisattva who has just ascended the one-vehicle's signless path; the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā-śāstra*'s simpler gloss, that a bodhisattva here first tastes the flavor of wholesome dharmas and so is filled with joy; and the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s parallel formula, newly gaining the noble nature, realizing the twofold emptiness, and thereby benefiting both self and other — before turning to the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own extended, threefold catalogue of this ground's joy: nine kinds arising directly (reverent joy toward the Three Jewels, loving joy in contemplating suchness, joy at one's own attainment measured against what came before, joy in bodily and mental benefit fully accomplished, joy in that benefit's further increase, joy at how near enlightenment now feels, joy in an unshaken mind unruffled by debate, joy in a gentle, compassionate mind even while teaching others, and joy in patient forbearance even when others fail to practice as taught); ten kinds of joyful recollection of what is yet to be attained (recalling the buddhas, their teaching, the great bodhisattvas, their practice, the pure quality of the pāramitās, the surpassing excellence of the bodhisattva grounds, the irreversibility of a bodhisattva's power, the Tathāgatas' own work of teaching, the benefit one is able to bring to beings, and entry into every Tathāgata's own wisdom-conduct); and ten kinds of joyful recollection of what is already attained (having turned away from every worldly entanglement, having drawn near to the Tathāgatas, having become far removed from the ordinary being's own ground, having drawn near to the ground of wisdom, having cut off every evil destiny, having become a refuge for all beings, having drawn near to seeing every buddha, having been born into the buddhas' own domain, having entered the suchness common to every bodhisattva, and having become free of every fear and every trembling) — a joy, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, so thoroughly catalogued precisely because it marks the single most decisive turning point in the entire bodhisattva path, the moment ordinary being first becomes noble one.

12. Section: Free from Defilement and Emitter of Light — Names Drawn from What Each Ground Overcomes

Because one becomes free of every subtle transgression of the precepts, the second is called Free from Defilement. Because the concentration and the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard that one attains there are able to serve as the basis for an immeasurable light of wisdom, the third is called Emitter of Light.

Chinese Source

遠離一切微細犯戒，是故第二名離垢地。由彼所得三摩地及聞持陀羅尼能為無量智光依止，是故第三名發光地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because one becomes free of every subtle transgression of the precepts, the second is called Free from Defilement. Because the concentration and the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard that one attains there are able to serve as the basis for an immeasurable light of wisdom, the third is called Emitter of Light.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the second ground's name follows directly from what the eleven-part division already established: a first-ground bodhisattva still lapses, without intending to, into subtle transgressions of the precepts through a momentary loss of mindfulness, while a second-ground bodhisattva is free even of these, hence Free from Defilement, the defilement in question being specifically the stain of broken precepts. He surveys the same parallel-text pattern as before — the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s verse on this ground's bodhisattva, styled a "world-transcending noble one" appearing across a thousand lands; the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own gloss, that leaving behind stinginess, envy, and the mental stains that break the precepts through cultivating giving and pure discipline is what earns the name; the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s formula, that this ground is named for its utmost freedom from the stain of broken precepts, its precepts now naturally perfected rather than merely guarded through deliberate reflection as on the first ground; and the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā-śāstra*'s simpler reading, that practicing the ten wholesome courses of action is itself what leaves every stain behind. The third ground's name, Emitter of Light, follows just as directly: the concentration attained there emits the light of cultivation-wisdom, while the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard emits the light of hearing- and reflection-wisdom — the same fourfold dhāraṇī (retaining the Dharma, retaining its meaning, retaining mantra, and retaining the very forbearance realized through the other three) already introduced at the second ground's own account of what remained unperfected there, now become the basis for an immeasurable light of wisdom. Master Cháng Bǎi again surveys the parallel translations and glosses — "the ground of brightness" in the *Sub-commentary*'s citation of two other translations of the sūtra, "wisdom-brightness" in the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s own verse, and the *Suvarṇaprabhāsa-sūtra*'s note that concentration unshakeable by anything, joined with the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard as its very root, is precisely why the third ground bears the name it does — closing this pair of etymologies exactly where the eleven-part division itself left off, with the second and third grounds' defining accomplishments now explained in full.

13. Section: Blazing with Wisdom and Utmost Difficulty to Conquer — Names from What Is Burned and What Is Joined

Because the wisdom belonging to the factors of awakening attained there burns away every affliction like a flame, the fourth is called Blazing with Wisdom. Because the skillful means by which one cultivates those very factors of awakening are attained only with the utmost difficulty before mastery is reached, the fifth is called Utmost Difficulty to Conquer.

Chinese Source

由彼所得菩提分法，燒諸煩惱智如火焰，是故第四名焰慧地。由即於彼菩提分法方便修習，最極艱難方得自在，是故第五名極難勝地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the wisdom belonging to the factors of awakening attained there burns away every affliction like a flame, the fourth is called Blazing with Wisdom. Because the skillful means by which one cultivates those very factors of awakening are attained only with the utmost difficulty before mastery is reached, the fifth is called Utmost Difficulty to Conquer.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi surveys, as before, a wide range of parallel translations and commentaries for each name — the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s verses styling the fourth ground "blazing wisdom, wondrous light, great diligence" and the fifth "supreme wisdom"; the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own gloss that the fourth ground's wisdom-light burns day and night without ceasing, like a jewel whose radiance no other light extinguishes; the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā-śāstra*'s reading that this wisdom burns the firewood of afflictions the practitioner is not even aware of, since innate afflictions arise so readily that wisdom must be constantly, automatically vigilant; and the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s formula for the fifth, that mundane wisdom (skillful means grasping conventional truth) and supramundane wisdom (the very factors of awakening realizing ultimate truth) are by nature opposed, one bearing characteristics and one signless, and joining what by nature resists joining is precisely what makes this ground so supremely difficult to conquer — a difficulty the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra* glosses two further ways as well: the difficulty of tirelessly teaching beings without being troubled by it, and the difficulty of remaining untroubled even when beings refuse the teaching offered, both difficulties a fifth-ground bodhisattva has learned to overcome. The *Sub-commentary*'s own formula draws the two names together: this ground cultivates, by means of the factors of awakening already attained, the resolute understanding of emptiness itself — that the imagined nature is not existent while the wordless dependent and perfected natures are not non-existent — and it is the sheer difficulty of holding worldly and ultimate wisdom together in a single, unified understanding that earns the ground both its difficulty and, once mastered, its name of conquest.

14. Section: Manifest Presence and Far-Reaching — Two Wisdoms Now Present, the Furthest Reach of Effort

Because the observation of the flow of conditioned formations now stands directly present, and because the attention cultivated extensively toward the signless likewise now stands directly present, the sixth is called Manifest Presence. Because one is able to reach far enough to realize the signless attention without gap and without interruption, bordering directly upon the pure ground beyond it, the seventh is called Far-Reaching.

Chinese Source

現前觀察諸行流轉，又於無相多修作意方現在前，是故第六名現前地。能遠證入無闕無間無相作意，與清淨地共相鄰接，是故第七名遠行地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the observation of the flow of conditioned formations now stands directly present, and because the attention cultivated extensively toward the signless likewise now stands directly present, the sixth is called Manifest Presence. Because one is able to reach far enough to realize the signless attention without gap and without interruption, bordering directly upon the pure ground beyond it, the seventh is called Far-Reaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies two wisdoms now present on the sixth ground: subsequently attained wisdom, directly observing the flow of dependent arising through ignorance, formations, and onward to aging-and-death; and root wisdom, the signless attention toward the sevenfold suchness now genuinely manifesting rather than merely being approached. He cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s formula that reliance on the wisdom of dependent arising is what lets prajñāpāramitā stand constantly present, and the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra*'s gloss that this ground's wisdom neither dwells in saṃsāra nor in nirvāṇa — noting, with a touch of humility, that every earlier ground already involved some form of "presence" too, this ground simply being singled out for how fully and freely that presence is now realized. The seventh ground's name, Far-Reaching, carries three senses of "far," per the *Sub-commentary*: temporally far, since two of the three great incalculable eons of practice are now nearly complete; far in effort, since this is the very outer limit of effortful practice before the effortless practice of the eighth ground begins; and far in attainment, since a seventh-ground bodhisattva has journeyed far beyond both the impure world and the two lesser vehicles' own characteristic-bound practice, arriving at the very border of the "pure ground" that follows. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own formula — this ground reaches the very final edge of effortful activity — and Vasubandhu's gloss that though no characteristic-bearing appearance can any longer disturb a seventh-ground bodhisattva, sustaining the signless still counts, technically, as an "activity," the last such activity before the eighth ground's activity-free abiding begins.

15. Section: Immovable and Excellent Wisdom — Untroubled by Affliction, Mastery in Every Kind of Teaching

Because effortlessness is attained with regard to the signless, and one is no longer moved by any currently arising affliction amid characteristic-bearing appearances, the eighth is called Immovable. Because one attains mastery in teaching the Dharma in every way, and gains immeasurable, vast wisdom, the ninth is called Excellent Wisdom.

Chinese Source

由於無相得無功用，於諸相中不為現行煩惱所動，是故第八名不動地。於一切種說法自在，獲得無量廣大智慧，是故第九名善慧地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because effortlessness is attained with regard to the signless, and one is no longer moved by any currently arising affliction amid characteristic-bearing appearances, the eighth is called Immovable. Because one attains mastery in teaching the Dharma in every way, and gains immeasurable, vast wisdom, the ninth is called Excellent Wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the eighth ground earns its name specifically through what is left untroubled rather than what is newly attained: signless wisdom now operates entirely without exertion, so that neither the subtle characteristic-appearances of the dependent nature nor any currently active affliction can any longer disturb it — the *Bahulū Abhidharma Commentary* citing this as the point at which a bodhisattva finally "relinquishes the storehouse [name]," the eighth consciousness ceasing to be called the ālaya and becoming instead simply the maturation consciousness, since not even the subtlest trace of self-clinging remains to give the storehouse-name its meaning. He cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s twofold reading — neither the true, signless object nor the conventional, characteristic-bearing object can any longer move an eighth-ground bodhisattva's mind — and the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s formula that non-discriminating wisdom now continues effortlessly of its own accord, unmoved by characteristic, function, or affliction alike. The ninth ground, Excellent Wisdom, is named for the four kinds of unhindered eloquence now fully mastered — teaching, per the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra*'s vivid illustration, so that a single instant's utterance can simultaneously answer every different question, in every different language, posed by every different kind of being (human, heavenly, or otherwise) across a thousand million worlds, resolving every doubt at once. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the *Sub-commentary*'s gloss that "immeasurable" here also carries the sense of "faultless" — an eloquence with nothing left to correct — closing this pair of names exactly where the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own account (juan 48) of the eighth and ninth grounds already anticipated them.

Because the body of coarseness is as vast as space, and the Dharma-body, now fully perfected, is like a great cloud able to cover it entirely, the tenth is called Cloud of Dharma. Because one has permanently severed the utmost subtle afflictive obstruction and cognitive obstruction, becoming unattached and unobstructed, and directly realizes perfect, complete enlightenment with regard to every kind of knowable object, the eleventh is called the Buddha ground.

Chinese Source

麤重之身廣如虛空，法身圓滿，譬如大雲皆能遍覆，是故第十名法雲地。永斷最極微細煩惱及所知障，無著無礙，於一切種所知境界現正等覺，故第十一說名佛地。

Ru-Yi Translation

Because the body of coarseness is as vast as space, and the Dharma-body, now fully perfected, is like a great cloud able to cover it entirely, the tenth is called Cloud of Dharma. Because one has permanently severed the utmost subtle afflictive obstruction and cognitive obstruction, becoming unattached and unobstructed, and directly realizes perfect, complete enlightenment with regard to every kind of knowable object, the eleventh is called the Buddha ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses this dual image plainly: an ordinary being's coarseness — the residue of the afflictive and cognitive obstructions still weighing on body and mind — is as vast and pervasive as empty space itself, yet the tenth ground's now-perfected Dharma-body, likened to a great cloud, is able to cover that entire expanse and hold it in check. He surveys the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s influential threefold gloss: the great cloud of Dharma-wisdom holds within it every merit as a cloud holds water (the totality-comprehending wisdom of this ground, together with its dhāraṇī- and samādhi-gates); it covers over coarseness as a cloud covers the sky (concealing and cutting off the vast, space-like extent of the afflictive and cognitive obstructions); and it fills the Dharma-body as rain fills the earth (pouring forth boundless excellent qualities that fill what is realized and what it is based upon) — and cites the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own vivid image of this ground's bodhisattva as a great dragon-king raining down a Dharma-rain that only the ground itself, like the ocean alone among all bodies of water, is vast enough to receive without overflowing, its great compassion the cloud, its Dharma-teaching the thunder, its fearlessness the lightning, and its wisdom the wind that carries the rain to extinguish beings' afflictions everywhere at once. The eleventh and final name, the Buddha ground, is explained through what the *Buddhabhūmi-śāstra* calls its fivefold meaning — possessing both mundane and supramundane wisdom, free of both obstructions, penetrating both conventional and ultimate objects, awakened both for oneself and for others, and, freed from ignorance and craving alike, a mind liberated the way sleep gives way to waking and a lotus opens to the sun — and its threefold meaning of "ground": what a buddha relies upon (the pure dharma-realm), what a buddha's activity consists in (the four wisdoms — mirror-like, equality, wondrous-observing, and duty-fulfilling), and what a buddha's every quality is gathered up within. With this, the treatise's second of eight sections, the meaning behind each ground's name, closes entirely — completing, together with what each ground contains, the first two of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight-part structure.

17. Section: What Is Counteracted — Twenty-Two Delusions and Eleven Coarsenesses

World-Honored One! Within these grounds, how many kinds of delusion, and how many kinds of coarseness, are counteracted? Good sons! Within these grounds, twenty-two kinds of delusion and eleven kinds of coarseness are counteracted.

Chinese Source

世尊！於此諸地，有幾愚癡、有幾麤重為所對治？善男子！此諸地中，有二十二種愚癡、十一種麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

World-Honored One! Within these grounds, how many kinds of delusion, and how many kinds of coarseness, are counteracted? Good sons! Within these grounds, twenty-two kinds of delusion and eleven kinds of coarseness are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara now opens the third of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections — what each of the eleven grounds counteracts — with a question whose answer, twenty-two delusions and eleven kinds of coarseness (two delusions and one coarseness for each of the eleven grounds), is the very source Master Cháng Bǎi has already been quoting throughout fascicle 77's own account of the treating-obstructions door. Master Cháng Bǎi clarifies what "delusion" (愚) means here: primarily the cognitive obstruction, understood in two complementary ways — as the current activity of clinging to self and to dharmas directly, and, more broadly, as the karmic causes and ripened results that such clinging gives rise to, which are themselves not literally delusion but are so called because they arise entirely from delusion's own operation, exactly as the *Cheng Weishi Lun* (juan 9) explains at length: the first ground's two delusions correspond to what that treatise calls the obstruction of an ordinary being's own nature and to the karmic ripening bound up with the evil destinies, with "coarseness" naming either the dormant seeds of these two delusions or the incapacity they leave behind, comparable to how a meditative attainment is said to "cut off the root of suffering" even though no literal root, as a seed or a current activity, is what is directly severed. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* adds an important qualification worth carrying forward through this whole taxonomy: though what the first ground truly cuts off spans both obstructions, the delusion named "an ordinary being's own nature" is specifically keyed to the cognitive obstruction alone, since the ten forms of ignorance named throughout this eleven-ground scheme are, by definition, non-defiling in the technical sense a śrāvaka's own path also severs — the treatise is deliberately isolating what only a bodhisattva's path uniquely overcomes, ground by ground, leaving the afflictive obstruction itself to be finished only at the very threshold of buddhahood, when the diamond-like concentration finally, all at once, severs what a bodhisattva has until then deliberately left intact in order to be reborn again and again for the sake of beings.

18. Section: The First and Second Grounds' Delusions — Clinging to Person and Dharma, and the Ways of Karma

Namely: on the first ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of clinging to person and to dharmas; second, the delusion of the defilement belonging to the evil destinies — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the second ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of subtle, inadvertent transgression; second, the delusion of the various destinies of karma — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Chinese Source

謂於初地有二愚癡，一者、執著補特伽羅及法愚癡，二者、惡趣雜染愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。於第二地有二愚癡，一者、微細誤犯愚癡，二者、種種業趣愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely: on the first ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of clinging to person and to dharmas; second, the delusion of the defilement belonging to the evil destinies — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the second ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of subtle, inadvertent transgression; second, the delusion of the various destinies of karma — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first pair in detail: a practitioner not yet on the path of seeing still clings to a self and to dharmas, the first a form of the afflictive obstruction, the second of the cognitive; overcoming both discriminated forms of this clinging, and thereby realizing the selflessness of persons and of dharmas both apart from and within the aggregates, is precisely what carries a bodhisattva into the first ground. The second delusion, the defilement belonging to the evil destinies, names the karma and its ripened results tied to hell, hungry-ghost, and animal rebirth — technically not delusion itself but so named because it arises entirely from delusion's activity, exactly as explained above. A first-ground bodhisattva realizes the "pervading suchness" (so named, per the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, because it is disclosed equally by the twofold emptiness pervading every conditioned activity without exception) and thereby severs both delusions' seeds together with the incapacity they leave behind. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* offers an alternate gloss worth noting: read differently, these same two delusions correspond respectively to what is bound up with sharper and with duller obstructions — clinging to self and dharmas being the sharper, evil-destiny defilement the duller — a variant reading, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, that changes nothing about which two things are actually overcome. On the second ground, the delusion of subtle, inadvertent transgression names exactly the lapse of mindfulness already identified in the eleven-part division as what the first ground still lacked; the delusion of the various destinies of karma, citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own definition (juan 48), names a still-incomplete wisdom regarding the full workings of karma across every destiny and every vehicle — which specific actions, defiled or undefiled, lead to which specific ripened or habitually recurring results. A second-ground bodhisattva realizes the "most excellent suchness" (so named because the very nature of moral discipline it discloses is itself supreme, transforming what were once the stains of broken precepts into boundless virtue) and thereby severs both of these delusions' seeds together with their coarseness as well — the *Cheng Weishi Lun* naming what is overcome here, taken as a whole, the obstruction of deviant conduct.
