Fascicle 78 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十八 · The Bodhisattva Ground, Determined

Avalokiteśvara Asks: How Many Kinds of Purity Do the Ten Grounds Contain?

This is the seventh part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground. With the Chapter Distinguishing Yoga now closed in the previous fascicle — Maitreya's twenty-six questions on how calm-abiding and insight, practiced as one indivisible path, carry a bodhisattva from an ordinary being's first resolve to unsurpassed enlightenment — the Chapter on Excellent Qualities' citation of the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra turns to its sixth topic: the Chapter Distinguishing the Ground-Pāramitās. Where the previous chapter's "resolute practice" (勝解行) concerned a bodhisattva still on the grounds of ordinary conviction, this chapter's "true practice" (如實行) concerns the ten grounds themselves — Utmost Joy through Cloud of Dharma, and the Buddha ground besides. It opens with a new questioner, the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, asking how many kinds of purity these eleven grounds contain, and how many parts each is divided into — the Buddha's answer beginning with a fourfold purity (superior intention, precepts, mind, and wisdom) that maps onto the grounds in turn.

1. Section: Avalokiteśvara's Question — How Many Kinds of Purity Do the Eleven Grounds Contain?

Further, in dependence on the vehicle provisionally established, distinguishing and explaining in full the true Mahāyāna practice, it should be known exactly as the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra describes: the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara said to the Buddha: World-Honored One! As the Buddha has taught, bodhisattvas have ten grounds — namely Utmost Joy, Free from Defilement, Emitter of Light, Blazing with Wisdom, Utmost Difficulty to Conquer, Manifest Presence, Far-Reaching, Immovable, Excellent Wisdom, and Cloud of Dharma — and the Buddha ground is further spoken of as an eleventh. Of these grounds, how many kinds of purity do they contain? Into how many parts are they divided? The Buddha told the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara: Good sons! You should know that these grounds contain four kinds of purity, and are divided into eleven parts.

Chinese Source

復次，依乘假立，分別解說如實大乘，當知如解深密經中，觀自在菩薩白佛言：世尊！如佛所說菩薩十地，所謂極喜地、離垢地、發光地、焰慧地、極難勝地、現前地、遠行地、不動地、善慧地、法雲地，復說佛地為第十一。如是諸地，幾種清淨？幾分所攝？佛告觀自在菩薩曰：善男子！當知諸地，四種清淨，十一分攝。

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi opens fascicle 78 by placing it within the Decisive-Discussion chapter's larger structure: "the seventh part of the Determination of the Bodhisattva Ground" now moves from rebutting nihilistic emptiness and citing the Saṃdhinirmocana on ultimate truth, mind, the three natures, and the practice of yoga (fascicles 75 through 77) to a new citation, the Chapter Distinguishing the Ground-Pāramitās, unpacking how the ten pāramitās map onto the ten grounds. He explains "vehicle" (乘) as a means of conveyance carrying beings from the shore of birth-and-death to the shore of nirvāṇa, and glosses pāramitā itself through four traditional renderings — reaching the far shore, a deed brought to its utmost completion without limit, and Master Yìnshùn's twofold gloss distinguishing pāramitā as "a task perfectly accomplished" (its static sense) from pāramitā as "the path and method by which that accomplishment is reached" (its dynamic sense), two readings of a single word rather than two different things. He introduces the new questioner at length: Avalokiteśvara, who appears for the first time in this treatise's citations, already attained buddhahood in a past inconceivable eon as the Tathāgata Right Dharma Brightness, and who, out of great compassion, turned back from that attainment to appear as a bodhisattva, tirelessly ferrying beings across both this present world and Amitābha's Pure Land, destined to attain buddhahood there once more in the future as the Tathāgata King of the Universally Radiant Merit Mountain and to succeed Amitābha in guiding that land's beings — presently manifesting as an eighth-ground bodhisattva, acting effortlessly and abiding in signlessness, compassion and wisdom perfectly joined within, the three karmic activities perfectly observed without. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the deliberate shift in questioner and register: where Maitreya's twenty-six questions concerned "resolute practice" (勝解行), the still-aspirational cultivation proper to a bodhisattva before the grounds, Avalokiteśvara's questions concern "true practice" (如實行), the actual, realized cultivation of a bodhisattva already standing on the grounds themselves — the first oriented mainly toward one's own inward cultivation, the second toward the skillful outward work of guiding others. The Buddha's opening answer names two frameworks that will structure everything to follow: a fourfold purity (superior intention, precepts, mind, and wisdom) spanning the eleven grounds, and an elevenfold division within which each single ground itself contains both a portion already perfected and a portion still to be perfected as one advances toward the next.

2. Section: The Fourfold Purity — Superior Intention, Precepts, Mind, and Wisdom

How do the four kinds of purity contain the grounds? Namely: the purity of superior intention contains the first ground; the purity of superior precepts contains the second ground; the purity of superior mind contains the third ground; and the purity of superior wisdom, becoming ever more excellent and sublime ground after subsequent ground, should be known to contain the fourth ground up through the Buddha ground.

云何四種清淨能攝諸地？謂增上意樂清淨，攝於初地；增上戒清淨，攝第二地；增上心清淨，攝第三地；增上慧清淨，於後後地轉勝妙故，當知能攝從第四地乃至佛地。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do the four kinds of purity contain the grounds? Namely: the purity of superior intention contains the first ground; the purity of superior precepts contains the second ground; the purity of superior mind contains the third ground; and the purity of superior wisdom, becoming ever more excellent and sublime ground after subsequent ground, should be known to contain the fourth ground up through the Buddha ground.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "superior" (增上), citing the Śrāvaka-bhūmi (juan 28), as carrying the sense of both a destination aimed at and a supremacy attained — "superior" because it is what practice tends toward, and superior because what it tends toward is itself supreme. "Intention" (意樂, Skt. āśaya) he traces to a root sense of a resting-place or dwelling, hence also rendered aspiration or resolve — whatever the mind inclines toward wanting to do. Citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 37), he defines it as resting on a settled, unshakeable confidence — in one's own discerning reflection on the Dharma, in the Buddha's own well-taught teaching and discipline (which cannot be argued away), and in deep trust of what others have already realized — while Vasubandhu's commentary on the Mahāyānasaṃgraha reduces its essence to two components, aspiration (a wish to attain) and resolve (a settled conviction), toward the Three Jewels and toward benefiting self and other alike. "Purity" itself he defines simply as freedom from the afflictive and cognitive obstructions: intention purified enough to directly realize suchness, from the first ground up through the ninth, is called a bodhisattva's "pure intention," while intention purified to its utmost, on the tenth ground and the Buddha ground, is called "well-purified intention." Master Cháng Bǎi then surveys, at length, Master Woncheuk's tenfold gloss on what "superior intention" specifically names on the first ground — drawn from the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 48) and organized, via the Avataṃsaka-sūtra commentarial tradition, around the first ground's traditional ten great vows: reverence toward one's teachers without deceit (paired with the vow to make offerings to all buddhas); patient, gentle ease living among fellow practitioners (paired with the vow to protect the true Dharma); mastery over affliction and the works of Māra (paired with the vow to demonstrate the Buddha's own career in the world); clear insight into the faults of all conditioned activity (paired with the vow to embrace every wholesome practice); a clear vision of great nirvāṇa's own benefit (paired with the vow to bring all beings to maturity); tireless application to the excellent factors of awakening (paired with the vow to manifest in every world); delight in seclusion as the natural accompaniment of that application (paired with the vow to purify every buddha-land); indifference to worldly rank, gain, and honor however tainted (paired with the vow that all bodhisattvas share a single aim); turning away from the lesser vehicles toward the greater one alone (paired with the vow that no effort go to waste); and the wish to bring benefit to every single being (paired with the vow to swiftly realize enlightenment) — each of the ten, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, also perfecting a corresponding quality of directness and integrity of mind (an unbending, gentle, tempered, wholesome, tranquil, genuine, undivided, unresentful, resolute, or magnanimous directness) as it reaches its own full ripeness. He treats this dense tenfold correspondence as illustrative rather than exhaustive — a template, borrowed from the *Avataṃsaka*'s own account of the first ground, for how each of the eleven grounds' governing purity will unfold from here: superior intention perfecting the first ground alone, superior precepts (already treated at length in this treatise's own account of the bodhisattva's threefold discipline) perfecting the second, superior mind (concentration free of any further backsliding) perfecting the third, and superior wisdom — refining itself ever further, ground after ground, with no single new name required — perfecting every ground from the fourth all the way to buddhahood itself.

3. Section: The Eleven Parts — Utmost Joy, What Is Perfected and What Remains

Good sons! You should know that these four kinds of purity comprehensively contain all the grounds. How do the eleven parts contain the grounds? Namely: bodhisattvas who, while still on the ground of resolute practice, have through the ten practices of the Dharma most excellently cultivated the forbearance of resolute conviction, thereby surpass that ground and enter the bodhisattva's correct nature apart from birth. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to abide with correct awareness amid the current arising of subtle, inadvertent transgressions. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

善男子！當知如是四種清淨普攝諸地。云何十一種分能攝諸地？謂諸菩薩先於勝解行地，依十法行極善修習勝解忍故，超過彼地，證入菩薩正性離生。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能於微細毀犯誤現行中正知而住。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Good sons! You should know that these four kinds of purity comprehensively contain all the grounds. How do the eleven parts contain the grounds? Namely: bodhisattvas who, while still on the ground of resolute practice, have through the ten practices of the Dharma most excellently cultivated the forbearance of resolute conviction, thereby surpass that ground and enter the bodhisattva's correct nature apart from birth. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to abide with correct awareness amid the current arising of subtle, inadvertent transgressions. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi first closes the fourfold-purity account: though every ground from the fourth up through buddhahood technically possesses all four purities together, each of the first three grounds is named for whichever purity is most distinctively its own — intention for the first, precepts for the second, mind for the third — with each later ground inheriting every quality the earlier grounds already established, while the earlier grounds have not yet perfected what the later ones will add. He also cross-references the Decisive-Discussion chapter's own fourfold "abiding" scheme (juan 79) — utmost joy, superior precepts, superior mind, superior wisdom — mapping the same structure onto four accomplishments a bodhisattva achieves by relying on them in turn: never abandoning the great bodhi-resolve, steadily increasing one's own wealth and virtue toward the day of enlightenment, being able to return to the desire realm by the power of vow without being stained by its afflictions, and — through mastery of both what can and cannot be verbally established — swiftly realizing a buddha's own wisdom without waiting on any further stage or any other's instruction. The second framework, the elevenfold division, then examines each of the eleven grounds (the ten plus the Buddha ground) in turn through a fixed pattern: what portion is already fully perfected (which is to say, what qualified entry into this very ground) and what portion remains unperfected (which is to say, precisely what must still be cultivated to advance to the next). On the first ground, Utmost Joy, what is already perfected is the forbearance of resolute conviction won through the ten practices of engaging the Dharma cultivated during the stage of resolute practice — writing out the scriptures, making offerings to them, giving them to others, listening to them read, delighting in reading them oneself, silently receiving and retaining them, reciting them aloud, expounding them to others, reflecting on their meaning through the four principles of reasoning, and finally cultivating calm-abiding and insight upon them directly — the first eight belonging to the wisdom born of hearing, the ninth to the wisdom born of reflection, and the tenth to the wisdom born of cultivation; mastering all ten to the point of unshakeable conviction is what carries a bodhisattva past the stage of resolute practice into the correct nature apart from birth, whether "correct nature" is read as the very substance of the noble path or as nirvāṇa itself, and "apart from birth" as freedom from the discriminated afflictive and cognitive obstructions — what the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-sūtra* (juan 468) calls freedom from "attainment" itself, since ordinary clinging to gain breeds affliction while a mind with nothing to attain breeds none, and what the same sūtra (juan 484) calls freedom even from "Dharma-attachment," the subtle clinging to the very teachings one has used to counteract affliction, dissolved only when the wisdom realizing the emptiness of both self and dharmas actually manifests. What remains unperfected on this same first ground, however, is that a bodhisattva here still lacks the constant, correct awareness needed to avoid subtle, wholly inadvertent transgressions of the precepts — not deliberate wrongdoing, but a lapse of mindfulness, as when one unknowingly steps on an ant — and it is precisely this residual gap that the second ground exists to close.

4. Section: Free from Defilement — Precepts Perfected, Concentration and Dhāraṇī Still to Come

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain the mundane, fully perfected states of concentrated absorption and meditative attainment, nor the fully perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能得世間圓滿等持、等至，及圓滿聞持陀羅尼。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to attain the mundane, fully perfected states of concentrated absorption and meditative attainment, nor the fully perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent further cultivation of exactly the correct awareness the first ground still lacked is what perfects the second ground, Free from Defilement: a bodhisattva here genuinely never transgresses the precepts, whether deliberately or through an inadvertent lapse of mindfulness. What remains unperfected, though, is twofold: the mundane, fully stabilized states of concentrated absorption (samādhi, spanning everything from the threshold concentration up through the four dhyānas and four formless attainments) and meditative attainment (samāpatti, the deeper equipoise from the first dhyāna upward) are not yet secure against regression; and the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard — one of the four dhāraṇīs the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 45) describes, alongside dhāraṇī retaining meaning, retaining mantra, and retaining the very forbearance realized through the other three — is not yet fully perfected either. Master Cháng Bǎi explains dhāraṇī broadly as a kind of total retention: holding all dharmas within a single dharma, all words within a single word, all meanings within a single meaning, so that a bodhisattva who has attained it can hear teachings never before encountered and retain them across any span of time without forgetting, becoming capable of fearless, unhindered teaching precisely because nothing already learned is ever again lost. It is this same dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard — the ability to hold the Dharma's own words and letters in memory without limit — whose remaining incompleteness marks exactly what a bodhisattva must still perfect to advance from the second ground to the third.

5. Section: Emitter of Light — Dhāraṇī Perfected, Craving for Concentration Still Remains

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to dwell in extensive cultivation of the factors of awakening they have attained, and their minds are not yet able to relinquish craving for meditative attainments and craving for the Dharma. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Ru-Yi Translation

Yet they are not yet able to dwell in extensive cultivation of the factors of awakening they have attained, and their minds are not yet able to relinquish craving for meditative attainments and craving for the Dharma. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Further diligent cultivation perfects the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard, carrying a bodhisattva into the third ground, Emitter of Light — so named because the light of concentration and total retention now illuminates the Dharma one has heard and practiced, or, on another reading, because an inwardly purified mind is itself what emits this light, hence its alternate name, the abode of superior mind. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Sub-commentary*'s gloss on precisely what "fully perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard" means: a bodhisattva who has attained this power of mindfulness and wisdom can hear teachings never before encountered, unfamiliar in their very words, scattered across an immeasurable number of scriptures compiled in every order and every arrangement, spoken directly or indirectly, and retain every one of them across any span of time without forgetting — a single hearing yielding a thousand realizations, the very meaning of "total retention." What remains unperfected on this third ground, however, is twofold: a bodhisattva here is not yet able to dwell in extensive, sustained cultivation of the thirty-seven factors of awakening already attained (the four foundations of mindfulness and the rest), and the mind has not yet relinquished two subtle cravings — craving for the meditative attainments themselves, and craving even for the Dharma-dhāraṇī just perfected. It is precisely the relinquishing of these two lingering attachments, to concentration and to the Dharma one has mastered, that the fourth ground exists to accomplish.

6. Section: Blazing with Wisdom — The Factors of Awakening Perfected, the Truths Still Only Partly Seen

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the principle of the truths as it truly is; nor are they yet able to relinquish the intention that turns exclusively away from saṃsāra and toward nirvāṇa; nor are they yet able to cultivate the factors of awakening embraced by skillful means. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能於諸諦道理如實觀察，又未能於生死涅槃棄捨一向背趣作意，又未能修方便所攝菩提分法。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the principle of the truths as it truly is; nor are they yet able to relinquish the intention that turns exclusively away from saṃsāra and toward nirvāṇa; nor are they yet able to cultivate the factors of awakening embraced by skillful means. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent, extensive cultivation of the thirty-seven factors of awakening, together with the relinquishing of craving for concentration and for the Dharma, perfects the third ground's remaining portion and carries a bodhisattva into the fourth, Blazing with Wisdom — named because wisdom now burns like a flame vigorous and unbroken enough to consume the very firewood of affliction, illuminating the true teaching; its alternate name, the abode of superior wisdom joined with the factors of awakening, names the same thing. What remains unperfected here is threefold: a bodhisattva has not yet observed the four noble truths, or equally the ultimate and conventional truths, with full, penetrating clarity; has not yet relinquished the still-lingering intention that turns exclusively away from saṃsāra toward nirvāṇa; and has not yet cultivated the factors of awakening as embraced by skillful means — meaning not the factors themselves but the further expedient means that support and complete their cultivation. It is precisely these three gaps that the fifth ground exists to close.

7. Section: Utmost Difficulty to Conquer — Three Skillful Means, and the Buddha Who Bathed a Sick Monk

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the flow of saṃsāra as it truly is, and, because they still generate considerable weariness toward it, are not yet able to dwell extensively in the intention toward the signless. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Chinese Source

為令此分得圓滿故，精勤修習便能證得。彼諸菩薩由是因緣，此分圓滿。而未能於生死流轉如實觀察，又由於彼多生厭故，未能多住無相作意。由是因緣，於此分中猶未圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

In order to bring this portion to full perfection, through diligent cultivation they are able to realize it. Because of this, that portion, for these bodhisattvas, is fully perfected. Yet they are not yet able to observe the flow of saṃsāra as it truly is, and, because they still generate considerable weariness toward it, are not yet able to dwell extensively in the intention toward the signless. Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent cultivation of the three remaining gaps carries a bodhisattva into the fifth ground, Utmost Difficulty to Conquer, whose difficulty and whose conquest Master Cháng Bǎi glosses through the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* (juan 3): mundane and supramundane wisdom are, by nature, opposed — the one bearing characteristics and grasping at attainment, the other signless and without attainment — yet a fifth-ground bodhisattva succeeds in harmonizing the two so that the mundane simply is the supramundane, affliction simply is bodhi, and saṃsāra simply is nirvāṇa, an achievement so difficult that conquering it earns the ground its name. The observation of the four noble truths, or equally the ultimate and conventional truths, is perfected by direct, unobstructed insight into their mutual non-opposition; the exclusive turning away from saṃsāra is overcome by directly seeing that saṃsāra simply is nirvāṇa; and the factors of awakening embraced by skillful means are perfected through what the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 36) names as the great expedient means for realizing a buddha's own wisdom — mastery of emptiness itself, the direct understanding that the imagined nature is empty while the dependent and perfected natures, apart from mere verbal designation, genuinely exist. Citing the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*, Master Cháng Bǎi details the three skillful means embraced here: the practice of never being satisfied with merit accumulated, illustrated by the Buddha's own famous conduct — despite being fully perfected in blessing and wisdom, the Buddha still personally threaded a needle for a blind elderly monk who could no longer sew his own robes, and personally bathed a gravely ill young monk unable to rise from his bed, telling the astonished Aniruddha that no one in the world seeks merit more tirelessly than the Tathāgata, who is never satisfied in giving, instruction, patience, teaching by word and by meaning, protecting beings, and seeking the unsurpassed true path; the practice of tireless diligence in seeking the wisdom of prajñāpāramitā that has nothing to attain, contrasted with an ordinary being's habitual grasping after gain; and the practice of tireless cultivation of great loving-kindness and compassion, so that concern for others' benefit never wearies. What remains unperfected on this fifth ground, however, is that a bodhisattva here still cannot observe the very flow of dependent arising through birth-and-death — ignorance conditioning volitional formations, and so on through aging-and-death — with complete clarity, and, still generating considerable weariness toward it, cannot yet dwell extensively in the signless intention that the sixth ground will bring to completion.

Because of this, that portion, in this respect, is not yet fully perfected.

Diligent cultivation of the fifth ground's remaining gap — clear observation of saṃsāra's own flow, together with the weariness that blocked extensive dwelling in the signless — carries a bodhisattva into the sixth ground, Manifest Presence, so named because prajñā's own wisdom now stands directly present before the mind, no longer merely a matter for exclusive weariness but observed exactly as it is, dwelling extensively (though not yet perfectly without gap or interruption) in the signless intention. What remains unperfected here is precisely that residual gap and interruption: even a sixth-ground bodhisattva's signless contemplation is not yet entirely free of the least momentary lapse, nor entirely free of conventional, characteristic-bearing thought occasionally intruding upon it — a flaw the seventh ground, Far-Reaching, exists to correct, sitting as it does at the very boundary between effortful and effortless practice, the furthest reach a bodhisattva can attain while still requiring some exertion to sustain the signless without interruption. Even here, though, two things remain unperfected: a seventh-ground bodhisattva can dwell continuously in signless samādhi only through continued, deliberate exertion, not yet effortlessly; and mastery over characteristics themselves — the free, effortless functioning appropriate to benefiting both oneself and others amid conditioned appearances — is not yet complete. Both gaps together are exactly what the eighth ground will finally close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Diligent cultivation of the seventh ground's two remaining gaps — effortless persistence in the signless, and mastery over characteristic-bearing appearances — carries a bodhisattva into the eighth ground, Immovable, so named because nothing, not even affliction itself, can any longer disturb an effortless activity no longer requiring exertion to sustain. What remains unperfected here concerns the four kinds of unhindered eloquence: mastery of synonymous expression (unhindered understanding of the Dharma's own words and terms), of manifold characteristics (unhindered understanding of the many meanings those words convey), of etymological explanation (unhindered ability to gloss and re-gloss a single term — suchness, true nature, the dharma-realm, reality, the limit of reality, the signless realm, the realm free of all discursive elaboration — through any number of different formulations), and of teaching itself in every possible arrangement (unhindered, spontaneous eloquence, saying whatever needs saying in whatever way it needs saying) — none of these four is yet fully mastered. It is precisely this fourfold eloquence that the ninth ground, Excellent Wisdom, brings to completion, so that a ninth-ground bodhisattva can teach the Dharma with total freedom in any form whatsoever; citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's parallel fourfold scheme (juan 43 and 45) — wisdom regarding the Dharma's synonyms, wisdom regarding its characteristics, wisdom regarding its verbal explanation, and wisdom regarding its manifold kinds — Master Cháng Bǎi confirms these are simply the same four unhindered forms of eloquence under a different name. What remains unperfected even on the ninth ground, however, is the direct, personally realized experience of the fully perfected Dharma-body itself — a completion reserved for the tenth ground alone.

Diligent cultivation of the ninth ground's remaining gap carries a bodhisattva into the tenth ground, Cloud of Dharma: the Dharma-body, like a great cloud filling all of space, is now fully perfected — though Master Cháng Bǎi notes that even this ground's fullness still falls short of a buddha's own, whose perfected Dharma-body is called nothing less than fully "accomplished." What remains unperfected even here is the unattached, unobstructed wondrous knowledge and vision with respect to absolutely every knowable object: unattached in the sense of being free of the afflictive obstruction, unobstructed in the sense of being free of the cognitive obstruction, since a tenth-ground bodhisattva has not yet entirely severed the most subtle remainder of either. Only at the Buddha ground, the eleventh and final part, is this last gap closed: diligent cultivation brings the unattached, unobstructed wondrous wisdom and vision itself, together with omniscience, to full completion, and because this eleventh and highest part is perfected, every one of the preceding ten parts is thereby fully and simultaneously perfected as well — buddhahood being not one more portion added alongside the rest, but the single completion in which all eleven are gathered up together. With this, the elevenfold division brought to its close, Master Cháng Bǎi notes the treatise's own summary line still to come: these eleven parts, taken together, comprehensively contain the entire scope of all eleven grounds, closing the first of the eight sections making up this chapter's account of the ground-pāramitās — the section on what each of the eleven grounds contains.

Ru-Yi Translation

Good sons! You should know that these eleven parts comprehensively contain all the grounds. World-Honored One! Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

With the eleven-part division closed, Master Cháng Bǎi notes this completes the first of eight sections making up the chapter's account of the ground-pāramitās — what each ground contains — and Avalokiteśvara now moves to the second section, the meaning behind each ground's name. "Great benefit" is glossed as the virtue of simultaneously accomplishing benefit for oneself and for others — an achievement ordinary beings find perpetually at odds, since pursuing one's own interest and pursuing another's typically pull in opposite directions, but which a first-ground bodhisattva, once joined with non-discriminating wisdom, is able to accomplish together at every moment; "an outward-transcending mind never attained before" names this very non-discriminating wisdom itself, free of the characteristic-bearing, discriminating quality that mundane wisdom always carries. Possessing both — the accomplishment of others' and one's own benefit together, and wisdom never before tasted — a first-ground bodhisattva generates such joy that whatever is done is done in accord with the Dharma, hence the name Utmost Joy. Master Cháng Bǎi surveys several parallel explanations from the *Sub-commentary* (juan 8) and elsewhere — the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s verse on a bodhisattva who has just ascended the one-vehicle's signless path; the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā-śāstra*'s simpler gloss, that a bodhisattva here first tastes the flavor of wholesome dharmas and so is filled with joy; and the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s parallel formula, newly gaining the noble nature, realizing the twofold emptiness, and thereby benefiting both self and other — before turning to the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own extended, threefold catalogue of this ground's joy: nine kinds arising directly (reverent joy toward the Three Jewels, loving joy in contemplating suchness, joy at one's own attainment measured against what came before, joy in bodily and mental benefit fully accomplished, joy in that benefit's further increase, joy at how near enlightenment now feels, joy in an unshaken mind unruffled by debate, joy in a gentle, compassionate mind even while teaching others, and joy in patient forbearance even when others fail to practice as taught); ten kinds of joyful recollection of what is yet to be attained (recalling the buddhas, their teaching, the great bodhisattvas, their practice, the pure quality of the pāramitās, the surpassing excellence of the bodhisattva grounds, the irreversibility of a bodhisattva's power, the Tathāgatas' own work of teaching, the benefit one is able to bring to beings, and entry into every Tathāgata's own wisdom-conduct); and ten kinds of joyful recollection of what is already attained (having turned away from every worldly entanglement, having drawn near to the Tathāgatas, having become far removed from the ordinary being's own ground, having drawn near to the ground of wisdom, having cut off every evil destiny, having become a refuge for all beings, having drawn near to seeing every buddha, having been born into the buddhas' own domain, having entered the suchness common to every bodhisattva, and having become free of every fear and every trembling) — a joy, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, so thoroughly catalogued precisely because it marks the single most decisive turning point in the entire bodhisattva path, the moment ordinary being first becomes noble one.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the second ground's name follows directly from what the eleven-part division already established: a first-ground bodhisattva still lapses, without intending to, into subtle transgressions of the precepts through a momentary loss of mindfulness, while a second-ground bodhisattva is free even of these, hence Free from Defilement, the defilement in question being specifically the stain of broken precepts. He surveys the same parallel-text pattern as before — the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s verse on this ground's bodhisattva, styled a "world-transcending noble one" appearing across a thousand lands; the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own gloss, that leaving behind stinginess, envy, and the mental stains that break the precepts through cultivating giving and pure discipline is what earns the name; the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s formula, that this ground is named for its utmost freedom from the stain of broken precepts, its precepts now naturally perfected rather than merely guarded through deliberate reflection as on the first ground; and the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā-śāstra*'s simpler reading, that practicing the ten wholesome courses of action is itself what leaves every stain behind. The third ground's name, Emitter of Light, follows just as directly: the concentration attained there emits the light of cultivation-wisdom, while the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard emits the light of hearing- and reflection-wisdom — the same fourfold dhāraṇī (retaining the Dharma, retaining its meaning, retaining mantra, and retaining the very forbearance realized through the other three) already introduced at the second ground's own account of what remained unperfected there, now become the basis for an immeasurable light of wisdom. Master Cháng Bǎi again surveys the parallel translations and glosses — "the ground of brightness" in the *Sub-commentary*'s citation of two other translations of the sūtra, "wisdom-brightness" in the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s own verse, and the *Suvarṇaprabhāsa-sūtra*'s note that concentration unshakeable by anything, joined with the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard as its very root, is precisely why the third ground bears the name it does — closing this pair of etymologies exactly where the eleven-part division itself left off, with the second and third grounds' defining accomplishments now explained in full.

Master Cháng Bǎi surveys, as before, a wide range of parallel translations and commentaries for each name — the *Sūtra of the Benevolent King*'s verses styling the fourth ground "blazing wisdom, wondrous light, great diligence" and the fifth "supreme wisdom"; the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own gloss that the fourth ground's wisdom-light burns day and night without ceasing, like a jewel whose radiance no other light extinguishes; the *Daśabhūmi-vibhāṣā-śāstra*'s reading that this wisdom burns the firewood of afflictions the practitioner is not even aware of, since innate afflictions arise so readily that wisdom must be constantly, automatically vigilant; and the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s formula for the fifth, that mundane wisdom (skillful means grasping conventional truth) and supramundane wisdom (the very factors of awakening realizing ultimate truth) are by nature opposed, one bearing characteristics and one signless, and joining what by nature resists joining is precisely what makes this ground so supremely difficult to conquer — a difficulty the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra* glosses two further ways as well: the difficulty of tirelessly teaching beings without being troubled by it, and the difficulty of remaining untroubled even when beings refuse the teaching offered, both difficulties a fifth-ground bodhisattva has learned to overcome. The *Sub-commentary*'s own formula draws the two names together: this ground cultivates, by means of the factors of awakening already attained, the resolute understanding of emptiness itself — that the imagined nature is not existent while the wordless dependent and perfected natures are not non-existent — and it is the sheer difficulty of holding worldly and ultimate wisdom together in a single, unified understanding that earns the ground both its difficulty and, once mastered, its name of conquest.

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies two wisdoms now present on the sixth ground: subsequently attained wisdom, directly observing the flow of dependent arising through ignorance, formations, and onward to aging-and-death; and root wisdom, the signless attention toward the sevenfold suchness now genuinely manifesting rather than merely being approached. He cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s formula that reliance on the wisdom of dependent arising is what lets prajñāpāramitā stand constantly present, and the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra*'s gloss that this ground's wisdom neither dwells in saṃsāra nor in nirvāṇa — noting, with a touch of humility, that every earlier ground already involved some form of "presence" too, this ground simply being singled out for how fully and freely that presence is now realized. The seventh ground's name, Far-Reaching, carries three senses of "far," per the *Sub-commentary*: temporally far, since two of the three great incalculable eons of practice are now nearly complete; far in effort, since this is the very outer limit of effortful practice before the effortless practice of the eighth ground begins; and far in attainment, since a seventh-ground bodhisattva has journeyed far beyond both the impure world and the two lesser vehicles' own characteristic-bound practice, arriving at the very border of the "pure ground" that follows. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s own formula — this ground reaches the very final edge of effortful activity — and Vasubandhu's gloss that though no characteristic-bearing appearance can any longer disturb a seventh-ground bodhisattva, sustaining the signless still counts, technically, as an "activity," the last such activity before the eighth ground's activity-free abiding begins.

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the eighth ground earns its name specifically through what is left untroubled rather than what is newly attained: signless wisdom now operates entirely without exertion, so that neither the subtle characteristic-appearances of the dependent nature nor any currently active affliction can any longer disturb it — the *Bahulū Abhidharma Commentary* citing this as the point at which a bodhisattva finally "relinquishes the storehouse [name]," the eighth consciousness ceasing to be called the ālaya and becoming instead simply the maturation consciousness, since not even the subtlest trace of self-clinging remains to give the storehouse-name its meaning. He cites the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*'s twofold reading — neither the true, signless object nor the conventional, characteristic-bearing object can any longer move an eighth-ground bodhisattva's mind — and the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s formula that non-discriminating wisdom now continues effortlessly of its own accord, unmoved by characteristic, function, or affliction alike. The ninth ground, Excellent Wisdom, is named for the four kinds of unhindered eloquence now fully mastered — teaching, per the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra*'s vivid illustration, so that a single instant's utterance can simultaneously answer every different question, in every different language, posed by every different kind of being (human, heavenly, or otherwise) across a thousand million worlds, resolving every doubt at once. Master Cháng Bǎi notes the *Sub-commentary*'s gloss that "immeasurable" here also carries the sense of "faultless" — an eloquence with nothing left to correct — closing this pair of names exactly where the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own account (juan 48) of the eighth and ninth grounds already anticipated them.

Because the body of coarseness is as vast as space, and the Dharma-body, now fully perfected, is like a great cloud able to cover it entirely, the tenth is called Cloud of Dharma. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses this dual image plainly: an ordinary being's coarseness — the residue of the afflictive and cognitive obstructions still weighing on body and mind — is as vast and pervasive as empty space itself, yet the tenth ground's now-perfected Dharma-body, likened to a great cloud, is able to cover that entire expanse and hold it in check. He surveys the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s influential threefold gloss: the great cloud of Dharma-wisdom holds within it every merit as a cloud holds water (the totality-comprehending wisdom of this ground, together with its dhāraṇī- and samādhi-gates); it covers over coarseness as a cloud covers the sky (concealing and cutting off the vast, space-like extent of the afflictive and cognitive obstructions); and it fills the Dharma-body as rain fills the earth (pouring forth boundless excellent qualities that fill what is realized and what it is based upon) — and cites the *Daśabhūmika-sūtra*'s own vivid image of this ground's bodhisattva as a great dragon-king raining down a Dharma-rain that only the ground itself, like the ocean alone among all bodies of water, is vast enough to receive without overflowing, its great compassion the cloud, its Dharma-teaching the thunder, its fearlessness the lightning, and its wisdom the wind that carries the rain to extinguish beings' afflictions everywhere at once. The eleventh and final name, the Buddha ground, is explained through what the *Buddhabhūmi-śāstra* calls its fivefold meaning — possessing both mundane and supramundane wisdom, free of both obstructions, penetrating both conventional and ultimate objects, awakened both for oneself and for others, and, freed from ignorance and craving alike, a mind liberated the way sleep gives way to waking and a lotus opens to the sun — and its threefold meaning of "ground": what a buddha relies upon (the pure dharma-realm), what a buddha's activity consists in (the four wisdoms — mirror-like, equality, wondrous-observing, and duty-fulfilling), and what a buddha's every quality is gathered up within. With this, the treatise's second of eight sections, the meaning behind each ground's name, closes entirely — completing, together with what each ground contains, the first two of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight-part structure.

Ru-Yi Translation

World-Honored One! Within these grounds, how many kinds of delusion, and how many kinds of coarseness, are counteracted? Good sons! Within these grounds, twenty-two kinds of delusion and eleven kinds of coarseness are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara now opens the third of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections — what each of the eleven grounds counteracts — with a question whose answer, twenty-two delusions and eleven kinds of coarseness (two delusions and one coarseness for each of the eleven grounds), is the very source Master Cháng Bǎi has already been quoting throughout fascicle 77's own account of the treating-obstructions door. Master Cháng Bǎi clarifies what "delusion" (愚) means here: primarily the cognitive obstruction, understood in two complementary ways — as the current activity of clinging to self and to dharmas directly, and, more broadly, as the karmic causes and ripened results that such clinging gives rise to, which are themselves not literally delusion but are so called because they arise entirely from delusion's own operation, exactly as the *Cheng Weishi Lun* (juan 9) explains at length: the first ground's two delusions correspond to what that treatise calls the obstruction of an ordinary being's own nature and to the karmic ripening bound up with the evil destinies, with "coarseness" naming either the dormant seeds of these two delusions or the incapacity they leave behind, comparable to how a meditative attainment is said to "cut off the root of suffering" even though no literal root, as a seed or a current activity, is what is directly severed. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* adds an important qualification worth carrying forward through this whole taxonomy: though what the first ground truly cuts off spans both obstructions, the delusion named "an ordinary being's own nature" is specifically keyed to the cognitive obstruction alone, since the ten forms of ignorance named throughout this eleven-ground scheme are, by definition, non-defiling in the technical sense a śrāvaka's own path also severs — the treatise is deliberately isolating what only a bodhisattva's path uniquely overcomes, ground by ground, leaving the afflictive obstruction itself to be finished only at the very threshold of buddhahood, when the diamond-like concentration finally, all at once, severs what a bodhisattva has until then deliberately left intact in order to be reborn again and again for the sake of beings.

18. Section: The First and Second Grounds' Delusions — Clinging to Person and Dharma, and the Ways of Karma

Namely: on the first ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of clinging to person and to dharmas; second, the delusion of the defilement belonging to the evil destinies — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the second ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of subtle, inadvertent transgression; second, the delusion of the various destinies of karma — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

謂於初地有二愚癡，一者、執著補特伽羅及法愚癡，二者、惡趣雜染愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。於第二地有二愚癡，一者、微細誤犯愚癡，二者、種種業趣愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

Namely: on the first ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of clinging to person and to dharmas; second, the delusion of the defilement belonging to the evil destinies — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the second ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of subtle, inadvertent transgression; second, the delusion of the various destinies of karma — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the first pair in detail: a practitioner not yet on the path of seeing still clings to a self and to dharmas, the first a form of the afflictive obstruction, the second of the cognitive; overcoming both discriminated forms of this clinging, and thereby realizing the selflessness of persons and of dharmas both apart from and within the aggregates, is precisely what carries a bodhisattva into the first ground. The second delusion, the defilement belonging to the evil destinies, names the karma and its ripened results tied to hell, hungry-ghost, and animal rebirth — technically not delusion itself but so named because it arises entirely from delusion's activity, exactly as explained above. A first-ground bodhisattva realizes the "pervading suchness" (so named, per the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*, because it is disclosed equally by the twofold emptiness pervading every conditioned activity without exception) and thereby severs both delusions' seeds together with the incapacity they leave behind. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* offers an alternate gloss worth noting: read differently, these same two delusions correspond respectively to what is bound up with sharper and with duller obstructions — clinging to self and dharmas being the sharper, evil-destiny defilement the duller — a variant reading, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, that changes nothing about which two things are actually overcome. On the second ground, the delusion of subtle, inadvertent transgression names exactly the lapse of mindfulness already identified in the eleven-part division as what the first ground still lacked; the delusion of the various destinies of karma, citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own definition (juan 48), names a still-incomplete wisdom regarding the full workings of karma across every destiny and every vehicle — which specific actions, defiled or undefiled, lead to which specific ripened or habitually recurring results. A second-ground bodhisattva realizes the "most excellent suchness" (so named because the very nature of moral discipline it discloses is itself supreme, transforming what were once the stains of broken precepts into boundless virtue) and thereby severs both of these delusions' seeds together with their coarseness as well — the *Cheng Weishi Lun* naming what is overcome here, taken as a whole, the obstruction of deviant conduct.

On the fourth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of craving for meditative attainment; second, the delusion of craving for the Dharma — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Chinese Source

於第三地有二愚癡，一者、欲貪愚癡，二者、圓滿聞持陀羅尼愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。於第四地有二愚癡，一者、等至愛愚癡，二者、法愛愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

On the third ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of desirous craving; second, the delusion obstructing the perfected dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the fourth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of craving for meditative attainment; second, the delusion of craving for the Dharma — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* names what the third ground overcomes the obstruction of dullness: desirous craving obstructs the superior uncontaminated concentration this ground requires, and the delusion obstructing the dhāraṇī of retaining what is heard obstructs the hearing-, reflection-, and cultivation-wisdom that same concentration and total retention give rise to — Master Cháng Bǎi noting that desirous craving itself is, from beginningless time, simply an offshoot of this very cognitive obstruction, so that once its root is severed the branch withers with it. A third-ground bodhisattva realizes the "most excellent flowing suchness" (so named because the teachings that flow from it are themselves most excellent, the light of hearing-, reflection-, and cultivation-wisdom flowing directly from this very suchness). The fourth ground's obstruction, which the *Cheng Weishi Lun* names the obstruction of subtle affliction currently arising, concerns the innate self-view and related afflictions still tied to the sixth consciousness — so faint that they arise without any deliberate intention and persist almost imperceptibly, hence "subtle." Craving for meditative attainment is simply craving for concentration itself, not yet released even after the third ground's stability is won; craving for the Dharma is the third ground's own lingering attachment to the dhāraṇī and teachings just mastered, since a bodhisattva who has endured every hardship to attain the Dharma naturally develops some fondness for it. Master Cháng Bǎi cites several commentarial glosses distinguishing this refined "Dharma-craving" — the compassionate wish that beings attain nirvāṇa, or love of wholesome teaching itself — from the ordinary craving of an unliberated being, quoting the *Nirvāṇa Sūtra*'s own contrast: "there are two kinds of craving — the craving of a hungry ghost, and Dharma-craving; one who is truly liberated is free of the hungry ghost's craving, but out of compassion for beings retains Dharma-craving, and such Dharma-craving is itself true liberation." A fourth-ground bodhisattva realizes the "suchness without appropriation" (so named because, once realized, the sixth consciousness's innate view of a body-based self is permanently subdued and no longer active, no longer appropriated by any notion of "I" or "mine").

On the sixth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of directly observing the flow of conditioned formations; second, the delusion of the frequent arising of characteristics — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Chinese Source

於第五地有二愚癡，一者、一向作意棄背生死愚癡，二者、一向作意趣向涅槃愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。於第六地有二愚癡，一者、現前觀察諸行流轉愚癡，二者、相多現行愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

On the fifth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of an intention turned exclusively toward abandoning saṃsāra; second, the delusion of an intention turned exclusively toward nirvāṇa — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the sixth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of directly observing the flow of conditioned formations; second, the delusion of the frequent arising of characteristics — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* names the fifth ground's obstruction the obstruction of entering nirvāṇa the way a lesser vehicle does: even a fourth-ground bodhisattva can still generate the pure wish to turn wholly away from saṃsāra's suffering and toward nirvāṇa's peace, exactly as a śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha does — a wish overcome only when a fifth-ground bodhisattva realizes the "undifferentiated suchness" (so named because ultimate and mundane wisdom, by nature mutually opposed, are here made to operate as one, the very difficulty that makes this ground so hard to conquer). A sixth-ground bodhisattva then overcomes two further delusions the *Cheng Weishi Lun* together names the obstruction of the frequent arising of coarse characteristics: first, still treating the observed flow of dependent arising — ignorance through aging-and-death — as a real, substantially existing defiled process; second, still clinging to the purified characteristic on the other side of that same process as though it too were substantially real, and consequently dwelling in characteristic-bearing contemplation far more than in the signless — precisely the excess a sixth-ground bodhisattva, realizing the "suchness free of both defilement and purity" (so named because this suchness's own nature is neither originally defiled nor subsequently purified, prajñā itself arising only once both extremes are relinquished), finally corrects, becoming able to abide extensively, though not yet perfectly, in signless attention.

On the eighth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of exerting effort with regard to the signless; second, the delusion of not yet having mastery with regard to characteristics — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Ru-Yi Translation

On the seventh ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of the arising of subtle characteristics; second, the delusion of the expedient means of an intention turned exclusively toward the signless — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the eighth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of exerting effort with regard to the signless; second, the delusion of not yet having mastery with regard to characteristics — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* names the seventh ground's obstruction the obstruction of the arising of subtle characteristics: a sixth-ground bodhisattva, even after overcoming the coarse marks of defilement and purity, still subtly clings to a residual arising-characteristic and a residual ceasing-characteristic within the very flow of dependent arising just observed, and consequently applies deliberate intention exclusively toward the signless rather than resting there without effort — both overcome only when a seventh-ground bodhisattva realizes the "suchness without distinction of dharmas" (so named because every different verbal teaching, however many names it uses, is understood to be no different from suchness itself, equally signless at root), reaching what this fascicle's own account of the treating-obstructions door already called the very outer limit of effortful practice. The eighth ground's obstruction, which the *Cheng Weishi Lun* names the obstruction of exerting effort within the signless, is exactly the residue that limit still carries: even effortless, continuous signless contemplation still technically requires exertion to sustain, and mastery over manifesting characteristic-bearing appearances (adorned forms, pure or impure buddha-lands) at will is not yet complete — both finally overcome when an eighth-ground bodhisattva realizes the "suchness of neither increase nor decrease" (so named because it is no longer affected by defilement's increase or purity's decrease, or the reverse), gaining the twin freedoms of manifesting appearances and manifesting lands entirely at will. Master Cháng Bǎi notes that from the eighth ground onward the afflictions of the three realms no longer arise at all in active function, though the seventh consciousness's own subtlest cognitive obstruction can still technically arise, since the eighth ground's realization of the emptiness of persons does not yet directly conflict with the seventh consciousness's remaining attachment to dharmas.

On the tenth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion regarding great supernormal powers; second, the delusion obstructing penetration of the subtle and secret — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

於第九地有二愚癡，一者、於無量說法、無量法句文字、後後慧辯陀羅尼自在愚癡，二者、辯才自在愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。於第十地有二愚癡，一者、大神通愚癡，二者、悟入微細祕密愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

On the ninth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion obstructing mastery of the dhāraṇī of boundless teaching, boundless words and letters, and ever-refining eloquence; second, the delusion obstructing mastery of eloquence itself — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted. On the tenth ground there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion regarding great supernormal powers; second, the delusion obstructing penetration of the subtle and secret — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The *Cheng Weishi Lun* names the ninth ground's obstruction the obstruction of an unwillingness to act for others' benefit: even an eighth-ground bodhisattva, fully capable now of benefiting beings, still leans toward cultivating only his own benefit and lacks full eagerness to labor for others' sake — a reluctance overcome, together with the first delusion covering mastery of the three related dhāraṇīs (meaning, words, and ever-refining eloquence, each capable of manifesting the whole within a single instance), when a ninth-ground bodhisattva realizes the "suchness that is the basis of mastery over wisdom" (so named because, once realized, one gains full mastery over the four kinds of unhindered eloquence and becomes able to expound the Dharma to any vessel skillfully and freely). The tenth ground's obstruction, which the *Cheng Weishi Lun* names the obstruction of not yet attaining mastery within the dharmas, is what still limits a ninth-ground bodhisattva even after every eloquence is mastered: the great cloud of totality-comprehending wisdom that the tenth ground brings, together with everything it holds — every dhāraṇī- and samādhi-gate, every supernormal activity for benefiting beings — remains not yet fully accessible. Overcoming this delusion regarding great supernormal powers (obstructing the manifest activity of benefiting others) and this delusion obstructing penetration of the subtle and secret (obstructing full access to that same great cloud of Dharma-wisdom and everything stored within it), a tenth-ground bodhisattva realizes the "suchness that is the basis of mastery over ten kinds of activity" (so named because, once realized, one gains complete mastery over supernormal power, karmic activity, total retention, and every gate of samādhi at once) — reaching, in every practical sense, the very threshold of buddhahood itself.

23. Section: The Buddha Ground's Delusions — Utmost Subtle Attachment and Obstruction, All Twenty-Two Now Closed

On the ground of the Tathāgata there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of an utmost subtle attachment with regard to every knowable object; second, the delusion of an utmost subtle obstruction — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Chinese Source

於如來地有二愚癡，一者、於一切所知境界極微細著愚癡，二者、極微細礙愚癡，及彼麤重為所對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

On the ground of the Tathāgata there are two kinds of delusion — first, the delusion of an utmost subtle attachment with regard to every knowable object; second, the delusion of an utmost subtle obstruction — together with the coarseness belonging to these, which are counteracted.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The *Sub-commentary* glosses the two final delusions through their opposite: to bring a knowable object to mind and have wisdom respond instantly, without the least hesitation, is called "unattached"; to have that wisdom operate without any obstruction whatsoever, across every realm, every matter, every category, and every time, is called "unobstructed" — attachment and obstruction being simply their negation. Even a tenth-ground bodhisattva, the *Cheng Weishi Lun* explains, still carries an innate remainder of subtle cognitive obstruction and a dormant, functionally inactive seed of afflictive obstruction, both severed all at once only when the diamond-like concentration manifests directly before buddhahood itself — the *Abhidharmasamuccaya*'s own formula that at the very moment of enlightenment, afflictive and cognitive obstructions are both instantly severed together, arhatship and buddhahood, nirvāṇa and bodhi, realized as one. With this, the entire structure of twenty-two delusions and eleven kinds of coarseness — two delusions and one coarseness distinguished for every one of the eleven grounds — closes in full, and Master Cháng Bǎi appends the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s own summary of exactly when each obstruction is actually severed. Of the afflictive obstruction: its discriminated seeds are cut the instant non-discriminating wisdom first arises on the first ground's true path of seeing (not by the subsequently attained wisdom of the corresponding path); its innate seeds are cut only when the diamond-like concentration manifests, though a bodhisattva may deliberately allow some affliction to arise through the first seven grounds — never actually transgressing because of it — purely in order to remain reborn for the sake of beings, ceasing even this deliberate allowance from the eighth ground onward. Of the cognitive obstruction: its discriminated seeds and manifest activity are likewise cut on the first ground, while its innate seeds are cut gradually, ground by ground, across the ten grounds, with the portion tied to the sixth consciousness ceasing to manifest from the eighth ground onward and the portion tied to the seventh consciousness's own attachment to dharmas persisting until the very moment of the diamond-like concentration. The *Cheng Weishi Lun* further distinguishes three successive stages at which each obstruction's own coarseness — as opposed to its seeds outright — is progressively thinned: for the afflictive obstruction, the first ground (cutting its upper and middle grades), the stage of certainty (cutting its lower grade), and the diamond-like stage (cutting the last habitual traces of all three grades together); for the cognitive obstruction, likened to skin, flesh, and marrow, the path of seeing (skin), the eighth ground (flesh), and the Buddha ground (marrow) — three stages singled out for special mention, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, even though the thinning itself in fact continues at every single ground along the way. He closes by observing that this entire twenty-two-delusion structure is best read as the direct mirror image of the eleven grounds' own names just given: where naming the grounds described the virtue attained at each stage, counteracting their delusions describes the very same progress from its opposite face — the affliction and cognitive obstruction left behind at each step being nothing other than what that stage's own virtue has, at last, overcome.

World-Honored One! Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha closes the third of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections by naming what the whole twenty-two-delusion, eleven-coarseness structure was for: these are precisely what distinguish one ground from the next, and unsurpassed enlightenment itself is nothing other than the state finally free of every one of these bonds. Avalokiteśvara responds with unprompted praise — delusion pictured as a net cast layer upon layer over every being, its dormant seeds pictured as a dense, nearly impassable thicket — marveling that enlightenment alone is what breaks the net and clears the thicket entirely. Master Cháng Bǎi notes how naturally this praise arises here: having just walked through the whole grinding, ground-by-ground labor of severing affliction, even a bodhisattva of Avalokiteśvara's own advanced attainment is moved to exclaim at how rare and precious the fruit of that labor actually is — a reminder, he adds, that noble beings themselves never stop advancing in the path, marveling anew at each stage of what still lies ahead. With this exchange, the third section, on what each ground counteracts, comes to its true and complete close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara now opens the fourth of the chapter's eight sections: not what each ground contains, is named for, or overcomes, but the eight qualities every single ground possesses, each growing more excellent ground after ground until reaching its own utmost perfection at buddhahood. The first, the purity of superior intention, is glossed by the *Yogācārabhūmi Commentary* as attention itself in its very substance — citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own definition (juan 47) as resolute conviction preceded by pure faith and by the discernment of the Dharma, unshakeable trust in every buddha-teaching — the same superior intention already treated at length in this fascicle's account of the fourfold purity, here simply confirmed as present, in ever more refined form, at every one of the eleven grounds. The second, the purity of mind, names the very same superior concentration — the four dhyānas and four formless attainments — also already introduced, each ground's version more excellent than the last; the *Sub-commentary* notes this concentration is elsewhere simply called "the training of mind," since it is what the Śrāvaka-bhūmi (juan 28) identifies as what first enters the direct realization of the noble truths and the correct nature apart from birth.

26. Section: The Purity of Compassion — Seven Ways a Bodhisattva's Great Compassion Differs from Ordinary Pity

The third kind of excellence, the purity of compassion, likewise grows more excellent from ground to ground.

Chinese Source

三者、悲清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

The third, the purity of compassion.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi devotes his longest treatment to this third quality, since a bodhisattva's compassion is emphatically not the same thing as an ordinary practitioner's pity, however sincere. Citing the Decisive-Discussion chapter (juan 74) and the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 44) — which catalogues one hundred and ten distinct forms of suffering as the very object this compassion arises in response to — he turns to the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s sevenfold contrast between ordinary pity and a bodhisattva's great compassion. Great compassion actually accomplishes what it sets out to do, where ordinary pity often does more harm than good despite good intentions; it perseveres across countless eons of diligent effort, where ordinary pity is fleeting, a momentary gesture rather than a sustained undertaking; it proceeds from a wisdom-eye's clear-sighted, resolute determination to end suffering altogether, where ordinary pity merely wishes to help without any settled resolve; it brings manifold, genuine benefit, where ordinary pity's help is often accidental or even counterproductive; it is entirely unobstructed, extending equally even to those an ordinary person could never bring themselves to pity, since a bodhisattva sees through every apparent good and evil to their shared emptiness; it treats every being with perfect equality, unlike ordinary pity's inevitable favoritism toward the people one is fond of; and it is willing to relinquish one's own benefit entirely for the sake of another's, where ordinary pity, however well-meant, rarely gets past its own instinct for self-preservation. Master Cháng Bǎi adds the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own fourfold account of what makes this compassion "pure": it takes as its object a suffering so subtle and deeply hidden within beings' own minds that only a bodhisattva's insight can perceive it at all, addressing the interior wound rather than merely its outward symptoms; it is the fruit of habituation sustained across countless hundreds of thousands of great eons, not a single lifetime's charitable impulse; it arises from an intensity of resolve so forceful that a bodhisattva would give up a hundred thousand lifetimes, not merely this one, to relieve a single being's suffering, enduring any punishment or pain in the process; and it grows utmost pure precisely because it is joined, from the first ground onward, with non-discriminating wisdom itself — a compassion that, unlike an ordinary person's, only deepens the more clearly its object is actually understood. This fourfold, sevenfold-differentiated compassion, purer at every successive ground, is what the third of the eight excellences names.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The fourth excellence, reaching the far shore, names the ten pāramitās themselves — giving, discipline, patience, diligence, meditation, and wisdom, together with skillful means, vow, power, and knowledge — each cultivated with increasing freedom from attachment, from lingering regret, from any taint of wrongdoing, from discrimination, and with ever more correctly directed dedication, ground after ground more excellent than the last. The fifth, beholding buddhas and offering service to them, Master Cháng Bǎi explains through the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own account (juan 47) of the two conditions by which a bodhisattva actually beholds buddhas directly: a coarse but genuine faith, formed either by hearing the bodhisattva canon or by an inward conviction that buddhas of countless names dwell in countless world-systems, and the simple wish to behold them fulfilled exactly as wished; or the power of a correctly formed vow — resolving to be reborn wherever a buddha is currently teaching — likewise fulfilled exactly as resolved. Having beheld a buddha, a bodhisattva then offers reverent service according to his own capacity, presenting fine offerings and honoring the monastic community as well, listening to and correctly retaining the true Dharma taught, diligently practicing what accords with it, bringing beings to maturity through the four means of attraction, and dedicating every resulting root of goodness to unsurpassed enlightenment. Citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own extended account (juan 44) of ten kinds of offering to a buddha — offering the physical relics themselves, offering stūpas and shrines built in a buddha's honor, offering in a buddha's actual living presence, offering in a buddha's absence (through images and stūpas after his final nirvāṇa, said to spare the offerer countless eons of rebirth in the evil destinies), offering personally, offering by instructing others to offer, offering material wealth and reverent conduct alike, offering on a vast and sustained scale with everything dedicated to enlightenment, offering entirely free of any taint of self-interest, and — the most excellent of all ten — the offering of correct practice itself, cultivating the four immeasurables and the three concentrations, one's own genuine cultivation being the finest offering any buddha could receive — Master Cháng Bǎi observes that without a buddha there could be no disciples of the Buddha at all, so that beholding, honoring, and serving buddhas remains an excellence a bodhisattva cultivates without pause from the first arousal of the bodhi-mind until buddhahood itself is reached.

28. Section: Maturing Beings, and Rebirth by the Power of Vow — Each Ground's Bodhisattva a Different Kind of King

The sixth kind of excellence, the purity of bringing sentient beings to maturity, and the seventh, the purity of rebirth, likewise grow more excellent from ground to ground.

Chinese Source

六者、成熟有情清淨，七者、生清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sixth, the purity of bringing sentient beings to maturity; the seventh, the purity of rebirth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains bringing beings to maturity through the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own sixfold account (juan 37): its essential nature (ripening a being's own wholesome seeds, both dormant and currently active), the four kinds of being it applies to (those fixed in the śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, or buddha lineage, and those of no fixed lineage still capable of a favorable rebirth), its specific varieties (maturing the faculties, the roots of goodness, and wisdom itself, each by degrees from lesser through middling to superior), the twenty-seven skillful means by which it is accomplished, the seven successive stages of bodhisattva attainment capable of accomplishing it (from the ground of settled lineage before the first ground, through the grounds of proper practice, decisiveness, decisive practice, all the way to the ground of ultimate completion spanning the tenth ground and buddhahood), and the many different profiles a being already brought to maturity can show. In practice, maturing the faculties equips a being with the full complement of favorable conditions — lifespan, appearance, lineage, autonomy, credible speech, influence, human dignity, and strength; maturing the roots of goodness thins out a being's underlying afflictive tendencies, weakens unwholesome inclination, and cultivates a gentle, upright receptivity; and maturing wisdom equips a being with sharp, capable mindfulness able to receive, retain, and rightly penetrate both well-spoken and poorly-spoken teachings alike, until the mind is at last utterly free of every affliction — each of the three growing, ground by ground, from lesser to middling to superior degree. The seventh excellence, the purity of rebirth, describes the five distinct ways a bodhisattva takes birth within the three realms purely to benefit beings, utterly unlike an ordinary being's karma-driven rebirth: birth to avert calamity (manifesting, for instance, as a great fish during famine so starving beings might eat and survive, or as a healing tree or physician during plague, or as a wealthy benefactor amid war's devastation); birth according to kind (appearing as a human, an animal, a hungry ghost, or even a hell-being, by vow rather than karma, wherever that particular kind of being most needs to be reached — Avalokiteśvara's own manifestation as the flame-faced hungry ghost being one famous example); birth of great power (bearing eight kinds of ripened excellence that let a bodhisattva richly benefit every being encountered and give rise to every buddha-quality); birth of ever-increasing excellence, each life more excellent than the last — a first-ground bodhisattva typically born a wheel-turning king ruling one continent, a second-ground bodhisattva ruling all four, a third a heavenly Śakra, and successively through the fourth Suyāma heaven, fifth Tuṣita heaven, sixth Nirmāṇarati heaven, seventh Paranirmita-vaśavartin heaven, eighth and ninth a great Brahmā ruling a thousand and then two thousand world-systems, and the tenth Maheśvara himself; and finally the last birth, the single lifetime in which a tenth-ground bodhisattva descends from Tuṣita heaven to manifest the eight stages of a buddha's career and attain complete enlightenment before the world — a fivefold rebirth entirely unlike anything an ordinary being's own karma could ever produce, and, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, no longer needed once buddhahood itself is reached, since a buddha benefits ground-bound bodhisattvas through the reward-body and every other being through countless manifestation-bodies instead.

29. Section: The Purity of Majestic Power — Threefold, and Tenfold Greater at Every Ground

Chinese Source

八者、威德清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eighth, the purity of majestic power.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the eightfold list of excellences with the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own threefold account of majestic power (juan 37, its Chapter on Majestic Power): the majestic power of the noble ones, meaning mastery over concentration itself and the six supernormal powers it makes possible, achieved through a mind thoroughly tamed and well cultivated; the majestic power of the Dharma, meaning the vast, excellent fruit and benefit inherent in the six — or, more fully, ten — pāramitās themselves; and co-arisen majestic power, meaning the extraordinary capacities a bodhisattva has accumulated through countless past lives of vast merit — the ability to endure the most intense, unbroken suffering across the longest span of time without deliberation, and the willing acceptance of whatever hardship benefiting beings requires, up to and including the eight stages of a buddha's own career — elaborated at length in that same chapter. These three, sometimes further divided into five (adding what is shared with the two lesser vehicles and what is unique to a bodhisattva alone), together comprise majestic power; Master Cháng Bǎi notes that a bodhisattva's noble power (the six supernormal powers) and Dharma-power (the pāramitās) each grow tenfold more excellent from one ground to the next, ground after ground, all the way to buddhahood. With this eighth excellence closed, the fourth of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections — the excellent qualities every single ground possesses, each surpassing the last — comes to its complete close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes the fourth section's supplementary analysis with two clarifications. First, all eight excellences continue growing more refined at every single ground all the way to buddhahood, with exactly one exception: the purity of rebirth, since a buddha no longer takes birth within the three realms at all, benefiting ground-bound bodhisattvas through the reward-body and every other being through manifestation-bodies instead, rather than through any of the five kinds of rebirth already described. Second, every quality present on the first ground remains present on every ground above it — nothing already gained is ever lost — yet each ground is still named for and distinguished by its own particular signature quality (intention for the first, discipline's purity for the second, concentration for the third and named Emitter of Light, wisdom joined with the factors of awakening for the fourth and named Blazing with Wisdom, and so on through the tenth ground's perfected Dharma-body), citing the *Sub-commentary*'s cross-reference (juan 75) to this fascicle's own earlier account of the eight excellences growing ever more excellent ground by ground. The Buddha's closing line then draws the sharpest possible line between the ten grounds and buddhahood itself: however excellent a tenth-ground bodhisattva's own qualities may be, something still surpasses them; only a buddha's qualities have, in the fullest sense, nothing whatsoever left above them, having realized the complete threefold body and fourfold wisdom in their entirety. With this, the fourth of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections, on each ground's own excellence, is brought to its true and final close.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara opens the fifth of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections — the fifth excellence, rebirth, singled out here for its own dedicated treatment. Master Cháng Bǎi first addresses a natural question the honorific "good sons" might raise: whether Avalokiteśvara, so often depicted in female form, is male or female — explaining that a bodhisattva of such attainment manifests in whatever form best reaches a given being, male or female, human, heavenly, or otherwise, exactly as the fivefold rebirth already described allows, so that the address carries no fixed claim about the bodhisattva's own "true" form. The four reasons themselves map directly onto the fivefold rebirth already introduced: the first, birth gathered by utmost pure roots of goodness, is rebirth of ever-increasing excellence — bearing, upon rebirth, the eightfold ripened excellence of lifespan, appearance, lineage, autonomy, credible speech, influence, human dignity, and strength; the second, birth taken up through deliberate discernment, is rebirth according to kind — a bodhisattva who has attained the bliss of the higher realms deliberately relinquishing it to be reborn in the desire realm instead, precisely in order to reach and guide beings there, illustrated at length in the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own account (juan 48) of a bodhisattva taking birth even among warring animals, heretical brahmins, or beings enslaved to their own desires, becoming their leader precisely so as to refrain from their evil and model the good they lack, gently drawing them toward the true teaching from within their own circumstances rather than from outside them; the third, birth out of compassion to rescue beings, is birth to avert calamity — descending amid famine, plague, war, enmity, tyranny, or wrong view purely out of compassionate urgency; and the fourth, being oneself free of defilement and thereby able to remove others' defilement, is the last birth, the single final lifetime in which a bodhisattva's own provisions for enlightenment are already utterly complete and only the demonstration of a buddha's own career remains — though Master Cháng Bǎi notes this fourth reason in fact underlies every one of the other three as well, since a bodhisattva could remove no one else's defilement without first being free of it personally, citing the *Vimalakīrti Sūtra*'s own maxim that one who cannot heal his own sickness can hardly heal another's, and that only one already unbound can genuinely loosen another's bonds. He closes by noting the *Mahāyāna-sūtrālaṃkāra*'s parallel fourfold scheme of rebirth by the power of karma, vow, concentration, and supernormal ability — the very same territory covered here, minus only karma-driven rebirth, since nothing a bodhisattva from the first ground onward undergoes is any longer compelled by karma at all.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara opens the sixth of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections by asking about the traditional three vows every ordained disciple is taught at ordination — to end all evil, to cultivate all good, and to liberate all beings — here called the vast vow (liberating every being, since its object is vast beyond measure), the wondrous vow (cultivating every good, the wondrous aspiration toward enlightenment itself), and the supreme vow (ending every evil, surpassing both the two lesser vehicles and a bodhisattva's own pre-ground aspiration). Master Cháng Bǎi surveys the *Yogācārabhūmi Commentary*'s and *Sub-commentary*'s several ways of mapping these three names onto the four reasons the Buddha actually gives, before turning to the reasons themselves. The first two — skillfully knowing nirvāṇa's bliss and being fully capable of swiftly realizing it, yet deliberately relinquishing that swift realization — describe the same signless mastery already treated in this fascicle's own account of the eleven-part division, now read as the very basis of the vow: a bodhisattva who could not actually attain nirvāṇa would have nothing genuine to relinquish, so the vow's power rests precisely on this being a real, freely chosen forgoing rather than an inability. The third, giving rise to the great resolve without any condition and without expecting anything in return, describes what Master Cháng Bǎi calls unconditioned great loving-kindness and same-substance great compassion — a mind so free of self-other division that benefiting every being requires no calculated reason and awaits no repayment, dedicated entirely through the principle of non-attainment. The fourth, dwelling amid manifold great sufferings across a long span of time for beings' sake, names the three full incalculable eons a bodhisattva willingly spends taking rebirth again and again within the difficult desire and form realms rather than resting in nirvāṇa's ease — the very hardship, Master Cháng Bǎi notes, that already appeared throughout the fivefold rebirth just discussed, whether as birth averting calamity or the final birth demonstrating a buddha's own career. Taken together, these four reasons — capability freely set aside, compassion requiring no cause, and endurance freely embraced — are precisely why the Buddha can call a bodhisattva's vow vast, wondrous, and supreme all at once, closing the sixth of the chapter's eight sections in full.

Avalokiteśvara now opens the seventh and, per Master Cháng Bǎi's own preview at this fascicle's very start, most extensive of the ground-pāramitā chapter's eight sections: what a bodhisattva at every one of the ten grounds must actually learn and practice — first summarized as the six pāramitās taken as a whole, then, as the coming sections will show, unpacked through seventeen further distinct angles of analysis. Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "provisions" through the *Bodhisaṃbhāraka-śāstra*'s fivefold sense of the term: they fill out the pāramitās to completion, sustain what is being cultivated, nourish one's own accumulating virtue, serve as the direct cause of unsurpassed enlightenment, and bring the path to full sufficiency. Giving, discipline, and patience together comprise the provisions of merit; wisdom alone comprises the provisions of wisdom; diligence and meditative concentration pervade both, since diligence may be applied either to merit-building practice or to the threefold wisdom of hearing, reflection, and cultivation, and meditative concentration may cultivate either the four immeasurables (merit) or insight into the aggregates, the four truths, dependent arising, and every other object of non-discriminating wisdom (wisdom) — everything turning on what, specifically, the diligence or concentration in question is actually directed toward. Citing the Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 36) and the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s own detailed fourfold analysis of how the pāramitās contain one another (juan 9), Master Cháng Bǎi summarizes: by essential nature, discipline contains only the discipline-pāramitā, concentration only the meditation-pāramitā, and wisdom contains the wisdom-pāramitā together with the four further pāramitās of skillful means, vow, power, and knowledge that round out the fuller tenfold scheme; by mutual support, every pāramitā in fact assists every other — giving weakens the very craving that would break discipline, giving also cultivates patience (since a giver inevitably meets ungrateful recipients), and wisdom pervades and completes all the preceding five, since even giving requires the discernment of what, how, and when to give, given freely and without regret; by function, exactly as this passage states, discipline contains the first three, concentration contains meditation, and wisdom contains the remaining five; and by what a pāramitā's name most directly displays, discipline is said to contain the first four (giving as its provision, discipline as its own substance, patience as its companion, and diligence as what guards it), concentration contains meditation, and wisdom contains the remaining five.

The third sub-topic turns from what the six pāramitās are to how they are actually cultivated. The first aspect calls for unshakeable conviction in the Mahāyāna's own scriptures — its sūtras, vinaya, and treatises devoted to the pāramitās, profound enough to demand real strength of faith before one can even begin, since without this settled conviction a bodhisattva's resolve to seek enlightenment above and liberate beings below has no foundation to stand on. Third, guarding and protecting the bodhi-mind; fourth, drawing near to a genuine good spiritual friend.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains guarding the bodhi-mind through the Bodhisattva-bhūmi's own fivefold scheme of protective effort (juan 46): guarding one's own sharp intelligence, so that innate wisdom grasps the teaching swiftly; guarding correct mindfulness, so that whatever the intelligence has grasped is retained without forgetting; guarding correct wisdom, so that what mindfulness has retained is rightly examined and its meaning correctly penetrated, cultivating whatever supports and strengthens intelligence, mindfulness, and wisdom while avoiding whatever would let them decline; guarding one's own mind, by carefully protecting the sense-faculties' own gates; and guarding others' minds, by correctly attending to how one's own conduct affects them — five forms of vigilance without which the bodhi-mind, once generated, could easily be lost. Drawing near to a genuine good spiritual friend Master Cháng Bǎi treats at greater length, citing the Śrāvaka-bhūmi (juan 25) and Bodhisattva-bhūmi (juan 44) on the eight marks that together identify such a friend: abiding in discipline, keeping the precepts purely, both the two lesser vehicles' own and the fuller Mahāyāna discipline grounded in great compassion; extensive learning, deep enough in the teachings, especially the Mahāyāna's own, to have genuinely mastered their essential meaning; personal realization, having actually attained at least some level of concentration in which to reflect on the Mahāyāna teaching directly, itself the fruit of many past lives' cultivation; compassion, the capacity for genuine loving concern; fearlessness, teaching the Dharma to anyone, however exalted, exactly as it needs to be taught, without hesitation; forbearance, patiently enduring every hardship and difficulty that teaching others brings; tirelessness, never wearying of either learning or teaching, willing to benefit beings for as long as time itself continues; and perfected speech, able to draw on the scriptures freely and express the Dharma's meaning so skillfully that listeners' minds are genuinely won over. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Buddha's own answer to Ānanda's question of whether a good spiritual friend accounts for half of the path — no, the Buddha replied, but for the whole of it — before turning to how such a friend, once found, should actually be approached: with unwavering affection, respect, and pure faith whether that friend is well or ailing (never abandoning care the moment illness makes it burdensome); with constant respectful attentiveness — greeting, joining palms, bowing, and maintaining harmonious, respectful conduct; with the traditional four offerings of clothing, food, bedding, and medicine; and, most importantly, by actually practicing exactly as instructed rather than substituting one's own preferred method, confessing openly whatever faults arise along the way rather than concealing them — since a student who merely attends teachings without genuinely following their guidance gains nothing more than the appearance of study.

38. Section: Cultivating Wholesome Qualities Without Interruption — Five Aspects of Cultivation Close

The fifth and final aspect of cultivating the six pāramitās is cultivating wholesome qualities diligently and without interruption, closing the fivefold account of how a bodhisattva actually undertakes this study.

Chinese Source

五者、無間勤修善品。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fifth, cultivating wholesome qualities diligently and without interruption.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi closes this third sub-topic by drawing together everything the five aspects have established: giving, discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, and wisdom, together with calm-abiding and insight themselves and every other wholesome quality tied to merit and wisdom alike, must all be cultivated without a single interruption, jointly perfecting merit and wisdom together, so that unsurpassed enlightenment is reached as swiftly as possible. He notes that these five aspects are best read as a natural sequence rather than five disconnected instructions: unshakeable conviction in the teaching comes first, since nothing else can proceed without it; disciplined study through the ten kinds of engaging the Dharma follows, converting that conviction into actual understanding; guarding the bodhi-mind and drawing near a genuine spiritual friend then provide the internal vigilance and external support needed to sustain the resolve once formed; and cultivating every wholesome quality without interruption is simply what carrying all of this through to completion, across three full incalculable eons, actually requires. With this, the third of the seventeen sub-topics distinguishing the six pāramitās — how they are cultivated — comes to its close.

39. Section: Why Precisely Six — Removing Six Opposing Obstacles, and the Middle Way Between Two Extremes

World-Honored One! For what reason are these matters that should be learned established as precisely six in number?

Chinese Source

世尊！何因緣故，施設如是所應學事但有六數？

Ru-Yi Translation

World-Honored One! For what reason are these matters that should be learned established as precisely six in number?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi previews the Buddha's coming answer — that the first three pāramitās chiefly benefit beings while the last three chiefly counteract affliction — before surveying the *Cheng Weishi Lun*'s fuller, fourfold explanation (juan 9) of why exactly six. First, each pāramitā removes one of six specifically opposing obstacles: giving removes stinginess, discipline removes transgression, patience removes hatred, diligence removes laziness, concentration removes distraction, and wisdom removes wrong views. Second, the six form a natural sequence for practice, each preparing the ground for the next, from the shallowest, giving, to the deepest, wisdom. Third, the same sequence forms a natural progression for maturing beings, the first three chiefly securing a favorable rebirth (giving yielding future wealth, discipline averting the evil destinies, patience yielding an attractive, well-supported form) and the last three chiefly securing final liberation (diligence, concentration, and wisdom directly advancing toward nirvāṇa) — the very same benefiting-versus-counteracting division this passage itself will shortly give. Fourth, the six together keep a bodhisattva from settling into either extreme: compassion for beings, cultivated through the first three, keeps one from resting in nirvāṇa alone, while wisdom, cultivated through the last three, keeps one from remaining bound to saṃsāra — a bodhisattva thereby "abiding nowhere," neither the mundane nor the supramundane path claiming him entirely. Master Cháng Bǎi adds two further explanations from the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* (juan 46) — that the Buddha, as Dharma-king, simply teaches whatever number of pāramitās a given audience's capacity calls for, sometimes fewer, sometimes (as in the *Bhadrakalpika-sūtra*) as many as eighty-four thousand, six here matching this particular audience's need; and that six specifically correspond to the six destinies of rebirth, whose suffering the six pāramitās together are meant to relieve — as well as the *Upāsaka-śīla Sūtra*'s account of the six pāramitās answering, point for point, a group of heretics' mistaken worship of the six directions (giving as the east, discipline as the south, patience as the west, diligence as the north, meditation as the nadir, and wisdom as the zenith), a skillful means for winning them over to the true teaching through an image already familiar to them.

40. Section: Benefiting Beings — How Giving, Discipline, and Patience Serve Others

First, because of benefiting sentient beings; second, because of counteracting the afflictions. It should be known that the first three benefit sentient beings, while the last three counteract every affliction. As to the first three benefiting sentient beings: because of giving, bodhisattvas gather requisites and thereby benefit sentient beings; because of discipline, they refrain from harming, oppressing, or troubling others, and thereby benefit sentient beings; and because of patience, they are able to bear with being harmed, oppressed, or troubled by others, and thereby benefit sentient beings.

Chinese Source

一者、饒益諸有情故；二者、對治諸煩惱故。當知前三饒益有情，後三對治一切煩惱。前三饒益諸有情者，謂諸菩薩由布施故，攝受資具，饒益有情；由持戒故，不行損害逼迫惱亂，饒益有情；由忍辱故，於彼損害逼迫惱亂堪能忍受，饒益有情。

Ru-Yi Translation

First, because of benefiting sentient beings; second, because of counteracting the afflictions. It should be known that the first three benefit sentient beings, while the last three counteract every affliction. As to the first three benefiting sentient beings: because of giving, bodhisattvas gather requisites and thereby benefit sentient beings; because of discipline, they refrain from harming, oppressing, or troubling others, and thereby benefit sentient beings; and because of patience, they are able to bear with being harmed, oppressed, or troubled by others, and thereby benefit sentient beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The Buddha's answer divides cleanly along the line already previewed: the first three pāramitās are directed outward, toward benefiting others, and the last three inward, toward counteracting one's own affliction. Master Cháng Bǎi cites the familiar verse — every being seeks happiness, yet happiness is impossible without the material means to sustain it, and since all such means flow from giving, the Buddha teaches giving first of all — noting that a bodhisattva's own precepts permit, unlike a śrāvaka's, the deliberate accumulation of fine bedding and requisites, not for personal comfort but precisely so as to have something to give when a being in need appears; discipline benefits others chiefly through what it prevents, its power to restrain wrongdoing meaning a disciplined bodhisattva simply does not become a source of harm, oppression, or disturbance to those around him; and patience benefits others by absorbing whatever unreasonable harm or provocation comes without resentment, since retaliation in kind would only compound whatever injury already occurred — a bodhisattva's capacity to bear wrongdoing without striking back being itself already a benefit conferred on the one who wronged him.

41. Section: Counteracting Affliction — How Diligence, Concentration, and Wisdom Free the Self

As to the last three counteracting every affliction: because of diligence, bodhisattvas, though not yet having permanently subdued every affliction, nor yet having permanently destroyed every dormant tendency, are still able to courageously cultivate every wholesome quality, so that no affliction can shake their diligent application to it; because of meditative concentration, they permanently subdue affliction; and because of wisdom, they permanently destroy the dormant tendencies themselves.

Chinese Source

後三對治諸煩惱者，謂諸菩薩由精進故，雖未永伏一切煩惱，亦未永害一切隨眠，而能勇猛修諸善品，彼諸煩惱不能傾動善品加行；由靜慮故，永伏煩惱；由般若故，永害隨眠。

Ru-Yi Translation

As to the last three counteracting every affliction: because of diligence, bodhisattvas, though not yet having permanently subdued every affliction, nor yet having permanently destroyed every dormant tendency, are still able to courageously cultivate every wholesome quality, so that no affliction can shake their diligent application to it; because of meditative concentration, they permanently subdue affliction; and because of wisdom, they permanently destroy the dormant tendencies themselves.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out a careful distinction the root text itself makes precise: diligence alone does not eliminate affliction, either its currently active form or its dormant seeds, but it does keep affliction from derailing one's cultivation of the six pāramitās and of calm-abiding and insight — a person of genuine diligence stays the course regardless of what afflictions still simmer beneath the surface. Meditative concentration goes further, actually subduing affliction's currently active form — the still, unruffled composure of someone insulted who somehow answers without a trace of anger in his voice is real evidence of concentration's power — yet even this leaves the underlying seeds of affliction untouched, ready to spring back to life the moment conditions favor them. Only wisdom — specifically the wisdom of prajñāpāramitā, seeing directly into an object's own emptiness and unattainability — reaches all the way to the seed itself and permanently destroys it, since anything short of directly seeing through an object's emptiness amounts only to endurance or suppression, not genuine release; Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that what people commonly call "letting go" is, without this wisdom, almost always endurance in disguise, and only prajñā-vision can make it real. Cultivating the first three pāramitās perfects benefiting others; cultivating the last three perfects benefiting oneself; together, the six pāramitās perfect a bodhisattva's benefit to self and other both, closing the fourth of the seventeen sub-topics on this account of why exactly six pāramitās are taught.

42. Section: The Other Four Perfections — Skillful Means as Helper to Giving, Discipline, and Patience

World-Honored One! For what reason are the remaining pāramitās established as precisely four in number? Good sons! Because they serve as helpers to the preceding six pāramitās. Namely: bodhisattvas, with regard to the sentient beings contained within the first three pāramitās, draw them in through the skillful means of the means of attraction, and establish them in wholesome qualities. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of skillful means serves as helper to the first three.

Chinese Source

世尊！何因緣故，施設所餘波羅蜜多但有四數？善男子！與前六種波羅蜜多為助伴故。謂諸菩薩，於前三種波羅蜜多所攝有情，以諸攝事方便善巧而攝受之，安置善品。是故我說方便善巧波羅蜜多，與前三種而為助伴。

Ru-Yi Translation

World-Honored One! For what reason are the remaining pāramitās established as precisely four in number? Good sons! Because they serve as helpers to the preceding six pāramitās. Namely: bodhisattvas, with regard to the sentient beings contained within the first three pāramitās, draw them in through the skillful means of the means of attraction, and establish them in wholesome qualities. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of skillful means serves as helper to the first three.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara now asks about the fuller, tenfold scheme of pāramitās, and the Buddha explains that the additional four exist purely to help the original six reach full completion. The pāramitā of skillful means works through the traditional four means of attraction — giving, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and cooperation — helping a bodhisattva not merely practice giving, discipline, and patience personally but actually draw other beings into practicing them as well: teaching and encouraging others toward giving, discipline, and patience through kind speech to weaken their stinginess, wrongdoing, and hatred in turn, and then going further still, instructing them in calm-abiding and insight and practicing alongside them, so that the benefit conferred is not merely mundane comfort but the supramundane benefit of genuine liberation. It is this capacity to extend one's own giving, discipline, and patience outward into others' cultivation as well — not simply modeling the three pāramitās but actively drawing beings into them — that earns skillful means its place as their helper.

43. Section: The Vow — What Carries a Weak Bodhisattva's Diligence Through Difficulty

If bodhisattvas, because affliction is abundant in their present condition, lack the capacity for uninterrupted cultivation, owing to a feeble intention and a resolve still fixed on the lower realm; if their mind lacks the capacity to abide inwardly, and, unable to properly hear and reflect upon the bodhisattva canon, the meditative concentration they attain is unable to give rise to supramundane wisdom, so that they gather only a small, narrow portion of the provisions of merit — then, generating a correct resolve that afflictions in future lives may grow lighter, this is called the pāramitā of vow. Because of this vow, afflictions grow lighter and one becomes able to practice diligence. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of vow serves as helper to the pāramitā of diligence.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，於現法中煩惱多故，於修無間無有堪能，羸劣意樂故，下界勝解故。於內心住無有堪能，於菩薩藏不能聞緣善修習故，所有靜慮不能引發出世間慧，彼便攝受少分狹劣福德資糧。為未來世煩惱輕微，心生正願，如是名願波羅蜜多。由此願故，煩惱微薄，能修精進。是故我說願波羅蜜多，與精進波羅蜜多而為助伴。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas, because affliction is abundant in their present condition, lack the capacity for uninterrupted cultivation, owing to a feeble intention and a resolve still fixed on the lower realm; if their mind lacks the capacity to abide inwardly, and, unable to properly hear and reflect upon the bodhisattva canon, the meditative concentration they attain is unable to give rise to supramundane wisdom, so that they gather only a small, narrow portion of the provisions of merit — then, generating a correct resolve that afflictions in future lives may grow lighter, this is called the pāramitā of vow. Because of this vow, afflictions grow lighter and one becomes able to practice diligence. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of vow serves as helper to the pāramitā of diligence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads this passage as addressing an ordinary bodhisattva's honest predicament: abundant present affliction disturbs the mind and blocks uninterrupted practice; a still-feeble intention and a resolve still weighted toward the desire realm's own attractions mean the wish to practice the six pāramitās remains weak, desirous craving not yet severed, attention still pulled outward; and, unable to properly immerse oneself in the bodhisattva canon's own teaching, one's meditative concentration never develops the various skillful discernments (the aggregates, the four truths, the four kinds of examination) needed to give rise to supramundane wisdom, so that only the four immeasurables — themselves still part of the provisions of merit rather than wisdom — can be cultivated, and even those only in small measure. Facing all of this honestly, a bodhisattva's only genuine remedy is to vow: resolving now, even while unable to act on it immediately, that affliction may grow lighter in lives to come, exactly as one might vow to give away a possession one cannot yet part with, or a body part one cannot yet offer, once future circumstances make it possible. This vow becomes the very seed of a *faith-vow-practice* progression: trusting the Mahāyāna's excellence generates the vow, and the vow, once generated, lets a bodhisattva meet whatever circumstance arises with correct attention, afflictions correspondingly thinning and diligence becoming possible where it was not before — which is precisely why the pāramitā of vow is named helper to the pāramitā of diligence specifically, since diligence pervades every pāramitā but needs the vow's own motive force to actually get underway.

44. Section: The Pāramitā of Power, and the Pāramitā of Knowledge — Helpers to Concentration and Wisdom

If bodhisattvas, drawing near a good teacher, hearing the true Dharma, and giving correct attention as the causal conditions, transform a lesser intention into a superior one, and are thereby also able to attain resolve toward the higher realm, this is called the pāramitā of power. Because of this power, one gains the capacity to abide inwardly. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of power serves as helper to the pāramitā of meditative concentration. If bodhisattvas, having already properly heard and reflected upon the bodhisattva canon and skillfully cultivated it, are thereby able to give rise to meditative concentration, this is called the pāramitā of knowledge. Because of this knowledge, one is capable of giving rise to supramundane wisdom. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of knowledge serves as helper to the pāramitā of wisdom.

Chinese Source

若諸菩薩，親近善士、聽聞正法、如理作意為因緣故，轉劣意樂成勝意樂，亦能獲得上界勝解，如是名力波羅蜜多。由此力故，於內心住有所堪能。是故我說力波羅蜜多，與靜慮波羅蜜多而為助伴。若諸菩薩，於菩薩藏已能聞緣善修習故，能發靜慮，如是名智波羅蜜多。由此智故，堪能引發出世間慧。是故我說智波羅蜜多，與慧波羅蜜多而為助伴。

Ru-Yi Translation

If bodhisattvas, drawing near a good teacher, hearing the true Dharma, and giving correct attention as the causal conditions, transform a lesser intention into a superior one, and are thereby also able to attain resolve toward the higher realm, this is called the pāramitā of power. Because of this power, one gains the capacity to abide inwardly. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of power serves as helper to the pāramitā of meditative concentration. If bodhisattvas, having already properly heard and reflected upon the bodhisattva canon and skillfully cultivated it, are thereby able to give rise to meditative concentration, this is called the pāramitā of knowledge. Because of this knowledge, one is capable of giving rise to supramundane wisdom. For this reason I say that the pāramitā of knowledge serves as helper to the pāramitā of wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

The pāramitā of power builds directly on the vow just discussed: a bodhisattva whose vow has already begun to thin out affliction now draws near a genuine teacher, listens to the true Dharma, and lets that hearing shape correct attention, so that a formerly feeble, desire-realm-bound intention transforms into a genuinely superior one, strong enough to attain resolve toward the form and formless realms' own concentration — power in the specific sense of newfound capacity to abide the mind inwardly on its object, which is exactly why this pāramitā is named helper to meditative concentration in particular. The pāramitā of knowledge then builds on power in turn: a bodhisattva who has now properly heard, reflected on, and skillfully cultivated the bodhisattva canon is able to give rise to genuine meditative concentration, and it is this same concentration, joined with the wisdom gained through proper study of the Mahāyāna canon and even the five fields of learning, that becomes capable of giving rise to supramundane wisdom itself — Master Cháng Bǎi citing the *Sub-commentary*'s note that this knowledge may arise either just before or just after non-discriminating wisdom's own arising, and is precisely why it is named helper to the wisdom-pāramitā specifically. With all four of the additional pāramitās now explained — skillful means helping the first three, vow helping diligence, power helping concentration, and knowledge helping wisdom — the fifth of the seventeen sub-topics distinguishing the ten pāramitās, on why the remaining four number exactly four, comes to its close.

45. Section: The Order of Teaching — Each Perfection the Basis for Drawing Forth the Next

World-Honored One! For what reason is the order in which the six pāramitās are taught precisely as it is? Good sons! Because each is able to serve as the basis for drawing forth the one that follows.

Chinese Source

世尊！何因緣故，宣說六種波羅蜜多如是次第？善男子！能為後後引發依故。

Ru-Yi Translation

World-Honored One! For what reason is the order in which the six pāramitās are taught precisely as it is? Good sons! Because each is able to serve as the basis for drawing forth the one that follows.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Avalokiteśvara now asks the sixth of the seventeen sub-topics: why the six pāramitās are always taught in this specific order — giving, discipline, patience, diligence, concentration, wisdom — rather than, say, wisdom first and giving last. The Buddha's answer states the underlying principle in a single line before the coming section unpacks it pāramitā by pāramitā: each earlier one creates the very conditions from which the next naturally arises, so that the traditional sequence is not arbitrary but reflects the actual causal order in which a bodhisattva's practice deepens, each perfection drawing forth the one beyond it in turn.
