Fascicle 70 · 瑜伽師地論卷第七十 · The Śrāvaka Ground, Continued

Wisdom's Own Light Outshines the Sun in Fifteen Ways

Continuing directly from fascicle 69, Maitreya opens the eighth of the śrāvaka canon's own thirteen topics, a wide-ranging discussion of miscellaneous meanings gathered under no single earlier heading, beginning with the five excellent benefits genuine wisdom brings and a fifteen-point comparison showing exactly how the light of wisdom surpasses the light of the sun, the moon, or any lamp.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 70 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Section: Five Excellent Benefits Genuine Wisdom Brings

This fourth part of the Śrāvaka Ground's own decisive discernment continues directly from fascicle 69, taking up the eighth of the śrāvaka canon's own thirteen topics, explaining miscellaneous meanings, a wide-ranging discussion gathered under no single earlier heading yet still genuinely connected to a practitioner's own path. The topic opens with the light of wisdom itself, the illumination that follows once consciousness has genuinely been transformed into wisdom, said to bring five excellent benefits. The first is dispelling every darkness regarding what can be known, the ignorance that once took the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields as permanent, blissful, a genuine self, and inherently pure now genuinely cleared away, replaced by direct knowledge that all such things are impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self. The second is bringing genuine joy and benefit to one's own body through both mundane and transcendent virtue, since whatever meditative attainment or noble accomplishment a practitioner achieves brings some form of pleasurable or equanimous feeling that leaves body and mind genuinely at ease. The third is rightly seeing meaning never seen before, the outflow-free wisdom that directly and correctly penetrates the profound truths of impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness for the very first time. The fourth is bringing supreme bliss in this very life, since a practitioner who sustains calming and insight and dwells within the four absorptions experiences a present-life ease born of seclusion and stillness, while a practitioner who has genuinely realized the four foundations of mindfulness experiences the still more excellent bliss of enlightenment itself, bound up with ultimate truth. And the fifth is bringing the supreme destination after the body itself perishes, this supreme destination being nirvana, the highest state of all, a śrāvaka's own successful practice culminating in nirvana without remainder at life's end, and a Mahāyāna practitioner's own ultimate accomplishment culminating in the non-abiding nirvana that carries this same supreme merit further still.

Chinese Source

復次，諸智光明有五勝利。一、能於所知滅一切闇；二、能以世間、出世間功德，適悅攝受所依止身；三、能正觀見所未見義；四、能於現法與第一樂；五、能身壞後與第一趣。

Ru-Yi Translation

The light of wisdom brings five excellent benefits: dispelling every darkness regarding what can be known; bringing joy and benefit to the body through mundane and transcendent virtue; rightly seeing meaning never seen before; bringing supreme bliss in this very life; and bringing the supreme destination, nirvana, after the body perishes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening fivefold catalogue as a fitting overture to an entire topic explicitly gathered from miscellaneous material, wisdom's own light serving here as a unifying thread capable of touching everything from present-moment bodily ease through the most distant destination after death. He highlights the fourth benefit's own careful distinction between meditative bliss and the still more excellent bliss bound up with ultimate truth as continuing this treatise's consistent refusal to let genuine meditative attainment be mistaken for the deeper realization it can, at best, only prepare the way toward.

2. Section: Wisdom's Light Belongs to Mind, Not to Matter

A further comparison then opens: fifteen distinct qualities by which the light of wisdom is shown to surpass every external light, whether the sun, the moon, or a lamp. The very first of these fifteen concerns their underlying nature: external light is form itself, a physical phenomenon, while the light of wisdom is wisdom itself, a mental phenomenon, the two belonging to fundamentally different categories of existence from the very outset, one material and one purely of the mind.

Chinese Source

復次，由十五種德差別故，諸智光明勝外光明。何等十五？謂外光明以色為性，諸智光明以慧為性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through fifteen distinct qualities, the light of wisdom surpasses every external light. What are the fifteen? Regarding their underlying nature: external light is form itself, while the light of wisdom is wisdom itself.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this opening distinction between form and mind as this entire fifteen-point comparison's own necessary foundation, every subsequent point of superiority tracing back to this same basic difference in kind between a physical illumination that merely reveals what is already there and a mental illumination capable of genuinely transforming the one who possesses it.

3. Section: Sunlight Clears an Outer Film, Wisdom Clears the Inner Darkness of Ignorance

The comparison continues by contrasting their actual function. External light, such as sunlight, moonlight, or lamplight, can remove only an outer obstruction, restoring sight to a being whose eyes are otherwise clouded by a film or cataract obscuring the outer world; the light of wisdom, by contrast, removes an inner obstruction, the ignorance that otherwise conceals the true nature of all things, a genuinely wise practitioner illuminating what was previously hidden within their own mind rather than merely revealing what already lay outside it.

Chinese Source

又外光明能害外翳，諸智光明能害內翳。

Ru-Yi Translation

External light can remove only an outer obstruction. The light of wisdom removes an inner obstruction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the outer-film and inner-darkness pairing as this comparison's own most immediately graspable point of contrast, a single vivid image capturing the entire distinction this fifteen-point catalogue goes on to elaborate, external light restoring vision of a world that was already there all along, wisdom restoring vision of a truth the mind itself had been obscuring from within.

4. Section: A Skeleton Overrides a Beautiful Face, Closing Fifteen Ways Wisdom Outshines the Sun

The remaining points of this fifteenfold comparison bring the entire discussion to its close. External light can only lead beings toward destinations already familiar, places daylight has always made accessible for ordinary living and labor, while the light of wisdom leads beings toward a destination genuinely unprecedented, the non-conceptual wisdom a practitioner attains having never been reached at any point across beginningless time before. External light cannot open what remains genuinely covered, leaving certain shadowed places, such as a cave or an underground chamber, dark even at midday, while the light of wisdom opens every such covering, ignorance itself no longer able to conceal anything once non-conceptual wisdom has actually been attained, the true nature of every phenomenon then fully manifest to the mind. External light, once it has revealed something, cannot conceal that same thing again, sunlight or lamplight unable to make an already-illuminated object simply disappear from view; the light of wisdom, remarkably, can do precisely this, a practitioner's contemplation of impurity able to override an already-apprehended appearance of purity, so that a face once seen as beautiful is instead seen filled with pus and blood, or a contemplation of bare bones overrides the very same appearance until only an exposed skeleton remains, impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness likewise overriding the appearances of permanence, pleasure, and self, and a signless contemplation overriding every appearance whatsoever. External light cannot generate boundless illumination reaching every place at once, while the light of wisdom, the outflow-free wisdom of the path, is itself boundless, illuminating the true nature of all things everywhere without limit. And external light, when too intense, can actually harm the very eyes meant to perceive it, while the light of wisdom only clarifies vision further the brighter it becomes, never once harming what it illuminates. **This closes the entire fifteenfold comparison, and with it the discussion of wisdom's own excellent benefits, the first of the five sub-topics gathered under the śrāvaka canon's own eighth topic of miscellaneous meanings.**

Chinese Source

不能隱覆已所開顯者：謂如不淨作意，映奪淨相；無常、苦、無我作意，映奪常、樂、我相；無相作意，映奪一切眾相。如是一切能治映奪所治，令不可得，名能隱覆已所開顯。違害、不違害於見：外面的陽光太強時，會違害於眼睛所見，智慧的光明越強看得越清楚，不會違害所見。

Ru-Yi Translation

Concealing what has already been revealed: a contemplation of impurity overrides an appearance of purity; contemplations of impermanence, suffering, and selflessness override appearances of permanence, pleasure, and self; a signless contemplation overrides every appearance. Too-bright external light can harm the eyes; the brighter wisdom's light becomes, the clearer the seeing, never harming it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eleventh point, wisdom's own power to conceal what has already been revealed, as this entire fifteenfold comparison's own most arresting claim, since it shows that wisdom's light does something external light could never do even in principle, actively overwriting a compelling but false appearance rather than merely adding further illumination alongside it. He highlights the closing point, that wisdom's light never harms what it illuminates however bright it grows, as a fitting final contrast to close on, external light's own physical intensity eventually becoming an obstacle to the very seeing it enables, wisdom's own intensity never posing any such limit at all.

5. Section: Three Ways of Being Genuinely Tamed, Three Ways of Being Genuinely Still

A further sub-topic then opens: distinctions among being genuinely tamed, tranquil, and still, approached first from the angle of the underlying phenomena themselves. Being genuinely tamed comes in three kinds. The first, elimination, refers to an inwardly tamed mind actually eliminating unwholesome conduct already committed, no longer repeating it going forward. The second, suppression, refers to an inwardly tamed mind suppressing the actual arising of affliction, as when genuine meditative concentration prevents affliction from arising at all. And the third, severing the underlying seed, refers to an inwardly tamed mind severing affliction's own dormant seed entirely, as when attaining the noble path severs that same seed once and for all, this third kind being the most excellent taming of all. A further threefold tranquility likewise applies. The first, being undisturbed by unwholesome deliberation, refers to a genuinely tranquil mind no longer disturbed by any of the eight unwholesome trains of thought, thoughts of desire, hostility, harm, homeland, family, deathlessness, status, or contempt for others. The second, being unmoved by appearances, refers to a genuinely tranquil mind no longer stirred by the many appearances of form, sound, smell, taste, and touch, or by craving, aversion, delusion, and the distinctions of gender. And the third, spontaneous inward joy, refers to a genuinely tranquil mind that rests in constant, spontaneous joy and ease, a mind that has attained the first absorption and beyond dwelling free of dullness, agitation, and restlessness, evenly and uprightly poised, its joy arising entirely of its own accord, and a mind that has attained the noble path itself remaining constantly free of affliction, joined always to joy, pleasure, or equanimity.

Chinese Source

復次，有三種調善。一、除遣故；二、制伏故；三、害隨眠故。復有三種寂靜。一、諸惡尋思不能擾故；二、不為諸相所動亂故；三、任運於內常喜樂故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being genuinely tamed comes in three kinds: elimination, suppression, and severing the underlying seed. Tranquility likewise comes in three kinds: being undisturbed by unwholesome deliberation, being unmoved by appearances, and spontaneous inward joy.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the threefold taming as this treatise's own precise map of spiritual progress measured by depth rather than mere appearance, a practitioner who has simply stopped repeating an unwholesome act, a practitioner whose concentration has suppressed affliction from arising at all, and a practitioner who has severed affliction's own seed at the root all looking outwardly similar yet standing at genuinely different depths of actual accomplishment. He highlights spontaneous inward joy's own defining word, spontaneous, as the whole threefold tranquility's own culminating insight, a joy that no longer requires any effort to sustain being the surest sign that genuine tranquility, rather than mere effortful restraint, has actually been reached.

6. Section: Bodily, Verbal, and Mental Stillness, Culminating in Cessation-Attainment

A further threefold stillness closes this same discussion of the underlying phenomena themselves. Bodily stillness refers most precisely to the stilling of the breath, the fourth absorption's own breath grown entirely motionless, though a being who has entered a formless attainment or been reborn into a formless realm, possessing no bodily activity at all, may likewise be said to have attained bodily stillness in a further sense. Verbal stillness refers to speech itself falling still, already accomplished upon attaining the first absorption if speech alone is meant, or, if inner mental speech is included as well, only accomplished upon attaining the second absorption and beyond. And mental stillness refers to any of five states in which the sixth consciousness, discriminating mind itself, temporarily ceases to function, a fainting spell, an extremely deep sleep, the attainment of mindless concentration, rebirth into a mindless heaven, or the attainment of cessation itself, each temporarily halting discrimination, though genuine mental stillness in the fullest sense refers specifically to cessation-attainment, in which not only the sixth consciousness but the seventh consciousness bound up with the sense of self likewise temporarily ceases to function altogether. **This closes the discussion of being tamed, tranquil, and still approached from the angle of the underlying phenomena themselves.**

Chinese Source

復有三種寂止。一、身寂止，二、語寂止，三、意寂止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Stillness likewise comes in three kinds: bodily stillness, verbal stillness, and mental stillness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fivefold catalogue of mental stillness as this treatise's own careful refusal to let a merely unconscious state, a faint or a deep sleep among them, be mistaken for genuine spiritual attainment, cessation-attainment alone, in which even the subtle seventh consciousness bound to self-view falls still, earning the name of mental stillness in its fullest and most exacting sense. He highlights the progression from bodily through verbal to mental stillness as tracing an increasingly subtle path inward, the breath's own motion the coarsest thing to still, discrimination itself the last and most difficult.

7. Section: Three Kinds of Seeker on the Path Toward Genuine Peace

A further angle then opens: these same distinctions of taming, tranquility, and stillness considered from the standpoint of the actual persons who embody them. Three kinds of brahmacārin are distinguished first, brahmacārin here naming anyone whose heart genuinely aspires toward brahma, a term carrying two related senses, freedom from desire and the peace of nirvana itself. The person progressing toward the goal refers to a practitioner on any of the four paths of progress, toward the first, second, third, or fourth fruit, still advancing rather than having yet arrived. The person abiding in a fruit already attained refers to a practitioner who has actually reached the first, second, or third of these fruits. And the person who has reached the very end refers to the arhat, the fourth and final fruit, who has nothing further left to learn. The wandering ascetic likewise comes in these same three kinds, progressing toward the goal, abiding in a fruit attained, or having reached the very end, this same term carrying its own connotation of diligent, sustained effort in stilling unwholesome states.

Chinese Source

復有三種梵志。一、趣向梵志，二、住果梵志，三、到究竟梵志。如是沙門亦有三種。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are three kinds of brahmacārin: one progressing toward the goal, one abiding in a fruit already attained, and one who has reached the very end. The wandering ascetic likewise comes in these same three kinds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold division, progressing, abiding, and having reached the end, as a structure this treatise applies with striking consistency across many different vocabularies throughout the śrāvaka canon, the same underlying map of spiritual progress recognizable beneath whichever specific term, brahmacārin, wandering ascetic, or another entirely, a given passage happens to use. He highlights the etymology behind brahmacārin itself, a heart genuinely aspiring toward freedom from desire and toward nirvana together, as clarifying that this entire threefold division was never meant as a merely external classification but as a map of a single sustained inward aspiration at different stages of its own fulfillment.

8. Section: Three Kinds of Brahmin, Only One Defined by Genuine Conduct

A further threefold distinction concerns the brahmin, a term this same treatise applies in three genuinely different senses. The nominal brahmin refers to anyone merely called a brahmin, whether by others' custom or by simple self-designation, as when a people from beyond the northern Himalayas styled themselves brahmins without belonging to that lineage at all. The caste brahmin refers to anyone actually born into a brahmin family by birth alone. And the brahmin of genuine conduct refers to someone who has actually driven out unwholesome states and taken up wholesome practice instead, cultivating right conduct in this world and beyond it, drawing near to genuine spiritual friends, listening to the true teaching, reflecting on it properly, practicing in accordance with that same teaching, carefully guarding the sense-faculties, keeping bodily, verbal, and mental conduct free of fault, diligently applying wakeful vigilance, and dwelling always in clear awareness; only this third kind, defined by actual conduct rather than by name or birth alone, genuinely earns the name brahmin in its fullest sense. Historically, the brahmin caste occupied the highest station among the four traditional castes of India, priests and scholars believed to have been born from the very mouth of the creator deity Brahmā himself, entrusted uniquely with the study, teaching, and performance of the Vedic sacrifices that the other castes, apart from the lowest, might recite for themselves but never perform on another's behalf; a brahmin's own traditional life unfolded across four successive stages, the first of which, the stage of celibate study, began at age eight and continued twelve years, devoted entirely to studying the Vedas and mastering their sacrificial rites.

Chinese Source

復有三種婆羅門。一、假名婆羅門，二、種姓婆羅門，三、正行婆羅門。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are three kinds of brahmin: the nominal brahmin, the caste brahmin, and the brahmin of genuine conduct.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold distinction as this treatise's own deliberate reclaiming of a term deeply rooted in India's own caste system, insisting that genuine spiritual standing belongs only to the third kind, defined entirely by actual conduct, while the mere name and the accident of birth alone, however socially prestigious, earn the title in name only. He highlights the extensive historical background on the traditional brahmin caste as this treatise's own way of making the underlying point unmistakable to its original audience, since redefining so exalted and privileged a term around conduct rather than birth would have carried genuine social force in a way a merely abstract argument about virtue could not.

9. Section: Four Stages of a Brahmin's Life, Then What Corrupts a Monastic's Own Discipline

A brahmin's traditional life continues through three further stages beyond the first period of celibate study. The householder stage follows, a brahmin returning home to marry, raise children, honor departed ancestors, and manage ordinary worldly affairs. The forest-dweller stage follows in old age, a brahmin passing the family estate on to a son and retiring into the forest to practice asceticism and devote themselves wholly to reflection and religious life. And the wandering-renunciant stage completes this same sequence, a brahmin renouncing every worldly attachment entirely, wearing coarse robes, carrying only a water vessel, and wandering from place to place; a considerable number of practices belonging to this final stage were later adopted into Buddhist monastic life as well, wandering, alms-begging, and the rains retreat among them, monastics of this same wandering-renunciant type themselves sometimes called by the very terms bhikṣu, śramaṇa, or wanderer that Buddhism later made its own. **With this closes the entire discussion of six distinct sets of three, taming, tranquility, stillness, the brahmacārin, the wandering ascetic, and the brahmin, the second of the five sub-topics gathered under the eighth topic of miscellaneous meanings.** A further sub-topic then opens: the provisions genuinely needed for renunciation, beginning with what actually corrupts a monastic's own discipline. Four distinct causes are named: committing an unpardonable offense; failing to confess and purify a pardonable offense already committed, or failing even to recall and reflect on whether such an offense occurred at all; wrongly regarding what is genuinely no violation as a violation, as when Devadatta established his own five spurious ascetic rules never actually prescribed by the Buddha, insisting that lifelong alms-begging, wearing only discarded cloth, sitting only beneath trees, and refusing ghee, salt, fish, and meat were themselves required precepts; and wrongly regarding what is genuinely a violation as no violation at all.

Chinese Source

復次，由四因緣，令尸羅壞；尸羅壞故，依止尸羅所應生善皆不得生。謂於無餘罪起毀犯故；於有餘罪不悔除故，於諸所犯不憶念故；於無犯中執有犯故；於有犯中執無犯故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four causes corrupt discipline, and once corrupted, the wholesome qualities that depend on discipline cannot arise: committing an unpardonable offense; failing to confess a pardonable offense or even recall whether one occurred; wrongly regarding a non-violation as violation; and wrongly regarding a violation as non-violation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Devadatta reference as this treatise's own vivid historical warning against a particular kind of well-intentioned error, mistaking an invented, more severe standard for genuine precept, since a fabricated rule can corrupt discipline just as surely as an actual violation once a practitioner begins measuring themselves and others against a standard the Buddha himself never set. He highlights the pairing of this same error with its mirror opposite, wrongly excusing a genuine violation, as showing that discipline's corruption runs in both directions at once, an overly rigid misjudgment and an overly lax one doing comparable damage to a monastic's own wholesome development.

10. Section: Four Marks of Fulfilled Discipline, Then Right View, Deportment, and Livelihood

Precisely the reverse of these same four causes constitutes discipline genuinely fulfilled: never committing an unpardonable offense; confessing and purifying any pardonable offense that does occur; correctly recognizing a genuine non-violation as no violation at all; and correctly recognizing a genuine violation as a violation. **This closes the discussion of receiving and upholding discipline in its fullness.** A further discussion then opens: what makes the discipline one has actually received genuinely pure and complete, traced to two distinct causes, purity of companion factors and purity of intrinsic nature. Purity of companion factors itself comprises three further elements: purity of view, purity of proper deportment, and purity of livelihood. Purity of view refers to holding correct understanding, genuinely knowing the substance and characteristics of discipline itself, what is permitted and what is prohibited, the proper means of confession, and the various further distinctions bound up with reverence toward the Buddha and similar matters, correct view standing always as discipline's own necessary foundation. Purity of proper deportment refers to conduct in walking, standing, sitting, and lying down, in undertaking any task, and in every occasion for wholesome practice, that neither violates the ordinary conventions of the world nor the specific rules the vinaya itself establishes, including never frequenting any of the five improper venues already named earlier in this fascicle's discussion of speaking of a fellow monastic's faults.

Chinese Source

由四因緣，名戒具足；與上相違，應知其相。復次，由二因緣，令所受戒清淨具足。一、助伴清淨故；二、自性清淨故。云何名為助伴清淨？謂見清淨、軌清淨、命清淨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four causes constitute discipline fulfilled, the reverse of the four causes of corruption. Two causes make received discipline genuinely pure: purity of companion factors and purity of intrinsic nature. Purity of companion factors comprises purity of view, purity of proper deportment, and purity of livelihood.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the mirrored structure between corrupted and fulfilled discipline as this treatise's own consistent teaching method throughout this entire fascicle, a practitioner who has genuinely understood one side of the pairing thereby already possessing everything needed to recognize its opposite as well. He highlights purity of view's own placement first among the three companion factors as no accident, correct understanding of discipline itself necessarily preceding correct conduct and correct livelihood, since a practitioner who misunderstands what discipline actually requires cannot reliably embody it in either deportment or livelihood no matter how sincere the underlying intention.

11. Section: Four Wrongful Ways of Earning a Living, Then Complete Fidelity to Every Precept Received

Purity of livelihood, the third companion factor, refers to genuinely knowing that the source of one's robes, food, dwelling, and any money involved is entirely lawful, obtained through none of five wrongful means, feigned dignified bearing, deceptive speech, a false display of spiritual attainment, harsh self-affliction meant to impress others, or seeking further gain by advertising gain already received, and free as well from four specific wrongful ways of obtaining food. The first, food from looking downward, refers to earning a livelihood through farming or through mixing medicines. The second, food from looking upward, refers to earning a livelihood through astrology, astronomy, or the reading of wind, rain, thunder, and similar natural signs. The third, food from the four directions, refers to earning a livelihood through flattering the powerful, serving as a messenger between distant parties, or through cunning speech aimed at securing repeated favor. And the fourth, food from the four corners, refers to earning a livelihood through mantras, divination, and fortune-telling. A monastic whose livelihood remains free of all these wrongful means, along with genuine purity of view and proper deportment, thereby fulfills purity of companion factors in full. Purity of intrinsic nature, the second of the two causes making received discipline pure, refers to reverent and complete compliance with every precept actually received, a monastic who has received the full two hundred fifty precepts of a monk, or the full three hundred forty-eight precepts of a nun, upholding every one of them in its entirety, with genuine reverence and without partial or selective observance, this same reverent completeness itself constituting purity of intrinsic nature.

Chinese Source

云何自性清淨？謂恭敬隨學、具分隨學。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purity of intrinsic nature? Reverent compliance and complete compliance with the precepts received.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourfold catalogue of wrongful food as this treatise's own remarkably comprehensive sweep across nearly every conceivable exploitative use of religious standing for material gain, farming and fortune-telling, astrology and flattery of the powerful, all gathered under this single shared category precisely because each substitutes worldly cunning for the simple, transparent almsround a monastic's livelihood is meant to depend on instead. He highlights purity of intrinsic nature's own closing emphasis on completeness, upholding every single received precept rather than a convenient selection, as this whole discussion's own final and most demanding standard, reverence toward discipline measured not by sincerity of feeling alone but by the actual, comprehensive scope of what a practitioner genuinely upholds.

12. Section: Seeking Liberation, Never a Heavenly Rebirth, as Discipline's Own Genuine Motivation

A further threefold cause likewise makes received discipline genuinely pure: purity of motivation, purity of livelihood, and purity of conduct. Purity of motivation refers to cultivating pure conduct for the sake of liberation, freeing oneself from craving and views bound to the three realms and realizing nirvana, or freeing oneself from the two obstructions of affliction and cognition and realizing unsurpassed enlightenment, and never for the sake of a mere heavenly rebirth instead; a practitioner whose underlying motivation for receiving and upholding discipline is genuinely aimed at liberation rather than at securing pleasant future rebirth alone possesses purity of motivation in this fullest sense.

Chinese Source

復有差別。謂三因緣，令所受戒清淨具足。一、意樂清淨故；二、命清淨故；三、行清淨故。云何意樂清淨？謂為解脫修行梵行，不為生天。

Ru-Yi Translation

A further threefold cause makes received discipline genuinely pure: purity of motivation, purity of livelihood, and purity of conduct. What is purity of motivation? Cultivating pure conduct for the sake of liberation, never for the sake of a heavenly rebirth.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this threefold cause as running parallel to, rather than duplicating, the twofold cause just completed, motivation, livelihood, and conduct together addressing a practitioner's inward aim, outward means of support, and actual ongoing behavior as three genuinely distinct dimensions of the very same underlying discipline. He highlights the explicit rejection of heavenly rebirth as an acceptable motivation as this treatise's own consistent insistence that discipline exists in service of genuine liberation alone, a comfortable future rebirth, however far preferable to an unfortunate one, still falling short of the actual purpose discipline is meant to serve.

13. Section: A Monk Who Belittles a Modest Gift to Shame the Donor into Giving More

Purity of livelihood, the second of these three causes, refers to seeking one's living lawfully through proper begging alone. Lawful begging itself comprises two aspects: seeking as one properly should, and seeking from those one properly should. Seeking as one properly should is further explained through five distinct points. The first, avoiding deceptive display, means never feigning a dignified, desireless bearing before others purely to display one's own apparent virtue and thereby attract offerings. The second, avoiding flattering speech, means never boasting of one's own learning or attainment, whether genuine or merely embellished, in order to secure support rather than out of any genuine wish to teach. The third, avoiding a false display of poverty, means never deliberately wearing old and shabby robes despite having no actual lack, purely to appear especially frugal and thereby draw a donor's particular sympathy and generosity. The fourth, avoiding coercive pressure, means never badgering a donor with repeated requests or harsh words when a hoped-for offering fails to materialize, wearing the donor down through sheer persistent pressure until they relent from simple exhaustion rather than genuine willingness. And the fifth, never using gain to seek further gain, means never disparaging a gift already received in hopes of shaming the donor into giving more, as when a monastic receiving some modest offering complains to the donor directly that another far poorer benefactor still managed to give so much more generously, implying the present donor, despite greater apparent means, has given shamefully little by comparison.

Chinese Source

云何名為如所應求？謂不矯詐而有所求，亦不綺說而有所求，亦不現相而有所求，亦不抑逼而有所求，亦不以利而希於利。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is seeking as one properly should? Never seeking through deceptive display, never through flattering speech, never through a false display of poverty, never through coercive pressure, and never using gain already received to seek further gain.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fifth point's own vivid illustration, a monastic openly shaming a donor by comparing their gift unfavorably to a poorer benefactor's greater generosity, as this entire catalogue's own most uncomfortably specific example, precisely because it shows corrupted livelihood taking a form that could easily masquerade as simple honesty rather than obvious deception. He highlights the underlying thread running through all five points, that any request motivated by defiled self-interest corrupts livelihood regardless of its outward form, while the same words spoken from a genuinely undefiled wish to benefit others, without any actual desire for gain attached, remain entirely blameless.

14. Section: Constant Mindfulness and Swift Confession Close the Discipline Category

Seeking from those one properly should, the second aspect of lawful begging, refers simply to avoiding the same five improper venues already named earlier in this fascicle, a household connected to ritual slaughter, a brothel, a tavern, a royal court, or an outcaste butcher's dwelling, seeking support instead only from households whose own means are lawfully obtained. **This closes the discussion of purity of livelihood.** Purity of conduct, the third and final cause, refers to a practitioner's own deep conviction that violation brings genuinely unwanted results, sustaining mindful reflection on the teaching whether walking on the almsround or dwelling in the monastery, never deliberately committing a violation; should some violation nonetheless occur, that same practitioner immediately confesses and purifies it before a fellow monastic exactly as the Dharma prescribes, vowing thereafter to guard the sense-faculties firmly and never repeat the same fault again. **This closes the entire discussion of purity of conduct, and with it the entire threefold cause of purified discipline, the entire discussion of discipline's own fullness and purity together, and the first of the four categories, the precept-discipline category, gathered under the provisions genuinely needed for renunciation.**

Chinese Source

云何行清淨？謂深信所犯有不愛果，若行、若住繫念思惟，終不故犯；設有所犯，即便從他如法悔除，誓於當來堅牢防護。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is purity of conduct? Deep conviction that violation brings unwanted results, sustained mindful reflection whether walking or dwelling, never deliberately violating; should violation occur, immediately confessing and purifying it as the Dharma prescribes, vowing firm guard against repeating it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the closing vow, guarding firmly against repeating the same fault rather than merely confessing it and moving on, as purity of conduct's own decisive test, distinguishing genuine repentance from an empty ritual repeated daily without any real change in underlying behavior, a distinction this treatise has returned to more than once across its discussion of confession throughout the vinaya's own thirteen divisions. He highlights the completion of this entire precept-discipline category as a genuinely substantial accomplishment in its own right, four full causes, corruption avoided, companion purity, intrinsic purity, and this final threefold cause together, now established before the provisions for renunciation even turn to the three further categories still to come.

15. Section: Four Ways Forgetfulness Undermines a Monastic Guarding the Sense-Doors

A further category then opens: the sense-faculty discipline category, the second of the four categories gathered under the provisions needed for renunciation. A monastic genuinely diligent in guarding the sense-doors, preventing the six senses from reaching out toward their objects and giving rise to affliction, should understand four distinct aspects of the fault of forgetfulness, along with four further aspects of the fault of improper attention. The four aspects of forgetfulness are named first: deficient mindfulness, inferior mindfulness, lost mindfulness, and confused mindfulness. Deficient mindfulness refers to a genuine lack of mindfulness altogether. Inferior mindfulness refers to mindfulness present but weak and easily overpowered. Lost mindfulness refers to mindfulness that was present but has since slipped away entirely. And confused mindfulness refers to mindfulness that has become genuinely inverted, mistaking what is actually undefiled for something defiled, or mistaking what is actually defiled for something undefiled. A monastic seeking to guard the sense-doors should understand that each of these four distinct failures of mindfulness carries its own genuine fault.

Chinese Source

復次，若有苾芻欲勤加行密護根門，應以四相了知忘念過失，及以四相了知不如理作意。云何四相了知忘念過失？一、闕念，二、劣念，三、失念，四、亂念。

Ru-Yi Translation

A monastic diligently guarding the sense-doors should understand four aspects of the fault of forgetfulness, along with four aspects of the fault of improper attention. What are the four aspects of forgetfulness? Deficient mindfulness, inferior mindfulness, lost mindfulness, and confused mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold catalogue of forgetfulness as this treatise's own unusually fine-grained diagnostic tool, distinguishing mindfulness never established, mindfulness present but too weak to hold, mindfulness that held for a time and then slipped, and mindfulness that has actively inverted itself, four genuinely different failures a less careful analysis might collapse into a single undifferentiated lapse of attention. He highlights confused mindfulness as this fourfold list's own most dangerous member, since a practitioner suffering from the first three failures at least knows something has gone wrong, while one suffering from confused mindfulness may feel entirely confident and mindful while actually mistaking defilement for its opposite.

16. Section: Knowing but Not Doing, the Difference Between Inferior and Lost Mindfulness

Each of the four aspects of forgetfulness is now explained in turn. Deficient mindfulness refers to a practitioner who has never actually studied the method of guarding the sense-doors, never listened to it, never accepted it, or never properly understood it, and so genuinely lacks the mindfulness needed to guard those same doors at all, not yet even knowing why sense-restraint matters or how to practice it. Inferior mindfulness refers to a practitioner who has listened to this same method, accepted it, and genuinely understood it, yet fails to put it into constant, thorough practice, never cultivating, habituating, or repeatedly practicing it in actual daily life; such a practitioner knows what to do but fails to actually do it, a fault genuinely distinct from deficient mindfulness's own simple lack of knowledge. Lost mindfulness refers to a practitioner who does cultivate and repeatedly practice this same guarding, yet occasionally still acts without proper understanding in a given moment, a temporary lapse rather than any settled failure to know or to practice. And confused mindfulness refers to a mind that has become genuinely inverted, regarding the undefiled teachings of discipline, concentration, and wisdom as though they were themselves defiled, while regarding some genuinely defiled worldly pursuit, such as clever secular argumentation, as though it were undefiled instead. Each of these four distinct failures, a practitioner should understand, prevents the sense-doors from being genuinely guarded.

Chinese Source

闕念者，謂於密護諸根門法，不聽、不受、不善了知。劣念者，謂於彼法雖聽、雖受、雖善了知，而不常作，非委悉作，若修、若習、若多修習。失念者，謂雖修習亦多修習，然或有時不正了知而有所行。亂念者，謂即於彼非雜染中生雜染想，雜染中生非雜染想。

Ru-Yi Translation

Deficient mindfulness: never having studied the method of sense-restraint at all. Inferior mindfulness: understanding it but failing to practice it constantly. Lost mindfulness: practicing it constantly yet occasionally lapsing. Confused mindfulness: mistaking the undefiled for defiled, or the defiled for undefiled.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between inferior and lost mindfulness as this catalogue's own most practically useful pairing, since both describe a practitioner who genuinely knows the method, yet one fails through simple lack of sustained habituation while the other fails only through an occasional lapse within an otherwise well-established practice, two situations that call for genuinely different remedies. He highlights confused mindfulness's own example, secular cleverness mistaken for something undefiled, as a caution any modern reader can readily recognize, worldly wit and eloquence carrying a prestige that can easily be mistaken for genuine virtue by a mind not yet vigilant enough to tell the two apart.

17. Section: An Alluring Face Seen in Parts, the First Way Improper Attention Breeds Affliction

A further fourfold analysis then opens: four aspects of the fault of improper attention. The first, being a cause generating affliction, the second, co-arising with defilement, the third, destroying one's own sense of shame, and the fourth, giving rise to confused violation, are named first before each is explained in turn. Being a cause generating affliction refers to a practitioner who, encountering some object, grasps at its overall appealing appearance and further grasps at each of its particular alluring details in turn, and through this same grasping, unwholesome states such as craving inevitably follow the mind wherever that same object is encountered, the mind itself growing scattered and unrestrained. A practitioner who encounters an attractive person, for instance, first grasps the overall impression of beauty, this being the general appearance, and then dwells further on each particular alluring feature in turn, captivating eyes, a fine profile, an appealing smile, this being the particular alluring detail; through this same twofold grasping, craving and attachment inevitably follow, the mind growing scattered and unrestrained in that same person's presence.

Chinese Source

云何以四相了知不如理作意？一、是煩惱生因；二、與雜染生相應；三、毀壞羞恥；四、起錯亂犯。煩惱生因者，謂如有一，執取於相、執取隨好，由此因緣，於是處所惡不善法隨心流逸。

Ru-Yi Translation

Improper attention carries four aspects: being a cause generating affliction, co-arising with defilement, destroying one's own sense of shame, and giving rise to confused violation. Being a cause generating affliction refers to grasping an object's overall appearance and its particular alluring details, through which unwholesome states inevitably follow, the mind growing scattered.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction between grasping an overall appearance and grasping its particular alluring details as this treatise's own precise anatomy of how attraction actually builds, a single overall impression alone rarely sufficient to fully capture the mind until attention lingers further on this or that specific captivating feature in turn. He highlights this same twofold grasping as the direct target of formal sense-restraint practice, a monastic trained to notice the very moment attention begins settling on particular alluring details being far better positioned to interrupt the process before craving has fully taken hold.

18. Section: Shamelessness Breeds Violation, Closing the Sense-Faculty Discipline Category

Three further aspects close this same fourfold analysis. Co-arising with defilement refers simply to unwholesome states actually arising together with body, speech, and mind once improper attention has taken hold, affliction no longer remaining merely latent but now actively present alongside a practitioner's own conduct. Destroying one's own sense of shame refers to a practitioner who, mind now joined with craving, aversion, or delusion, feels no genuine shame where shame is actually warranted, remaining entirely unembarrassed even as these same unwholesome states stand plainly present. And giving rise to confused violation refers to the direct consequence of this same absence of shame, a practitioner who, having lost all sense of shame, either goes on to actually violate the very precept in question or begins seriously entertaining the thought of abandoning the training altogether. Taken together, these four aspects trace a single continuous decline: improper attention first generates affliction, that affliction then arises openly alongside conduct rather than remaining hidden, the resulting shamelessness leaves no inner check remaining, and this same shamelessness finally issues in outright violation or even abandonment of the path, a practitioner left with no recourse but continued wandering in birth and death. **This closes the entire discussion of the sense-faculty discipline category, the second of the four categories gathered under the provisions needed for renunciation.** A further category then opens: moderation in eating, the third of these four categories, requiring the elimination of eight specific things before genuine moderation in eating can properly be said to be fulfilled.

Chinese Source

起錯亂犯者，謂即因彼無羞恥故，或犯所犯罪，或思捨所學。復次，於食知量勤修行者，斷除八處，乃名具足於食知量。何等為八？

Ru-Yi Translation

Giving rise to confused violation refers to the direct consequence of shamelessness: either actually violating the precept in question, or seriously entertaining the thought of abandoning the training. A practitioner genuinely moderate in eating must eliminate eight specific things. What are the eight?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these four aspects of improper attention as tracing a single unbroken causal chain rather than four independent faults, grasping an object's appeal generating affliction, affliction surfacing openly, the resulting shamelessness dissolving any remaining inner restraint, and that same shamelessness finally issuing in an actual violation or the abandonment of practice altogether, a progression closely mirroring the very same obstruction-chain this treatise traced earlier in fascicle 69's own discussion of violation. He highlights the completion of this entire sense-faculty discipline category as marking real progress through the fourfold structure of renunciation's own provisions, discipline and sense-restraint now both established, moderation in eating and wakefulness still remaining ahead.

19. Section: Eight Things to Eliminate, Closing Moderation in Eating

The eight things to eliminate for genuine moderation in eating are now named and explained. The first, craving food itself, refers to an indulgent attachment to eating that only breeds further fault the more it is indulged, Subhūti himself once declaring that craving food breeds sorrow rather than any genuine pursuit of the Dharma, just as seeking praise, seeking bodily comfort, or clinging protectively to one's own life can never themselves be called seeking the Dharma either. The second, craving one's own body, refers to eating purely to adorn or beautify one's own physical appearance rather than to sustain genuine practice. The third, destroying the life-faculty, refers to eating so far beyond or so far short of what the body actually needs, whether through habitual overindulgence or through refusing food altogether, that the body itself fails before the path has even been accomplished. The fourth, weakness from hunger, refers to eating too little, leaving the body too weak and depleted to sustain practice at all. The fifth, heaviness from excess, refers to eating too much, leaving the body sluggish, prone to weight gain, and ill-suited to sustained practice. The sixth, inviting illness, refers to eating improper food that brings on sickness, since illness so often enters, as the saying goes, through the very mouth that speaks careless words. The seventh, impure livelihood, refers to resorting to improper means purely to obtain especially fine food. And the eighth, excessive activity, refers to taking on far more worldly business than necessary purely to satisfy a craving for especially fine food. A practitioner who eliminates all eight of these fully accomplishes moderation in eating, taking food only to sustain the body, to briefly relieve hunger and thirst, to support pure conduct, to end an already-arisen discomfort, to prevent a new discomfort from arising, and to preserve the strength, ease, and blamelessness genuine practice requires. **This closes the entire discussion of moderation in eating, the third of the four categories gathered under the provisions needed for renunciation.**

Chinese Source

一、耽著飲食，二、耽著自身，三、命根壞滅，四、饑劣，五、身重，六、非無病，七、命不清淨，八、多營事業。若能斷處以上所說八種過患，能使於食知量具足圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

The eight things to eliminate: craving food, craving one's own body, destroying the life-faculty, weakness from hunger, heaviness from excess, inviting illness, impure livelihood, and excessive activity. Eliminating these eight fully accomplishes moderation in eating.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Subhūti's own citation, that craving food breeds sorrow rather than any genuine pursuit of the Dharma, as this entire eightfold catalogue's own governing spirit, eating treated neither as an indulgence to be maximized nor an enemy to be starved into submission but simply as a bare, practical support for a task that lies entirely elsewhere. He highlights the third and fourth items together, destroying the life-faculty through excess and weakening it through deprivation, as showing this treatise's own consistent commitment to a genuine middle way even in something as basic as eating, both extremes equally condemned rather than asceticism favored over indulgence.

20. Section: Sit Like a Bell, the First Way Wakefulness-Yoga Guards Against Fatigue

A further category then opens: wakefulness-yoga, the fourth and final of the four categories gathered under the provisions needed for renunciation, likewise requiring the elimination of eight specific faults before it can properly be said to be fulfilled. The first fault, bodily fatigue from improper posture, concerns a practitioner cultivating calming and insight who fails to sit in the proper cross-legged position capable of being sustained for a genuinely long time, leaning instead against a wall or slumping from simple laziness; a saying holds that walking should resemble the wind and sitting should resemble a bell, steady and unmoving, and a practitioner who keeps the spine genuinely upright finds the breath moving freely of its own accord, while a practitioner who slouches or leans soon finds the breath obstructed and the body quickly overcome by fatigue, dullness, and drowsiness. An ordinary being's own restless mind often makes sustained upright sitting genuinely difficult, a persistent sense of insufficient energy tempting a practitioner to lean against some support, yet this same leaning only worsens matters further, the properly cross-legged posture alone allowing a practitioner to sit for a genuinely long time without undue fatigue, the body's own subtle energy moving smoothly through a spine held upright without any special exertion required to hold it there.

Chinese Source

復次，常勤修習覺悟瑜伽者，斷除八處，乃得名為常勤修習覺悟瑜伽正行具足。何等為八？一、由威儀其身疲弊。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner constantly diligent in wakefulness-yoga must eliminate eight faults before this practice can be called fulfilled. What are the eight? The first: bodily fatigue arising from improper posture.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the walking-like-wind, sitting-like-a-bell saying as this treatise's own most memorable image for proper meditative posture, a bell's own steady, grounded stillness capturing exactly the upright, unmoving quality genuine sitting practice requires without the strain a merely rigid or forced posture would introduce instead. He highlights the observation that an upright spine allows the breath to move freely without special exertion as this whole point's own practical payoff, correct posture treated not as an arbitrary formality but as the actual physical condition that makes sustained practice possible in the first place.

21. Section: A Monk Who Never Returns to Bed After Waking

Three further faults continue this same eightfold catalogue. The second, delighting in reclining and finding pleasure in sleep, refers to a habitual fondness for lying down at every available opportunity, seizing any free moment to rest rather than sustaining wakeful practice; one particularly disciplined practitioner is remembered for never once returning to bed after rising each morning, resting only briefly seated at midday and not lying down again until night, a simple habit sustained unwaveringly for decades and well worth learning from. The third, following defiled appearances, refers to improper attention joined with affliction, since a mind actively caught up in affliction has no capacity left to cultivate calming and insight. And the fourth, failing to diligently cultivate antidotes to defilement, refers to a practitioner who, faced with craving, aversion, delusion, or any other affliction, simply fails to apply the proper corrective practice, leaving calming and insight equally impossible to sustain.

Chinese Source

二、愛味偃臥，睡眠為樂；三、隨雜染相；四、不勤修習雜染對治。

Ru-Yi Translation

The second: delighting in reclining and finding pleasure in sleep. The third: following defiled appearances. The fourth: failing to diligently cultivate the antidote to defilement.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the story of the practitioner who never returned to bed after morning rising as this treatise's own concrete, human-scale illustration of what the second fault's own avoidance actually looks like in an ordinary life, decades of unwavering simple discipline offered as more persuasive than any abstract description could be on its own. He highlights the third and fourth faults together as showing this same discussion returning once more to the theme of antidotes already explored in the sense-faculty discipline category, wakefulness itself found to depend on the same underlying vigilance against defilement that guards the sense-doors.

22. Section: Falling Asleep with Amitābha in Mind, Closing the Provisions' Own Fullness

The final four faults close out this eightfold catalogue and, with it, the entire discussion of what the provisions for renunciation require in their fullness. The fifth, waking at an improper time, refers to waking too early, as when a practitioner wakes at one or two in the morning and, by the time the proper hour for morning practice arrives, finds their own strength already spent and drowsiness returning instead. The sixth, wastefully squandering waking hours, refers to a practitioner who, once genuinely awake, allows a defiled mind to arise and fails to diligently apply calming and insight, mindfulness altogether absent, time itself wasted on matters that bring no genuine benefit to the path. The seventh, sleeping at an improper time, refers to retiring either too early or too late relative to the community's own proper schedule, a practitioner who retires hours before the community's own appointed rest and wakes again in the middle of the night, or one who delays sleep well past the appointed hour and finds themselves nodding off during the very next morning's practice from simple exhaustion. And the eighth, sleeping wastefully, refers to falling asleep with a defiled mind rather than genuine mindfulness; a practitioner should instead fall asleep reciting the Buddha, the Dharma, the Sangha, and every wholesome quality, since should death itself arrive unexpectedly during sleep, sustained mindfulness of Amitābha Buddha in particular carries the practitioner onward at that very moment, received into his pure land rather than lost to a mind gone unguarded, and there is accordingly no need to fear falling asleep at all so long as this same mindfulness is genuinely sustained; a practitioner should always remember to fall asleep mindfully, never wastefully. **This closes the entire discussion of wakefulness-yoga, the fourth and final category gathered under the provisions needed for renunciation, and with it the entire discussion of these provisions' own fullness in every one of its four categories: discipline, sense-faculty restraint, moderation in eating, and wakefulness-yoga together.**

Chinese Source

五、非時而覺；六、虛棄而覺；七、非時而眠；八、虛棄而眠。若能斷除如上所說八種過失，才能使常勤修習覺悟瑜伽的正行具足。

Ru-Yi Translation

The fifth: waking at an improper time. The sixth: wastefully squandering waking hours. The seventh: sleeping at an improper time. The eighth: sleeping wastefully. Eliminating all eight faults as described fulfills wakefulness-yoga's own genuine practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eighth fault's own closing teaching, falling asleep mindful of Amitābha Buddha so that even death arriving unexpectedly during sleep becomes an occasion for rebirth in his pure land rather than something to fear, as this entire discussion's own quietly reassuring conclusion, sleep itself transformed from a mere biological necessity into a genuine field of practice. He highlights the completion of all four categories, discipline, sense-restraint, moderation in eating, and wakefulness together, as marking real structural progress through the provisions for renunciation, only their genuine fullness now established, their still-remaining completeness in a fuller sense yet to come.

23. Section: Six Kinds of Renunciation Needed to Establish Every Renunciation-Ground

A further discussion then opens: the provisions for renunciation considered in a fuller sense still, beginning with an overall account of renunciation itself. Six kinds of renunciation, it is explained, together establish every ground of renunciation from the three realms. The first, non-conducive renunciation, names whatever fails to accord with genuine practice and so must itself be renounced. The second, deficient renunciation, names an absence of the genuine supporting conditions practice requires, a genuine teacher, the true teaching actually available to hear, or the basic material requisites practice depends upon, each lack itself something to be overcome through gathering sufficient provisions. The third, household renunciation, carries two related senses, renunciation of household life through ordination itself, and, in a further figurative sense, renunciation of affliction, affliction itself likened to a household a practitioner must leave behind. The fourth, incomplete renunciation, names discipline, concentration, and wisdom not yet brought to their own genuine completeness, a completeness a practitioner must continue cultivating toward. The fifth, lower-ground renunciation, names the desire realm specifically, overcome through cultivating and actually attaining genuine meditative concentration. And the sixth, self-view renunciation, names the view of an abiding self, overcome by relying on concentration to cultivate contemplation of the four noble truths and thereby attain the noble path itself, severing self-view along with every affliction bound up with craving and views together.

Chinese Source

復次，依六出離，應知建立諸出離地。何等為六？一、不隨順出離，二、闕出離，三、家出離，四、不圓滿出離，五、下地出離，六、薩迦耶出離。

Ru-Yi Translation

Six kinds of renunciation establish every ground of renunciation: non-conducive renunciation, deficient renunciation, household renunciation, incomplete renunciation, lower-ground renunciation, and self-view renunciation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sixfold catalogue as this treatise's own comprehensive map of everything standing between a practitioner and genuine freedom from the three realms, ranging from bare external conditions still lacking through the coarsest attachments of the desire realm to the very subtlest attachment of all, the view of an abiding self itself. He highlights household renunciation's own deliberate double meaning, ordination itself and the renunciation of affliction together, as this treatise's own reminder that the outward act of leaving home was always meant as a beginning rather than an end, the deeper household of affliction still requiring its own genuine renunciation long after the first has been accomplished.

24. Section: Five Conditions That Leave a Practitioner's Own Circumstances Non-Conducive

Non-conducive renunciation, the first and most difficult of the six kinds to explain, is now examined through five distinct supporting conditions capable of leaving a practitioner's own circumstances genuinely non-conducive to renunciation. The first, an unsuitable destiny, is explained two ways: one reading holds it to mean a being genuinely lacking the very lineage for liberation, such a being simply incapable of ever reaching nirvana regardless of effort; another reading holds it more simply to mean falling into an unfortunate rebirth, since a being fallen into the lower realms obviously cannot practice at all, this second reading judged the more reasonable of the two. The second, an unsuitable birth-circumstance, refers to being born into a location without genuine opportunity to practice, one of eight specific difficulties, including being born blind, deaf, or mute, or reborn into a mindless heaven, the realm of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, or the northern continent, or born in a remote place where the Buddha's own teaching has never spread; keeping the eight precepts of a single day and night is said to counter precisely these eight difficulties. The third, an unsuitable degree of diligence, refers to genuine laziness, since wholesome qualities cannot grow, and affliction cannot be severed or escaped, without sustained effort. The fourth, unsuitable obstruction, refers to the various obstacles created by past karmic ripening, the three obstructions of affliction, karma, and karmic retribution familiar from the common dedication verse asking that all three obstructions and every affliction be dissolved, together with further obstructions of body and mind, affliction itself so persistent and prolonged that practice becomes impossible, grave past misdeeds ripening into unfortunate rebirth, and a body itself so afflicted, through blindness, deafness, missing limbs, or chronic illness, that practice grows genuinely difficult. And the fifth, an unsuitable object of delight, refers either to delighting in distorted views that only true listening to the Dharma can dispel, or to a defiled attachment to whatever pleasant result the three realms themselves might yield.

Chinese Source

不隨順者，謂五種依止。一、趣不隨順，二、生不隨順，三、精進不隨順，四、障不隨順，五、愛樂不隨順。

Ru-Yi Translation

Non-conducive renunciation rests on five supporting conditions: an unsuitable destiny, an unsuitable birth-circumstance, an unsuitable degree of diligence, unsuitable obstruction, and an unsuitable object of delight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the eight-day-and-night precepts' own stated power to counter all eight birth-difficulties as this treatise's own practical reassurance following an otherwise sobering catalogue, a lay practitioner genuinely unable to renounce household life altogether still possessing a concrete means of working against these same unfavorable conditions rather than simply hoping to avoid them by chance. He highlights the three obstructions cited in the familiar dedication verse, affliction, karma, and retribution, as showing how thoroughly this treatise's own technical vocabulary has entered ordinary devotional practice, a verse recited daily by countless practitioners resting on exactly the same careful distinctions this fascicle has spent so much effort establishing.

25. Section: Four Missing Conditions That Leave Genuine Renunciation Out of Reach

Unsuitable obstruction, the fourth supporting condition, refers to obstacles created by unwholesome karma ripening from a former life into present hindrance; and an unsuitable object of delight, the fifth, refers to delighting in distorted views and conduct born of keeping bad company, hearing a mistaken teaching, and reflecting improperly upon it, a genuine absence of right view and right conduct alike. **This closes the discussion of non-conducive renunciation approached from a practitioner's own incompleteness.** A further discussion then opens: this same non-conducive renunciation approached instead from an incompleteness in one's own external circumstances, deficient renunciation itself resting on four missing conditions. The first, a missing spiritual friend, refers to lacking any genuine teacher or companion in practice at all, a true spiritual friend traditionally understood as combining three roles together, an instructing teacher who guides through formal teaching, a fellow practitioner who spurs continued effort by shared example, and a protecting benefactor who sustains a practitioner materially, all three together needed for genuine accomplishment. The second, missing hearing, refers to lacking any opportunity to actually hear the true teaching, without which right view and proper reflection alike remain impossible. The third, a missing teaching, refers to an age in which the Buddha's own scriptures, discipline, and treatises have already vanished from the world and no one remains who has genuinely realized them, a genuine age of decline in which practice grows correspondingly difficult. And the fourth, missing donor support, refers to lacking robes, food, bedding, and medicine from any sincere benefactor, a practitioner unable even to sustain daily life left with no real opportunity to practice at all. **With every one of these conditions genuinely fulfilled, a renunciant is finally able to practice in earnest, closing the entire overall discussion of renunciation itself.**

Chinese Source

闕者，謂闕四種緣。一、親友闕；二、聽聞闕；三、隱沒闕，若教、若證皆隱沒故；四、施主臥具闕。

Ru-Yi Translation

Deficient renunciation rests on four missing conditions: a missing spiritual friend, missing hearing, a missing teaching in an age when both its formal instruction and its actual realization have vanished, and missing donor support.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the traditional threefold definition of a genuine spiritual friend, an instructing teacher, a fellow practitioner, and a protecting benefactor together, as this treatise's own reminder that no single relationship, however excellent, can supply everything a practitioner genuinely needs, formal instruction, shared companionship, and material protection each addressing a distinct and equally necessary dimension of support. He highlights the third missing condition, an age in which the teaching itself has vanished, as this treatise's own sober acknowledgment that circumstance beyond any individual's control can genuinely obstruct practice, a caution against ever measuring a practitioner's own effort without accounting for the historical moment that effort actually unfolds within.

26. Section: Four Powers That Give Rise to a Genuine Wish for Wholesome Practice

A further discussion then opens: the provisions for renunciation shown individually, established through eight distinct categories in turn. The first category, wholesome aspiration, describes four powers capable of giving rise to a genuine wish to pursue wholesome practice, whether the wish to go forth into monastic life itself or the wish to actually practice once there. The first power, the power of external conditions, arises from the influence of an instructing teacher, a fellow practitioner, a protecting benefactor, or the Dharma itself, each guiding a practitioner toward genuine aspiration to practice. The second power, the power of cause, arises from wholesome seeds already cultivated in former lives, a practitioner naturally drawn toward monastic practice in this present life simply because those same seeds were planted long before. The third power, the power of wisdom, arises from wisdom either innate or cultivated through study, a practitioner who has heard the true teaching and reflected on it properly coming to understand that only genuine practice can free a being from the sufferings of life, this same understanding itself giving rise to wholesome aspiration. And the fourth power, the power of practice, arises from actual sustained practice itself, a practitioner who genuinely cultivates discipline, concentration, and wisdom naturally finding their own diligence strengthened the more they continue, aspiration deepening the more the path is actually walked, until even a practitioner already ordained in this present life comes to vow renunciation across every future life to come.

Chinese Source

復次，由四種力生善法欲。一、由緣力，二、由因力，三、由智力，四、由行力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four powers give rise to wholesome aspiration: the power of external conditions, the power of cause, the power of wisdom, and the power of practice.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these four powers as tracing genuine aspiration back to four genuinely different sources, an outward influence, an inward seed already planted, a reasoned understanding, and the self-reinforcing momentum of practice itself, no single source treated as the only legitimate origin of a wish to renounce. He highlights the fourth power's own striking implication, that practice itself becomes the very engine of deepening aspiration until a practitioner vows renunciation across every future life, as this treatise's own reminder that motivation need not precede practice entirely, since practice faithfully sustained can itself generate the very aspiration a practitioner may have lacked at the very outset.

27. Section: Four Completenesses Needed for a Genuine Going Forth

A further category then opens: genuine going forth itself, the second of the eight categories individually establishing the provisions for renunciation, requiring four distinct completenesses before a going forth within this well-taught Dharma and discipline can properly be called complete. The Buddha's own teaching is called well-taught, it should first be understood, precisely because it proves good at the beginning, bringing genuine joy upon first hearing it; good in the middle, free of extremity and grounded instead in the middle way as it is actually practiced; and good in the end, bringing a practitioner all the way to nirvana itself, genuinely free of both the affliction and cognitive obstructions at last; its own words and phrases are further said to be genuinely elegant and its meaning genuinely accessible, both qualities together earning it the name well-taught. Within such a well-taught teaching, four completenesses together constitute a genuine going forth: completeness of outward form, completeness of conduct, completeness of motivation, and completeness of dwelling place, each still to be explained individually in what follows.

Chinese Source

復次，由四圓滿故，於善說法毗奈耶中出家圓滿。一、形相圓滿，謂能隨順無所雜染，不染汙故。二、業圓滿，謂如佛說法，善隨順故。三、意樂圓滿。四、住處圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four completenesses together constitute a genuine going forth within this well-taught Dharma and discipline: completeness of outward form, remaining undefiled and free of taint; completeness of conduct, genuinely according with the Buddha's own teaching; completeness of motivation; and completeness of dwelling place.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the threefold explanation of why the teaching itself is called well-taught, good at the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end, as this treatise's own reminder that a teaching's genuine excellence must be judged across its entire arc rather than by its opening appeal alone, since a teaching that merely pleases at first hearing but leads nowhere lasting would hardly deserve the name. He highlights the fourfold completeness about to unfold as extending this same standard from the teaching itself to the practitioner who receives it, outward form, conduct, motivation, and dwelling place together constituting the practitioner's own complete embodiment of a teaching already established as complete in its own right.

28. Section: Touching One's Own Head Each Morning to Remember, "I Am Now a Monastic"

Completeness of outward form, the first of the four completenesses, refers to a monastic's own external appearance, deportment, and requisites remaining at their most modest and basic, genuinely conducive to the noble path and never itself becoming a source of defiled affliction, a monastic strictly guarding both precept-discipline and sense-faculty restraint, diligently cultivating discipline, concentration, and wisdom, and extinguishing craving, aversion, and delusion, remembering always that even a single grain of rice offered in genuine faith carries a weight as heavy as Mount Sumeru itself, unworthy reception of it returning as a debt to be repaid even through rebirth as a beast bearing fur and horns. At ordination, a monastic vows to accept precisely this lowly form, deportment, and simple requisites, the head shaved, robes dyed a deliberately unassuming color rather than any of the fine primary colors, an alms bowl used in place of an ordinary bowl, monastic footwear distinct from lay footwear, every visible detail marking a genuinely different way of life; sustaining this same simple, undefiled bearing while constantly remembering one's own monastic identity constitutes completeness of outward form. One beloved modern teacher, the elder monk Guǎng Qīn, is remembered for touching his own shaved head each morning upon waking, silently reminding himself, "I have already gone forth; I must practice diligently; I may no longer live as a layperson does," rising early and keeping a disciplined schedule rather than sleeping late and combining breakfast with the midday meal as an ordinary layperson freely might.

Chinese Source

一、形相圓滿，謂能隨順無所雜染，不染汙故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Completeness of outward form refers to remaining genuinely undefiled, conducive to the path and free of any taint of affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the elder monk Guǎng Qīn's own simple morning ritual, touching his shaved head to remind himself of his monastic identity before the day's first thought can wander elsewhere, as this treatise's own most tender illustration of completeness of outward form actually lived, a single small bodily gesture doing more to sustain genuine remembrance than any amount of abstract resolution alone could manage. He highlights the grain-of-rice image, a single offering carrying the weight of Mount Sumeru itself, as this section's own sharpest warning against complacency, outward form alone counting for nothing if the inward vigilance it is meant to support ever grows careless.

29. Section: Bodhicitta as Root, Great Compassion Foremost, Non-Attainment as the Means

Completeness of conduct, the second completeness, refers simply to a monastic's bodily, verbal, and mental conduct genuinely according with the Buddha's own teaching, constantly examining whether that same conduct remains lawful and acting always with clear awareness in accordance with the scriptures, the discipline, and their authoritative commentaries. Completeness of motivation, the third completeness, differs somewhat between the two vehicles: a practitioner of the lesser vehicle should cultivate an ever-increasing motivation with nirvana as its foremost aim across every stage of training, while a Mahāyāna practitioner should ground that same motivation in the awakening-mind itself as its root, place great compassion foremost among its concerns, and take non-attainment as its skillful means, as the Bodhisattva Ground itself explains, a bodhisattva going forth for the sake of the Dharma rather than mere survival, genuinely seeking great enlightenment, seeking the renunciant's own path, and seeking nirvana, never resting in laziness or half-hearted effort, and never becoming entangled once more in the many unwholesome states, defiled future existence, and the blazing sufferings of birth, old age, sickness, and death that such motivation is meant to leave behind entirely. A further fourfold completeness appears elsewhere in this treatise as well, increased motivation, faculty, wisdom, and being born in a noble region where a buddha has actually appeared in the world, increased motivation there defined as going forth only once one's own aspiration toward nirvana has been thoroughly purified, never under compulsion from a creditor or from any fear whatsoever, precisely the standard already discussed earlier in this same fascicle's account of the obstacles that can block a genuine going forth.

Chinese Source

二、業圓滿，謂如佛說法，善隨順故。三、意樂圓滿。云何名為意樂圓滿？謂諸菩薩為法出家，不為活命；求大菩提，非為不求；為求沙門、為求涅槃、非為不求。

Ru-Yi Translation

Completeness of conduct refers to conduct genuinely according with the Buddha's teaching. What is completeness of motivation? A bodhisattva goes forth for the sake of the Dharma, not for mere survival, genuinely seeking great enlightenment, seeking the renunciant's path, and seeking nirvana.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the threefold Mahāyāna formula, the awakening-mind as root, great compassion foremost, and non-attainment as the skillful means, as this treatise's own compact summary of the entire bodhisattva path, a single sentence capable of orienting a practitioner correctly even where the fuller elaboration found elsewhere in this treatise's own bodhisattva-ground material is not immediately at hand. He highlights the cross-reference back to this same fascicle's own earlier discussion of the four obstacles blocking ordination as a fitting connection, completeness of motivation here simply naming, in positive form, the very thing that discussion's obstacle of motivation had already named in negative form.

30. Section: Five Marks of a Dwelling Place Genuinely Suited to Practice

Completeness of dwelling place, the fourth and final completeness, refers to a monastic's own place of residence, ideally a forest, the base of a tree, or an empty, secluded room, a genuinely quiet setting proving far more conducive to sustained practice than a crowded or bustling one, though a sufficiently advanced practitioner may indeed dwell amid the world without disturbance so long as ordinary worldly affairs are never allowed to intrude; for a practitioner not yet so advanced, a genuinely secluded dwelling remains considerably safer. Five specific marks are said to constitute a truly suitable dwelling place. The first, the terrain itself pleasing, clean, and well-appointed, level ground free of thorns, rubble, and broken tile, gardens and pools genuinely present, open and pleasant enough that simply seeing it inclines the mind toward genuine peace and delight in practice. The second, the setting itself quiet, free of daytime commotion, nighttime noise, mosquitoes, extreme wind or sun, or venomous creatures. The third, the setting free of danger, no fierce lions, tigers, leopards, wolves, hostile enemies, bandits, or threatening beings of any kind, allowing body and mind to remain genuinely at ease without lingering apprehension. The fourth, the setting easily supplied, robes, food, and other basic necessities not unduly difficult to obtain. And the fifth, the setting home to genuine spiritual friends, wise teachers and fellow practitioners of genuine understanding dwelling nearby, capable of clarifying what remains unclear, deepening what has already been understood, and skillfully explaining even the most profound teachings so that a practitioner's own wisdom and insight may swiftly grow pure.

Chinese Source

四、住處圓滿。若處所從本已來形相端嚴、眾所喜見、清淨無穢、園林池沼悉皆具足。若處所晝無憒鬧、夜少音聲。若處所無惡師子、虎、豹、豺、狼、怨敵、盜賊。若處所隨順身命眾具易得。若處所有善知識之所攝受、及諸有智同梵行者之所居止。

Ru-Yi Translation

Completeness of dwelling place has five marks: pleasing, clean, well-appointed terrain; a quiet setting free of commotion; a setting free of danger from beasts or bandits; a setting where necessities are easily obtained; and a setting home to wise teachers and fellow practitioners of genuine understanding.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five marks as this treatise's own remarkably holistic account of what genuinely supports practice, physical beauty, quiet, safety, material sufficiency, and genuine spiritual companionship together, no single condition treated as dispensable so long as sustained practice is the actual goal. He highlights the fifth mark's own placement last as no accident, every other favorable condition, however genuinely helpful, ultimately falling short of what a wise teacher and genuine fellow practitioners can provide, since only living guidance can clarify what remains unclear and deepen what has only partially been understood.

31. Section: Never Deeming a Small Fault Too Small to Fear, Five Causes of Complete Discipline

A further category then opens: the precept-discipline category, the third of the eight categories individually establishing the provisions for renunciation, explained here through five distinct causes of genuinely complete discipline. The first, not falling into ruin, refers to a monastic who abides in complete discipline thereby never falling into an unfortunate rebirth. The second, being able to escape, refers to skillfully guarding the individually stated pratimoksha precepts, thereby attaining genuine purity, liberation, and escape from the bondage of the three realms. The third, being beyond reproach, refers to a monastic whose deportment and conduct in every activity remain fully proper, according with both worldly convention and the vinaya's own specific rules, never frequenting any of the five improper venues already discussed, and thereby earning no reproach from the world nor from any wise and virtuous fellow practitioner, any noble one who has already attained the fruits of the path, or any master or student of the vinaya itself. The fourth, being free of any gap, refers to a monastic who genuinely fears even the smallest fault, never deeming a small good deed too insignificant to perform nor a small wrongdoing too trivial to avoid, precisely the spirit later captured in the saying that no good, however small, should go undone, and no evil, however small, should go committed. And the fifth, never being satisfied, refers to a monastic who, though having already studied every precept required at ordination, continues refining genuine mastery of discipline's own substance, characteristics, permissions, and prohibitions well beyond that initial study, a monk expected to spend at least five years and a nun at least six years in this same sustained study before receiving robes of their own, never resting content with a merely adequate understanding, until the precepts' own text and meaning can be upheld without any distortion whatsoever. A monastic who fulfills all five of these causes together thereby attains discipline that grows ever more excellent and ever more refined.

Chinese Source

復次，由五因緣，應知尸羅律儀圓滿。一、不墜墮故；二、能出離故；三、不可訶故；四、無穿闕故；五、不知足故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Five causes constitute genuinely complete discipline: not falling into ruin, being able to escape, being beyond reproach, being free of any gap, and never being satisfied.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth cause's own governing spirit, fearing even the smallest fault while never deeming a small good deed too insignificant to perform, as this fivefold catalogue's own moral center, since genuine discipline is ultimately built not from a handful of dramatic decisions but from countless small choices most observers would never even notice. He highlights the fifth cause's own considerable demand, years of sustained study even after every precept has technically already been learned, as this treatise's own reminder that discipline is a living mastery to be continually deepened rather than a fixed body of knowledge a practitioner can simply check off and set aside.

32. Section: Two Powers of Antidote Behind Four Kinds of Sense-Faculty Discipline

A further category then opens: the sense-faculty discipline category, the fourth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions, again distinct from the identically named category already closed under the provisions' own completeness, explained here through two distinct powers of antidote underlying four specific kinds of discipline. The first power, the power of deliberative discernment, belongs to sense-faculty discipline as found on the path of accumulation, affliction suppressed from manifesting through the strength of wisdom born of reflection alone; a practitioner possessing only this power sees clearly the faults inherent in any given object yet cannot thereby eliminate those faults outright, afflictive entanglement toward the object at hand prevented from arising or manifesting while the underlying seeds of affliction lodged within body and mind remain unsevered, never permanently uprooted. The second power, the power of cultivation, belongs to sense-faculty discipline as found on the stage of freedom from desire, affliction's very seeds severed through the strength of wisdom born of meditative cultivation; a practitioner possessing this power sees the same faults just as clearly and can eliminate them outright, the underlying latent tendencies themselves, not merely their manifest entanglement, prevented from ever arising again, severed and permanently uprooted at every moment regardless of the object encountered.

Chinese Source

復次，依二種對治，應知四種根律儀。二種對治者，一、思擇力，二、修習力。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sense-faculty discipline is fourfold, established through two powers of antidote: the power of deliberative discernment and the power of cultivation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this twofold distinction as marking the difference between a practitioner still on the path of accumulation, capable only of suppressing affliction's manifest activity, and a practitioner who has reached the stage of freedom from desire, capable of severing affliction's own underlying seeds outright. He highlights this same graduated structure, suppression achieved well before eradication, as continuing this treatise's consistent refusal to let a genuinely useful preliminary attainment be mistaken for the deeper severing it can only prepare the way toward.

33. Section: Four Kinds of Sense-Faculty Discipline, Each Building on the Last

The four kinds of sense-faculty discipline themselves are then listed. The first, guarding against objects, protects the mind from wandering amid whatever object is encountered while dwelling in seclusion and practicing diligently, sustained through mindfulness alone. The second, guarding against affliction, protects a practitioner going about outside with a mind resting in an equanimous state, neither wholesome nor unwholesome, so that greed or sorrow never arises toward the six sense objects encountered along the way. The third, guarding against entanglement, protects a practitioner already engaged in formal practice within right concentration, applying the power of cultivation the instant an object's general mark, or its finer characteristics, is grasped, forestalling affliction before it can take hold. And the fourth, guarding against latent tendency, protects a sense faculty such as the eye so thoroughly, whether in a scattered or a concentrated state, that affliction never manifests at all, denying the very conditions that would otherwise let its dormant seed arise. The four are said to stand in close mutual dependence, each building directly on what the guarding before it has already secured.

Chinese Source

四種根律儀者，一、境界護，二、煩惱護，三、纏護，四、隨眠護。

Ru-Yi Translation

Sense-faculty discipline comprises four kinds: guarding against objects, guarding against affliction, guarding against entanglement, and guarding against latent tendency.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the four kinds' own close mutual dependence as the passage's real point, each kind of guarding presupposing what the guarding before it has already secured, so that a practitioner who has not yet mastered guarding against objects should not expect to succeed at guarding against affliction, still less at the far subtler guarding against entanglement or latent tendency.

34. Section: Guarding the Mind from Wandering, Guarding Against Greed and Sorrow While Out

Guarding against objects means guarding oneself through mindfulness while dwelling in a quiet place and practicing diligently, whether the object of that mindfulness is watching the breath, contemplating impurity, or recollecting the Buddha, the mind never scattering amid the many objects of sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, or thought encountered along the way. Guarding against affliction means that when a practitioner goes about outside with a mind resting in an equanimous state, neither wholesome nor unwholesome, greed and sorrow remain far removed from whatever object is met; discerning that object without grasping its general mark, and without dwelling on its finer characteristics through subsequent reflection, down to never letting the mind flow outward in careless distraction. Guarding against affliction is itself the general heading under which both guarding against entanglement and guarding against latent tendency belong.

Chinese Source

境界護者，謂住寂靜勤修行時，以念自守，於諸境界心不流散故。煩惱護者，謂等位行而遊行時，於諸境界遠離貪憂故；即分別此不取其相，乃至心不流逸者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Guarding against objects means guarding oneself through mindfulness while dwelling in seclusion and practicing diligently, the mind never scattering amid any object encountered. Guarding against affliction means that, going about outside with a mind resting in equanimity, one remains free of greed and sorrow toward any object encountered, discerning that object without grasping its mark, down to never letting the mind flow outward in distraction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites both a Táng-dynasty and a Silla-monk reading of this "equanimous state," one treating it as describing the ordinary practitioner whose afflictions are neither unusually strong nor unusually faint and can therefore genuinely be guarded against, the other treating it simply as a mind correctly resting in a wholesome or neutral state. He treats the difference as one of emphasis rather than substance, since both readings converge on the same practical guidance: guard the mind before greed or sorrow ever gets a foothold.

35. Section: An Eye That No Longer Chases a Beautiful Form Closes Sense-Faculty Discipline

Guarding against entanglement and guarding against latent tendency are then illustrated through a single example, the eye faculty encountering a beautiful form while out and about. Should a practitioner grasp that form's general mark, and then, through further reflective awareness, grasp its finer characteristics as well, vigilant awareness arises at once, a guarding practice immediately applied through the power of cultivation to protect the eye faculty, this being guarding against entanglement. Once that protection has genuinely been realized, the eye faculty no longer even pursues a beautiful form's finer characteristics at all, affliction never manifesting and therefore never perfuming any new seed of affliction into the mind stream, this being guarding against latent tendency. Every instance of manifest affliction only strengthens its own underlying seed, so the surest way to protect that dormant seed from ever growing stronger is simply never letting its manifest form arise in the first place.

Chinese Source

若於爾時執取彼相，復起隨覺執取隨好，則便於彼修防護行，以修習力守護眼根，是名纏護。證眼根護，是隨眠護。

Ru-Yi Translation

Guarding against entanglement means that, upon grasping an object's mark and then further grasping its finer characteristics through subsequent awareness, one immediately applies a guarding practice, protecting the eye faculty through the power of cultivation. Guarding against latent tendency is realizing that same protection of the eye faculty fully.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the progression from guarding against entanglement to guarding against latent tendency as this fourfold catalogue's own lesson in cause and effect, every instance of manifest affliction perfuming its own seed more deeply into the mind stream, so that the surest protection a dormant seed can receive is simply never letting its manifest form arise at all. He highlights the single eye-root example carrying all four kinds of guarding across their full arc as this treatise's own way of showing how one careless glance at something beautiful can either strengthen or, guarded correctly, permanently weaken the very seed a practitioner is trying to uproot, closing the sense-faculty discipline category, the fourth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions.

36. Section: Five Causes of Genuinely Complete Moderation in Eating

A further category then opens: the moderation-in-eating category, the fifth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions, explained here through five distinct causes. The first, relying on the antidote, refers to eating only after correctly reflecting on and discerning what is about to be eaten. The second, forsaking what needs to be countered, refers to never eating for the sake of wantonness, arrogance, self-adornment, or physical beauty. The third, relying on one's own proper function, refers to eating so that one's own life continues, remains strong, and stays free of illness and at ease. The fourth, relying on the basis, refers to eating so as to remain strong enough to practice the noble path and other genuine goods. And the fifth, discernment, refers to correctly distinguishing the fault of eating nothing at all from the further difference, once food is taken, between balanced and unbalanced eating. Fascicle 23 of the Basic Section's own Śrāvaka Ground, on page 815, sets out the correct manner of taking food in considerably fuller detail.

Chinese Source

復次，由五因緣，當知於食知量圓滿。一、依止對治故；二、遠離所治故；三、依自作業故；四、依處故；五、分別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Moderation in eating is genuinely complete through five causes: relying on the antidote, forsaking what needs to be countered, relying on one's own proper function, relying on the basis, and discernment.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five causes as together describing not a single rule about quantity but an entire disposition toward food, why one eats mattering at least as much as how much, since the same bowl of rice eaten to sustain the path differs entirely from the same bowl eaten for vanity or indulgence even when the amount served is identical. He directs students to fascicle 23 of the Basic Section's own Śrāvaka Ground for the fuller treatment this same fivefold structure receives there.

37. Section: What the Basic Section Itself Says Each of the Five Causes Means

A subcommentary then draws out exactly what the Basic Section itself says under each of these five causes. Relying on the antidote means eating only after correctly reflecting on and discerning what is eaten. Forsaking what needs to be countered means not eating for wantonness, not for arrogance, not to adorn oneself, and not for physical beauty. Relying on one's own proper function means eating to remove hunger and thirst, to end painful feeling already present, to prevent new painful feeling from arising, and to sustain whatever strength or ease the body still needs. Relying on the basis means eating so as to uphold the pure conduct of the holy life and to dwell free of fault and genuinely at ease. And discernment means that, in brief, there are two cases, not eating at all and eating; eating itself further divides into balanced eating and unbalanced eating, with considerably more detail given at length elsewhere.

Chinese Source

本地分說：由正思擇食於所食，是名依止對治。又說：不為倡蕩、不為憍逸、不為飾好、不為端嚴食於所食，是名遠離所治。又說：為除饑渴、為斷故受、為令新受當不更生、為當存養若力若樂食於所食，是名依自作業。又說：為攝梵行、為得無罪安隱而住食於所食，是名依處。又說：略義有二，一、無所食，二、有所食；有所食者，復有二種，一、平等食，二、不平等食，乃至廣說，是名分別。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Basic Section explains: relying on the antidote is eating only after correctly reflecting on what is eaten; forsaking what needs to be countered is not eating for wantonness, arrogance, adornment, or beauty; relying on one's own proper function is eating to remove hunger and thirst, end old painful feeling, prevent new painful feeling, and sustain strength or ease; relying on the basis is eating to uphold pure conduct and dwell free of fault and at ease; and discernment is distinguishing not eating at all from eating, itself further divided into balanced and unbalanced eating.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this subcommentary's own careful citation of the Basic Section as confirming that the Determination's abbreviated five causes are no shorthand invention but a faithful compression of material already laid out at length elsewhere in this same treatise, the two sections mutually verifying one another across many fascicles' distance.

38. Section: Five Contemplations Before Every Meal

Northern Buddhist practice elaborates this same moderation through five contemplations traditionally observed, palms joined, before every meal. The first, reckoning the labor behind the food and tracing its origin, recalls how much toil, tilling, weeding, harvesting, husking, and cooking, stands behind even a single bowl of rice; the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise pictures the sweat of the laborers who grew it as outweighing the rice itself, food that becomes, within a single day, nothing but waste, warning that a practitioner who grows attached to its taste risks falling into the hells to swallow burning iron pellets, and beyond that into the animal realm to repay the debt as a beast of burden or a scavenging dog. The second, weighing one's own virtue against worthiness to receive the offering, asks whether one has genuinely meditated, recited the scriptures, or served the Three Jewels that day, the Vinaya-Mother Sutra warning that a monastic who accepts a donor's offering without such practice, or who overeats out of pride once accepted, remains liable for having fallen short of the offering's own worth. The third, guarding the mind against fault, greed foremost among them, distinguishes fine food, apt to provoke delight, intoxicating pride, vanity about one's complexion, or a wish to appear well filled out, from poor food, apt to provoke aversion leading toward rebirth as a hungry ghost, and middling food, apt to provoke an indifferent confusion leading toward rebirth as an animal that feeds on filth, the same three foods, rightly met, instead becoming causes for the three wholesome destinies. The fourth, regarding food strictly as medicine for the body's own withering, treats eating purely as treatment for hunger and thirst, never a matter of good or poor flavor, food taken the way a starving traveler on a desperate road might, out of dire necessity alone, without ever tasting for pleasure. And the fifth, accepting food only for the sake of accomplishing the path, gives three further reasons: a body still bound to the desire realm cannot persist without material food; this same reported body's continued life sustains, in turn, the Dharma-body's own wisdom-life; and only a body thus sustained can continue cultivating precepts, concentration, and wisdom, subduing self-view and the afflictions that depend upon it.

Chinese Source

食存五觀：一、計功多少，量彼來處。二、忖己德行，全缺應供。三、防心離過，貪等為宗。四、正事良藥，為療形枯。五、為成道業，方受此食。

Ru-Yi Translation

Five contemplations accompany every meal: reckoning the labor behind the food and tracing its origin; weighing one's own virtue against worthiness to receive it; guarding the mind against fault, greed foremost among them; regarding food strictly as medicine for the body's own withering; and accepting food only for the sake of accomplishing the path.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the Great Perfection of Wisdom Treatise's own image of a laborer's sweat outweighing the rice it produced as this whole practice's most memorable teaching device, deliberately unsettling enough that a practitioner tempted to savor a meal for its own sake is made to see, instead, exactly what stands behind it. He highlights the fifth contemplation's threefold reasoning, sustaining the physical body only in order to sustain the life that in turn sustains the Dharma-body's own wisdom, as this whole practice's real center of gravity, food never treated as an end but always, deliberately, as a means.

39. Section: Old Affliction, New Affliction, and the Five Fruits That Close the Food Category

A final passage then distinguishes two kinds of painful feeling bound up with eating and lists the five fruits genuinely complete moderation brings, closing the entire moderation-in-eating category. Old painful feeling arises from hunger itself, the ripened fruit of past karma already carried within this present body. Feeding-caused painful feeling arises instead from the very act of eating, whenever eating itself, wrongly done, becomes a source of discomfort. Both are ultimately traceable to food, and both are to be eliminated through moderation rightly practiced. Five fruits then follow: nourishing, since genuinely complete moderation increases the practice of pure conduct itself; strength, since eating correctly leaves one able to overcome whatever affliction still needs overcoming; bliss, since it supports the ease of dwelling well within this very life; blamelessness, since a practitioner who eats without any trace of greed, anger, or delusion becomes a genuinely pure field of merit for whoever makes the offering; and dwelling at ease, since moderation rightly practiced allows a practitioner to bear witness to the very cessation of affliction and suffering.

Chinese Source

此中舊受者，饑所起。苦受者，食所起。撫育者，增梵行故。力者，能害所治故。樂者，現法樂住故。無罪者，淨福田故。安隱住者，煩惱苦斷能作證故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Old painful feeling arises from hunger itself; feeding-caused painful feeling arises from the act of eating. Nourishing means increasing pure conduct. Strength means being able to overcome what needs overcoming. Bliss means dwelling in present-life ease. Blamelessness means being a pure field of merit. Dwelling at ease means being able to bear witness to affliction and suffering's own cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the old-and-new painful-feeling distinction and the fivefold fruit that follows it together as this category's own reminder that even something as basic as eating, approached correctly, becomes a genuine support for the entire path rather than a mere biological necessity simply to be gotten through, closing the moderation-in-eating category, the fifth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions.

40. Section: Two Kinds of Diligent Practice Behind Complete Wakefulness-Yoga

A further category then opens: the wakefulness-yoga category, the sixth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions. Diligence itself reaches genuine completeness here through reducing sleep and sustaining two further kinds of practice without a single gap, uninterrupted practice and earnest, deliberate practice, together making up the whole of wakefulness-yoga's own diligence.

Chinese Source

減省睡眠，無間、殷重二加行故，精進圓滿。

Ru-Yi Translation

Reducing sleep and sustaining uninterrupted practice together with earnest, deliberate practice without a single gap bring diligence to its own completeness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats reduced sleep itself as merely the precondition here, the real substance of wakefulness-yoga lying in the two kinds of practice, uninterrupted and earnest, that reclaimed time is then actually spent on, since a practitioner who merely sleeps less without filling that time with genuine practice gains nothing this category is actually pointing toward.

41. Section: Which Hindrance Calls for Sitting, Which for Walking

Earnest, deliberate practice is accomplished through both formal walking meditation and seated meditation, though different hindrances call for different postures. The first hindrance, desire, the second, ill will, and the fourth, restlessness and remorse, are best countered while seated, desire through contemplating impurity, ill will through cultivating loving-kindness, and restlessness and remorse through the nine stages of settling the mind in calming meditation. The third hindrance, sloth and torpor, is best countered while walking, cultivated through the six recollections. And the fifth hindrance, doubt, whether doubt regarding the Three Jewels or doubt regarding one's own capacity to attain concentration, may be countered through contemplating dependent origination in either posture, walking or seated alike.

Chinese Source

殷重加行者，謂行坐時而成辦故。於第一、第二、第四蓋中宜坐時，第三蓋中宜行時，第五蓋中宜俱時。

Ru-Yi Translation

Earnest, deliberate practice is accomplished through both walking meditation and seated meditation. The first, second, and fourth hindrances are best countered while seated; the third hindrance is best countered while walking; and the fifth hindrance may be countered in either posture.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this posture-matching as a genuinely practical instruction rather than an arbitrary rule, sitting still suiting the more agitated hindrances of desire, ill will, and restlessness precisely because stillness itself denies them room to build momentum, while walking suits sloth and torpor precisely because movement itself is what breaks a dulled mind's own inertia.

42. Section: A Lion's Sleep to Avoid, Closing Wakefulness-Yoga

Uninterrupted practice governs how the day and night itself are divided. Across the day's first period, from six to nine in the morning, the day's last period, from three to six in the afternoon, and the night's first and last periods, from six to nine at night and from three to six the following morning, a practitioner should remain constantly awake, sustaining calming and insight without a single gap. Only the night's middle period, nine at night until three in the morning, is set aside for sleep, and even then a practitioner lies on the right side, legs stacked one over the other, sustaining a perception of light, mindfulness, and clear comprehension, reflecting on the intention to rise even while falling asleep. Such practice is meant to avoid a sleep resembling a lion's own, prolonged and so heavy that mindfulness is entirely lost without a single break; lying instead with the left leg stacked over the right and the body turned onto its right side, so that the heart is never compressed and the mind remains easily roused rather than sunk in heavy slumber, sustaining a perception of light and abiding in mindfulness and clear comprehension throughout, with the specific intention set beforehand, for instance to rise at a fixed hour to sit in meditation, so that upon waking in the night's last period one rises swiftly and without delay to resume walking meditation or seated practice once again. This closes the wakefulness-yoga category, the sixth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions.

Chinese Source

無間加行者，謂於晝日、夜初後分應常覺悟，於夜中分正習睡眠。為離師子相似長時極重失念無間睡故，重累其足，乃至思惟起想正習睡眠。

Ru-Yi Translation

Uninterrupted practice means remaining constantly awake through the day's periods and the night's first and last periods, sleeping properly only during the night's middle period. To avoid a prolonged, heavy, mindfulness-losing sleep resembling a lion's own, one lies with the legs stacked, sustaining the intention to rise even while properly sleeping.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the lion's-sleep image as this whole category's own memorable warning, a lion sleeping heavily both day and night without any genuine break, exactly the pattern a practitioner must guard against by fixing, in advance, both the posture and the intention with which sleep itself is entered. He highlights the closing detail, reflecting on the intention to rise even as one falls asleep, as showing that wakefulness-yoga governs not only the hours spent awake but the very quality of the sleep in between, closing the sixth of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions.

43. Section: Five Places Where Correct Knowledge Governs How One Dwells

A further category then opens: the correct-knowledge-dwelling category, the seventh of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions, explained across five distinct places. The first, the place of going about, concerns correct knowledge sustained while walking or traveling from place to place. The second, the place of seeing, concerns correct knowledge sustained the moment the eye meets any visible form. The third, the place of receiving offerings and veneration, concerns correct knowledge sustained while accepting whatever is offered. The fourth, the place of using requisites, concerns correct knowledge sustained while making use of robes, food, and other basic necessities, first weighing one's own virtue and the offering's own source before use, and remaining moderate throughout. And the fifth, the place of practicing wholesome factors, concerns correct knowledge sustained while cultivating calming and insight, precepts, concentration, and wisdom, staying moderate in speech and in silence, in movement and in stillness, and in dispelling drowsiness through restorative rest when genuinely needed.

Chinese Source

復次，應於五處知量正知而住。一、於行處，二、於觀處，三、於攝受利養恭敬處，四、於受用資具處，五、於善品加行處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Correct knowledge should govern how one dwells across five places: the place of going about, the place of seeing, the place of receiving offerings and veneration, the place of using requisites, and the place of practicing wholesome factors.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these five places as together covering the entire span of a monastic's ordinary day, movement, perception, receiving support from others, using that support, and formal practice itself, correct knowledge required not only during meditation but across every activity a genuinely complete monastic life actually involves.

44. Section: Never Wandering to the Wrong Place, Never Losing the Eye's Own Guard

Correct knowledge sustained in the place of going about means never frequenting places one ought never to frequent, wine-shops, brothels, royal courts, executioners' quarters, or outcaste dwellings among the traditional examples, karaoke parlors and internet cafés among their modern equivalents, and never going out once dusk has already fallen. Correct knowledge sustained in the place of seeing means that, should the eye happen upon some sight without any prior intention to look, the eye faculty is swiftly withdrawn and well guarded, while should one deliberately set out to observe something, mindfulness remains well established throughout, neither grasping the object's general mark nor its finer characteristics in either case, this same guarding extending, by the same principle, to all six sense faculties meeting their respective objects.

Chinese Source

由初處故，終不遊行非所行處，亦不曛暮而出遊行。由第二故，先不作意而觀視者，速攝其根；若先作意而觀視者，善住其念。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the first place, one never frequents an improper place, nor goes out once dusk has fallen. Through the second place, an unintended sight is met by swiftly withdrawing the sense faculty, while a deliberately sought sight is met by remaining well established in mindfulness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi updates the traditional list of improper venues with contemporary equivalents precisely so that this instruction is not mistaken for archaic advice with no modern application, karaoke parlors and internet cafés genuinely functioning, in his reading, exactly as the wine-shops and brothels of the original list once did.

45. Section: Neither Clenching a Fist Nor Fidgeting, Weighing Every Gift Received

Correct knowledge sustained in the place of receiving offerings and veneration means that, when something is offered or veneration itself is received, the hand neither clenches into a fist nor withdraws, and the feet never shift about restlessly, a practitioner perhaps inviting the donor to first bow before the Buddha, contemplating all the while that even receiving itself is a form of suffering, mindfulness sustained throughout the exchange. Correct knowledge sustained in the place of using requisites means knowing the correct measure when making use of robes, alms bowl, food, and drink alike, neither excessive nor insufficient.

Chinese Source

由第三故，若有所受及他禮時，手不拳縮，足不躁動。由第四故，受用衣鉢及與飲食皆知其量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the third place, whatever is received, or however one is venerated, the hand neither clenches nor withdraws, and the feet do not stir restlessly. Through the fourth place, robes, alms bowl, food, and drink are all used with correct knowledge of their proper measure.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the small physical details, an unclenched hand, a still foot, as this passage's own way of making an inward attitude outwardly visible, since a mind genuinely at ease with the truth that receiving is itself a form of suffering has no reason left to fidget or flinch when a gift is actually placed in one's hands.

46. Section: Knowing the Measure of a Day's Practice and a Night's Sleep

Correct knowledge sustained in the place of practicing wholesome factors covers both day and night in turn. During the day, dwelling in a quiet place, whether engaged in walking meditation, seated meditation, going out, standing, sitting upright, or simply waking, a practitioner skillfully knows the correct duration of each posture, changing posture at the right measure while sustaining mindfulness throughout, this being correct knowledge governing the body's own activity by day. During the night, a practitioner likewise skillfully knows the correct measure of sleep taken during the night's middle period, most people needing roughly eight hours, a genuinely diligent practitioner needing perhaps only five or six, and one who has already accomplished meditative concentration needing perhaps only three or four hours, or even just one or two, the correct measure varying from person to person and to be discovered through one's own honest observation.

Chinese Source

由第五故，若居寂靜，於晝日分經行、宴坐，若行、若住、若坐、若覺善知其量。於其夜分所習睡眠亦善知量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Through the fifth place, while dwelling in a quiet place, one skillfully knows the correct measure of walking meditation, seated meditation, going out, standing, sitting, and waking through the day, and likewise skillfully knows the correct measure of sleep practiced through the night.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the sliding scale of sleep, eight hours down to as little as one or two depending on a practitioner's own level of accomplishment, as a genuinely honest acknowledgment that correct measure is never a single fixed number but something each practitioner must discover through their own sustained practice and honest self-observation.

47. Section: Knowing the Measure of Debate, Silence, and Rest, Closing Correct-Knowledge Dwelling

A final aspect of correct knowledge in the place of practicing wholesome factors covers speech, silence, and rest together. Whether engaged in discussion and resolving doctrinal questions together with fellow practitioners, or resting instead in silent contemplation, a practitioner skillfully knows the correct measure of speaking and of remaining silent alike; and so as to keep both body and mind genuinely well regulated, a practitioner likewise skillfully knows the correct measure needed to dispel drowsiness and fatigue through restorative rest when it is genuinely warranted. Across body, speech, and mind, and across all five places already discussed, going about, seeing, receiving, using requisites, and practicing wholesome factors, a practitioner who knows the correct measure throughout thereby dwells with correct knowledge in every activity. This closes the correct-knowledge-dwelling category, the seventh of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions.

Chinese Source

若有修習論議決擇，若語、若默亦善知量；為令二種所依調適，除遣睡眠及諸勞倦亦善知量。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whether engaged in discussion and doctrinal resolution, one knows the correct measure of speaking and of silence alike; and so as to keep body and mind well regulated, one likewise knows the correct measure needed to dispel drowsiness and fatigue through rest.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing instruction on speech and rest as extending correct knowledge into the two areas most easily neglected, doctrinal discussion drifting into idle debate without a sense of measure, and needed rest mistaken for indulgence or, just as often, denied altogether out of misplaced zeal, closing the seventh of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions.

48. Section: Four Factors That Subsume Every Abandoning-Factor, Opening the Final Category

A further category then opens: the samādhi-cultivation category, the eighth and final of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions. Whenever a monastic diligently cultivates the bases of supernatural power, four factors, in brief, subsume every one of the eight abandoning-factors already discussed elsewhere in this treatise and bring samādhi to completion: the factor of practice, the factor of realizing further progress, the factor of guarding against and penetrating affiliated affliction, and the factor of drawing forth what purifies affiliated affliction. These bases are called bases of supernatural power precisely because the concentration they yield, whether the mundane excellence a worldly practitioner may reach or the transcendent excellence available only beyond the world, is itself something scarcely conceivable, while desire, effort, mind, and investigation, the four specific paths that lead there, function like legs capable of going, returning, and leaping vigorously toward exactly that attainment, able, as this treatise elsewhere puts it, to reach and to realize whatever excellent quality is genuinely worth reaching.

Chinese Source

復次，若有苾芻勤修神足，略由四支攝諸斷行。一、修習支，二、證勝進支，三、護隨煩惱通達支，四、引發能淨隨煩惱支。

Ru-Yi Translation

Whenever a monastic diligently cultivates the bases of supernatural power, four factors, in brief, subsume all the abandoning-factors: the factor of practice, the factor of realizing further progress, the factor of guarding against and penetrating affiliated affliction, and the factor of drawing forth what purifies affiliated affliction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Abhidharma-mahāvibhāṣā's own gloss on why concentration and its four approaches are together named a "base of supernatural power," concentration itself named the marvel and desire, effort, mind, and investigation named merely the feet that carry a practitioner toward it, a foot metaphor this treatise's own Śrāvaka Ground develops further still, one able to reach the marvels of the mundane world, the other able to reach the far greater marvel of a transcendent attainment.

49. Section: Aspiration and Effort Together Complete the Factor of Practice

The factor of practice is explained first, comprising two basic means, aspiration and effort. Through aspiration, a practitioner gives rise to the genuine wish, when will my own samādhi at last reach completion, when will I finally sever every latent tendency belonging to every unwholesome state; through effort, that same practitioner never abandons the sustained practice of calming and insight already underway. Wherever the wholesome aspiration to accomplish samādhi and sever affliction's own latent tendencies exists, a genuine wish naturally follows, and that wish in turn drives the practitioner forward, aspiration and effort together countering idleness and carrying samādhi's own cultivation to completion.

Chinese Source

修習支者，謂欲、精進。何以故？依欲、精進修神足故。

Ru-Yi Translation

The factor of practice comprises aspiration and effort. Why is this so? Because the bases of supernatural power are cultivated in reliance on aspiration and effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats aspiration and effort as this whole eightfold structure's own starting engine, a wish genuinely felt before any effort is ever exerted, and an effort that, without a genuine wish behind it, rarely sustains itself for long, the two factors functioning less as separate steps than as a single motive force viewed from two angles.

50. Section: Faith and Pliancy Together Realize Further Progress

The factor of realizing further progress comprises faith, the third abandoning-factor, and pliancy, the fourth. Realizing samādhi's own excellent, ever-advancing qualities requires, first, a genuinely pure faith, confidence in a still higher liberation beyond one's present attainment, never resting content with what has already been reached, countering idleness and never abandoning correct practice until the fundamental meditative absorption itself is realized; and, second, the pliancy that fundamental absorption itself brings, a pervasive ease throughout the whole body that stills every coarseness still remaining in body and mind alike.

Chinese Source

證勝進支者，謂信、輕安。何以故？由證勝進故，以淨信心，信上解脫；以其輕安，止息所有身心麤重。

Ru-Yi Translation

The factor of realizing further progress comprises faith and pliancy. Why is this so? Because realizing further progress requires a pure faith confident in a still higher liberation, and the pliancy that stills all coarseness remaining in body and mind.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats faith's own placement here, confidence specifically in a liberation still beyond one's present attainment, as this factor's real safeguard against complacency, a practitioner who mistakes today's genuine progress for the final goal having no further reason to press on toward the pliancy and stillness still waiting beyond it.

51. Section: Mindfulness Guards, Correct Knowledge Penetrates, Affiliated Affliction Met on Both Fronts

The factor of guarding against and penetrating affiliated affliction comprises mindfulness and correct knowledge. Through mindfulness, the fifth abandoning-factor, a practitioner guards against sinking and agitation and every other affliction affiliated with calming and insight practice before they have even arisen, preventing them from arising at all and countering the fault of forgetting what has genuinely been learned. Through correct knowledge, the sixth abandoning-factor, that same practitioner penetrates and clearly recognizes whatever affiliated affliction has already arisen, countering the fault of sinking or agitation by swiftly discerning its presence and returning at once to correct mindfulness. The Śrāvaka Ground of the Basic Section explains mindfulness as belonging to calming's own nine stages of mental abiding, and correct knowledge as belonging to insight's own discerning wisdom, guarding what has not yet arisen through calming and penetrating what has already arisen through insight.

Chinese Source

護隨煩惱通達支者，謂念、正知。何以故？由正念故，防護未生止觀隨惑；由正知故，通達已生止觀隨惑。

Ru-Yi Translation

The factor of guarding against and penetrating affiliated affliction comprises mindfulness and correct knowledge. Why is this so? Because correct mindfulness guards against affliction affiliated with calming and insight before it arises, and correct knowledge penetrates such affliction once it has already arisen.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats mindfulness and correct knowledge as covering, between them, the entire timeline of an affliction's own life span, mindfulness stationed before it ever arises and correct knowledge stationed the moment it does, so that between the two no affiliated affliction is ever left genuinely unguarded.

52. Section: Intention Rouses a Sinking Mind, Equanimity Steadies an Agitated One, Closing the Four Factors

The factor of drawing forth what purifies affiliated affliction comprises intention and equanimity, the seventh and eighth abandoning-factors, closing the fourfold structure that together subsumes all eight. Through intention, a practitioner rouses and lifts a mind that has begun sinking toward dullness, recollecting, for instance, the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, generosity, precepts, or the heavens, whichever recollection genuinely brings joy, countering sinking through deliberate, willed reflection. Through equanimity, that same practitioner counters a mind that has instead begun scattering toward agitation, releasing it from whatever has stirred it and gathering it back inward to continue calming and insight practice undisturbed. Once desire, effort, mind, and investigation, together with these eight abandoning-factors, bring samādhi to full completion, the result is named, according to which of the four bases predominates, the base of supernatural power accomplished through desire, through effort, through mind, or through investigation, each fully realized through its own accompanying abandoning-factors.

Chinese Source

引發能淨隨煩惱支者，謂思及捨。何以故？由思故，勵沈下心；由捨故，若心掉舉，攝持於內。

Ru-Yi Translation

The factor of drawing forth what purifies affiliated affliction comprises intention and equanimity. Why is this so? Because intention rouses a mind that has begun sinking, and equanimity gathers a mind that has begun scattering back inward.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats intention and equanimity as this fourfold structure's own closing pair precisely because they are corrective rather than foundational, applied only once sinking or agitation has actually appeared, exactly the moment-to-moment fine adjustment that keeps the practice steady between the coarser safeguards mindfulness and correct knowledge already provide.

53. Section: Four Conditions That Powerfully Support Attaining Concentration

A second aspect of samādhi-cultivation then opens: four further factors that function as a powerfully supportive condition for actually attaining concentration. The first, attentive listening, refers to a practitioner who gives rise to a genuine wish to hear the true teaching and listens to it with a pure, confident mind, single-pointed attention on one object thereby achieved. The second, receiving correct instruction, refers to instruction given in proper sequence and without any distortion, a practitioner thereby roused to vigorous, unbroken diligence in cultivating the factors of enlightenment through every skillful means. The third, prior familiarity, refers to a practitioner who, in lives closely preceding this one, has already repeatedly entered various states of meditative absorption, that same single-pointed attention thereby readily achieved once again in this life. And the fourth, being genuinely well learned, refers to a practitioner who has heard, retained, and accumulated extensive teaching concerning concentration itself, and who then reflects, weighs, and carefully examines that same teaching alone in a quiet place until single-pointed attention is achieved.

Chinese Source

復次，有四種法，於所得定為增上緣。一、審諦聽聞，二、得正教授，三、宿世串習，四、具足多聞。

Ru-Yi Translation

Four further factors function as a powerfully supportive condition for attaining concentration: attentive listening, receiving correct instruction, prior familiarity, and being genuinely well learned.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats these four conditions as together acknowledging what the eightfold factor structure alone leaves unstated, that a practitioner's own effort never operates in isolation but always draws on what has genuinely been heard, correctly taught, previously cultivated, and thoroughly studied, samādhi's attainment owing as much to these supporting conditions as to the effort applied on top of them.

54. Section: Listening Well and Being Taught Well, Two Conditions That Support Concentration

Attentive listening means giving rise to a genuine wish and aspiration for samādhi and listening to the true teaching with a mind of pure faith, single-pointed attention thereby achieved through this attentive listening as its condition, a practitioner needing first to hear whatever teaching concerns the correct sequence of calming and insight practice itself. Receiving correct instruction means that, because a genuinely wise teacher provides instruction given in proper sequence and free of any distortion, a practitioner is roused to vigorous, unbroken diligence, applying every skillful means to the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment without a single lapse, single-pointed attention thereby achieved through this correct instruction as its condition. The Śrāvaka Ground of the Basic Section describes four kinds of instruction altogether, undistorted instruction, sequential instruction, teaching-instruction, and correct instruction, of which the sequential and undistorted kinds are exactly what is meant here.

Chinese Source

審諦聽聞者，謂發起樂欲，生淨信心聽聞正法；由此因此緣，得心一境性。得正教授者，謂因次第教授、無倒教授故，發起勇猛精進而住，無間常委，於菩提分精勤方便修習而住；由此因此緣，得心一境性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Attentive listening means giving rise to a genuine wish and listening to the true teaching with a mind of pure faith, single-pointed attention thereby achieved. Receiving correct instruction means that, through sequential and undistorted instruction, one is roused to vigorous, unbroken diligence in cultivating the factors of enlightenment, single-pointed attention thereby achieved.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi directs students to fascicle 27 of the Basic Section's own Śrāvaka Ground for its fuller fourfold classification of instruction, noting that the two kinds named here, sequential and undistorted, are specifically the first two of that longer list, teaching-instruction and correct instruction reserved for a different context entirely.

55. Section: A Familiarity Carried from Past Lives and a Learning Gathered in This One, Closing the Four Conditions

Prior familiarity means that a practitioner belonging to a certain type, in lives closely preceding this present one, has already repeatedly entered the various levels of meditative absorption and attainment, that same single-pointed attention consequently arising with unusual ease once this life's own teaching is encountered, a debt of prior practice simply being collected again. Being genuinely well learned means that a practitioner has heard extensively regarding concentration's own related teachings, retained what was heard, and accumulated it firmly in mind, then, alone in a quiet place, applies that same accumulated learning to analyzing, weighing, and truthfully, carefully observing whatever object of concentration is at hand, single-pointed attention thereby achieved through this thorough learning as its condition. This closes the four conditions that powerfully support attaining concentration.

Chinese Source

宿世串習者，謂於宿世鄰近生中，於諸靜慮及諸等至數已證入；由此因此緣，得心一境性。具足多聞者，謂多聞、聞持、其聞積集，即於彼法獨處空閑，思惟、籌量、審諦觀察；由此因此緣，得心一境性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Prior familiarity means that, in lives closely preceding this one, one has already repeatedly entered various states of absorption and attainment, single-pointed attention thereby achieved. Being genuinely well learned means having heard, retained, and accumulated extensive teaching, then reflecting, weighing, and carefully observing it alone in a quiet place, single-pointed attention thereby achieved.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats prior familiarity and thorough learning together as this list's own acknowledgment that a single lifetime rarely tells the whole story, some practitioners arriving with an ease that can only be traced to lives already spent in the same practice, others building that same readiness deliberately, within this one life, through sustained and careful study, closing the four conditions that powerfully support attaining concentration.

56. Section: Seven Things a Practitioner Seeking Samādhi Must Correctly Know

A third aspect of samādhi-cultivation then opens: seven things a practitioner genuinely seeking to attain samādhi should correctly know. The first, the internal cause of retrogression, is idleness itself, the root cause of the dullness and drowsiness that erodes concentration from within. The second, the external cause of retrogression, is agitation, the root cause of the distraction toward external objects that erodes concentration from without. The third, internal retrogression itself, is the dullness and drowsiness that actually obstructs concentration, whether concentration not yet attained or already attained. The fourth, external retrogression itself, is the very distraction toward the five desirable objects that likewise obstructs concentration in either case. The fifth, the antidote to internal retrogression and its cause, is skillfully grasping and correctly contemplating the perception of light, thereby countering idleness together with the dullness and drowsiness it produces. The sixth, the antidote to external retrogression and its cause, is contemplating one's own body and another's as impure, thereby countering agitation together with the distraction toward the five desirable objects it produces. And the seventh, the support underlying both antidotes, is this same perception of light, the very basis on which both the settling-lifting-releasing method and the contemplation of impurity themselves depend.

Chinese Source

復次，有七種法，為欲證得三摩地者，應正了知。一、內定退因，二、外定退因，三、內定退，四、外定退，五、內定退及因對治，六、外定退及因對治，七、彼二對治依持。

Ru-Yi Translation

A practitioner genuinely seeking to attain samādhi should correctly know seven things: the internal cause of retrogression, the external cause of retrogression, internal retrogression itself, external retrogression itself, the antidote to internal retrogression and its cause, the antidote to external retrogression and its cause, and the support underlying both antidotes.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this sevenfold structure as a genuinely diagnostic framework rather than a simple warning list, cause, effect, and remedy laid out in matching pairs for both the internal and external routes by which concentration is lost, so that a practitioner who notices retrogression can trace it immediately back to its actual cause rather than merely lamenting its arrival.

57. Section: Idleness Erodes from Within, Agitation Erodes from Without

The internal cause of retrogression is idleness, the root cause by which concentration is lost through an inward failing. The Compendium of Mahāyāna Abhidharma describes idleness as an indolence, in matters of both cultivating good and abandoning evil, that obstructs diligence and increases defilement, even a practitioner who exerts great energy toward defiled ends still counted as idle in this sense, since genuine good is thereby left to retrogress regardless. A practitioner overtaken by idleness fails to diligently cultivate concentration, concentration not yet attained thus remaining unattained, and concentration already attained thus retrogressing all the same. The external cause of retrogression is agitation, the root cause by which concentration is lost instead through an outward distraction, the mind stirred into restless excitement by recollecting one's own relatives, homeland, past amusements, laughter, and pleasures, obstructing the very stillness calming meditation depends on. A practitioner overtaken by agitation likewise fails to settle the mind, concentration not yet attained thus remaining unattained, and concentration already attained thus retrogressing all the same.

Chinese Source

內定退因者，謂懈怠。外定退因者，謂掉舉。

Ru-Yi Translation

The internal cause of retrogression is idleness. The external cause of retrogression is agitation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites both the Compendium of Mahāyāna Abhidharma and the Extended Treatise on the Five Aggregates to sharpen these two causes considerably beyond ordinary usage, idleness meaning not mere laziness but any indolence in the specific work of cultivating good and abandoning evil, and agitation meaning not mere restlessness but the mind's own inability to settle once memory of past pleasure has been stirred, both definitions considerably more demanding than the words might otherwise suggest.

58. Section: Dullness Retrogresses Concentration from Within, Distraction from Without

Internal retrogression itself is dullness and drowsiness, concentration already attained genuinely lost once idleness has given rise to this dulled state, dullness differing from outright sleep in that some trace of the object of concentration still lingers, however unclear, whereas sleep loses that object entirely. External retrogression itself is distraction toward the five desirable objects, concentration already attained likewise lost once agitation has scattered the mind toward sound, sight, smell, taste, or touch encountered outside.

Chinese Source

內定退者，謂惛沈睡眠。外定退者，謂於五妙欲散亂。

Ru-Yi Translation

Internal retrogression itself is dullness and drowsiness. External retrogression itself is distraction toward the five desirable objects.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the fine distinction drawn here between dullness and outright sleep, dullness still retaining some faint trace of the object of concentration even as clarity fades, a distinction worth noticing precisely because a practitioner who cannot tell the two apart may mistake a genuinely recoverable dullness for a total loss of practice already beyond repair.

59. Section: The Perception of Light Rouses a Mind Dulled from Within

The antidote to internal retrogression and its cause is skillfully grasping the perception of light and correctly contemplating it, a practitioner recognizing that idleness is what produces internal retrogression's dullness and drowsiness, and countering both, together with idleness itself, through diligent, skillful cultivation of this same perception. The Basic Section describes cultivating the perception of light as skillful, diligent, and earnest application to grasping, reflecting on, and thoroughly penetrating light itself, sustaining a mind accompanied by both outward brightness and inward clarity, whether walking back and forth in a sheltered place unseen by others or in an open, exposed one, deliberately bringing to mind, while walking, some pure and excellent object, such as the Buddha, the Dharma, the Saṅgha, precepts, generosity, or the heavens, so as to encourage, praise, and console one's own mind as it turns toward what is genuinely wholesome.

Chinese Source

內定退及因對治者，謂善取相而正觀察。

Ru-Yi Translation

The antidote to internal retrogression and its cause is skillfully grasping the perception of light and correctly contemplating it.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Basic Section's own fuller description of this same practice, walking meditation deliberately paired with recollecting some genuinely joyful, wholesome object, as showing that countering dullness is never a matter of forcing the mind awake through sheer effort alone but of actively redirecting it toward something the mind itself finds encouraging.

60. Section: Contemplating the Body's Impurity and Resting on the Light Behind Both Antidotes, Closing the Seven

The antidote to external retrogression and its cause is contemplating one's own body, and another's, as impure. A practitioner recognizes that agitation is what produces external retrogression's own distraction toward the five desirable objects, and counters both, together with agitation itself, by observing directly that whatever appears beautiful, whether one's own body or another's, is in truth subject to decay, genuinely unlovely, and simply another realm of continued birth and death, this contemplation of impurity thereby dissolving the very desire that distraction depends on. And the support underlying both antidotes, the settling-lifting-releasing method used to counter internal retrogression and the contemplation of impurity used to counter external retrogression alike, is this same perception of light, described elsewhere as threefold, the light that dispels darkness, the light of the Dharma, and the light dependent on the body itself, cultivating this perception of light serving as the shared foundation both antidotes ultimately rest upon. These same seven things correspond to the Śrāvaka Ground's own account of eight abandoning-factors overcoming five specific faults, idleness, forgetting what has been learned, dullness and agitation, failing to act, and acting when one should instead have released, the four factors aspiration, effort, faith, and pliancy countering idleness, mindfulness countering forgetting, correct knowledge countering dullness and agitation, intention countering failing to act, and equanimity countering acting when release was called for instead. This closes the seven things a practitioner genuinely seeking samādhi must correctly know.

Chinese Source

外定退及因對治者，謂即於身觀察不淨。彼二對治依持者，謂光明想。

Ru-Yi Translation

The antidote to external retrogression and its cause is contemplating the body as impure. The support underlying both antidotes is the perception of light.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the final cross-reference back to the earlier eight abandoning-factors as this sevenfold structure's own way of tying two separate teachings on the same subject together, five faults matched against eight remedies in one account and seven diagnostic points in another, both converging on the same practical conclusion that idleness and agitation, however differently named, remain concentration's two real adversaries throughout. This closes the seven things a practitioner genuinely seeking samādhi must correctly know.

61. Section: A Body Born of Past Karma and Present Food, Destined for Death

A fourth aspect of samādhi-cultivation then opens: correctly contemplating the four foundations of mindfulness, beginning with the body itself as the first of these foundations, explained through seven distinct aspects. The first, contemplating the body's own arising, observes that this present body arises from karma accumulated across past lives together with the nourishment of present-life food, the very existence one now inhabits already the ripened fruit of causes long since set in motion. The second, contemplating the body's own cessation, observes that this same body, in every life yet to come, remains a phenomenon bound for death, no amount of careful nurturing ever exempting it from eventual dissolution, a truth meant to loosen attachment to the body rather than encourage its neglect, since a body correctly understood as impermanent becomes, precisely because of that understanding, worth using well while it lasts. The Buddha's own teaching to Nanda in the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya-vastu makes this same point at considerable length, leading Nanda step by step to acknowledge that form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are all impermanent, all suffering, and therefore none of them a self to be clung to, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, coarse or subtle, superior or inferior, far or near, until aversion toward the five aggregates gives way to freedom from attachment, and freedom from attachment gives way to liberation itself, known directly as liberation, birth exhausted, the holy life established, what needed doing done, no further existence to be undergone.

Chinese Source

云何於身觀集法住？謂觀此身，從過去世及諸飲食現在而生。云何於身觀滅法住？謂觀此身，於當來世是死滅法。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contemplating the body's arising as a foundation of mindfulness? Observing that this body arises from past lives together with present food. What is contemplating the body's cessation as a foundation of mindfulness? Observing that this body, in future lives to come, is a phenomenon bound for death.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Buddha's own dialogue with Nanda at length as an early, remarkably systematic catechism on non-self, each aggregate in turn confirmed as impermanent, then as suffering, then as therefore no fit basis for the notion "I" or "mine," the same three-step logic simply repeated across form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness until liberation itself follows almost as a matter of course.

62. Section: A Body Sustained Yet Bound to Perish, and Suffering Understood Four Different Ways

The third aspect, contemplating the body's arising and cessation together, first observes, with respect to this present body, that it is presently sustained and allowed to grow through the condition of food, yet remains, even as it grows, already certain to eventually break apart; and then, with respect to the body considered as belonging to the three realms of continued existence, observes that a practitioner should abide mindfully in what this treatise calls the body's own true suchness, the plain, unchanging truth that this same reported body is, in its own nature, impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self. Buddhist scholastic tradition recognizes that this same truth of suffering is understood differently depending on a practitioner's own capacity, the Tiantai school's fourfold classification of teachings offering one influential account: practitioners of the Tripiṭaka teaching use an analytic emptiness that painstakingly confirms suffering's own arising truth by truth; practitioners of the Shared teaching use an inherent emptiness that recognizes suffering's illusory, dreamlike character directly, without needing to break it apart first; practitioners of the Distinct teaching recognize suffering as boundless in its own forms, as many as the differing destinies across the ten dharma-realms themselves; and practitioners of the Perfect teaching recognize that the aggregates and sense-fields are themselves already suchness, no suffering even left to be abandoned. This same fourfold distinction applies equally well to how this treatise's own "true suchness of the body" may itself be contemplated, depending on the depth of insight a given practitioner has actually reached.

Chinese Source

云何於身觀集滅法住？謂觀此身，於現在世由飲食緣增長久住，必當破壞。於有身者，謂於此身善住其念於真如身。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contemplating the body's arising and cessation together as a foundation of mindfulness? Observing that this body, presently sustained and made to grow through the condition of food, must eventually break apart. With respect to the body of continued existence, one abides mindfully in this same body's true suchness.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws on the Tiantai school's fourfold classification of teachings, drawn in turn from the Collected Commentaries on the Tiantai Fourfold Doctrine, to show that "true suchness" is never a single fixed insight but a depth of understanding that itself deepens as a practitioner's own capacity matures, from the śrāvaka's careful analysis through to the fully awakened bodhisattva's direct recognition that suffering was never, in truth, separate from suchness to begin with.

63. Section: Calming Yields Transcendent Wisdom, Insight Yields Transcendent Vision, Mutually Inclusive Throughout

The fourth aspect, contemplating the body solely through transcendent wisdom, refers to the internal path of calming meditation, a practitioner cultivating calming in reliance on contemplating the Four Noble Truths until calming and insight operate together as one, transcendent wisdom free of any discrimination between subject and object finally realized, wisdom itself settling still exactly as calming does, which is why this attainment is described here through the path of calming specifically. The fifth aspect, contemplating the body solely through transcendent vision, refers instead to the path of insight, transcendent, outflow-free correct vision obtained through reflective reasoning and careful observation, vision here carrying the sense of active inquiry, which is why this attainment is described through the path of insight specifically. Calming alone yields wisdom free of discrimination, while genuine transcendent vision additionally requires insight's own active observation to directly realize impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and non-self; in practice, though, calming is never cultivated apart from insight, nor insight apart from calming, the two operating jointly and mutually reinforcing one another throughout, exactly as later commentarial tradition would put it, discrimination stilled is itself calming found within insight, while emptiness still actively seen through wisdom is itself insight found within calming, a single inconceivable operation only the sages themselves accomplish.

Chinese Source

或唯出世間智者，謂由於內奢摩他道。或唯出世間見者，謂由毗鉢舍那道。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contemplating the body solely through transcendent wisdom? By way of the internal path of calming meditation. What is contemplating the body solely through transcendent vision? By way of the path of insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Huayan tradition's own account of calming-insight mutuality, stillness that is itself a form of insight and insight that never abandons stillness, as this pair's real point, wisdom and vision named separately here only because this passage is isolating each contribution for the sake of clear explanation, never because the two genuinely operate apart from one another in practice.

64. Section: The Wisdom Attained After Realizing Suchness Directly

The sixth aspect, contemplating the body solely through recollection, refers to the transcendent path subsequently attained once the fundamental wisdom directly realizing suchness has already been achieved. The Basic Section describes practitioners of various faculties, having already entered direct realization of the Four Noble Truths or being on the verge of doing so, no longer dwelling on the specific characteristics of whatever absorption first led them there, instead contemplating the five aggregates as diseased, as a tumor, growing weary and fearful of every conditioned phenomenon while sustaining recollection fixed on the deathless realm, thereby entering the outflow-free absorption proper. Kuiji's own commentary distinguishes this subsequently attained wisdom from the fundamental wisdom that precedes it: fundamental wisdom takes principle itself as its object, according directly with reality exactly as it truly is, while subsequently attained wisdom takes phenomena as its object instead, observing the full extent of whatever exists, itself further divided into a transcendent subsequent wisdom, whose object remains true suchness, and a mundane subsequent wisdom, whose object is ordinary worldly phenomena such as permanence, impermanence, or the presence or absence of outflow. A further fivefold breakdown of this subsequently attained wisdom appears in Asvabhāva's commentary on the Compendium of Mahāyāna: penetrating deliberation, which inwardly confirms what has already been directly realized concerning suchness; recollective deliberation, which later calls that same realization back to mind; establishing deliberation, which sets out from that realization to teach others what has been penetrated; unifying deliberation, which observes every phenomenon through its shared general characteristics in order to advance toward the transformation of one's own basis; and deliberation according with wish, which manifests whatever is intended exactly as wished, as when earth itself is transformed into gold. This subsequently attained wisdom bears directly on the path of severing affliction as well, reflecting on its own object so that whatever the fundamental wisdom has already severed never arises again, the fundamental wisdom alone advancing only the transcendent path of cultivation, while this subsequent wisdom advances both the mundane and the transcendent paths together, no purely mundane path ever capable, on its own, of permanently destroying affliction's underlying latent tendencies.

Chinese Source

或唯憶念者，謂由此後所得出世間道。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contemplating the body solely through recollection? By way of the transcendent path subsequently attained after this.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats Asvabhāva's fivefold breakdown, penetrating, recollective, establishing, unifying, and wish-according deliberation, as tracing this subsequently attained wisdom's own natural life cycle, first confirmed inwardly, then recalled, then taught to others, then generalized across all phenomena, and only at the very end capable of the effortless mastery the final kind describes, a sequence worth noticing precisely because it shows how much groundwork stands behind what might otherwise look like an instantaneous attainment.

65. Section: Clinging to No Concentration Whatsoever Closes Body-Mindfulness, the Eight Categories, and the Provisions for Renunciation Themselves

The seventh and final aspect, contemplating the body through dwelling with no support whatsoever, means that, even while relying on any of the various levels of concentration to cultivate this same foundation of mindfulness, a practitioner harbors no craving whatsoever for that concentration itself, still less any attachment or clinging to it. Concentration is meant to be cultivated in steady, unbroken progression, never mistaking a merely adequate attainment for the final goal; craving or clinging to whatever level has already been reached only prevents further progress and, in serious cases, invites concentration's own retrogression, especially once such attachment becomes entangled with desire itself. This closes the seven aspects belonging to the body as the first foundation of mindfulness. A brief closing statement then covers all four foundations of mindfulness together: correctly grasping nothing whatsoever regarding worldly existence means that the four inverted views, mistaking what is impure for pure, what is suffering for bliss, what is without self for self, and what is impermanent for permanent, have all been permanently severed, a practitioner who has permanently severed these four inversions never again clinging to the body, or to feeling, mind, or mental phenomena, as pure, blissful, a self, or permanent. This single closing statement brings the entire discussion of correctly contemplating the four foundations of mindfulness to a close, and with it, category by category, an extraordinary cascade of closures completes at once: the samādhi-cultivation category itself, the eighth and final of the eight categories individually establishing renunciation's own provisions; the eight categories of individual establishment as a whole; the completeness of renunciation's own provisions that those eight categories exist to establish; and, with that completeness now fully accounted for, the entire discussion of renunciation's own provisions first opened many sections earlier under this same eighth topic's account of miscellaneous meanings.

Chinese Source

云何於身無所依住？謂依諸定修習念住，即於彼定無有愛味，乃至無有住著。云何於世間無所取執？謂於四顛倒已永斷滅；永斷滅故，於彼身等終不執取淨、樂、我、常。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is contemplating the body through dwelling with no support? Relying on various levels of concentration to cultivate this foundation of mindfulness, yet harboring no craving, still less any attachment, toward that concentration itself. What is correctly grasping nothing whatsoever regarding worldly existence? The four inverted views having been permanently severed, one never again clings to the body and the rest as pure, blissful, a self, or permanent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing pair, clinging to no concentration whatsoever and the permanent severing of all four inverted views, as together summarizing the entire, many-sections-long discussion of renunciation's provisions in miniature, since a practitioner who still clings to any attainment, however genuine, has not yet completed what these eight categories were actually built to establish. He highlights the scale of what closes at this single point, the samādhi-cultivation category, all eight categories together, the completeness of renunciation's own provisions, and the discussion of those provisions themselves, as marking one of this fascicle's most significant structural milestones, the treatise now free to open an entirely new topic within its own broader discussion of miscellaneous meanings.

66. Section: Five Kinds of Persons, Sorted by What They Delight In and How Far They've Traveled

A new topic then opens: the discussion of virtue and fault across the pure and the defiled, laid out through thirty-one distinct aspects, beginning with five kinds of persons established according to what each delights in and pursues. The first delights in the pleasure ordinary beings pursue. The second delights in severing obstruction along the path of insight. The third delights in a liberation pursued along a path of view outside this teaching. The fourth reaches what is mistaken for the ultimate along that same path of view. And the fifth reaches the genuine ultimate along the path of insight.

Chinese Source

復次，依止施設建立五種補特伽羅。云何為五？一、欣樂喜樂諸異生者，二、欣樂障斷見跡行者，三、欣樂解脫見趣行者，四、到究竟見趣行者，五、到究竟見跡行者。

Ru-Yi Translation

Five kinds of persons are established: those who delight in pleasure, ordinary beings all; those who delight in severing obstruction, following the path of insight; those who delight in liberation, following a path of view; those who reach what is mistaken for the ultimate, following the path of view; and those who reach the genuine ultimate, following the path of insight.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the distinction running through all five, "path of view" naming an intellectual conviction that may or may not correspond to genuine realization, "path of insight" naming direct realization itself, as this whole fivefold classification's real organizing principle, the third and fourth kinds both following a mere path of view even though only the fourth actually mistakes its own destination for the truth.

67. Section: Four Kinds of Ordinary Beings, Sorted by Which Pleasure They Delight In

The first kind, ordinary beings who delight in pleasure, comprises four further distinctions. The first delights in the pleasure born of desire itself, the joy and bliss arising from the desire realm's own forms, sounds, smells, tastes, and touches. The second delights in the pleasure born of the first absorption's concentration, still accompanied by investigation and analysis. The third delights in the pleasure born of the intermediate concentration between the first and second absorptions, free of investigation yet still accompanied by analysis. And the fourth delights in the pleasure born of the second absorption and beyond, free of both investigation and analysis altogether. Among ordinary beings within the three realms, delighting in the pleasure desire itself brings counts as genuinely defiled, while delighting in the pleasure any of the three higher concentrations brings counts as comparatively pure, at least relative to a being still bound to desire.

Chinese Source

云何欣樂喜樂異生補特伽羅？應知此有四種。謂欣樂欲生喜樂、欣樂有尋有伺定生喜樂、欣樂無尋有伺定生喜樂、欣樂無尋無伺定生喜樂差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

What are ordinary beings who delight in pleasure? Four kinds: those who delight in the pleasure born of desire; those who delight in the pleasure born of concentration accompanied by investigation and analysis; those who delight in the pleasure born of concentration free of investigation yet still accompanied by analysis; and those who delight in the pleasure born of concentration free of both.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fourfold scale, from desire's own pleasure up through concentration entirely free of investigation and analysis, as a genuinely relative rather than absolute measure of purity, the meditative pleasures still counting, from an ordinary being's own vantage point, as an improvement worth pursuing, even though every one of the four remains, from the standpoint of the path as a whole, still squarely within the condition this treatise calls "ordinary."

68. Section: Wisdom-Liberated and Both-Liberated Arhats, Sorted by What Obstruction They've Severed

The second kind, those who delight in severing obstruction along the path of insight, comprises two further kinds, those who delight in severing the afflictive obstruction and those who delight in severing the concentration obstruction, the two obstructions genuinely distinct in their effect, the concentration obstruction never itself blocking liberation from birth and death the way the afflictive obstruction does. Severing the afflictions born of craving and view is generally sufficient to attain arhatship, yet not every arhat has thereby also severed the concentration obstruction, giving rise to two kinds of arhat, the wisdom-liberated arhat, who has not attained the cessation-of-feeling-and-perception absorption, and the both-liberated arhat, who has severed the afflictions of craving and view across all three realms and has also fully severed the concentration obstruction, thereby accomplishing that same cessation absorption as well. Those who delight in severing the afflictive obstruction specifically are further distinguished by whether they have or have not yet attained the present-life ease belonging to the four fundamental absorptions, and by whether they have or have not yet attained any of the various states possessing perception, this difference itself tracing back to a difference in what each relies on and what each observes.

Chinese Source

欣樂煩惱障斷補特伽羅，於現法樂住未得已得，於一切種有想等至未得已得，謂於依止及於觀察所知有差別故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who delight in severing the afflictive obstruction may or may not yet have attained the present-life ease of the four absorptions, and may or may not yet have attained the various states possessing perception, this difference tracing back to a difference in what is relied upon and what is observed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites Master Yìn Shùn's own studies of how various early schools treated the relationship between concentration and wisdom, some keeping the two sharply distinct, others folding the cessation absorption directly into the eight liberations as this treatise itself does, as background for why the wisdom-liberated and both-liberated distinction became necessary in the first place, severing affliction alone never automatically guaranteeing that every level of meditative mastery has also been reached.

69. Section: Eight Bases of Mastery and Ten Bases of Totality Close the Second Kind

Those who delight in severing the concentration obstruction specifically may or may not yet have attained any of the eight bases of mastery, and may or may not yet have attained any of the ten bases of totality. The eight bases of mastery, perceiving form internally while observing a small or a large amount of form externally, perceiving no form internally while observing a small or a large amount of form externally, and perceiving no form internally while observing external forms as blue, yellow, red, or white, together with the three lower formless absorptions, bring physical form itself under a practitioner's own control once mastered. The ten bases of totality extend this same practice further still, taking blue, yellow, red, white, earth, water, fire, wind, boundless space, and boundless consciousness each as an all-pervading object of concentration, both groups aimed at cultivating genuine supernatural ability. A practitioner who has not yet attained these bases possesses no such ability; one who has fully severed both the afflictive obstruction and the concentration obstruction together, possessing supernatural ability and the cessation absorption alike, is the both-liberated arhat already described. This closes the second of the five kinds of persons.

Chinese Source

欣樂定障斷補特伽羅，於一切勝處未得已得，及於一切遍處未得已得。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who delight in severing the concentration obstruction may or may not yet have attained any of the bases of mastery, and may or may not yet have attained any of the bases of totality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the bases of mastery and the bases of totality together as this treatise's own graduated curriculum for supernatural ability, mastery over form itself practiced first at a smaller scale before the bases of totality extend that same mastery outward to fill the entire field of perception, closing the second of the five kinds of persons.

70. Section: A Non-Buddhist's Selfless View, A Meditator's Mistaken Peak, and the Arhat's Genuine Ultimate, Closing the Five Kinds of Persons

The third kind, those who delight in liberation along a path of view outside this teaching, refers to certain non-Buddhist practitioners who hold the view that no self and nothing belonging to a self exists in the present, and that no self and nothing belonging to a self will exist in the future either, a view some among them, having since entered the Buddha's own teaching and directly realized the truths for themselves, have genuinely attained, while others have not yet done so; those who do attain it reach this same correct view of non-self precisely by observing the impermanence of the five aggregates in the present, from which the same certainty extends naturally into the future as well. The fourth kind, those who reach what is mistaken for the ultimate along that same path of view, refers to practitioners who either have or have not yet attained the absorption of neither perception nor non-perception, a state some among them mistake for nirvana's own final peak, though it remains, in truth, merely the most subtle level of continued existence rather than genuine liberation. And the fifth kind, those who reach the genuine ultimate along the path of insight, are the arhats themselves, whose six sense faculties encounter their six respective objects without giving rise to any affliction whatsoever, every trace of craving and view permanently severed, nirvana genuinely and completely realized, entirely free of attachment to any state whatsoever. Established according to the differing pure and defiled destinations sentient beings themselves pursue, these five kinds of persons together close this opening account.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

What are those who delight in liberation along a path of view? Certain non-Buddhist practitioners hold that no self or what belongs to a self exists now or will exist in the future, a view some have attained and others have not. What are those who reach what is mistaken for the ultimate along the path of view? Those who have or have not yet attained the absorption of neither perception nor non-perception. What are those who reach the genuine ultimate along the path of insight? Those who have permanently and completely severed affliction at all six sense-bases. These five kinds of persons are thus established according to the pure and defiled destinations sentient beings pursue.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi highlights the fourth kind's own cautionary force, mistaking the absorption of neither perception nor non-perception for nirvana itself being precisely the error tradition attributes to the two teachers the Buddha studied under before his own awakening, both reaching this same subtle peak of existence and both, in the Buddha's own later judgment, still short of genuine liberation, closing the five kinds of persons established according to the pure and defiled destinations sentient beings pursue.

71. Section: Three Virtues of a Genuine Lay Buddhist

A second topic then opens: three virtues belonging to the upāsaka, the lay Buddhist man, purity, capability in action, and capability in drawing forth wisdom. Purity itself comprises three further kinds: purity of aspiration, meaning freedom from any doubt regarding the virtues of the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, together with no longer seeking worldly prosperity or regarding worldly success itself as auspicious; purity of precept conduct, meaning the ability to fulfill every precept required of a lay Buddhist completely; and purity of realization, meaning the ability to attain mundane purity, up through the four form and four formless absorptions, and, for a more advanced upāsaka, transcendent purity as well, up through the first, second, or third fruit, since attaining the fourth and final fruit necessarily means either going forth as a monastic or entering nirvana outright, at which point one is no longer, strictly speaking, an upāsaka at all. Upāsaka, a term used broadly across Indian religious traditions to mean one who serves or attends a renunciant, became within Buddhism the standard term specifically for a lay Buddhist man.

Chinese Source

復次，鄔波索迦有三種德。一、清淨，二、能造作，三、能引發。清淨者，謂意樂清淨、戒行清淨、證清淨。意樂清淨者，謂於佛寶等遠離疑惑，不希世事謂作吉祥。戒行清淨者，謂能圓滿所有學處。證清淨者，謂能證得世出世清淨故。

Ru-Yi Translation

A lay Buddhist man possesses three virtues: purity, capability in action, and capability in drawing forth wisdom. Purity comprises purity of aspiration, meaning freedom from doubt regarding the Three Jewels and no longer seeking worldly prosperity; purity of precept conduct, meaning fulfilling every required precept completely; and purity of realization, meaning the ability to attain both mundane and transcendent purity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats purity of realization's own upper limit, the third fruit rather than the fourth, as a structurally necessary boundary rather than an arbitrary one, since a lay Buddhist who genuinely reaches full liberation no longer remains, by definition, a layperson at all, either going forth or passing directly into nirvana, the very identity "upāsaka" itself built around this specific, bounded range of attainment.

72. Section: Serving the Three Jewels and Drawing Forth Wisdom in Others, Closing the Lay Buddhist's Three Virtues

Capability in action means being able to carry out whatever tasks serve the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha, whether genuine practice and realization or the accumulation of merit and wisdom through service itself. Capability in drawing forth wisdom means being able to draw forth mundane wisdom in fellow practitioners following the same teaching, and transcendent wisdom in those following a different path entirely, an alternate reading understanding this instead as drawing forth wisdom in fellow lay practitioners studying alongside oneself, and, beyond that same immediate circle, the still more excellent, outflow-free wisdom belonging to the monastic assembly or to learned noble ones still dwelling as householders. This closes the three virtues belonging to a genuine lay Buddhist man.

Chinese Source

能作三寶所作事故，名能造作。能引發同法、不同法者智故，名能引發。

Ru-Yi Translation

Being able to carry out whatever serves the Three Jewels is called capability in action. Being able to draw forth wisdom in those following the same teaching and those following a different one is called capability in drawing forth wisdom.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the two competing readings of "capability in drawing forth wisdom," one aimed outward toward practitioners of an entirely different path and one aimed inward toward fellow lay Buddhists and the monastic assembly alike, as not genuinely in conflict, a lay Buddhist of real virtue naturally becoming, in either reading, a source of encouragement for whoever comes into contact with them, this closing the three virtues belonging to a genuine lay Buddhist man.
