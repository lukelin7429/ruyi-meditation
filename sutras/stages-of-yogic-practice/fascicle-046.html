Fascicle 46 · 瑜伽師地論卷第四十六 · The Four Seals, and the Qualities of a Bodhisattva

Impermanence, Suffering, No-Self, and Nirvāṇa's Peace

This fascicle closes the seventeenth chapter, the factors of awakening, with its fifteenth and final topic — the four dharma-summaries, the classical four seals every Buddhist teaching ultimately rests on — before opening the eighteenth and last chapter of the Bodhisattva Stage's foundational section: the qualities that actually define a bodhisattva.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: in progress Source: Taishō T30, no. 1579, fascicle 46 Commentary guide: Master Cháng Bǎi's lecture notes

1. Milestone: Fascicle 46 Opens — the Fifteenth and Final Topic, the Four Dharma-Summaries Named as the Classical Four Seals

Fascicle 46 opens as the third and final installment of the seventeenth chapter, the factors of awakening. Its fifteenth and closing topic, the four dharma-summaries, is spoken by every buddha and bodhisattva specifically to purify beings' own bodily, verbal, and mental conduct. Four verses are named: all conditioned activities are impermanent; all conditioned activities are suffering; all phenomena are without self; and nirvāṇa is peace — the classical four seals underlying the entire Buddhist teaching, whether taught to the lesser vehicle or the great.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處菩提分品第十七之三。復有四種法嗢柁南，諸佛菩薩欲令有情清淨故說。何等為四？一切諸行皆是無常，是名第一法嗢柁南。一切諸行皆悉是苦，是名第二法嗢柁南。一切諸法皆無有我，是名第三法嗢柁南。涅槃寂靜，是名第四法嗢柁南。

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter seventeen, part three: the factors of awakening. There are, further, four dharma-summaries, spoken by every buddha and bodhisattva in order to purify beings. What are these four? All conditioned activities are impermanent — this is called the first dharma-summary. All conditioned activities are suffering — this is called the second dharma-summary. All phenomena are without self — this is called the third dharma-summary. Nirvāṇa is peace — this is called the fourth dharma-summary.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies these four verses directly with the classical four dharma seals every school of Buddhism recognizes as foundational, and unpacks "udāna," rendered here "dharma-summary," through four overlapping senses the Yogācārabhūmi's own commentarial tradition preserves: gathering many teachings into a compact verse easy for a student to hold in memory; a seal, in the sense of an unchanging mark stamped onto reality itself — impermanence sealing every conditioned thing, suffering sealing every defiled thing, non-self sealing every phenomenon whatsoever, and nirvāṇa's peace sealing the unconditioned; recitation, since the Buddha spoke these four verses constantly and repeatedly; and summary meaning, condensing the entire teaching's own essential content into its sharpest possible form — impermanence as the defining mark of conditioned existence, suffering as the defining mark of contaminated existence, non-self as the defining mark of every phenomenon without exception, and nirvāṇa's peace as the defining mark of the unconditioned.

He treats each of the four verses through its own textual grounding in the Śrāvaka Stage's own earlier chapters: conditioned activities are impermanent because they arise from causes and conditions, having been genuinely absent before and dispersing again once arisen — true equally of the sentient world's own beings, whom the Buddha's own pure celestial eye directly observes dying and being reborn without pause, and of the container world itself, which the Buddha likewise describes as enduring only for a time before eventually being consumed by fire, right up through Mount Sumeru and the Brahmā heavens themselves, leaving not even ash behind. Conditioned activities are suffering because impermanence in the five aggregates necessarily brings birth, aging, sickness, death, and the sorrow that inevitably follows. All phenomena are without self because that same suffering leaves a being genuinely without self-mastery, its very powerlessness proving the absence of any controlling self — a proof extending, in the great vehicle's own reading, to a further absence of any fixed essence in phenomena themselves, not merely in persons. And nirvāṇa is peace because it is the point at which every form of suffering is definitively put to rest and every affliction is finally, completely extinguished. This closes the naming of the four dharma-summaries; the treatise turns next to the three distinct senses in which the word "udāna" itself applies to them.

2. Milestone: Three Meanings of "Udāna" — Correspondence With Reality, the Buddhas' Own Constant Repetition, and the Very Path That Leads Beyond Existence Itself

The treatise names three distinct reasons these four verses earn the specific name "dharma-summary." First, correspondence: buddhas and bodhisattvas teach these verses to beings precisely because each verse genuinely corresponds to its object — impermanence and suffering corresponding to conditioned activities, non-self corresponding to every phenomenon whatsoever, and peace corresponding to nirvāṇa. Second, constant repetition: every buddha, whose mind has been utterly stilled since beginningless time, has spoken these same four verses across past, present, and future without exception, which is precisely why they are called dharma-summaries. Third, the path itself: these four verses are the very track leading toward favorable rebirth, and, more importantly, the track leading a practitioner entirely beyond the very highest point still contained within conditioned existence.

Chinese Source

諸佛菩薩多為有情宣說如是法相應義，是故說名法嗢柁南。又從曩昔，其心寂靜諸牟尼尊，於一切時展轉宣說，是故說此名嗢柁南。又此行跡能趣大生，亦復能趣出第一有，是故說此名嗢柁南。

Ru-Yi Translation

Buddhas and bodhisattvas, teaching beings this meaning that genuinely corresponds to the dharma, are for this reason said to speak a dharma-summary. Furthermore, since time immemorial, the sages whose minds have become genuinely still have, at every time, repeatedly proclaimed this same teaching — this is why it is called a dharma-summary. Furthermore, this very track leads toward favorable rebirth, and is likewise able to lead one entirely beyond the very highest state of existence — this is why it is called a dharma-summary.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first meaning as simply confirming that these four verses were never arbitrary slogans but genuine, verified correspondences between statement and reality — impermanence truly does characterize every conditioned activity, non-self truly does characterize every phenomenon, and so through each of the four in turn. He treats the second meaning, constant repetition, as underscoring the sheer weight of authority behind these four verses: not spoken once as an isolated remark but proclaimed, identically, by every awakened being across the entire span of past, present, and future, precisely because a mind that has genuinely extinguished every trace of conceptual proliferation and entered great, unconditioned nirvāṇa sees reality in exactly this way without exception or variation.

He treats the third meaning, "track" or "footprint," as this passage's own most evocative image: exactly as a path through open country bears the visible marks of everyone who has walked it before, these four verses mark out the actual route a practitioner's own progress follows — Master Miaojing's own gloss specifies that "favorable rebirth" here names the excellent fruits of human and celestial existence a lesser-vehicle practitioner reaches by walking this path, while "beyond the very highest state of existence" names the summit of the formless realm, the sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, traditionally called "the first existence" for standing at the very peak of conditioned being — a summit genuinely surpassed only by walking this same fourfold path all the way through to nirvāṇa itself. He notes Elder Miaojing's own further point that a great-vehicle practitioner walking the identical track reaches something greater still, supreme, complete awakening itself, since the same four verses, read with the great vehicle's own understanding of emptiness, extend beyond the lesser vehicle's own more limited horizon without ever contradicting it. This closes the three meanings of "dharma-summary" itself; the treatise turns next to unpacking each of the four verses in full detail, beginning with the first: that all conditioned activities are impermanent.

3. Milestone: Impermanence Opens — No Conditioned Activity Ever Possessed a Fixed Verbal Essence in the First Place, Across Four Rising Levels of Genuine Reality

Unpacking the first dharma-summary, the treatise names two ways a bodhisattva observes impermanence in every conditioned activity across the sentient and container worlds together. The first: observing that the verbally-labeled essence of any conditioned activity was never, at any time whatsoever, actually present — what ordinary language and concept name and grasp at was empty from the very start, so this absence itself is called impermanence. This absence of any fixed essence in what is merely conceptually constructed is precisely the single mark that discloses the emptiness of phenomena, whether examined internally, externally, or in the process arising between the two.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩等隨觀察一切諸行皆是無常？謂諸菩薩，觀一切行言說自性，於一切時常無所有。如是諸行常不可得，故名無常。

Ru-Yi Translation

How do bodhisattvas universally and thoroughly observe that all conditioned activities are impermanent? Bodhisattvas who observe that the verbally-labeled essence of every conditioned activity is, at all times, never actually present at all — since these conditioned activities are thus always ungraspable in this sense, this is called impermanence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own later chapter of determinations directly: "penetrating the emptiness of phenomena" is disclosed by this single mark of ungraspability alone, applied to the aggregates, elements, and sense-bases, whether examined as the six inner faculties, the six outer objects, or the six consciousnesses arising between them — in every case, the fixed, independently existing essence that an unlearned mind habitually projects onto these categories through mere verbal labeling turns out, on examination, never to have been genuinely present. He cites the Ornament of the Mahāyāna Sūtras' own concise formula directly: "nonexistence" is precisely what impermanence means here, since the conceptually-constructed characteristic is, in the end, utterly without substance.

He treats this first sense of impermanence as resting on the treatise's own fourfold hierarchy of genuine reality, already introduced in earlier fascicles but worth recalling in full here: worldly genuine reality, the common-sense world every ordinary person already agrees on without needing to think twice — earth is earth, not fire; pleasure is pleasure, not pain — inherited through beginningless habitual conditioning; rational genuine reality, reached through careful investigation using direct perception, inference, and scriptural authority to correctly discern the exhaustive particulars and essential nature of the aggregates, sense-bases, and elements; genuine reality disclosed by wisdom purified of afflictive obstruction, available to noble śrāvakas who have directly realized selflessness through the four truths, still using conventional language while no longer clinging to any fixed self behind it; and genuine reality disclosed by wisdom purified of the obstruction to complete knowledge, available only to buddhas and bodhisattvas who have realized that every phenomenon's wordless nature is neither simply nonexistent nor simply existent, resting in a genuinely non-discriminating wisdom beyond both extremes. An ordinary being, lacking any of these four levels of genuine insight, mistakes the merely conceptually-constructed for something real, and it is precisely this basic confusion that generates the entire cycle of affliction, karma, and continued rebirth this teaching aims to interrupt. This closes the first sense of impermanence; the treatise turns next to its second, deeper sense — moment-to-moment arising and ceasing within the dependently arisen process itself.

4. Milestone: Moment-to-Moment Arising — Every Conditioned Activity Is Genuinely New, Never the Same Instant Twice

The second, deeper sense of impermanence turns from the mere absence of fixed verbal essence to the actual, moment-to-moment birth and death of the dependently arisen process itself. Ordinary beings, not understanding that conditioned activities are illusion-like — mere reflections, echoes, moons in water, dreams — mistake them for solid and enduring, and it is precisely this ignorance that drives the karma producing birth, aging, sickness, and death. Correctly seen, however, every dependently arisen activity is arising and ceasing genuinely moment by moment, never persisting even for a second instant — and this ceaseless, moment-to-moment birth and death is what impermanence, in its second and deeper sense, actually names.

Chinese Source

又即觀彼離言說事，由不了知彼真實故；無知為因，生滅可得。如是諸行離言自性有生有滅，故名無常。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, observing this same matter beyond words, since its genuine reality is not understood, ignorance becomes the cause through which arising and ceasing become apparent. In this way, the wordless essence of conditioned activities genuinely has both arising and ceasing — this is why it is called impermanence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Yogācārabhūmi commentary's own vivid illustration of exactly how personal and untranslatable each moment of direct experience actually is: what the eye directly registers of a color, or the body directly feels of a touch, cannot actually be conveyed in words at all — everyone standing before the same green mountain and clear water sees a genuinely different shade of green, moment to moment, and no amount of shared vocabulary actually transmits that private, momentary experience from one person to another, exactly as no one else can feel precisely how cold or hot water someone else is drinking actually is. Ordinary beings never pause to notice this, instead clinging to the shared, general label as though it named something genuinely fixed and continuous, and this clinging plants karmic seeds in the store-consciousness that ripen into the very body and world encountered in a future life.

He treats the underlying diagnosis — ignorance as the specific cause producing apparent arising and ceasing — as the treatise's own way of locating impermanence's ultimate root not in the phenomena themselves but in the mind's own failure to correctly understand what those phenomena actually are: a being who genuinely understood every dependently arisen activity as illusion-like from the very start would not be driven by that same ignorance into further karma-producing craving and aversion. This closes the second sense of impermanence in outline; the treatise turns next to observing this same moment-to-moment impermanence through the specific lens of past, present, and future.

5. Milestone: Neither Cause Nor Effect Genuinely Persists — Observing Impermanence Across Past, Present, and Future

A bodhisattva observes impermanence through the three times in turn. Regarding the past: conditioned activities have already arisen and already ceased, their cause already fully spent and their own nature already exhausted, so neither cause nor nature remains to be found there at all. Regarding the present: conditioned activities have already arisen but not yet ceased, their cause already spent in producing this very result yet their nature still genuinely present since cessation has not yet occurred — present activity, in other words, has nature but no longer any findable cause. Regarding the future: conditioned activities have not yet arisen and so cannot yet have ceased, their cause already present as potential yet their nature not yet actual since arising itself has not yet occurred — future activity, in other words, has cause but not yet any nature.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，觀過去行已生已滅，由彼諸行無因可得，亦無自性，是故觀彼因性、自性皆無所有。觀現在行已生未滅，由彼諸行因不可得，已與果故；自性可得，猶未滅故；是故觀彼自性是有，而無有因。觀未來行未生未滅，由彼諸行有因可得，未與果故；無有自性，猶未生故；是故觀彼唯有因性，而無自性。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas observe that past conditioned activities have already arisen and already ceased; since their cause is no longer to be found, and they possess no nature of their own any longer, one observes that both their causal quality and their own nature are entirely absent. One observes that present conditioned activities have already arisen but have not yet ceased; since their cause cannot be found, having already produced its result, yet their own nature can still be found, not having ceased as yet, one observes that their nature exists, though no cause remains. One observes that future conditioned activities have not yet arisen and have not yet ceased; since their cause can still be found, not having yet produced its result, yet they possess no nature of their own, not having yet arisen, one observes that only their causal quality is present, with no nature yet to be found.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through this threefold analysis with a concrete illustration for each: reflecting on oneself at age three, that entire causal chain has already been fully spent producing the person now present, and the three-year-old's own momentary nature has likewise already completely passed — even yesterday, even the immediately preceding instant, is already gone in exactly this same sense, cause and nature both equally unfindable. The present, by contrast, resembles a seed that has already become a sprout — the seed itself, as cause, is spent and gone the moment the sprout appears, since a sprout is simply not a seed, yet the sprout's own nature is genuinely, presently available for use, not yet having ceased. The future resembles someone currently cultivating the causes for rebirth in a pure land: the cause is being planted right now, genuinely present as such, yet the pure land itself, as an actual future nature, has not yet appeared and so cannot yet be found.

He treats this entire threefold analysis as intentionally the coarser, more accessible layer of the argument — easier to grasp intuitively than the more exacting moment-to-moment analysis still to come, since anyone can recognize that the past is genuinely gone and the future genuinely not yet arrived. What this analysis already establishes, even at this comparatively coarse level, is that nothing within the three times ever holds both cause and nature together at once, existing complete and stable in itself — everything observed moves continuously from having neither, to having only nature, to having only cause, and back to having neither again, which is itself already a demonstration of impermanence, prior to the still finer moment-to-moment analysis the treatise turns to next.

6. Milestone: The Finer Analysis Opens — Death and Rebirth as Simultaneous as the Two Ends of a Scale, and Four Marks Packed Into a Single Instant

Having observed impermanence through gross temporal segments, a bodhisattva then observes it at the level of each single instant, finding within every one of these instants three positive marks of conditioned existence — arising, abiding, and aging — and, immediately following that same instant, a fourth mark, cessation. Genuinely, however, birth and cessation occur without any actual gap between them at all: exactly as the two ends of a balance scale rise and fall together in a single motion, the very instant one state of being ceases, the next state of being arises simultaneously, with no interval whatsoever separating them — a being's own death and the immediately following rebirth occurring together in precisely this way, one continuous motion rather than two separate events.

Chinese Source

菩薩如是見三世中分段諸行相續轉已，等隨觀見一一剎那有為諸行，皆有三種有為之相。於剎那後，復有第四有為之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas who, having thus seen how conditioned activities, divided into segments across the three times, continue on in succession, universally and thoroughly observe that each and every instant of conditioned activity possesses three marks of conditioned existence; and, immediately following that same instant, there is a fourth mark of conditioned existence as well.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the classical balance-scale image, preserved in the Yogācārabhūmi commentary, as this passage's own most memorable illustration: when weights are added to one side of a scale, that side does not first fully descend before the other side begins to rise — both motions occur as a single, continuous event, one side's fall and the other's rise being, in truth, the very same motion described from two vantage points. A being's own death and the very next moment's rebirth into an intermediate state work identically, he explains: the aggregates of this present life dissolve backward into the past at the exact instant the aggregates of the next existence arise forward into being, precisely as pushing through a revolving door sends the previous compartment backward while simultaneously drawing the next one forward — the two motions never actually separated by so much as an instant.

He treats the apparent tension in naming four marks yet insisting there is no actual gap between them as fully resolved once "arising, abiding, and aging" are properly understood as describing a single instant's own positive, present phase, while "cessation" describes that same instant's own necessary and immediate disappearance — not a separate later event requiring its own additional cause, but the automatic, built-in conclusion of the very instant that arose. Arising and ceasing, on this reading, are provisionally distinguished in language purely for the sake of analysis, while in actual fact occurring together as inseparably as the scale's own two ends. This closes the setup of the finer, instant-by-instant analysis; the treatise turns next to unpacking exactly what "arising," "abiding," "aging," and "cessation" each specifically mean within this single instant.

7. Milestone: Birth, Abiding, Aging, and Cessation Defined — Four Angles on One and the Same Fleeting Instant, Never Four Separate Substances

Within this single instant, the previous instant's own nature has already ceased; the uninterrupted, immediately following arising of a genuinely new instant's own nature is correctly observed as birth; that same newly arisen activity, not yet having perished, is correctly observed as abiding; that same already-arisen activity, differing from the already-ceased activity immediately preceding it, is correctly observed as aging; and, immediately following this very instant of arising, that same already-arisen activity's own nature perishing is correctly observed as cessation. A bodhisattva observes that this already-arisen instant's own nature is nothing beyond precisely this birth, this abiding, and this aging — no separate, additional substance called "birth" or "aging" exists apart from the activity itself, and, just as genuinely, the very same activity's own subsequent perishing carries no separate substance called "cessation" apart from the activity's own natural, uncaused ending either.

Chinese Source

即於此中，前剎那行自性滅壞。無間非先諸行剎那自性生起，正觀為生；諸行生已，即時未壞，正觀為住；此已生行，望前已滅諸行剎那自性別異，正觀為老；從此諸行生剎那後，即此已生諸行剎那自性滅壞，正觀為滅。菩薩觀此已生剎那諸行自性，即是生住老之自性，不見生等別有自性。如實觀見生剎那後，即此生等諸行剎那自性滅壞，無別有性。如是四種有為之相，總攝諸行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Within this very instant, the previous instant's own nature having already perished, the uninterrupted arising, immediately following, of a conditioned activity's own genuinely new instantaneous nature is correctly observed as birth. That same conditioned activity, having arisen, not yet having perished, is correctly observed as abiding. This same already-arisen activity, differing in its instantaneous nature from the previously ceased activity, is correctly observed as aging. Following this very instant of the activity's arising, that same already-arisen activity's own instantaneous nature perishing is correctly observed as cessation. Bodhisattvas observe that this already-arisen instantaneous activity's own nature is nothing other than the very nature of birth, abiding, and aging themselves, seeing no separate nature belonging to birth and the rest apart from the activity. They correctly and genuinely observe that, following the instant of birth, this same activity's own instantaneous nature of birth and the rest simply perishes, with no separate nature involved. These four marks of conditioned existence together comprehend the entirety of conditioned activity.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi offers the fluorescent tube as a modern, concretely available illustration of a truth easily missed by unaided perception: most people assume a fluorescent light's own glow is simply continuous, when in fact it flickers on and off far faster than the eye can register, each flash genuinely arising and ceasing rather than persisting as one unbroken light — conditioned activity generally, he stresses, works exactly this way, only far too quickly for ordinary awareness to detect directly. He cites the classical formula "existing because conditions are present, never old but always freshly new" to make the same point in words: at every single instant, the aggregates — earth, water, fire, wind, the very cells of the body, and every thought and sensation alike — arise and cease afresh purely because the necessary conditions happen to be present in that instant, never because some persisting substance from the previous instant survived intact into this one.

He treats the passage's own repeated insistence that birth, abiding, and aging possess "no separate nature" apart from the activity itself as ruling out a specific, tempting mistake: imagining that some underlying, continuous "thing" undergoes these four changes the way a single object might be painted four different colors in sequence — the truth is instead that the activity simply is its own birth in this instant, is its own abiding in this same instant, is its own aging relative to what came immediately before, and then simply, automatically ceases, with no further ingredient or agent required to make that cessation happen. Cessation, in particular, needs no separate cause of its own: an arisen thing's ceasing is built into what arising itself already is, exactly as the treatise's own earlier formula "birth is spontaneous cessation, cessation waits for no cause" already established. This closes the detailed analysis of the four marks within a single instant; the treatise turns next to observing that these same four marks, in the end, are also nothing beyond conditioned activity viewed from four angles rather than four independently real things.

8. Milestone: Why the Buddha Sometimes Teaches Three Marks Instead of Four — Merging Abiding Into Aging, or Aging Into Abiding

The treatise explains the four marks through a simpler, twofold division: what has a share, and what has no share. Arising and abiding-and-aging together belong to what has a share, since these mark a conditioned activity's own actual presence, while cessation alone belongs to what has no share, since it marks that activity's own absence. On this basis, the Buddha establishes arising as the first mark of conditioned existence based on the future, since an activity is genuinely absent before it arises and only comes to be through that future-directed arising; establishes cessation as the second mark based on the past, since an activity that has already arisen has already fallen away into the past; and merges abiding and aging together into a single third mark based on the present, since both describe an activity's own actual, current presence.

Chinese Source

以要言之，二分所顯。一者、有分所顯，二者、無分所顯。此中世尊依於有分，建立一種有為之相；依於無分，建立第二有為之相。住、異二種，俱是諸行有分所顯，建立第三有為之相。

Ru-Yi Translation

In brief, this is disclosed through two divisions: first, what is disclosed through having a share; second, what is disclosed through having no share. Here, the World-Honored One, based on what has a share, establishes one mark of conditioned existence; based on what has no share, establishes a second mark of conditioned existence. Abiding and aging, both being disclosed through what has a share belonging to conditioned activities, together establish the third mark of conditioned existence.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own later chapter of determinations directly for why the Buddha sometimes teaches only three marks rather than four: since every conditioned activity is disclosed across the three times, arising is established with reference to the future, because an activity that did not exist before comes into being specifically through that future-oriented arising; cessation is established with reference to the past, because an activity that has already arisen inevitably falls away into what is now past; and the present alone discloses both abiding and aging together, since the present is where an activity's own actual, current existence and its own ongoing change are both simultaneously visible.

He explains the two resulting classical formulas found across different scriptures — "arising, aging, and cessation," which absorbs abiding into aging, since both name aspects of an activity's own share of actual presence and aging is the one that most clearly conveys change; and "arising, abiding, and cessation," which absorbs aging into abiding instead, since aging and cessation both, in their own way, describe something moving toward absence, while abiding and aging are the two that most closely resemble one another in describing presence itself. Different scriptures and treatises, he notes, freely use four marks, or either of these two three-mark formulas, depending on which grouping best serves a given passage's own explanatory purpose — the underlying reality being described never actually changes, only the convenient number of labels applied to it. This closes the explanation of why sometimes three marks are taught instead of four; the treatise turns next to formally establishing the great vehicle's own correct position against several classical objections raised by other schools of thought.

9. Milestone: Refuting a Separate, Substantial "Arising" Behind Arising Itself — Debating the Sarvāstivāda School

The treatise now formally refutes a rival position, identified by the commentary as the Sarvāstivāda school's own claim that arising, abiding, aging, and cessation exist as genuine, separate substances distinct from the conditioned activities they characterize — as though a specific "arising-thing" caused activities to arise, quite apart from the activities themselves. Against this, a bodhisattva observes that when any conditioned activity arises, only that very activity itself is ever actually found — no separate substance called arising, abiding, aging, or cessation exists over and above it; and precisely the same holds when that activity abides, ages, or ceases: only the activity itself, in its own respective phase, is ever found.

Chinese Source

此中菩薩，觀一切時唯有諸行，除此更無生住老滅恆有實物自性成就。何以故？諸行生時，唯即如是諸行可得，無別有餘生住老滅。如是諸行住老滅時，唯即如是諸行可得，無別有餘生住老滅。

Ru-Yi Translation

Here, bodhisattvas observe that, at all times, only conditioned activities themselves are found; beyond this, there is no separately, permanently existing real substance called arising, abiding, aging, or cessation. Why is this so? When conditioned activities arise, only these very activities are found, with no separate arising, abiding, aging, or cessation beyond them. Likewise, when these same conditioned activities abide, age, or cease, only these very activities are found, with no separate arising, abiding, aging, or cessation beyond them.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the rival position being refuted here directly: the Sarvāstivāda school, one of the classical eighteen schools of the lesser vehicle, held that beyond the five aggregates' own activity, there genuinely exists a separate substance called "arising" that actually causes those aggregates to arise, and correspondingly separate substances for abiding, aging, and cessation — a position the treatise regards as a genuinely incomplete understanding still worth correcting even among fellow Buddhist practitioners.

He treats the treatise's own counter-claim as elegantly simple: whatever moment one examines a conditioned activity, whether at the instant it arises or at the instant it abides, ages, or ceases, the only thing ever actually found there is the activity itself in that particular phase — never some additional, separate entity called "arising" riding alongside it to make the arising happen. Arising, abiding, aging, and cessation are, in other words, simply names for the very same activity considered from four different angles, not four separate things that could, even in principle, be found apart from that activity. This closes the treatise's initial statement of its own position; the treatise turns next to a series of formal objections showing exactly why the rival view collapses into absurdity once examined closely.

10. Milestone: The Absurdity of Two Births, and the Dilemma of Sameness or Difference — Classical Objections to a Real Entity Called "Birth"

A bodhisattva further demonstrates through reasoning that no genuine substance corresponding to "arising" and the rest can be found. If a separate entity called "birth" genuinely existed apart from conditioned activities such as form, then exactly as those activities themselves are born, this very birth-entity would itself need its own further birth to bring it into being — producing an absurd double birth, the birth of the activity and the birth of birth itself. And this birth-of-birth, examined further, must be either identical to or different from the activity's own birth: if identical, then positing birth as a separate real entity accomplishes nothing at all, a wasted, empty exercise; if different, then the activity's own birth was never actually produced by this separate birth-entity in the first place, contradicting the very claim being defended. Either way, the position collapses — and precisely the same reasoning applies equally to abiding, aging, and cessation.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩以理推求生等實物亦不可得。謂若離彼色等諸行別有生法，是即應如色等諸行自體有生，如是此生亦應有生。如是即應有二種生，一者、行生，二者、生生。如是行生與彼生生為一為異？若言一者，計生實有即為唐捐。言別有生是實物有，不應道理。若言異者，如是即應非行生生；是行生生，不應道理。如說生相，如是廣說住老滅相，當知亦爾。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, seeking through reasoning, find that no genuine substance corresponding to birth and the rest can be found either. That is to say, if there genuinely existed, apart from form and the other conditioned activities, a separate entity called birth, then just as form and the other activities themselves are born, this very birth would itself also need to be born — producing exactly two kinds of birth: first, the birth of the activity; second, the birth of birth itself. Are this birth of the activity and this birth of birth identical or different? If identical, then positing birth as a genuinely separate real entity accomplishes nothing at all — this is unreasonable. If different, then the activity's birth would then not actually be a birth belonging to that activity at all — yet it is said to be the activity's own birth, which is likewise unreasonable. Exactly as stated regarding the mark of birth, so too, in full, regarding the marks of abiding, aging, and cessation — one should understand these in exactly the same way.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Yogācārabhūmi commentary's own terminology for this classical two-births objection: the activity's own birth is called "great birth," and the further, separate birth needed to produce that great birth is called "small birth" — a rival school's position requiring every single arising to be doubly underwritten, an activity's own birth and then a further birth to birth that birth, and so on, threatening an infinite regress the moment one presses the question even slightly further.

He walks through the dilemma's own two horns with care: if this "great birth" and "small birth" are simply identical, one and the same thing under two names, then the entire theoretical apparatus of positing birth as a separate, substantially real entity turns out to add nothing whatsoever — a distinction without a difference, wasted effort defending a position that collapses back into the treatise's own simpler claim that only the activity itself is ever found. If, instead, they are genuinely different things, then the activity's own birth was never actually produced by this separate "small birth" entity at all, since two genuinely different things cannot stand in the relationship of one directly constituting or producing the other in the way the rival position requires — the activity's birth would then need some further explanation entirely disconnected from the posited birth-entity, defeating the entire purpose of introducing it. He notes the treatise's own economical closing move, extending this identical two-horned argument to abiding, aging, and cessation without needing to restate it in full for each, since the underlying logical structure is identical in every case. This closes the two-births and sameness-or-difference objections to arising and abiding; the treatise turns next to a further, parallel set of objections targeting cessation specifically.

11. Milestone: If Cessation Were Genuinely Real, Either Everything Would Vanish at Once or Nothing Could Ever Stay Gone — Closing the Debate and Impermanence Itself

The treatise turns a further, dedicated objection specifically against cessation as a separate real entity. If cessation genuinely existed as its own substance, it would, exactly like any other genuine thing, need its own arising and its own further cessation. Two absurd consequences follow. First: if this cessation-entity itself needed to arise, then the very moment it arose, every single conditioned activity everywhere would have to cease all at once — meaning genuine liberation would require no effort whatsoever, arriving automatically the instant this cessation-entity appeared, exactly as effortlessly as the mental factors already stop functioning within cessation-attainment itself. Second: if this cessation-entity, having arisen, must itself then cease, then once it ceases, since cessation is now itself gone, every activity it had extinguished would have to spring back into existence again, having lost the very thing that had been suppressing it. Either consequence is absurd, and therefore cessation as a genuinely separate substance must be rejected — confirming that arising, abiding, aging, and cessation are all merely provisional labels for a single activity's own changing phases, and closing the entire, exhaustive treatment of the first dharma-summary, impermanence.

Chinese Source

Ru-Yi Translation

If cessation possessed a separate nature of its own, genuinely existing as a real thing, then this very cessation would itself need to have its own arising and its own further cessation. Were this cessation to arise, then every conditioned activity everywhere would have to cease at exactly the same time — meaning it would take only minimal effort, as though simply entering cessation-attainment, for every mental factor whatsoever to cease all at once. Were this cessation itself, having arisen, to then cease, then every conditioned activity, though already extinguished, would have to arise once again, since the cessation that had extinguished them would then itself be absent. This is why saying that cessation has its own arising and its own cessation is unreasonable. Furthermore, it would be unreasonable for a good son or good daughter, if arising, abiding, aging, and cessation were permanently, genuinely existing real substances at all times, to regard them as merely provisionally existing and thereby be capable of cultivating renunciation, freedom from craving, and liberation; the opposite position is the reasonable one. Through this manner of reasoning, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know that all conditioned activities are impermanent.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this final objection as the treatise's own decisive blow against the rival position, since it shows the theory failing under either of its own two possible elaborations: a cessation that merely needs to arise, without itself needing further cessation, would make total, universal cessation of every phenomenon everywhere trivially automatic the instant that single cessation-entity appeared — an absurd result, since the entire spiritual path would then require no sustained practice whatsoever, contradicting everything the Buddha actually taught about the genuine effort liberation demands. A cessation that also needs its own further cessation fares no better, since once that second cessation removes the first, every activity the first cessation had extinguished would necessarily spring back into being, meaning nothing extinguished could ever actually stay extinguished — equally absurd, and equally contrary to the plain fact that what has genuinely ceased does not simply reappear on its own.

He treats the closing remark about renunciation and liberation as the treatise's own final, practical seal on this entire argument: if arising, abiding, aging, and cessation truly were permanent, independently existing substances, as the rival position maintains, then no amount of correctly perceiving them as merely provisional could ever actually motivate or produce genuine renunciation, freedom from craving, and liberation — since one cannot correctly perceive something permanently, substantially real as merely provisional without simply being wrong. The very fact that correctly seeing these four marks as provisional labels genuinely does produce renunciation and liberation is itself proof that they were provisional labels all along, never separately existing substances. With this, the treatise's entire, exhaustive treatment of the first dharma-summary — impermanence, unpacked through its two basic senses, the three-times analysis, the instant-by-instant analysis, the two-part division into what has and lacks a share, and this formal refutation of a rival school's position — closes in full. The treatise turns next to the second dharma-summary, that all conditioned activities are suffering.

12. Milestone: Suffering Opens and Closes — the Classical Three Sufferings Underlying Every Conditioned Activity Without Exception

The second dharma-summary, that all conditioned activities are suffering, is unpacked through the classical three sufferings a bodhisattva observes underlying the ceaseless, moment-to-moment succession of impermanent activity just established. Conditioning-suffering names the deepest and most pervasive of the three: the very basis of existence within the three realms, produced by past affliction and karma, is inherently coarse, rigid, obstructed, incapable, and lacking genuine self-mastery — the neutral, neither-painful-nor-pleasant sensation itself, ultimate suffering in the truest sense, though rarely recognized as such by ordinary beings. Suffering-of-reversal names the pain that arrives the moment something pleasant — a beloved friend, family member, or circumstance — changes, is lost, or is met with slander. And suffering-of-suffering names painful sensation itself, outright: birth, aging, sickness, death, separation from the loved, encountering the hated, and failing to obtain what is sought.

又諸菩薩，觀無常行相續轉時，能為三種苦所依止。一者、行苦，二者、壞苦，三者、苦苦。如是菩薩如實了知一切諸行皆悉是苦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, observing impermanent activities continuing on in succession, find they serve as the basis for three kinds of suffering: first, conditioning-suffering; second, suffering of reversal; third, suffering of suffering. In this way, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know that all conditioned activities are entirely suffering.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats conditioning-suffering as this entire teaching's own most subtle and easily overlooked claim, directly citing the treatise's own description: the very basis of a being's existence anywhere within the three realms carries within it the coarse, rigid, obstructed quality of past affliction's own residue, incapable of genuine self-command, inclined toward further affliction and karma, and estranged from the genuine purity of discipline, concentration, and wisdom — this alone is why the Buddha and every tathāgata declare the entire basis of existence within the three realms to be suffering, called suffering specifically "through conditioning," identified with neutral, neither-painful-nor-pleasant sensation in its ultimate sense, a form of suffering ordinary people rarely notice precisely because it produces no obvious discomfort at all.

He treats suffering-of-reversal and suffering-of-suffering as comparatively more familiar and immediately recognizable: reversal-suffering names the pain of pleasant circumstances inevitably changing or ending, whether a beloved companion's absence, a cherished relationship's rupture, or a treasured possession's loss; suffering-of-suffering names painful sensation outright, the entire classical catalogue of birth, aging, sickness, death, parting from the loved, meeting the hated, and seeking without obtaining. Together, he notes, these three sufferings leave no conditioned activity whatsoever outside their reach: whatever feels neutral is conditioning-suffering, whatever feels pleasant is suffering-of-reversal waiting to happen, and whatever feels painful is simply suffering-of-suffering already arrived — a comprehensive net catching every possible experience within the three realms. This closes the second dharma-summary in full; the treatise turns next to the third, that all phenomena are without self.

13. Milestone: Non-Self Opens and Closes — No Fixed Person Found Within the Aggregates or Apart From Them, and No Fixed Essence in Any Verbal Label

The third dharma-summary, that all phenomena are without self, unfolds through two kinds of selflessness a bodhisattva correctly comes to know regarding both conditioned and unconditioned phenomena alike. Personal selflessness means that no genuinely real person can be found either identical to the aggregates themselves or as some further, separate thing existing apart from them — the aggregates are impermanent and lack any power of self-command, ruling out identifying a fixed self with them directly, while positing a self entirely separate from the aggregates would make that self either impossibly unconditioned or impossibly unconnected to the very body it supposedly inhabits. Phenomenal selflessness means that within everything language points to, the verbally-labeled essence itself simply does not exist at all — mere sound and concept, never a genuine corresponding reality.

又諸菩薩，如實了知有為、無為一切諸法二無我性。一者、補特伽羅無我性，二者、法無我性。於諸法中補特伽羅無我性者，謂非即有法，是真實有補特伽羅；亦非離有法，別有真實補特伽羅。於諸法中法無我性者，謂於一切言說事中，一切言說自性諸法都無所有。如是菩薩如實了知一切諸法皆無有我。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know the two kinds of selflessness belonging to all phenomena, conditioned and unconditioned alike: first, personal selflessness; second, phenomenal selflessness. Regarding personal selflessness within phenomena: it is not the case that an existing phenomenon is itself a genuinely real person, nor is it the case that, apart from existing phenomena, some further, separately real person exists. Regarding phenomenal selflessness within phenomena: within everything that verbal statement points to, the essence that verbal statement itself attributes simply does not exist at all. In this way, bodhisattvas correctly and thoroughly know that all phenomena are without self.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own earlier chapter on the stage with both thought and reflection for the classical threefold refutation underlying personal selflessness: if a self is simply identified with the aggregates themselves, then since the aggregates are impermanent, the self would have to be equally impermanent, contradicting the very claim of a permanent, unchanging self non-Buddhist schools typically defend; if a self is instead posited as existing somewhere entirely apart from the aggregates, it would, being unconditioned by definition, have to lack any activity or change whatsoever, which contradicts an active self capable of thought and deed; and if this separate self is not genuinely connected to the aggregates at all, it should remain permanently unaffected by whatever happens to the body, and there would be no coherent sense in which "my body" belongs to "me" — every possible position collapses under scrutiny.

He treats phenomenal selflessness as extending this identical logic beyond the person to every phenomenon whatsoever: the essence that any spoken or conceptual label seems to attribute to its object — a chair possessing genuine "chair-ness," a color possessing genuine "redness" — is, upon the same careful examination, never actually found corresponding to anything real, existing only as sound and concept layered over a dependently arisen process. This closes the third dharma-summary in full; the treatise turns next to the fourth and final dharma-summary, nirvāṇa as peace — the longest and richest of the four, unfolded through two extended classical parables.

14. Milestone: Nirvāṇa as Peace Opens — What the Noble Ones Actually Realize, and What Ordinary Bodhisattvas and Śrāvakas Merely Grasp Through Conviction

The fourth and final dharma-summary opens by distinguishing genuine realization from mere conviction. A bodhisattva observes that once the prior cause of conditioned activity — affliction and karma — has been permanently cut off, and no further remainder of that activity subsequently arises at all, this state is called nirvāṇa: a genuinely peaceful nature, since every form of suffering has been definitively put to rest and every affliction has been completely extinguished. Yet a bodhisattva who has not yet reached the ground of genuinely pure, excellent aspiration, and a śrāvaka who has not yet directly seen the noble truths, though they may form a powerful conviction about nirvāṇa and speak of it correctly as peace, have not yet genuinely, directly understood it — their own correct intellectual reflection notwithstanding, actual noble wisdom has not yet turned toward it directly.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，觀一切行先因永斷，後無餘滅，其餘畢竟不起不生，說名涅槃。當知涅槃其體寂靜，一切眾苦畢竟息故，一切煩惱究竟滅故。如是未得清淨增上意樂菩薩，未見聖諦諸聲聞乘，雖於涅槃發起勝解，如是說言涅槃寂靜；而於涅槃未如實解，未能如實正智見轉，然彼亦有如理作意。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas observe that once the prior cause of every conditioned activity has been permanently severed, with no remainder subsequently ceasing, and nothing further ever arises again at all — this is called nirvāṇa. One should understand that nirvāṇa's own nature is peace, since every form of suffering is thereby definitively put to rest, and every affliction is thereby completely extinguished. Bodhisattvas who have not yet attained the ground of genuinely pure, excellent aspiration, and śrāvaka practitioners who have not yet directly seen the noble truths, though they raise a genuine conviction regarding nirvāṇa and correctly speak of nirvāṇa as peace, have not yet genuinely understood nirvāṇa, and correct noble wisdom's own direct vision has not yet turned toward it — yet they do already possess correct, reasoned reflection on the matter.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi distinguishes nirvāṇa with residue, where the karmic cause of further rebirth is cut off yet the present body, itself a result of past affliction, still continues until its own natural end, from nirvāṇa without residue, where even that residual body finally ceases and nothing further arises at all — both states sharing the identical peaceful nature, differing only in whether a residual body remains. He cross-references the treatise's own later chapter on the bodhisattva grounds for a further, technical breakdown of four kinds of peace within each: within nirvāṇa with residue, peace from suffering, peace from affliction, peace from harming other beings, and the peace of equanimity itself; within nirvāṇa without residue, peace from the very count of remaining aggregates, peace from every possible basis of future existence, peace from the suffering inherent in any remaining basis, and peace from the anxious doubt that such a basis might otherwise provoke.

He treats the distinction between genuine realization and mere conviction as this passage's own crucial, humbling clarification: an ordinary bodhisattva still accumulating the provisions for awakening, or an ordinary śrāvaka still on the path of preparation, can sincerely study, deeply believe in, and even correctly explain nirvāṇa to others through the great vehicle's twofold emptiness or the lesser vehicle's own selflessness of persons — yet this remains conviction born of learning and reflection, not the direct, noble vision that only actual realization provides. Both, he stresses, genuinely engage in correct, reasoned reflection on the matter in the meantime, so this distinction is not a criticism of their sincerity, only an honest acknowledgment of exactly where they stand on the path. This closes the opening distinction between realization and conviction; the treatise turns next to a classical parable illustrating precisely this gap between conviction and realization — the story of a prince and a merchant's son growing up with toy chariots.

15. Milestone: The Parable of the Toy Chariots — Three Stages From Mistaking a Toy for the Real Thing to Finally Seeing the Genuine Animal Face to Face

The treatise illustrates the gap between conviction and realization through an extended parable. A prince and a merchant's son, raised from birth within the palace and never yet having left it, are each given toy chariots — shaped like deer, oxen, horses, and elephants — by their father, and play with these toys so joyfully that they come to believe these really are deer, oxen, horses, and elephants. Later, once the children have matured and their faculties ripened, their fathers begin describing the genuine animals these toys merely represent, provoking a first uncertain wondering: could these real animals their fathers now praise be the very toys they already possess? Finally, once fully grown, the fathers lead them out of the palace to actually encounter the real deer, oxen, horses, and elephants directly — at which point genuine understanding dawns all at once, and the children feel real embarrassment at ever having mistaken a mere toy for what they now see with their own eyes to be genuinely real.

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the Yogācārabhūmi commentary's own three-part title for this parable's own three moments: "mistaking the false for the true," "hearing the truth without yet understanding it," and "recognizing the truth and feeling genuine shame." Master Kuiji's own reading assigns each of the four toy vehicles its own doctrinal referent: the elephant-chariot representing a buddha's own great, unfixed nirvāṇa, unbound to either saṃsāra or mere extinction; the deer-chariot, since deer characteristically dwell in tranquil forests, representing the intrinsically pure nirvāṇa every being already possesses by nature; the ox, bearing horns, representing nirvāṇa with residue; and the horse, lacking horns, representing nirvāṇa without residue.

16. Milestone: Mapping the Parable — the Palace as Saṃsāra, the Father as the Buddha, and the Toy Chariot as Nirvāṇa's Own Rough, Preliminary Sketch

The treatise now explicitly maps every element of the parable onto its corresponding reality. The inner palace represents saṃsāra itself; the children raised within it represent bodhisattvas who have not yet attained pure, excellent aspiration and śrāvakas who have not yet directly seen the noble truths; the father represents both the buddhas and bodhisattvas who have already reached the great grounds; and the toy chariots first given represent nirvāṇa's own rough, preliminary characteristics taught through mere language. The father's later praise of the genuine animals represents the buddhas' and advanced bodhisattvas' own direct realization of true nirvāṇa, now described to those still relying on merely conceptual understanding, which produces in them only a derivative, sound-based intellectual conviction sustained across a long night of continued practice. And finally, once the provisions of the path have genuinely matured, those who reach the ground of pure aspiration or directly see the noble truths attain direct realized wisdom regarding true nirvāṇa — recognizing, with the identical embarrassment the children felt, that what earlier seemed already understood was merely a resembling image, never yet the reality itself.

Praising the genuine deer and the rest afterward represents buddhas and bodhisattvas, having directly realized and seen true nirvāṇa themselves, describing, exactly as they themselves have seen it, the genuine merit of nirvāṇa before those bodhisattvas and śrāvakas. Should the provisions of the path, at some point, mature and gradually increase, so that one becomes a bodhisattva of pure, excellent aspiration or a śrāvaka who has directly seen the truths, giving rise to directly realized wisdom regarding true nirvāṇa — at that very time, genuinely realized understanding spontaneously arises from within: this very nirvāṇa is exactly what every śrāvaka and solitary realizer has attained, exactly what buddhas and bodhisattvas had already praised beforehand, while we ourselves, previously possessing only an unenlightened being's own understanding, had given rise, toward what was not genuinely real but merely resembled it, merely its own reflection, to the conviction that we had already attained genuine nirvāṇa. Because of this, genuine shame arises in recalling that earlier conviction, now resting instead on this later, genuinely realized conviction.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this explicit mapping as the treatise's own deliberate insurance against any reader mistaking the parable for mere entertainment: every single element carries a precise doctrinal referent, from the palace itself down to the father's own two rounds of speech. He stresses the parable's own emotional honesty as its most valuable feature for practitioners at any stage: recognizing, in retrospect, that one's own formerly cherished spiritual understanding was only ever a resembling image rather than the reality itself is not meant to produce despair or self-recrimination, but the same natural, almost affectionate embarrassment a grown adult feels recalling a beloved childhood toy — a sign of genuine progress, not failure, since only real growth makes the earlier stage visible as what it actually was.

He treats the final line's own quiet reassurance as worth lingering over: shame at a former, merely conceptual conviction rests immediately upon the newly arisen, genuinely realized conviction that replaces it — the practitioner is never left in the shame alone, but moves through it directly into something more solid and complete. This closes the parable of the toy chariots in full; the treatise turns next to a second classical parable illustrating a related but distinct point — a sick person's own medicine, and why curing one ailment can still leave a deeper ailment concealed beneath it.

17. Milestone: The Parable of Medicine Opens — Curing One Disease Only to Discover a Deeper One Still Concealed Beneath It

A second parable illustrates why even genuine realization of selflessness in persons is not yet the whole of the path. A sick person visits a great physician, receives medicine suited to the illness, and takes it faithfully, developing genuine confidence and even fondness for this medicine once the original sickness is actually cured — yet a further illness then arises, requiring a different medicine still. The great physician, recognizing this new illness, advises abandoning the first medicine in favor of the one now needed; but the patient, foolish and unaware, remains so attached to the medicine that already worked once that they refuse to give it up, even though it can do nothing for the illness now actually present.

Chinese Source

又如病者往大醫所，為除病故，求隨順藥，得已常服。彼於是藥深生勝解、深生愛樂、唯見為實。由是因緣，先病除愈，復起餘病，應服餘藥。爾時大醫知先病愈，後病復生，更須餘藥；勸捨前藥，令服餘藥。時彼病者愚癡無識，於前所服深生勝解，起所宜想，不肯棄捨。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, suppose a sick person goes to a great physician and, in order to remove their illness, seeks medicine suited to it, and, upon obtaining it, takes it constantly. This person develops deep conviction in this medicine, deep fondness for it, seeing it as genuinely effective. Because of this, the former illness is thereby cured, yet a further illness then arises, requiring a different medicine. At that time, the great physician, knowing that the former illness has been cured but a later illness has now arisen requiring a different medicine, advises abandoning the earlier medicine in favor of the one now needed. But this sick person, foolish and lacking understanding, holds deep conviction in what was taken before, believing it still suitable, and refuses to give it up.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the referent behind each element directly: the sick person represents an ordinary bodhisattva or an ordinary śrāvaka, the great physician represents the Buddha, and the first medicine represents the śrāvaka path's own genuine and effective teaching of personal selflessness through the four noble truths — practiced diligently through learning, reflection, and cultivation, this medicine genuinely cures the first disease, afflictive obstruction, exactly as it claims to. Yet even after afflictive obstruction is genuinely cured, a further, subtler disease remains: the obstruction to complete knowledge, which the lesser vehicle's own medicine of personal selflessness alone was never actually designed to treat.

He treats the patient's own foolish refusal to abandon the first medicine as this parable's own pointed criticism of a genuinely widespread spiritual trap: a śrāvaka who has sincerely, effectively cured affliction through the four noble truths naturally develops real confidence in that method, and precisely this well-earned confidence can then become an obstacle, making the practitioner reluctant to hear that a further, different medicine — the great vehicle's own teaching of phenomenal selflessness — is now genuinely needed, content instead to rest in personal liberation alone rather than turning toward the fuller path leading to complete buddhahood. This closes the parable's own opening scene; the treatise turns next to its continuation, and to the specific plea the physician makes to persuade the reluctant patient to actually change course.

18. Milestone: The Medicine Parable Closes — a Patient Who Hears the Physician's Correct Diagnosis Yet Still Cannot Quite Let Go

The great physician explains plainly to the patient that the earlier medicine, however genuinely effective against the first illness, is unsuited to the illness now actually present, and that the newer medicine is what the current condition actually requires — yet the patient, upon hearing this correct explanation, still fails to develop genuine conviction, remaining unable to fully trust the physician's own word. The treatise maps this directly: the sick person represents ordinary bodhisattvas and śrāvakas still gripped by the more subtle disease of grasping at phenomena, a disease that obstructs complete knowledge itself; the great physician represents every buddha; and the medicine offered represents the buddhas' own teaching, calibrated across three matched tiers of superior, more superior, and most superior scope, addressing learning, reflection, and cultivation in turn — yet even having heard this teaching correctly explained, such practitioners still cannot enter it, still cannot form genuine conviction, and still cannot actually practice in accordance with what has been taught.

Chinese Source

時大良醫為其宣說前後藥性，於現所病前藥匪宜，後藥為勝。時有病者雖聞是語，不生勝解，猶未深信良醫所言。如是病者，喻諸凡夫菩薩、聲聞，為煩惱病之所執持。大良醫者，喻諸如來。其良藥等，喻為宣說若上上勝及以上極，若深深勝及以深極，若劣、若勝及以勝極，法教正教教授教誡。彼雖聞已，不能悟入，不生勝解，不能修行法隨法行。

Ru-Yi Translation

At that time, the great physician explains to the patient the properties of the earlier and later medicines — that, for the illness now actually present, the earlier medicine is unsuited while the later medicine is superior. At that time, though the sick person hears these words, no genuine conviction arises, and they still do not deeply trust what the great physician has said. This sick person represents ordinary bodhisattvas and śrāvakas still gripped by the disease of affliction. The great physician represents the various tathāgatas. The good medicine represents teaching that is superior, more superior, and utterly superior; that is profound, more profound, and utterly profound; and that is lesser, greater, and utterly excellent — teaching, correct instruction, guidance, and admonishment. Though they have heard this, they are unable to enter into it, unable to give rise to genuine conviction, and unable to practice in proper accordance with the teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "the disease of affliction" here specifically with the subtler grasping at phenomena as genuinely real, distinguished from the coarser afflictive grasping at a fixed self already cured by the first medicine — a practitioner can be genuinely free of self-grasping and still remain caught by this more refined attachment to phenomena, which is precisely why a second, different medicine becomes necessary even after real progress has already been made. He walks through the treatise's own threefold-times-three classification of this second medicine's own graded strength, preserving three separate commentarial readings: Master Jing's reading, dividing the nine-part formula into a general statement of the teaching itself, then its specific application to meditative cultivation across three grades, then to precept-keeping across three grades; Master Tai's reading, mapping the three sets of three instead onto the bodhisattva, solitary-realizer, and śrāvaka vehicles respectively, each with its own path of seeing, path of cultivation, and path beyond further training; and Master Kuiji's own three alternative readings, one mapping the sets onto learning, reflection, and cultivation, one onto the categories of teaching, guidance, and admonishment, and one distributing all three qualities across each of the three wisdoms individually.

He treats the patient's own continued inability to trust the physician, even after hearing the correct diagnosis stated plainly, as this parable's own honest final note: intellectual comprehension of a correct explanation and genuine, transformative conviction are simply not the same thing, and a practitioner can hear the great vehicle's own teaching explained with total clarity and still not yet be ready to actually let go of an earlier, well-worn confidence and step into something new. This closes the parable of medicine's own main narrative; the treatise turns next to a contrasting case — practitioners who, unlike the reluctant patient, already possess genuine, unwavering faith.

19. Milestone: Genuine Faith Never Doubts — Why a Noble One Who Has Actually Realized the Path Can Never Mistake the Great Vehicle for Anything Less Than the Buddha's Own Word

By contrast, bodhisattvas and śrāvakas who possess genuinely pure faith — those who have already reached the ground of pure aspiration or already directly seen the noble truths — never doubt what the Buddha has taught at all. Anyone who has genuinely realized the fruits of the path already recognizes that the lesser vehicle itself depends entirely on a buddha's own prior appearance in the world, so denying that the great vehicle is the Buddha's own word would be tantamount to denying the very source from which the lesser vehicle's own teaching originally came — a contradiction no genuinely realized practitioner would ever actually accept. Only certain ordinary, unrealized bodhisattvas remain genuinely uncertain on this question; a truly accomplished śrāvaka arhat never is.

Chinese Source

諸有淨信菩薩、聲聞，於佛所說不生疑惑；乘佛所說喻如一切支具圓滿妙莊嚴車無上法乘，如善御者，隨所行地、隨所應到，疾疾進趣，無所稽留。

Ru-Yi Translation

Bodhisattvas and śrāvakas who possess genuinely pure faith never give rise to doubt regarding what the Buddha has taught; relying on what the Buddha has taught is like riding an unsurpassed vehicle of the teaching, a wondrously adorned chariot complete with every one of its parts, and, like a skilled driver, advancing swiftly toward wherever one travels and wherever one is meant to arrive, without ever being delayed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this passage's own logical argument as genuinely decisive rather than merely rhetorical: since the lesser vehicle's own entire teaching depends on a buddha having first appeared in the world to proclaim it, anyone who genuinely accepts the lesser vehicle's own authority has thereby already implicitly accepted the authority of buddhahood itself — and denying that the great vehicle, buddhahood's own most complete teaching, is genuinely the Buddha's word amounts to sawing off the very branch one's own lesser-vehicle confidence is sitting on. He cites both the Lotus Sūtra and the Ornament of the Mahāyāna Sūtras directly for their shared claim that any śrāvaka who has genuinely attained the noble fruits already, necessarily, accepts the great vehicle's own existence and authority — an arhat, having directly verified the Buddha's own liberating power through personal realization, simply cannot coherently doubt that the same Buddha also taught something more complete still. Doubt on this specific question, he stresses, is a mark specifically of the unrealized, ordinary stage, never of genuine attainment.

This closes the contrast between reluctant, doubting practitioners and those of genuinely settled faith; the treatise turns next to the vivid image of the unsurpassed teaching itself as a magnificent, fully equipped chariot.

20. Milestone: Four Complete Wheels and a Driver Who Never Tires — the Unsurpassed Teaching as a Magnificent Chariot, Closing Chapter Seventeen Entirely

The treatise closes its entire seventeenth chapter with a final image: the Buddha's own teaching is like a wondrously adorned chariot, every one of its parts genuinely complete, requiring four specific wheels to actually move at all — a favorable place to practice, genuine guidance from a real teacher, correct conduct aligned with the noble path, and the complete provisions needed to sustain that practice. A noble practitioner who has attained genuine faith drives this chariot the way a skilled driver handles any vehicle: advancing steadily through every one of the ten bodhisattva grounds, all the way to buddhahood itself, without ever growing weary, without ever settling for partial progress, and without ever being delayed along the way.

Chinese Source

乘佛所說喻如一切支具圓滿妙莊嚴車無上法乘，如善御者，隨所行地、隨所應到，疾疾進趣，無所稽留。

Ru-Yi Translation

Relying on what the Buddha has taught is like riding an unsurpassed vehicle of the teaching, a wondrously adorned chariot complete with every one of its parts; like a skilled driver, one advances swiftly toward wherever one travels and wherever one is meant to arrive, without ever being delayed.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own later chapter on compiling discourses for the precise four conditions this chariot's own "four wheels" represent: a favorable place, meaning a location where the fourfold Buddhist community actually resides and the teaching can genuinely be transmitted and practiced; genuine guidance, meaning access to a spiritual friend who correctly teaches discipline, concentration, and wisdom without distortion; correct conduct, meaning practice genuinely aligned with the six perfections, the four means of gathering beings, and the noble path itself; and complete provisions, meaning the five specific supports — dwelling in pure discipline, guarding the sense faculties, moderation in eating, sustained wakeful diligence, and constant clear awareness — needed to actually sustain practice over time. Missing even one of these four wheels, he stresses, leaves the entire chariot unable to move at all, exactly as a physical cart cannot roll forward missing even a single wheel.

He treats the closing image of the skilled, untiring driver as directly echoing the treatise's own account, elsewhere, of the ten bodhisattva grounds: a noble practitioner equipped with all four wheels advances from ground to ground without the hesitation, fatigue, or premature satisfaction that would otherwise slow an ordinary traveler, all the way to the final, complete perfection of buddhahood itself. With this image, the seventeenth chapter — the factors of awakening, spanning fascicles 44 through 46 and unfolding across all fifteen topics from conscience and shame through the three concentrations to these closing four dharma-summaries — reaches its own complete and final close. The treatise turns next to the eighteenth and final chapter of the Bodhisattva Stage's own initial yoga foundation: the qualities that actually define a bodhisattva.

21. Milestone: Chapter Eighteen Opens — the Qualities of a Bodhisattva, the Final Chapter of the Initial Yoga Foundation

Chapter seventeen now closed in full, the treatise opens the eighteenth and final chapter of the Bodhisattva Stage's own foundational section, the initial yoga foundation, itself one of four major divisions spanning fascicles 35 through 50 of the entire Bodhisattva Stage. This closing chapter, on the qualities of a bodhisattva, asks simply: what are the qualities a bodhisattva actually possesses? The treatise answers across four summary-verse groupings, the first of which now opens with its own seven-part verse.

Chinese Source

本地分中菩薩地初持瑜伽處菩薩功德品第十八。云何菩薩所有功德？

Ru-Yi Translation

From the Bodhisattva Stage within the Basic Section, the chapter on the initial yoga foundation, chapter eighteen: the qualities of a bodhisattva. What are the qualities a bodhisattva actually possesses?

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi situates this closing chapter within the Bodhisattva Stage's own complete architecture: the entire Bodhisattva Stage spans twenty-eight chapters across fascicles 35 through 50, organized into four major divisions — the initial yoga foundation, comprising eighteen chapters and now reaching its own final chapter here; the yoga foundation according to the teaching, comprising four further chapters; the yoga foundation of ultimate attainment, comprising five chapters; and the yoga foundation of sequence, comprising a single closing chapter. Chapter eighteen, the qualities of a bodhisattva, thus closes out the very first and largest of these four divisions, gathering together, in effect, everything distinctive about what a bodhisattva actually is and possesses, before the treatise moves on to how that same bodhisattva's practice unfolds according to the teaching's own structure and toward its own ultimate attainment.

He notes that this chapter's own content is organized around four distinct summary verses, each gathering a cluster of specific bodhisattva qualities under its own heading — the first of these four groupings, opening next, names seven such qualities in its own opening verse. This closes the chapter's own brief introduction; the treatise turns next to that first summary verse and the seven qualities it names in outline.

22. Milestone: The First Summary Verse Opens — Five Truly Astonishing Qualities No Other Being Shares

The first of chapter eighteen's five summary verses names seven qualities in outline; the treatise now unpacks the first, five truly astonishing qualities belonging to a bodhisattva alone, shared with no other being whatsoever. First, feeling genuine affection toward every being without needing any prior personal connection at all. Second, willingly remaining within birth and death, enduring boundless suffering, purely for the sake of benefiting beings. Third, possessing genuine skill in taming even beings whose affliction runs deep and resists correction. Fourth, being able to penetrate directly into truths of the most extreme subtlety and difficulty. And fifth, possessing inconceivable, majestic spiritual power.

Chinese Source

嗢柁南曰：希奇不希奇　平等心饒益　報恩與欣讚　不虛加行性。謂諸菩薩，於其無上正等覺乘勤修學時，應知有五甚希奇法。何等為五？一者、於諸有情非有因緣而生親愛；二者、唯為饒益諸有情故，常處生死，忍無量苦；三者、於多煩惱難伏有情，善能解了調伏方便；四者、於極難解真實義理，能隨悟入；五者、具不思議大威神力。如是五種菩薩所有甚希奇法，不與一切餘有情共。

Ru-Yi Translation

A summary verse states: "Astonishing, not astonishing; a mind of equanimity, benefiting beings; repaying kindness and rejoicing in praise; a nature whose effort is never wasted." When bodhisattvas diligently train in the vehicle of supreme, correct awakening, one should understand there are five truly astonishing qualities. What are these five? First, feeling genuine affection toward beings without any prior personal connection. Second, purely for the sake of benefiting beings, constantly dwelling within birth and death, enduring boundless suffering. Third, possessing genuine skill in understanding the means of taming beings whose affliction runs deep and resists correction. Fourth, being able to penetrate directly into truths of the most extreme difficulty and subtlety. Fifth, possessing inconceivable, majestic spiritual power. These five truly astonishing qualities belong to bodhisattvas alone, shared with no other being whatsoever.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the first quality as directly recalling how radically a bodhisattva's own scope of care differs from an ordinary being's: an ordinary person naturally reserves genuine affection for parents, children, close friends, and teachers, treating strangers with polite indifference at best — while a bodhisattva, having from the very first arising of bodhicitta already gathered every being without exception into the circle of their own concern, extends the identical warmth to a stranger, an animal, or even a nonhuman being that an ordinary person would reserve only for their own closest relations, requiring no prior connection whatsoever to feel genuinely, personally invested in that being's welfare.

He treats the second quality as this list's own most demanding: even a sharp-minded bodhisattva genuinely capable of leaving saṃsāra behind for a pure buddha-land elsewhere chooses instead to return again and again to this difficult world specifically because beings here still need help. He treats the fourth and fifth qualities as, respectively, intellectual and spiritual — the capacity to directly realize the most subtle teaching on selflessness, and the capacity for majestic spiritual power exceeding even the śrāvaka's own accomplished abilities — and offers Kṣitigarbha's own vow never to attain buddhahood while hell remains unemptied as the clearest illustration available of just how astonishing a bodhisattva's own resolve can genuinely become, since beings are, in the ordinary sense, never actually exhausted, yet the vow is made anyway, without hesitation or despair. This closes the five truly astonishing qualities; the treatise turns next to five further qualities that appear, on the surface, entirely unremarkable, yet turn out, examined closely, to be equally astonishing in their own right.

23. Milestone: Five Qualities That Look Ordinary Yet Are Secretly Astonishing — Finding Genuine Joy in Suffering Undertaken Purely for Others

The treatise names a further five qualities that, considered merely by outward appearance, seem entirely unremarkable — the kind of thing anyone could in principle do — yet turn out, examined by the motivation actually behind them, to be equally astonishing as the first five. The first of these: a bodhisattva treats the very suffering that comes from working for others' benefit as their own genuine happiness, and consequently welcomes and sustains that suffering continuously rather than merely tolerating it as an unfortunate cost of doing good.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩成就五種不希奇法，而名成就甚希奇法。何等為五？謂諸菩薩，以因利他苦，即為自己樂；是故菩薩恆遍受行因利他苦。是名菩薩成就第一不希奇法，而名成就甚希奇法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas accomplish five kinds of quality that are not astonishing, yet are called accomplishing truly astonishing qualities. What are these five? Bodhisattvas who take the very suffering that comes from benefiting others as their own genuine happiness, and who therefore constantly and thoroughly undergo and sustain the suffering that comes from benefiting others — this is called a bodhisattva's accomplishment of the first not-astonishing quality, yet called accomplishing a truly astonishing quality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites two commentarial explanations for why these five qualities are named "not astonishing" at all despite being, in substance, just as remarkable as the first five: Master Jing's reading holds that helping others looks, from the outside, like simply going about one's own ordinary business, since a bodhisattva's compassion treats every being as intimately as family regardless of actual relation, making the help rendered appear unremarkable purely because it looks so natural and unforced; while Master Kuiji's reading holds that these are actions any being could, in principle, undertake — hence "not astonishing" by outward form — yet a bodhisattva's willingness to actually take them up without hesitation, treating them as though they were one's own personal concern rather than a burden reluctantly shouldered, is precisely what makes the underlying accomplishment astonishing after all. A second reading Cháng Bǎi also notes: measured against a bodhisattva's own other extraordinary qualities, these five seem comparatively modest, yet measured against what an ordinary being or even a lesser-vehicle practitioner could actually manage, they remain genuinely remarkable.

He cross-references the treatise's own chapter on patience for the concrete list of eleven kinds of beneficial activity a bodhisattva actually undertakes — acting as a helpful companion, teaching the true dharma, recognizing and repaying kindness, rescuing from fear, relieving grief, providing material support, guiding a community skillfully, accommodating others' genuine wishes, praising real virtue, correcting real faults, and displaying supernormal power — each one capable of producing real hardship and worry for the one performing it, exactly as caring parents willingly endure real difficulty for a child's sake without ever experiencing that difficulty as a burden at all. This closes the first of the five not-astonishing qualities; the treatise turns next to the second — a bodhisattva's own preference for continued rebirth over swift entry into nirvāṇa.

24. Milestone: Choosing Rebirth Over Nirvāṇa's Own Peace, and Choosing to Teach Over Silence's Own Sweetness — With Zhiyi's Own Touching Self-Assessment

Two further qualities continue the list. The second: though a bodhisattva genuinely understands both the real dangers of birth and death and the genuine benefits of nirvāṇa, they take greater joy in universally purifying beings than in nirvāṇa itself, and therefore, driven by the heightened aspiration to purify beings, deliberately vow to keep taking rebirth. The third: though a bodhisattva genuinely knows the sweetness of the silent, inward bliss available in deep absorption, they take greater joy in purifying beings, and therefore constantly, diligently seek out skillful means to teach the true dharma rather than resting in that silent bliss — even at the cost, as the tradition remembers of Master Zhiyi himself, of never fully reaching the meditative attainment that leading a community might otherwise have allowed.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，雖善了知生死過失、涅槃功德，而樂普令有情清淨，即為己樂；是故菩薩為淨有情增上力故，誓受生死。是名菩薩成就第二不希奇法，而名成就甚希奇法。又諸菩薩，雖善了知默然樂味，而樂普令有情清淨，即為己樂；是故菩薩為淨有情增上力故，恆勤方便為說正法。是名菩薩成就第三不希奇法，而名成就甚希奇法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, though genuinely understanding the faults of birth and death and the merits of nirvāṇa, take joy in universally purifying beings, treating this as their own genuine happiness; therefore, driven by the heightened aspiration to purify beings, they vow to undergo birth and death. This is called a bodhisattva's accomplishment of the second not-astonishing quality, yet called accomplishing a truly astonishing quality. Furthermore, bodhisattvas, though genuinely understanding the sweet taste of silent bliss, take joy in universally purifying beings, treating this as their own genuine happiness; therefore, driven by the heightened aspiration to purify beings, they constantly and diligently seek skillful means to teach the true dharma. This is called a bodhisattva's accomplishment of the third not-astonishing quality, yet called accomplishing a truly astonishing quality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi stresses that a bodhisattva's own dread of affliction and yearning for liberation actually exceeds even a śrāvaka's own, by a factor the tradition describes as hundreds of thousands of times greater — a bodhisattva's freedom from defilement genuinely surpasses an arhat's own — yet a bodhisattva deliberately sets this very depth of realization aside, not for lack of capacity but precisely because that capacity is turned outward, toward every being's own liberation rather than one's own alone, which is exactly what makes the choice astonishing rather than a mere failure to progress.

He treats the third quality's own citation of Master Zhiyi as this passage's own most personally affecting illustration: asked by his own disciples what level of attainment he had actually reached, Zhiyi's teacher Huisi had already told him that leading a community too early would cost him the deepest tier of realization he might otherwise have reached, leaving him only the lesser attainment of the ten stages of faith rather than the ten abodes; and Zhiyi himself, near death, when a disciple asked what stage he had attained, answered candidly that had he not taken on the burden of leading others, he would certainly have purified all six sense faculties, but having chosen to benefit others at his own expense, he had reached only the fifth of the six stages of contemplative practice — an admission Cháng Bǎi treats not as failure but as living proof of exactly the choice this passage describes: genuine capacity for deeper attainment deliberately set aside for others' sake, astonishing precisely because it was a real sacrifice knowingly made, not merely talked about in the abstract. This closes the second and third not-astonishing qualities; the treatise turns next to the fourth and fifth — giving without seeking any return, and treating others' own benefit as indistinguishable from one's own.

25. Milestone: Giving Without Seeking Any Return, and Making Others' Benefit Genuinely Indistinguishable From One's Own — Closing the First Two Groups of Seven

The fourth and fifth not-astonishing qualities close this section. The fourth: though a bodhisattva has genuinely accumulated the wholesome roots belonging to all six perfections, they take greater joy in universally purifying beings, and therefore, with a mind genuinely purified of self-interest, give to beings through the four means of gathering them in, never seeking any resulting fruit of future reward for themselves. The fifth: a bodhisattva treats benefiting others as identical to benefiting themselves, and therefore constantly, willingly takes on the task of benefiting every being — closing the full sevenfold structure of the first summary verse's own opening two groups, the five truly astonishing qualities and the five secretly astonishing ones.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩，雖積集六波羅蜜多所有善根，而樂普令有情清淨，即為己樂；是故菩薩為淨有情增上力故，以淨意樂施諸有情，然不希求施果異熟。是名菩薩成就第四不希奇法，而名成就甚希奇法。又諸菩薩，以利他事為自利事；是故菩薩恆現受行一切有情利益之事。是名菩薩成就第五不希奇法，而名成就甚希奇法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, though having accumulated the wholesome roots of all six perfections, take joy in universally purifying beings, treating this as their own genuine happiness; therefore, driven by the heightened aspiration to purify beings, they give to beings with a genuinely purified aspiration, yet never seek the ripened fruit of that giving for themselves. This is called a bodhisattva's accomplishment of the fourth not-astonishing quality, yet called accomplishing a truly astonishing quality. Furthermore, bodhisattvas take the work of benefiting others as identical to the work of benefiting themselves; therefore, they constantly and willingly take up the task of benefiting every being. This is called a bodhisattva's accomplishment of the fifth not-astonishing quality, yet called accomplishing a truly astonishing quality.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth quality as this section's own clearest expression of what "purified aspiration" concretely means: giving through the four means of gathering beings — generosity, kind speech, beneficial conduct, and working alongside others — with every trace of self-interested motivation genuinely removed, so that the giving is dedicated entirely toward supreme awakening rather than any hoped-for personal reward, whether worldly wealth or a favorable future rebirth.

He treats the fifth quality as this entire section's own natural culmination: once helping others has genuinely become indistinguishable from helping oneself, the very distinction between self-interest and altruism that made the previous four qualities seem like sacrifices in the first place quietly dissolves — a bodhisattva at this point does not experience taking up others' burdens as noble self-denial at all, simply as the obvious thing to do, exactly as a person would not think twice about tending their own arm if it were injured. With this fifth quality, the first summary verse's own opening two groups — five truly astonishing qualities and five secretly astonishing ones, ten qualities in total — close in full; the treatise turns next to the third named quality in the verse itself, a bodhisattva's own equanimity of mind toward all beings, unfolding through its own five distinct marks.

26. Milestone: Equanimity of Mind Opens — Five Marks From Sharing the Very Wish for Buddhahood to Loving Every Being as an Only Child

The third named quality, equanimity of mind toward every being, unfolds through five marks. First, from the very moment of first arousing bodhicitta, a bodhisattva wishes not only for their own awakening but equally for every being's own attainment of it, generating a genuinely shared aspiration. Second, a bodhisattva rests in equanimity joined with compassion toward all three kinds of being — those with neither suffering nor happiness, those suffering, and those already happy — cultivating the four immeasurables toward each in turn. Third, a bodhisattva loves every being as deeply as a parent loves an only child, extending to complete strangers the same intimate devotion an ordinary person reserves only for their own family. Fourth, recognizing that any one being's own dependently arisen nature is identical to every other being's, a bodhisattva rests in a mind of genuine equanimity grounded in this shared underlying nature rather than in personal closeness. And fifth, whatever beneficial action a bodhisattva undertakes for one being, they undertake identically for every being, with a mind of benefit extended equally to all.

He treats the second mark as simply the four immeasurables already unpacked at exhaustive length in fascicle 44, now recalled here as equanimity's own concrete practice.

He treats the third mark's own classical comparison — loving every being as one loves an only child — as drawing directly on the scriptural image of the Buddha regarding all beings exactly as he regarded his own son Rāhula, a love that plays no favorites whatsoever among the countless beings it embraces. The fourth mark he treats as equanimity's own most philosophically grounded form: recognizing that every being, without exception, is equally just five aggregates arisen through the identical twelvefold chain of dependent origination — mere activity, mere phenomena, mere event, mere cause, mere effect — a bodhisattva's compassion here rests not on personal affection at all but on the plain, shared fact that every being is caught in the identical structure of affliction, karma, and suffering, making distinctions of intimate or estranged, exalted or lowly, genuinely beside the point. He treats the fifth mark as this list's own practical conclusion: whatever help is actually given to any single being is given with the identical readiness to any other being whatsoever, closing the entire structure of equanimity. This closes the third of the seven qualities named in the opening verse; the treatise turns next to the fourth quality, benefiting beings, unfolding through its own five concrete methods.

27. Milestone: Benefiting Beings in Five Concrete Ways — Teaching Genuinely Right Livelihood First, With Its Own Five Noble Benefits

The fourth named quality, benefiting beings, unfolds through five concrete methods. First and foremost: teaching beings genuinely right livelihood — earning a living, whether lay or ordained, through means entirely free of wrongdoing, self-sustained through noble wisdom rather than deceit or exploitation. Second: correcting beings whose current occupation fails to produce genuine benefit, redirecting them toward livelihood that actually accords with cause and effect. Third: becoming a genuine refuge for beings with no one else to depend on — the impoverished elderly with no children, the orphaned with no family. Fourth: teaching beings who lack any seed of the three vehicles' own noble path the more basic path toward a favorable human or celestial rebirth through the five precepts and ten wholesome deeds. Fifth: teaching beings who do possess the seeds of the three vehicles the specific vehicle suited to their own capacity, guiding them toward its actual fruit.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cross-references the treatise's own chapter on the stage with both thought and reflection for the five noble benefits genuinely right livelihood produces: first, one who lives by genuine, noble wisdom remains free of the defilement that afflictive craving, karmic action, and their resulting suffering would otherwise produce; second, this same livelihood carries a practitioner all the way to the ultimate completion of the path, supreme awakening itself; third, it produces a settled, unwavering mind, resting in genuine equanimity rather than being pulled about by craving, hostility, or confusion; fourth, it is genuinely distinct from what non-Buddhist teachers merely claim to be a noble life; and fifth, it alone produces the true, ultimate happiness capable of overcoming the four classical māras of the aggregates, affliction, death, and the heavenly tempter — a comprehensive case, he stresses, for why teaching this specific foundation comes first among the five benefits, since nothing else that follows can actually help a being whose own means of survival remain rooted in deceit or exploitation.

28. Milestone: Repaying Kindness in Five Ways — Inviting Others Into One's Own Practice, and Encouraging Devotion to the Teaching Itself

The fifth named quality, repaying kindness owed to those who have shown a bodhisattva genuine benefit, unfolds through five concrete expressions. First, settling such beings well and inviting them to train in the very virtues the bodhisattva has already cultivated. Second, skillfully settling them instead in whatever path genuinely suits their own capacity — the śrāvaka or solitary-realizer vehicle for those of that lineage, the supreme vehicle for those of the buddha lineage. Third, becoming a genuine refuge, according to one's own actual strength, for such beings when they are without support, suffering, or impoverished. Fourth, encouraging them to make offerings to the buddhas. And fifth, encouraging them to receive, recite, copy, and revere the true teaching the Buddha has spoken.

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first mark directly with the fourth of the classical means of gathering beings, working alongside others — inviting a benefactor to actually join the bodhisattva's own path of practice, discipline, concentration, and wisdom together, rather than merely thanking them in words. He treats the second mark as this list's own necessary counterweight to the first: not every benefactor shares the bodhisattva's own specific aptitude, so a bodhisattva discerningly settles a śrāvaka-lineage benefactor into that vehicle's own path, a solitary-realizer-lineage benefactor into theirs, and a buddha-lineage benefactor into the supreme vehicle — genuine repayment means guiding someone toward their own actual flourishing, not merely replicating the bodhisattva's own path regardless of fit.

This closes the fifth quality, repaying kindness, in full; the treatise turns next to the sixth quality named in the opening verse, the specific occasions a bodhisattva always takes genuine delight in.

30. Milestone: Effort That Is Never Wasted — Closing the First Summary Verse's Seven Qualities Entirely

The seventh and final quality named in the opening verse, effort that produces genuine, non-wasted benefit for beings, rests on five causes: wishing to bring beings benefit and ease before they even ask; correctly, undistortedly knowing what genuinely constitutes that benefit and ease; and three further causes the treatise simply refers back to its own earlier, complete treatment in fascicle 44's account of venerating the three jewels — teaching with skillful means genuinely suited to what a given being can actually receive, sustaining tireless benefit without ever growing weary, and holding an entirely equal great compassion that plays no favorites between beings of lesser, middling, or superior capacity. With this seventh quality, the first of chapter eighteen's four summary verses, and all seven qualities it names, closes in full.

The treatise now turns to unpacking the first of the five kinds of undistorted effort, protective effort, asking what it actually is before naming, in brief outline, the five components into which it further divides: sustaining innate wisdom, correct mindfulness, correct understanding, one's own mind, and the minds of others.

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the treatise's own method here — first posing the question, then simply announcing the number before listing and explaining each item in turn — is the same threefold pattern (question, brief outline, detailed listing with explanation) it has used consistently throughout the entire chapter, and indeed throughout much of the Bodhisattva Stage. 33. Milestone: Sustaining Innate Wisdom, and Sustaining Correct Mindfulness

The first of the five components, sustaining innate wisdom, describes a bodhisattva whose innate, unlearned wisdom — sharp and ready from birth — allows the six sense faculties to grasp wholesome teachings with unusual speed, so that this natural gift is never squandered on worldly satisfaction but is instead turned entirely toward realizing the true nature of things and completing the six perfections. The second component, sustaining correct mindfulness, describes how a bodhisattva holds onto whatever has been learned through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating the teaching by means of correct mindfulness, so that it is retained in mind and never forgotten — since the treatise elsewhere explains correct mindfulness simply as not forgetting what the Buddha himself has taught.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi glosses "sharp" (聰) as keenness of the ear and "wise" (叡) as depth of insight — a bodhisattva born with unusually clear sense faculties who hears the teaching and immediately absorbs it as genuine wholesome roots, joined with a wisdom deep enough to be called sagacious. He walks through the commentary's own careful distinction between wisdom cultivated through effort (through hearing, reading, and the actual practice of calm-abiding and insight meditation) and wisdom that is simply innate, inherited from wisdom accumulated in past lives, noting that the Compendium of Determinations offers both an assignment and its reverse, and that the passage here follows the reading under which "sustaining innate wisdom" specifically names the swift uptake made possible by wisdom already present at birth.

Turning to correct mindfulness, he stresses that whatever teaching a bodhisattva has gathered through the causes and conditions proper to their own life should be reviewed again and again — its words and its meaning both rehearsed repeatedly in mind — since repeated recollection is what actually keeps a teaching retained, while a teaching only heard once and never again recalled is easily lost. He cites the treatise's own later definition, in fascicle 83, that correct mindfulness simply means not forgetting the Buddha's own instruction: mindfulness ties the mind to what the Buddha himself has taught, never letting that sacred teaching slip away.

The third component, sustaining correct understanding, describes how a bodhisattva, through wisdom joined with noble insight, skillfully examines the ultimate emptiness of whatever teaching has been retained and penetrates, with correct wisdom, the truth that all phenomena are without self. This protection works from two directions at once: from the side of adverse conditions, by staying free of whatever would cause the wisdom, mindfulness, and understanding already gained through sharpness and correct mindfulness to regress; and from the side of favorable conditions, by drawing close to whatever would let that same wisdom remain stable and continue advancing.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies "understanding" (覺) here as the innate wisdom already discussed and "insight" (慧) as wisdom cultivated through effort, so that "the wisdom of sharpness, correct mindfulness, and understanding" names the whole prior achievement — everything sustained by the first two components — now itself needing its own further protection. Sustaining correct understanding, on his reading, is what keeps a bodhisattva's body, speech, and mind pure and unshaken by outer conditions, so that afflictions do not arise and merit already gained is not lost, and it works by two complementary strategies at once.

From the side of adverse conditions, he explains, a bodhisattva stays free of whatever would cause this wisdom to regress — the five specific causes the treatise itself will name shortly: disrespecting the true teaching and its teachers, comparing oneself against other bodhisattvas to claim superiority, growing arrogant, and holding a distorted, arrogant view of the teaching. He notes that the treatise defers the full list of both sets of five to the sections immediately following in the source text, which the fascicle will take up once the five kinds of undistorted effort themselves are complete.

35. Milestone: Guarding One's Own Mind and Guarding the Minds of Others — Protective Effort Closes in Full

The fourth component, guarding one's own mind, describes a bodhisattva who skillfully guards the six sense-doors so that the mind does not chase after the six sense-objects — since the six faculties function as doors through which both defiling conditions and wholesome, purifying qualities pass in and out, and a mind that reaches outward easily gives rise to affliction along the way. The fifth and final component, guarding the minds of others, describes a bodhisattva who likewise protects the purity of other beings' minds, so that they too turn correctly and follow the true teaching — since a bodhisattva does not stop at guarding only their own mind toward the path, but also guides others toward accord with the ultimate truth. With these five, protective effort, the first of the five kinds of undistorted effort, closes in full as an accomplishment every bodhisattva should attain.

Chinese Source

四者、隨護自心。能善防守諸根門故。

五者、隨護他心。能於他心正隨轉故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, guarding one's own mind. This means being able to skillfully guard the doors of the sense faculties.

Fifth, guarding the minds of others. This means being able to cause the minds of others to turn correctly and follow along.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats guarding one's own mind as the natural culmination of the first three components: a bodhisattva first uses the learned teaching to purify the mind, since practice must begin by purifying oneself before one can genuinely take up the work of guiding others. Guarding the sense-doors, on his reading, means specifically guarding the sixth consciousness's correct mindfulness at each of the six gates, so that outward-facing attention does not slip into craving or aversion at the first contact with sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, or thought.

Guarding the minds of others, he notes, is where protective effort turns outward for the first time — not because a bodhisattva has finished purifying their own mind and only then begins to help others, but because the two concerns belong together within the same fivefold protective effort, self-guarding and other-guarding forming a single continuous practice rather than two separate stages. With this fifth component, he observes, the entire first of the five kinds of undistorted effort — protective effort in all its five parts — is now fully explained; the treatise turns next to the second kind, faultless effort.

36. Milestone: Faultless Effort

The treatise takes up the second of the five kinds of undistorted effort, faultless effort — meaning effort in which body, speech, and mind incur no fault whatsoever. Bodhisattvas apply themselves to the thirty-seven factors of awakening, the six perfections, the bodhisattva's own factors of enlightenment, the four means of gathering beings, and every other wholesome teaching, undistortedly and with blazing intensity, without interruption over a long span of time, and then dedicate the merit of all this cultivation of immeasurable wholesome qualities toward unsurpassed awakening. This is what is called faultless effort.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩無罪加行？謂諸菩薩，於諸善法無倒熾然，無量無間迴向菩提。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's faultless effort? It means that bodhisattvas, with respect to every wholesome teaching, undistortedly and with blazing intensity, dedicate immeasurable, uninterrupted cultivation toward awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "faultless" here in the strict sense of freedom from fault in all three karmic doors — body, speech, and mind each acting without error — applied specifically to the entire range of wholesome practices a bodhisattva undertakes: the classical thirty-seven factors of awakening shared with the broader tradition, the six perfections and the bodhisattva's own distinctive factors of enlightenment, and the four means of gathering beings used to draw others toward the teaching. He stresses the compound qualifier "undistorted and blazing" as naming a very particular quality of diligence — not merely correct in direction but vigorous in intensity, sustained without any lapse across a genuinely long span of practice, rather than a fervor that flares briefly and then cools.

He notes that the concluding phrase, dedicating this cultivation of immeasurable wholesome qualities toward unsurpassed awakening, is what finally makes the effort "faultless" in the fullest sense: an accumulation of wholesome action that is not dedicated toward awakening remains, on this reading, merely worldly merit, however vast, while the same accumulation turned toward bodhi becomes genuinely transcendent cultivation. Compared with protective effort, which concerns the five ways a bodhisattva's own understanding is kept from regressing, faultless effort concerns instead the purity and vigor of the practice itself across body, speech, and mind — a distinct but equally necessary dimension of the very first stage of the path, still belonging, as he notes ahead, to the provisioning stage together with protective effort.

37. Milestone: The Effort of Investigative Power, Belonging to the Stage of Resolute Practice

The third kind, the effort of investigative power, belongs specifically to bodhisattvas in the stage of resolute practice — the preparatory stage prior to the grounds proper. One should understand that this entire prior cultivation, protective effort and faultless effort both, takes on the specific character proper to this stage once a bodhisattva applies the power of correct investigation to it.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩思擇力加行？謂諸菩薩，即此一切在勝解行地，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's effort of investigative power? It means that this entire cultivation, in bodhisattvas who are in the stage of resolute practice, should be understood to take on its own particular character there.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi places protective effort and faultless effort both within the provisioning stage, and this third kind, investigative-power effort, within the very next stage, the stage of applied effort, more specifically named here the stage of resolute practice (勝解行地). He unpacks the name itself: calm-abiding accomplished gives the mind genuine power, called "resolute" (勝); insight capable of penetrating the true nature of phenomena is called "understanding" (解); and the sustained cultivation of both calm-abiding and insight together, gradually transforming defilement into purity, is called "practice" (行) — together, resolute practice.

A bodhisattva at this stage, he explains, already has the capacity to subdue affliction through the joint power of calm-abiding and insight, having completed the five components of protective effort and faultless effort as an outer ordinary being in the provisioning stage and now entering the inner ordinary stage that precedes the grounds. Having accomplished calm-abiding, such a bodhisattva relies on it to cultivate insight, investigating and determining the genuine meaning of the two kinds of selflessness in meditative absorption — undistortedly discerning truth and subduing affliction accordingly. Such a bodhisattva can generate faith, resolute understanding, receptive listening, and sustained reflection toward the great vehicle's own bodhisattva canon and toward the inconceivable, vast, supreme power of the buddhas and bodhisattvas revealed within it — and this capacity is precisely what is meant by the effort of investigative power.

38. Milestone: The Effort of Pure, Excellent Aspiration, Belonging to Grounds One Through Seven

The fourth kind, the effort of pure, excellent aspiration, belongs to bodhisattvas in the ground of pure, excellent aspiration and the ground of genuinely correct practice — that is, the first ground and the second through seventh grounds respectively. One should understand that this entire prior cultivation — protective effort, faultless effort, and the effort of investigative power together — takes on its own particular character once cultivated by the wisdom proper to these grounds.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩清淨增上意樂加行？謂諸菩薩，即此一切在淨勝意樂地及行正行地，應知其相。

《披》淨勝意樂地及行正行地者：極歡喜住，名淨勝意樂地；增上戒住、增上心住、三種增上慧住、有加行有功用無相住，名行正行地。如地品說。（陵本四十九卷一頁3910）

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's effort of pure, excellent aspiration? It means that this entire cultivation, in bodhisattvas who are in the ground of pure, excellent aspiration and the ground of genuinely correct practice, should be understood to take on its own particular character there.

Commentary: Regarding "the ground of pure, excellent aspiration and the ground of genuinely correct practice" — the abode of utmost joy is called the ground of pure, excellent aspiration; the abode of excellent moral discipline, the abode of excellent mind, the three abodes of excellent wisdom, and the abode with effort and application yet already free of marks are together called the ground of genuinely correct practice. This is as explained in the chapter on the grounds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads "pure" here as the freedom from affliction that the path of seeing brings, and "excellent aspiration" as the presence of two especially strong aspirations at this stage — the aspiration to accomplish the noble path oneself, and the supremely great compassion that vows to liberate every sentient being — together making up what the treatise elsewhere, in fascicle 47, enumerates as the bodhisattva's own fifteen kinds of excellent aspiration. Once a bodhisattva attains the acceptance of the non-arising of phenomena for the very first time at the first ground, that excellent aspiration and great compassion propel further practice toward the mark-free realm with genuinely superior conduct, cultivating the six perfections with a wisdom that grasps nothing at all.

He then walks through the commentary's own mapping of the second through seventh grounds by name: the second ground, where the perfection of moral discipline is completed, is called the abode of excellent moral discipline; the third ground, where the perfection of meditative concentration is completed, is called the abode of excellent mind; the fourth, fifth, and sixth grounds together form the three abodes of excellent wisdom — the fourth called the abode of excellent wisdom regarding the factors of awakening, also named the ground of blazing wisdom; the fifth called the abode of excellent wisdom regarding the truths, referring to the four noble truths in which this ground's wisdom is especially powerful, also named the ground hard to conquer; and the sixth called the abode of excellent wisdom regarding dependent origination, also named the ground of manifestation. The abode with effort and application yet already free of marks names the seventh ground, the ground of far-going. Across the second through the seventh grounds together, he notes, this cultivation gradually grows vast and expansive without any grasping at marks — and this entire span, from the first ground through the seventh, is precisely what constitutes the effort of pure, excellent aspiration.

39. Milestone: The Effort of Falling Into Certainty, Belonging to Grounds Eight Through Ten

The fifth and final kind, the effort of falling into certainty, belongs to bodhisattvas of the ground of certainty, the ground of certain conduct, and the ground of ultimate accomplishment — the eighth, ninth, and tenth grounds. One should understand that this entire cultivation of hearing, reflecting, and practicing the teaching, at these three grounds, is already able to abide effortlessly and spontaneously in the mark-free realm, its character to be understood accordingly.

Chinese Source

云何菩薩墮決定加行？謂諸菩薩，即此一切在決定地、決定行地、到究竟地，應知其相。

《披》決定地決定行地到究竟地者：無加行無功用無相住，名決定地，此地菩薩墮在第三決定中故；無礙解住，名決定行地；最上成滿菩薩住，名到究竟地。亦如地品中說。

Ru-Yi Translation

What is a bodhisattva's effort of falling into certainty? It means that this entire cultivation, in bodhisattvas who are in the ground of certainty, the ground of certain conduct, and the ground of arrival at the ultimate, should be understood to take on its own particular character there.

Commentary: Regarding "the ground of certainty, the ground of certain conduct, and the ground of arrival at the ultimate" — the abode without effort, without application, already abiding in the mark-free realm is called the ground of certainty, since bodhisattvas at this ground have fallen into the third kind of certainty; the abode of unobstructed understanding is called the ground of certain conduct; the abode of the bodhisattva of supreme, complete accomplishment is called the ground of arrival at the ultimate. This too is as explained in the chapter on the grounds.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains why the eighth ground marks such a decisive turning point: while bodhisattvas of the ground of pure, excellent aspiration and the ground of genuinely correct practice — the first through seventh grounds — can already abide in the mark-free realm in accord with ultimate truth, they still require deliberate mental application to do so, and are therefore not yet called "certain." From the eighth ground onward, no such application is needed at all; a bodhisattva abides effortlessly and unmoving in ultimate truth, and only at this point does the treatise apply the word "certain," meaning certain and irreversible. "Falling" here carries the sense of entering — entering effort that has fallen into certain, irreversible attainment, at which point what the treatise calls the mark-free cultivation's own result is said to be perfected.

He then unpacks the three kinds of certainty the treatise will name later in this same fascicle, since the commentary explicitly invokes them here: certainty by abiding in the lineage, meaning that an ordinary being of the buddha-lineage who encounters the genuinely excellent teaching of the three jewels is thereby certain to attain unsurpassed awakening eventually; certainty by arousing the mind of awakening, meaning specifically the arousal that comes with attaining the acceptance of non-arising and abiding in ultimate truth through non-discriminating wisdom — the genuine arousal grounded in realizing the nature of phenomena, as opposed to ordinary, worldly, discriminated arousal, which, once attained, guarantees unsurpassed, perfect awakening without any possibility of regression; and certainty by practice that is never in vain, meaning a bodhisattva who has attained great mastery, able to abide effortlessly and spontaneously in the mark-free realm, having thoroughly seen through the illusory nature of all appearances, and who is therefore able to benefit vast, immeasurable numbers of beings exactly as intended whenever engaged in the work of transforming them, never laboring in vain or achieving only half of what was intended. He notes that bodhisattvas of the eighth, ninth, and tenth grounds fall specifically into this third kind, practice never in vain — the abode of unobstructed understanding at the ninth ground naming a bodhisattva who has achieved the four kinds of unobstructed understanding and can therefore communicate with every being in their own language while certainly bringing about their liberation, and the abode of supreme, complete accomplishment at the tenth ground naming the very culmination of the bodhisattva path, arrival at the ultimate itself.

40. Milestone: The Conclusion — Five Kinds of Effort Universally Encompass All Undistorted Effort

The treatise closes this account with a brief concluding statement: these five kinds of bodhisattva effort — protective effort, faultless effort, the effort of investigative power, the effort of pure, excellent aspiration, and the effort of falling into certainty — together universally encompass every kind of undistorted effort a bodhisattva can undertake, across the entire path from an ordinary being's very first steps through to the final ground of ultimate accomplishment. With this, the first category of the second summary verse, undistorted effort, closes in full.

Chinese Source

如是五種菩薩加行，普攝一切無倒加行。

Ru-Yi Translation

Such are the five kinds of bodhisattva effort, which universally encompass every kind of undistorted effort.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws the five kinds together into a single continuous map of the entire bodhisattva path. Protective effort, comprising sustaining innate wisdom, correct mindfulness, correct understanding, one's own mind, and the minds of others, is what an ordinary being still outside the path must first accomplish. Faultless effort, keeping body, speech, and mind free of fault while dedicating boundless, uninterrupted cultivation toward awakening, belongs to this same early stage — together, he notes, protective effort and faultless effort both belong to the provisioning stage. The effort of investigative power belongs to the stage of applied effort that follows, where a bodhisattva, having accomplished calm-abiding, is able to use it as the basis for insight capable of correct, undistorted investigation and discernment, generating faith, resolute understanding, receptive listening, and reflection toward the bodhisattva canon and its own inconceivable content — investigative-power effort is thus the specific achievement of resolute practice.

The effort of pure, excellent aspiration belongs to the first through seventh grounds, where correct, thorough investigation has already perfected the ten practices of the teaching and propelled a bodhisattva into excellent aspiration, attaining the first ground and, from the second through the seventh, cultivating the mark-free path ever more vastly and expansively. The effort of falling into certainty belongs to the eighth through tenth grounds, where the result of mark-free cultivation reaches its own full perfection. He closes by noting that this fivefold structure — provisioning and applied effort together in the first three kinds, the seven grounds of genuine aspiration in the fourth, and the final three grounds of certainty in the fifth — is precisely why the treatise can say these five effort together universally encompass all undistorted effort: there is no stage of the entire bodhisattva path, from its very beginning to its very culmination, left uncovered by one or another of the five. With undistorted effort now closed in full, the treatise turns next to the second of the second summary verse's six categories, the causes and conditions of regression.

41. Milestone: Five Conditions Favoring Regression

The treatise now names the second category of the second summary verse: the five conditions that would cause a bodhisattva to regress from the very wisdom — sharpness, correct mindfulness, and correct understanding — that protective effort had just been cultivating. These are disrespecting the true teaching and the teachers who expound it; heedlessness and laziness; drawing close to and clinging to one's own afflictions; drawing close to and clinging to unwholesome conduct; and comparing oneself against other bodhisattvas to claim superiority out of arrogance, together with holding a distorted, arrogant view of the teaching itself.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩順退分法，當知有五。何等為五？一者、不敬正法及說法師。二者、放逸懈怠。三者、於諸煩惱親近執著。四者、於諸惡行親近執著。五者、與餘菩薩校量勝劣，起增上慢；及於法顛倒，起增上慢。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, one should understand that bodhisattvas have five conditions favoring regression. What are these five? First, disrespecting the true teaching and the teachers who expound it. Second, heedlessness and laziness. Third, drawing close to and clinging to one's own afflictions. Fourth, drawing close to and clinging to unwholesome conduct. Fifth, comparing oneself against other bodhisattvas to claim superiority, giving rise to arrogance; and holding a distorted view of the teaching, likewise giving rise to arrogance.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi unpacks the first cause by way of the classical fourfold structure of teaching, principle, practice, and fruit: the teaching is the bridge of language and text without which beings could never approach ultimate truth; the principle is the meaning that teaching reveals, such as the emptiness of self and phenomena, the four noble truths, dependent origination, and the six perfections; practice is the cultivation of insight within calm-abiding, undertaken in reliance on that teaching and principle; and the fruit is the realization such practice eventually produces, from the four fruits of the lesser vehicle through the ten grounds of the great vehicle. He cites the Avatamsaka Sutra's own image — a treasure sitting in darkness, invisible without a lamp, so that even the wisest cannot understand the Buddha's teaching if no one is there to expound it — to explain why disrespecting the teaching and its teachers severs a bodhisattva from these two foundational supports and makes regression from the path all but inevitable.

He explains heedlessness through the Compendium of Determinations' own threefold account: distorted thinking, dwelling habitually on nine topics that ought never to be dwelt upon (self, beings, the world, karmic results, the meditative states of those absorbed in dhyana, the domains of the buddhas, the fourteen unanswerable questions, what is not the true teaching, and whatever is drawn along by every affliction); distorted deliberation, the eight kinds of harmful deliberation involving desire, hostility, harm, one's homeland, one's relatives, deathlessness, family standing, and contempt for others; and distorted idle talk, discussions of kings, thieves, food and drink, or craftsmanship — all forms of frivolous chatter that only feed greed, hatred, and delusion. The third and fourth causes, he notes, follow naturally from the second: a bodhisattva who does not actively counter afflictions but instead grows comfortable dwelling alongside them lets those afflictions intensify and harm body and mind alike, and afflictions left unchecked in turn give rise to the ten unwholesome courses of action through body, speech, and mind. The fifth cause, he adds, combines two forms of the same underlying arrogance — falsely believing oneself superior to other bodhisattvas when one is not, and being satisfied with too little progress as a result, together with a distorted grasp of the great vehicle's own teaching that mistakes what has not yet been attained for attainment already achieved. Any bodhisattva who has genuinely aroused the aspiration for unsurpassed awakening, he concludes, should take particular care to avoid all five.

42. Milestone: Five Conditions Favoring Advancement — the Exact Mirror of Regression

The third category, conditions favoring advancement, is given simply as the precise reverse, point for point, of the five conditions favoring regression just explained: respecting the true teaching and its teachers; diligently abandoning what is unwholesome and cultivating what is wholesome, free of heedlessness and laziness; actively countering and staying free of affliction rather than drawing close to it; guarding one's own correct mindfulness and staying free of unwholesome conduct; and neither comparing oneself against other bodhisattvas to claim superiority nor holding any distorted, arrogant view of the great vehicle's teaching.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩順勝分法，當知有五。何等為五？謂與前五黑品諸法次第相違，應知其相。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, one should understand that bodhisattvas have five conditions favoring advancement. What are these five? One should understand that their character is, point by point, the exact reverse of the five preceding conditions belonging to the dark class of teachings just explained.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that the treatise, having just given the five conditions of regression in full detail, feels no need to repeat the same structure with different words for their positive counterparts — it simply states that advancement is regression's own mirror image, trusting the reader to invert each of the five in turn: respecting rather than disrespecting the teaching and its teachers, diligence rather than heedlessness and laziness, actively countering affliction rather than drawing close to it, guarding correct mindfulness against unwholesome conduct rather than clinging to it, and neither the arrogance of false comparison nor the arrogance of a distorted grasp of the teaching.

He treats this deliberate economy of expression as itself an instance of a broader stylistic habit running through the entire treatise — wherever a positive and negative pairing share an identical underlying structure, the text states one in full and simply inverts it for the other, again trusting a reader following the text continuously to hold both halves in mind together rather than needing each written out separately. With these five now given, he notes, the second summary verse's second and third categories — regression and advancement — both close in full; the treatise turns next to its fourth category, five kinds of merit that merely resemble genuine bodhisattva virtue but are, in truth, faults.

43. Milestone: Counterfeit Merit Opens — Abandoning the Vicious, and Feigning Dignified Deportment

The treatise now warns against five things that look, on the surface, like genuine bodhisattva merit but are in truth faults. The first is refusing to benefit beings who are vicious and have violated pure precepts, using their own wrongdoing as a pretext for abandoning them — which looks like righteous judgment but is actually the loss of compassion. The second is feigning a complete outward display of dignified deportment — walking, sitting, standing, and lying down with a studied, deliberate composure — purely to deceive others into believing one is deeply virtuous, and thereby drawing offerings and service under false pretenses.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩略有五種相似功德，當知實是菩薩過失。何等為五？一者、於其暴惡毀犯淨戒諸有情所，由是因緣作不饒益；二者、詐現種種具足威儀；

《披》於其暴惡毀犯淨戒諸有情所等者：戒品中說：於諸暴惡犯戒有情，懷嫌恨心、懷恚惱心，由彼暴惡犯戒為緣，方便棄捨，不作饒益，是名有犯，有所違越，是染違犯。（陵本四十一卷四頁3349）其義應知。

《披》詐現種種具足威儀者：謂為誑他，恆常詐現諸根無掉、諸根無動、諸根寂靜，由是令他謂其有德，當有所施、當有所作。如聲聞地說應知。（陵本二十二卷十頁1919）

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas have, in brief, five kinds of counterfeit merit that one should understand to actually be faults. What are these five? First, refusing to benefit beings who are vicious and have violated pure precepts, using this very circumstance as the pretext. Second, feigning a complete and elaborate display of dignified deportment.

Commentary: Regarding "refusing to benefit beings who are vicious and have violated pure precepts, using this very circumstance as the pretext" — the chapter on moral discipline explains that harboring resentment or harboring anger and distress toward beings who are vicious and have violated the precepts, and on the pretext of their viciousness and violation, contriving to abandon them and refuse to benefit them, constitutes a transgression, a violation, indeed a defiled transgression. Its meaning should be understood accordingly.

Commentary: Regarding "feigning a complete and elaborate display of dignified deportment" — this means constantly feigning, in order to deceive others, faculties that show no agitation, no movement, and complete tranquility, so that others believe one to be virtuous and are moved to make offerings or perform service. This should be understood as explained in the Stage of Disciples.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi draws out the subtlety of the first counterfeit merit: a bodhisattva who witnesses genuinely vicious, precept-violating conduct and responds by washing their hands of that being entirely may look, from the outside, like someone exercising sound moral judgment, refusing to enable further wrongdoing. But the treatise's own chapter on moral discipline identifies the actual motive at work — resentment, anger, and distress toward the offender — and names abandoning such a being on that pretext a defiled transgression of the bodhisattva precepts, precisely because it is compassion itself, not merely tolerance of wrongdoing, that has been abandoned. He cites the commentary to the Brahma Net Sutra's own instruction that a bodhisattva who hears heretics or wayward followers of the lesser vehicle speak against the true teaching should respond with compassion, working to convert them toward genuine faith in the great vehicle rather than writing them off — the correct attitude a bodhisattva should hold toward even a vicious being.

The second counterfeit merit, he explains, targets a specific and easily overlooked form of deception: cultivating an outwardly serene, unhurried bearing — walking like the wind, sitting like a bell, standing like a pine, lying like a drawn bow, as the traditional image has it — not because inner stillness has genuinely been achieved, but as a calculated performance designed to make onlookers believe real virtue and meditative attainment are present, so that they will be moved to offer support and service. He notes the treatise's own cross-reference to the Stage of Disciples for the fuller account of this deceptive display, underscoring that the fault here lies not in dignified conduct itself, which is entirely proper when it reflects genuine inner cultivation, but specifically in manufacturing its appearance for the sake of gain.

44. Milestone: Counterfeit Merit Continues — Worldly Cleverness, Tainted Giving, and Spreading Counterfeit Teaching

The third counterfeit merit is being counted among the wise and clever specifically for skill in worldly rhetoric, incantations, and the treatises of non-Buddhist schools — impressive by worldly standards, yet, within the Buddhist path, only a resemblance of merit rather than the real thing. The fourth is practicing giving and other wholesome conduct while still tainted by fault — giving, for instance, in order to win a reputation, or from wealth obtained by harming beings, or giving only after long, grudging accumulation rather than freely as opportunity allows. The fifth is preaching and establishing a counterfeit version of the true teaching and spreading it widely, whether through mistaken doctrine or mistaken practice, thereby damaging the very wholesome roots the true teaching is meant to nurture.

Chinese Source

三者、於順世間文辭、呪術、外道書論相應法中，得預智者、聰叡者數；四者、修行有罪施等善行；五者、宣說建立像似正法，廣令流布。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, being counted among the wise and the clever specifically for teachings concordant with worldly rhetoric, incantations, and the treatises of non-Buddhist schools. Fourth, practicing giving and similar wholesome conduct while it is still tainted by fault. Fifth, preaching and establishing a counterfeit version of the true teaching and causing it to spread widely.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the third counterfeit merit as a caution particularly relevant to genuinely accomplished worldly scholars — someone whose writing is so refined it earns a doctorate in literature or even a Nobel Prize, or whose command of incantations and non-Buddhist lore commands real worldly respect, remains, from the standpoint of the path, only a resemblance of merit, since none of it touches the liberating wisdom the treatise actually values. The fourth, tainted giving, he illustrates from the treatise's own Bodhisattva Stage discussion in fascicle 39: giving from wealth obtained by beating, binding, or harming other beings is tainted giving regardless of the gift's outward generosity; giving only after long, hoarded accumulation, rather than freely whenever the occasion for giving arises, is likewise tainted; and giving something that cannot actually benefit the recipient — his example is giving pages inscribed with the true teaching to a being with only an infant's understanding — is tainted giving as well, whether one performs it oneself or merely encourages another to do so. He extends the same principle to giving involving one's own body or dependents: giving them away without their own willing consent, in a way that provokes resentment, or knowing the gift will cause the recipient future harm, likewise counts as tainted giving rather than genuine merit.

The fifth, spreading counterfeit teaching, he expands using the Compendium on Mutual Association's own twofold division: counterfeit teaching in doctrine, which includes falsely fabricated scriptural phrasing, falsely adding to or subtracting from the meaning of impermanence and the like, wrongly analyzing the nature of the person, asserting that provisionally-existing phenomena truly exist, and falsely construing the meaning of ultimate nirvana; and counterfeit teaching in practice, which includes relying on views rooted in exaggeration, wrong grasping, or false knowledge, misplaced wrong conduct such as never reflecting at all, delighting in monastic affairs for their own sake, valuing giving above all else, treating mere hearing as final attainment, discarding the adornments proper to genuine practice, or simply conforming to worldly ways, and finally vicious precept-observance such as refusing to benefit beings, feigning dignified deportment, seeking gain through claimed merit, or expounding teachings with no genuine meaning. He notes this fuller list is found in the Compendium on Mutual Association, fascicle 99, should a reader wish to consult it directly.

45. Milestone: Genuine Merit Opens — Compassion for the Vicious, Natural Dignity, and Genuine Wisdom in the Teaching's Purity, Wonder, and Reality

Turning from counterfeit merit to its genuine counterpart, the treatise names five kinds of authentic bodhisattva merit, the exact reverse of the five just discussed. The first is generating supreme compassion, rather than abandonment, precisely toward beings who are vicious and violate pure precepts — since such beings, left unchecked, face the deepest suffering of the lower realms for a very long time to come. The second is naturally possessing complete, dignified deportment simply by one's own inborn nature, rather than manufacturing its appearance through deliberate performance. The third is being counted among the wise and clever specifically through genuine learning and realization of what the Buddha taught as pure, wondrous, and true — a purity, wonder, and reality the treatise's own commentary unpacks at length.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩略有五種真實功德。何等為五？一者、於其暴惡毀犯淨戒諸有情所，由是因緣起勝悲心；二者、本性成就具足威儀；三者、於佛所說淨妙真實若教若證，得預智者、聰叡者數；

《披》於佛所說淨妙真實等者：聞所成地說：云何淨？謂三清淨性。一、自體清淨性，二、境界清淨性，三、分位清淨性。云何妙？謂佛法僧寶名最微妙，墮最第一施設中故。云何真實？謂真如及四聖諦。（陵本十三卷十七頁1139）此應準知。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas have, in brief, five kinds of genuine merit. What are these five? First, generating supreme compassion, precisely because of this very circumstance, toward beings who are vicious and have violated pure precepts. Second, naturally possessing complete, dignified deportment through one's own inborn nature. Third, being counted among the wise and the clever through what the Buddha taught as pure, wondrous, and true, whether in doctrine or in realization.

Commentary: Regarding "what the Buddha taught as pure, wondrous, and true" — the Stage Attained Through Hearing explains: what is purity? It is the three natures of purity — the purity of essential nature, the purity of the objective sphere, and the purity of successive stages. What is wonder? It is that the three jewels, Buddha, teaching, and community, are called supremely subtle, since they fall within the very highest of all designations. What is reality? It is suchness and the four noble truths. This should be understood accordingly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts the first genuine merit directly with the first counterfeit one: a bodhisattva who understands that vicious, precept-violating beings, left to their own devices, will fall into the lower realms and suffer there for an immense span, with residual effects like ignorance and poverty still trailing them even after eventual release, responds not with abandonment but with an especially strong compassion, wishing to lead such beings toward repentance and the accumulation of merit rather than writing them off as unworthy of help. The second genuine merit, he notes, is dignified deportment that arises spontaneously from a bodhisattva's own inborn nature — an inner stillness naturally averse to agitation and disturbance, expressing real attainment rather than a rehearsed performance meant to deceive onlookers.

The third, he explains at length by way of the commentary's own definitions, concerns learning and realizing the Buddha's teaching under three headings. Purity has three natures: essential purity, meaning suchness itself — also called emptiness, the dharma-realm, the ultimate, the mark-free, the supreme truth, or nirvana, the intrinsically pure nature of the emptiness of self and phenomena; the purity of the objective sphere, meaning the sutras, discipline, and treatises as the utterly pure outflow of the dharma-realm, which practitioners study in order to gradually purify body, speech, and mind and progress toward the noble path; and the purity of successive stages, meaning the different degrees of purification marked out over time as afflictions are progressively abandoned, itself dividing into purity through freedom from defilement and purity through attainment of the path that produces it. Wonder names the three jewels — Buddha, teaching, and community — as supremely subtle among all things in the three realms, since the Buddha is fully self-awakened and awakens others with perfectly complete conduct, the teaching alone truly liberates and leads to suchness, and the community, whether the noble ones who have realized the path or ordinary followers acting in accord with wholesome teaching, all fall under this same supremely subtle designation. Reality names suchness itself, together with the four noble truths, the objective ground respectively of the wisdom that purifies the cognitive obscuration and the wisdom that purifies the afflictive obscuration. A bodhisattva who attains genuine learning and realization across purity, wonder, and reality in this full sense, he concludes, achieves genuine merit — worldly literary distinction, however impressive, is simply not what is being praised here, since any actual practice and realization within the Buddhist path already surpasses every purely worldly accomplishment, precisely because it is so much harder to attain.

46. Milestone: Genuine Merit Closes — Faultless Giving, and Refuting Counterfeit Teaching

The fourth genuine merit is practicing giving and other wholesome conduct entirely without fault — free of any taint, joined with the wisdom of non-attainment, and dedicated toward unsurpassed awakening. The fifth is expounding the true teaching in a way that refutes and extinguishes every counterfeit version of it. With these five, genuine merit, the fifth category of the second summary verse, closes in full — the exact positive counterpart to the counterfeit merit just discussed.

Chinese Source

四者、修行無罪施等善行；五者、開示正法，遮滅一切像似正法。

Ru-Yi Translation

Fourth, practicing giving and similar wholesome conduct entirely without fault. Fifth, expounding the true teaching so as to refute and extinguish every counterfeit version of the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats the fourth genuine merit as the direct mirror of the fourth counterfeit one: whether the practice in question is giving, moral discipline, patience, diligence, meditative concentration, or wisdom, what makes it faultless and genuinely meritorious is that it is untainted by any impure motive, joined with the wisdom that grasps at nothing, and dedicated toward unsurpassed awakening rather than toward reputation or worldly gain — practice performed with any degree of seeking or attachment, however outwardly generous, still falls short of genuine merit by this standard.

The fifth genuine merit, he notes, completes the entire fourfold pairing of counterfeit and genuine merit begun two milestones earlier: just as counterfeit teaching, whether mistaken in doctrine or mistaken in practice, damages the wholesome roots of beings who encounter it, a bodhisattva's genuine merit lies specifically in expounding the true teaching clearly enough to refute and extinguish such counterfeits wherever they appear, protecting beings from being misled by what only resembles the real path. With these two categories — counterfeit merit and genuine merit — now both closed in full, he observes, the treatise turns to the sixth and final category of the second summary verse, ten places in which a bodhisattva undistortedly tames the beings being taught and transformed.

47. Milestone: Undistorted Taming of Beings Opens — Leaving Behind Wrongdoing, and Leaving Behind Desire

The sixth and final category of the second summary verse names ten places in which a bodhisattva undistortedly tames the beings being taught, guiding them from coarser, more visible faults toward progressively subtler cultivation. The first place is leaving behind wrongdoing — first establishing beings in freedom from the ten unwholesome courses of action through body, speech, and mind, the coarsest and most visible faults. The second is leaving behind desire — guiding beings further to relinquish craving for sights, sounds, smells, tastes, touches, and sexual union, and, for those with the wholesome roots to do so, toward renunciate practice, though the treatise notes candidly that such beings remain few.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩略於十處，無倒調伏所化有情。何等十處？一者、遠離惡行處；二者、遠離諸欲處；

《披》遠離惡行處等者：施設遠離不善業道，是名遠離惡行處。施設遠離習非梵行及與歌舞、伎樂等事，是名遠離諸欲處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, in brief, undistortedly tame the beings being taught in ten places. What are these ten places? First, the place of leaving behind wrongdoing. Second, the place of leaving behind desire.

Commentary: Regarding "the place of leaving behind wrongdoing" and the like — establishing beings in freedom from the unwholesome courses of action is called the place of leaving behind wrongdoing. Establishing beings in freedom from the practice of non-celibate conduct, and from song, dance, and entertainment, is called the place of leaving behind desire.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi frames these ten places as a deliberately graduated curriculum, moving a being being taught from the coarsest, most externally visible faults toward increasingly subtle internal cultivation — a natural continuation of the chapter's earlier concern with the bodhisattva's own qualities, now turned toward how a bodhisattva actually guides others along the same path. The first place, he explains, uses fitting stories, examples, and reasoning consistent with cause and effect to establish beings in freedom from the ten unwholesome courses of action across body, speech, and mind — the most basic and universally applicable starting point for any moral cultivation whatsoever.

The second place, he notes, moves a step further, encouraging freedom from craving for the objects of the five senses together with sexual union, song, dance, and entertainment, all of which stir up desire in the mind and pose a serious obstacle to spiritual practice. He observes candidly, following the source text's own admission, that persuading worldly people to actually relinquish desire is genuinely difficult, and that while beings with sufficient wholesome roots may be willing to leave behind sensory pleasures and even take up renunciate life, such beings remain, realistically, quite rare — a frank acknowledgment, on his reading, that the graduated curriculum these ten places describe does not assume every being will progress through all ten, but simply maps the full range a bodhisattva must be prepared to guide beings through, as far as each is able to go.

48. Milestone: Undistorted Taming Continues — Precepts, Guarding the Senses, Right Knowledge, Solitude, and the Two Obstacles to the Path

The third through eighth places guide a being who has renounced further along the path: keeping precepts scrupulously and, if a fault occurs, knowing how to confess and restore purity; secretly guarding all six sense-doors so that contact with any object does not give rise to affliction; abiding with right knowledge in every activity of daily life; leaving behind noisy, distracting places for solitude conducive to calm-abiding and insight; within solitude, further leaving behind all eight kinds of unwholesome deliberation; and leaving behind the obstacles to the path, which the treatise's commentary divides at length into internal obstacles — arising from the five hindrances of scarce resources, intense affliction, frequent illness, bodily imbalance, and undigested food, together with hindrances to solitude and to inner stillness — and external obstacles, arising from lacking a genuine spiritual friend or dwelling in an unsuitable place.

Chinese Source

三者、專精無犯，犯已能出處；四者、密護一切諸根門處；五者、正知住處；六者、離憒鬧處；七者、於遠離處，遠離一切惡尋思處；八者、遠離障處；

《披》遠離障處者：障有二種，一者、依內，二者、依外。聲聞地中廣辯其相。（陵本二十五卷十頁2112）施設無障，是名遠離障處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, the place of single-minded, unblemished precept-keeping, and, once a fault has occurred, the place of being able to emerge from it through confession. Fourth, the place of secretly guarding every sense-door. Fifth, the place of abiding with right knowledge. Sixth, the place of leaving behind noisy, distracting settings. Seventh, within solitude, the place of leaving behind every unwholesome deliberation. Eighth, the place of leaving behind obstacles.

Commentary: Regarding "the place of leaving behind obstacles" — obstacles are of two kinds, those depending on internal conditions and those depending on external conditions; their characteristics are explained at length in the Stage of Disciples. Establishing beings free of obstacles is called the place of leaving behind obstacles.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi walks through the third through seventh places as describing the natural sequence a renunciate follows: after ordination, studying the precepts thoroughly enough to understand their exact scope, keeping them purely, and, should a lapse in mindfulness occur, knowing how to confess and restore that purity rather than abandoning the precept altogether; then, through extensive hearing, reflection, and cultivation, attaining correct mindfulness capable of secretly guarding all six sense-doors so that contact with sights, sounds, and the rest is recognized simply as the mind's own deluded discrimination rather than seized upon and elaborated into affliction; abiding with right knowledge in walking, standing, sitting, lying down, dress, food, and every action of body, speech, and mind, exactly as detailed in the Stage of Disciples' own account of the provisions for the path; leaving behind places where the mind grows confused and disturbed in favor of quiet settings suited to cultivating calm-abiding and insight; and, having achieved bodily solitude, further abandoning all eight kinds of unwholesome deliberation — desire, hostility, harm, homeland, relatives, deathlessness, contempt, and family standing — so that the mind becomes genuinely pure and still.

He then unpacks the eighth place, leaving behind obstacles, in its full detail from the Stage of Disciples: internal obstacles include an obstacle to effort itself, comprising five conditions — scarce resources from insufficient past giving, intensely afflicted states of greed, hatred, or delusion sustained over long periods, frequent illness from past unwholesome karma such as killing, recurring imbalances of wind, heat, or phlegm from irregular living and eating, and undigested food accumulating in the body — together with an obstacle to solitude, arising from overeating, excessive activity, or excessive socializing that wastes time in idle talk, and an obstacle to inner stillness, arising from pride in one's own lineage or learning, failure to diligently abandon the unwholesome, or dwelling somewhere unsuited to cultivating calm-abiding and insight. External obstacles, he continues, include the absence of a genuine spiritual friend capable of guiding one's meditative and moral cultivation, and dwelling in an unsuitable place — too noisy, too high, too cold, too remote, infested with insects, or exposed to dangerous animals or spirits. A bodhisattva guiding others, he concludes, must help them identify and remove both the internal and the external obstacles standing between them and genuine progress on the path.

49. Milestone: Undistorted Taming Closes — Leaving Behind the Active Afflictions and Their Dormant Seeds, Closing the Second Summary Verse in Full

The ninth and final refinements guide a being all the way to the subtlest level of cultivation: leaving behind every active manifestation of affliction, by cultivating calm-abiding to the point that meditative absorption keeps the afflictions from stirring at all; and leaving behind every dormant seed underlying the class of afflictions, by relying on that same meditative concentration to cultivate the insight that finally uproots the seeds of craving and views themselves, thereby accomplishing the noble path in full. With these ten places — moving from the coarsest external wrongdoing all the way to the subtlest dormant seeds of affliction — the sixth and final category of the second summary verse closes, and with it, chapter eighteen's entire second summary verse concludes in full.

Chinese Source

九者、遠離一切煩惱纏處；十者、遠離一切諸煩惱品諸麤重處。

Ru-Yi Translation

Ninth, the place of leaving behind every active manifestation of affliction. Tenth, the place of leaving behind every coarse, dormant seed belonging to the class of afflictions.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi reads the ninth place as guiding a being toward genuinely accomplishing meditative concentration, at which point the afflictions no longer actively stir at all — their active manifestations having been suppressed through the sheer stabilizing power of calm-abiding, even though their underlying seeds may not yet be eliminated. The tenth and final place goes further still, guiding a being to rely on that same concentration as the basis for cultivating the insight capable of directly severing the dormant seeds of craving and mistaken views themselves — the coarse, latent tendencies underlying the entire class of afflictions — thereby accomplishing the noble path in its fullest and most complete sense.

Surveying all ten places together, he traces a single continuous progression from shallow to profound: first leaving behind the ten unwholesome courses of action through body, speech, and mind; then leaving behind desire by taking up the five or eight precepts or the bodhisattva precepts; then renouncing and taking full ordination, keeping precepts scrupulously and restoring any lapse through confession; then guarding the sense-doors; then abiding with right knowledge; then leaving noisy settings for solitude; then, within that solitude, leaving behind unwholesome deliberation; then removing both internal and external obstacles to the path; then leaving behind affliction's active manifestations; and finally uprooting affliction's own dormant seeds. With this tenfold curriculum for undistortedly taming beings now complete, he notes, the entire second summary verse of chapter eighteen — undistorted effort, regression, advancement, counterfeit merit, genuine merit, and undistorted taming of beings, all six categories — reaches its full conclusion. The treatise turns next to the third of chapter eighteen's four summary verses, opening with how bodhisattvas receive the Buddha's own prophecy of their future awakening.

50. Milestone: The Third Summary Verse Opens — Six Marks of Receiving the Buddha's Prophecy of Awakening

Chapter eighteen's third summary verse names five further categories: bodhisattvas receiving the Buddha's own prophecy of their future awakening, falling into certainty, what a bodhisattva definitely must do, what a bodhisattva should always be doing, and ten supreme, culminating states. The first category unfolds into six marks by which the tathagatas bestow this prophecy: whether the bodhisattva has already aroused the aspiration for awakening or not yet done so despite abiding in the lineage; whether the Buddha bestows the prophecy in person, appearing directly before the bodhisattva, or without appearing in person at all; and whether a definite time is specified for when awakening will be attained, or no such time is given.

Chinese Source

謂諸菩薩略由六相，蒙諸如來於其無上正等菩提授與記別。何等為六？一者、安住種性未發心位；二者、已發心位；三者、現在前位；四者、不現前位；五者、有定時限，謂爾所時當證無上正等菩提；六者、無定時限，謂不宣說決定時限，而與授記。

《披》現在前位等者：蒙諸如來親面對前而為授記，是名現在前位。與此相違，是名不現前位。

Ru-Yi Translation

This means that bodhisattvas, in brief, through six marks, receive from the tathagatas a prophecy concerning their unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening. What are these six? First, the state of abiding in the lineage without yet having aroused the aspiration for awakening. Second, the state of having already aroused that aspiration. Third, the state of the Buddha appearing in person. Fourth, the state of the Buddha not appearing in person. Fifth, having a fixed time limit, meaning that after such-and-such a time one will attain unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening. Sixth, having no fixed time limit, meaning that the prophecy is given without specifying any definite time.

Commentary: Regarding "the state of the Buddha appearing in person" and the like — receiving a prophecy from the tathagatas who appear directly, face to face, before the bodhisattva is called the state of appearing in person. Its opposite is called the state of not appearing in person.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi organizes these six marks into three natural pairs, each distinguished by a different variable. The first pair distinguishes whether the aspiration for awakening has already been aroused: a bodhisattva may already possess the lineage capable of becoming a buddha yet not yet have aroused the aspiration for awakening, in which case the prophecy concerns only that inherent capacity, while a bodhisattva who has already aroused the aspiration and long cultivated the causes of awakening receives a prophecy that awakening will be swiftly attained. The second pair distinguishes whether the Buddha appears in person: in the first case, the Buddha manifests directly before the bodhisattva and declares outright, "you have already aroused the aspiration for unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening, and by cultivating the six perfections you will attain unsurpassed awakening in the future" — the pattern familiar from the Lotus Sutra's own chapter on bestowing prophecies — while in the second case, the Buddha bestows the same prophecy without any such direct, personal appearance.

The third pair distinguishes whether a definite time is specified: in some cases the Buddha, while bestowing the prophecy, also declares the specific future time when buddhahood will be attained, together with the name of the resulting buddha-land and the identity of one's future retinue, again exactly as the Lotus Sutra illustrates; in other cases the prophecy of eventual buddhahood is given without any statement of exactly when. He notes that the first pair distinguishes bodhisattvas by whether they have aroused the aspiration for awakening, the second by whether the Buddha appears in person or not, and the third by whether the time of buddhahood is fixed or left open — together, these three variables generate the full six marks by which the Buddha's prophecy can be bestowed. The treatise turns next to the second category of the third summary verse, the three kinds of bodhisattva who have fallen into certainty of eventual buddhahood.

51. Milestone: Falling Into Certainty Opens — Three Kinds, Beginning With Certainty by Abiding in the Lineage

The second category names three kinds of bodhisattva who have fallen into certainty of eventual buddhahood: certainty by abiding in the lineage, certainty by arousing the aspiration for awakening, and certainty by practice that is never in vain. The first, certainty by abiding in the lineage, describes any bodhisattva who simply abides in the lineage capable of buddhahood — since such a bodhisattva, upon encountering the excellent conditions of the three jewels and cultivating the noble path, is certain to become capable of realizing the very awakening the buddhas themselves have realized.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩略有三種墮於決定。何等為三？一者、安住種性墮於決定，二者、發菩提心墮於決定，三者、不虛修行墮於決定。

安住種性墮決定者，謂諸菩薩住種性位，便名為墮決定菩薩。何以故？由此菩薩若遇勝緣，必定堪任證於無上正等覺故。

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, in brief, have three kinds of falling into certainty. What are these three? First, certainty by abiding in the lineage. Second, certainty by arousing the aspiration for awakening. Third, certainty by practice that is never in vain.

Regarding certainty by abiding in the lineage — this means that bodhisattvas who abide in the state of the lineage are thereby called bodhisattvas who have fallen into certainty. Why is this? Because such a bodhisattva, upon encountering excellent conditions, is certainly capable of realizing unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites Elder Miao Jing's own gloss on what "abiding in the lineage" means: possessing buddha-nature, the seed capable of becoming a buddha, itself of two kinds — the unconditioned lineage identical with suchness itself, and a further, conditioned lineage cultivated through practice. Anyone who comes to trust the Buddhist teaching and, through studying it, recognizes birth-and-death as suffering naturally arouses two aspirations: the bodhisattva's own vow to accomplish the unsurpassed buddha-path, seeking liberation from birth-and-death through the teaching, and great compassion, wishing not only for one's own liberation but for every being to likewise escape suffering and attain the joy of the noble path shared across all three vehicles.

He stresses that this first kind of certainty belongs to any being who is, by nature, of the lineage capable of buddhahood — an inherent capacity present since beginningless time, not one merely acquired through habituation — such that whenever this being happens to encounter the excellent conditions of the three jewels and takes up cultivation of the noble path, arousing the unsurpassed aspiration for awakening becomes certain, and eventual attainment of the buddhas' own complete, correct realization is thereby guaranteed. This certainty, he notes, is the most foundational of the three, resting simply on the presence of the lineage itself, prior to any actual arousal of the aspiration or any accomplishment in practice — the treatise turns next to the second kind, certainty attained specifically through arousing that aspiration.

52. Milestone: Certainty by Arousing the Aspiration for Awakening — Genuine Realization of Suchness, Never to Regress

The second kind, certainty by arousing the aspiration for awakening, describes a class of bodhisattvas who have already given rise to a decisive resolve toward unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening, and who, from that point forward, never regress until awakening is actually attained. The treatise's own commentary is careful to specify that this arousal means genuinely realizing the true nature of phenomena directly, not merely an aspiration framed in conventional, worldly terms — a bodhisattva who has already passed through the first great incalculable eon of cultivation, attained the first ground of utmost joy, entered the fixed, non-arising stage of the bodhisattva path, truly understood unsurpassed awakening and the means to it, and realized that self and others are equally near to that great fruit, thereby gaining what the treatise calls a vast, expansive intention grounded in the equality of self and other rather than confined narrowly within it.

Chinese Source

發菩提心墮決定者，謂有一類諸菩薩眾，已於無上正等菩提起決定心，此後乃至證於無上正等菩提，無復退轉。

《披》發菩提心墮決定者等者：此中發菩提心，謂證法性發心，非依世俗發心。顯揚論說：證法性發心者，謂如有一，已過第一劫阿僧企耶，已證菩薩初極喜地，已入菩薩定無生位，已如實知無上菩提及菩提方便，已悟自身將近、等近大菩提果，證解自他悉平等故，得大我意，已至不住流轉寂滅菩薩道故，得廣大意。由如是故，於大菩提願不退轉，是謂證法性發心。（顯揚論二卷二十頁）此義正同。

Ru-Yi Translation

Regarding certainty by arousing the aspiration for awakening — this means that there is a class of bodhisattvas who have already given rise to a decisive resolve toward unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening, and who, from this point onward until they actually realize unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening, never again regress.

Commentary: Regarding "certainty by arousing the aspiration for awakening" and the like — the arousal of the aspiration for awakening spoken of here means the arousal that comes with realizing the true nature of phenomena, not an arousal grounded in conventional, worldly terms. The Compendium of Establishments explains: one who arouses the aspiration through realizing the true nature of phenomena is someone who, having already passed through the first incalculable eon, has already realized the bodhisattva's ground of utmost joy, has already entered the bodhisattva's fixed stage free of arising, has already truly known unsurpassed awakening and the means to it, has already understood that one's own person draws near, and equally near, to the great fruit of awakening — realizing that self and other are entirely equal, thereby gaining a great sense of self — and has already arrived at not dwelling in either the flow of birth-and-death or the extinction of nirvana with regard to the bodhisattva path, thereby gaining a vast, expansive intention. Because of this, such a one never regresses from the great aspiration for awakening — this is called the arousal that comes with realizing the true nature of phenomena. This meaning corresponds exactly.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi contrasts this genuine, ground-realizing arousal with the far more common conventional arousal that most beginning practitioners experience — an aspiration still framed through the provisional names and concepts of ordinary language, sincere but not yet grounded in direct realization of emptiness. The arousal described here belongs specifically to a bodhisattva who has completed the first of the three great incalculable eons of cultivation, accumulating the two provisions of merit and wisdom across the provisioning and applied-effort stages, and has thereby attained the first ground, where the genuine ultimate aspiration for awakening arises for the first time — a firm, decisive resolve toward buddhahood that continues unbroken through the second ground, the third, and onward through all ten grounds until the attainment of perfect, complete enlightenment.

He walks through the commentary's own citation from the Compendium of Establishments in detail: having attained the acceptance of the non-arising of phenomena and entered the noble stage, such a bodhisattva knows without doubt they will never regress; having truly realized unsurpassed awakening and its means — the six perfections and the bodhisattva's own factors of enlightenment — such a bodhisattva understands that their own person, from the eighth ground onward, draws close to the great fruit of buddhahood, with the tenth ground drawing equally close, nearly identical to that fruit itself. Having genuinely realized the emptiness of both self and phenomena at the eighth through tenth grounds, such a bodhisattva knows self and every other being to be fundamentally equal, and this wisdom naturally gives rise to great compassion capable of broadly liberating beings — what the passage calls gaining a great sense of self. Having thoroughly understood that the conventional truth posited through names and concepts is not truly existent, while the ultimate truth beyond language is not truly nonexistent, and that the two are neither identical nor entirely different, such a bodhisattva, unlike an ordinary being who remains caught in the deluded discrimination of affliction, karma, and suffering within samsara, and unlike a follower of the lesser vehicle who rushes to enter nirvana in this very life and clings to the extinction of the remainderless nirvana-realm, is instead able to fully realize nirvana without actually entering it, willingly choosing to remain within birth-and-death life after life to liberate beings without ever being swept along by its afflictions, karma, and suffering — what the passage calls gaining a vast, expansive intention. For this reason, such a bodhisattva's vow toward great awakening never regresses, and this is precisely what "certainty by arousing the aspiration for awakening" means, matching exactly what the Compendium of Establishments, fascicle 2, itself explains.

53. Milestone: Certainty by Practice Never in Vain — Ten Kinds of Mastery Attained From the Eighth Ground Onward

The third kind, certainty by practice that is never in vain, describes bodhisattvas from the eighth ground onward who have already attained genuine mastery, and who, in universally benefiting sentient beings, accomplish exactly what they intend in every action, their cultivation never going to waste. Such mastery, the treatise elsewhere explains in fascicle 48, comprises ten specific kinds of self-mastery: mastery over one's own lifespan, over the mind's entry into any meditative absorption at will, over material resources simply through mental resolve, over the manifestation of karmic results at any chosen time, over the realm of rebirth one takes on, over the fulfillment of every vow, over resolute understanding capable of transforming any object at will, over supernormal accomplishment through the five mundane superknowledges, over the skillful establishing of every teaching, and over knowing whatever object of knowledge one wishes to know.

Regarding certainty by practice that is never in vain — this means that bodhisattvas who have already attained mastery, in universally benefiting sentient beings through their conduct, accomplish exactly what they intend, and whatever they undertake and cultivate is never carried out in vain.

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates this third kind of certainty with the classical image of Avalokiteshvara answering a thousand simultaneous pleas in a thousand different places, and then walks through the treatise's own tenfold list of the mastery bodhisattvas from the eighth ground onward possess, drawn from fascicle 48. Mastery over lifespan lets a bodhisattva determine the length of their own life at will, living a hundred years or an entire eon as they choose, something no ordinary being can do. Mastery over mind allows entry into, dwelling within, and emergence from any meditative absorption or concentration entirely without obstruction. Mastery over resources lets a bodhisattva bring about any needed food or provision simply through mental resolve. Mastery over karma lets a bodhisattva manifest and undergo karmic results in accordance with whatever action is appropriate at whatever time, without any obstruction whatsoever.

Mastery over rebirth lets a bodhisattva take birth in whichever realm they vow to enter, free from the compulsion of contaminated karma that binds ordinary beings, taking birth entirely according to their own wish. Mastery over vows means that whereas an ordinary being's vows are difficult to fulfill and often remain empty wishes, a bodhisattva's every intended undertaking reaches its intended goal. Mastery over resolute understanding, also called mastery over supreme understanding, lets a bodhisattva transform earth into water, water into fire, and every circumstance according to their own resolved understanding. Mastery over spontaneous accomplishment, also called mastery through spiritual power, draws on the five mundane superknowledges besides the knowledge of the extinction of contamination — the divine eye, divine ear, the ability to move unobstructed through space, and knowledge of others' minds — allowing free and wondrous transformation throughout every buddha-land as wished. Mastery over the teaching lets a bodhisattva, having attained wisdom subsequent to realization, skillfully establish every manner of teaching in whatever form suits each audience, always according perfectly with correct principle across boundless, immeasurable approaches to the middle way. Mastery over wisdom lets a bodhisattva truly and fully know whatever object of knowledge they wish to know, manifesting the ten powers, four fearlessnesses, eighteen unshared qualities, thirty-two major marks, eighty minor marks, and complete, correct awakening itself. A bodhisattva with such thoroughly mastered wisdom and body and mind, he concludes, brings benefit to every being exactly as intended in every undertaking, cultivation never wasted — precisely what "practice never in vain" describes.

54. Milestone: The Eighth and Ninth Grounds Named by Their Ten Masteries — Closing All Three Kinds of Certainty in Full

The treatise's commentary clarifies that "practice never in vain" specifically names the ground of certainty (the eighth ground) and the ground of certain conduct (the ninth ground): the eighth-ground bodhisattva, having transcended the sufferings of the three realms and attained the acceptance of the non-arising of phenomena, is roused from any inclination toward premature nirvana by the Buddha's own encouragement, and thereby manifests boundless emanation-bodies possessing the full ten kinds of mastery just explained; the ninth-ground bodhisattva, having attained the four kinds of unobstructed understanding, brings purely wholesome benefit and ease to every being encountered. With this, the treatise concludes: among these three kinds of falling into certainty, it is specifically upon reaching the final one, certainty of practice never in vain, that the buddhas actually bestow upon a bodhisattva the formal prophecy of falling into certainty — closing the second category of the third summary verse in full.

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the ground of certainty as the eighth ground, also called the immovable ground, and the ground of certain conduct as the ninth ground, also called the ground of excellent wisdom — the two grounds jointly named by the commentary here as "falling into certainty." The ninth-ground bodhisattva, he continues, has attained the four kinds of unobstructed understanding and holds thoroughly pure intention toward benefiting and bringing ease to every being — which is why the treatise says such a bodhisattva "universally benefits beings." Taken together, he concludes, once a bodhisattva has attained the acceptance of the non-arising of phenomena and gained mastery without effort, exertion, or attachment to marks, arriving at this culminating mastery capable of universally benefiting beings through whatever vow and whatever cultivation is undertaken, never in vain, this constitutes the decisive achievement of practice. It is specifically at this final position among the three kinds of certainty — lineage, aspiration, and practice — that the buddhas formally bestow the prophecy of falling into certainty, since only at this point does a bodhisattva's own capacity to benefit beings become fully, irreversibly assured. With this, the second category of the third summary verse, falling into certainty, closes in full; the treatise turns next to its third category, five things a bodhisattva definitely must do.

55. Milestone: Five Things a Bodhisattva Definitely Must Do Opens — Arousing the Aspiration for Awakening, and Deep Compassion for Beings

The treatise now turns to the third category of the third summary verse: five things a bodhisattva definitely must do, without which unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening can never be attained. The first, most foundational of the five is simply arousing the aspiration for awakening itself — the guiding aspiration behind every bodhisattva action, since a bodhisattva does everything specifically to benefit all beings and to attain unsurpassed awakening. The second is generating deep, sorrowful compassion for all sentient beings — grieving over how beings, through ignorance, wander endlessly in the sea of birth-and-death — described elsewhere in the treatise as contemplating the one hundred and ten kinds of suffering pervading the realm of beings and cultivating the immeasurable of compassion in response.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains that a bodhisattva's very first arousal of the aspiration for awakening, though still conventional and not yet grounded in direct realization, is precisely what gives a bodhisattva's subsequent applied effort its sustaining power and direction — bodhicitta functions as a guiding vow that governs every later stage of practice, propelling continuous progress and making a bodhisattva capable of diligently cultivating the entire range of factors leading to awakening, exactly as the chapter on the lineage in fascicle 35 explains. Without this first arousal, he notes, none of the subsequent four requirements would have any foundation to stand on.

Turning to the second requirement, he cites the treatise's own fourfold explanation from fascicle 44 of why a bodhisattva's compassion is properly called "great" compassion: it arises by taking as its object the deepest, subtlest, and hardest-to-fathom suffering of beings; it has been accumulated and habituated over an immeasurable span of hundreds of thousands of eons; it arises through an especially intense, forceful engagement with its object, such that a bodhisattva sustained by this compassion would willingly give up hundreds of thousands of lives, let alone a single life or material wealth, in order to relieve beings' suffering, and can bear every manner of harsh treatment for the sake of beings; and it is utterly, supremely pure, reaching the ultimate purity proper to a bodhisattva, or, in the case of the tathagatas themselves, the ultimate purity of a buddha's own stage. He stresses the commentary's own citation of the Buddha's teaching that a bodhisattva's awakening is established upon compassion — without great compassion, wisdom alone, however profound, could never actually produce a buddha, since compassion and wisdom must mature together in full completion for buddhahood to be reached.

56. Milestone: Blazing Diligence, Skillful Cultivation of the Five Sciences, and Never Growing Weary — Closing All Five Requirements

The third requirement is blazing, vigorous diligence in practicing the bodhisattva path. The fourth is skillfully cultivating the five sciences — inner wisdom, medicine, logic, linguistics, and the arts and crafts — since without this range of both worldly and transcendent knowledge, a bodhisattva cannot genuinely benefit beings in practice. The fifth is never growing weary or fatigued, sustaining the six perfections and every bodhisattva practice tirelessly through both seeking awakening above and transforming beings below, never satisfied with partial progress but pressing on continuously from the first ground all the way to the tenth. With these five, the third category of the third summary verse, things a bodhisattva definitely must do, closes in full.

Chinese Source

三者、熾然精進，四者、於諸明處方便修習，五者、無有厭倦。

Ru-Yi Translation

Third, blazing diligence. Fourth, skillfully cultivating the sciences. Fifth, never growing weary.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi cites the treatise's own fascicle 48 to explain the three sources feeding a bodhisattva's blazing diligence: contemplating in meditation how every conditioned phenomenon is merely an illusory appearance arising and perishing through causes and conditions, with no truly existing being to be found within it, which produces a deep aversion toward and freedom from craving for such impermanent, empty, selfless, illusion-like activity, and this freedom from craving is itself freedom from heedlessness; delighting in the Buddha's own wisdom and, out of admiration for its excellence, urging oneself onward with vigorous energy to swiftly realize the true nature of all phenomena; and feeling compassion for beings' unceasing suffering, which gives rise to the vast, expansive intention to benefit and rescue every being without ever regressing. These three together, he notes, are what the source text calls blazing diligence.

The fourth requirement, he explains, names the five classical sciences — inner wisdom concerning liberation itself, medicine for healing the body, logic for correct reasoning, linguistics for mastering language, and the arts and crafts for practical skill — diligently cultivated with skillful means across both worldly and transcendent domains, since a bodhisattva lacking this breadth of knowledge could not actually put benefiting beings into practice. The fifth requirement, never growing weary, describes a bodhisattva who cultivates great compassion toward every suffering being and undertakes the entire range of practice proper to seeking awakening above and transforming beings below without ever tiring — never satisfied with attaining merely the first ground, but continuing diligently onward without pause all the way through the tenth; practicing giving, for instance, with a mind free of stinginess, unattached to wealth within or without, able to relinquish what is hardest to relinquish without any lingering regret. He closes by observing that a bodhisattva sustained by an intimate closeness to beings, a cherishing love for them, a wish to bestow kindness upon them, a willingness to bear suffering in their place, and a gentle, afflictionless, capable mind at ease with itself, can never grow weary of the work of benefiting and bringing ease to beings — and it is precisely these five requirements together, he notes, that a bodhisattva must definitely fulfill in order to attain buddhahood at all.

57. Milestone: Five Things a Bodhisattva Should Always Be Doing Opens — Freedom From Heedlessness, and Refuge for the Destitute

The treatise turns to the fourth category of the third summary verse: five things a bodhisattva should always be doing. The first is constantly guarding against heedlessness — since heedlessness, as the treatise elsewhere defines it, is simply distorted thinking, distorted deliberation, and distorted idle talk, and freedom from these three is what "freedom from heedlessness" actually means. The second is constantly serving as refuge and support for beings who have no refuge or protector, who suffer and are destitute — compassionately rescuing, protecting, and giving such beings something to rely on, so that they may escape their suffering and hardship.

Chinese Source

又諸菩薩於其五處常所應作。何等為五？一者、於不放逸常所應作；二者、無依無怙、有苦有貧諸有情所，常應為作依怙等事；

Ru-Yi Translation

Furthermore, bodhisattvas, in five places, should always be acting. What are these five? First, always acting to remain free of heedlessness. Second, always acting as refuge and support for beings who have no refuge or protector, and who suffer and are destitute.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi notes that this fourth category shifts from what a bodhisattva must accomplish once, at the outset, to what must be sustained continuously throughout the entire path — a subtle but important distinction from the "definitely must do" category just closed. He cites the Compendium on Mutual Association's own brief definition of heedlessness — in short, distorted thinking, distorted deliberation, and distorted idle talk, the same threefold structure already explained several milestones earlier in connection with conditions favoring regression — so that freedom from heedlessness here simply means staying continuously clear of all three, an ongoing vigilance rather than a single accomplishment.

The second requirement, he explains, describes compassion put directly into action: beings without any refuge or protector, suffering and destitute, are precisely those most in need of a bodhisattva's help, and a bodhisattva sustains this work of rescue, protection, and support as an unbroken, continuous practice rather than an occasional act of charity — always ready to serve as refuge whenever such beings are encountered, throughout the entire span of the path.

58. Milestone: Constant Offering to the Buddhas, and Constantly Examining Oneself for Fault Through Six Aspects of Mind

The third thing a bodhisattva should always be doing is constantly making offerings to the tathagatas, whether through material offerings or through the offering of practicing the teaching itself, sparing no day without at least a single bow of reverence. The fourth is constantly, thoroughly knowing whether one has fault or is free of fault — examining and reflecting on one's own conduct as a matter of course, as the wise of the world do, and as the noble ones rejoice to hear their own faults pointed out by others. The treatise's commentary specifies this self-examination through six aspects of mind to be checked while cultivating the precept of gathering wholesome teachings, and six areas to be checked while cultivating the precept of benefiting sentient beings.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi illustrates the third requirement by citing the treatise's own chapter on moral discipline, fascicle 41: a bodhisattva who abides in the pure precepts of a bodhisattva and lets even a single day and night pass without offering anything at all to a tathagata, a memorial built for a tathagata, the true teaching, or scriptures containing the true teaching — not even a single bow of respect, however small the offering — commits a transgression; if this happens through disrespect or laziness, it is a defiled transgression, underscoring how seriously the treatise treats this constant devotional practice.

For the fourth requirement, he unpacks the commentary's own two sets of six. Gathering wholesome teachings, he explains, itself has three sources: wholesome roots drawn out by one's own effort through hearing, reflecting, and cultivating; wholesome roots drawn out through others, by respecting elders, caring for juniors, and helping others resolve their difficulties, so that a bodhisattva should actually feel gratitude toward beings for the opportunity to cultivate such virtue; and wholesome roots drawn out through what is supreme, meaning constant offerings to the three jewels and constant cultivation of calm-abiding and insight. He maps these three sources onto the five things always to be done: self-drawn virtue corresponds to freedom from heedlessness and thoroughly knowing fault, other-drawn virtue corresponds to serving as refuge for the destitute, and supreme-drawn virtue corresponds to constant offering to the tathagatas. He then lists the six aspects of mind a bodhisattva must check to see whether the precept of gathering wholesome teachings is being kept well: a mind of contempt toward the teaching, indicating a lack of resolute faith in the three jewels' cause and effect; a mind accompanied by laziness, requiring swift confession and renewed diligence; a mind obscured by the five hindrances of craving, hostility, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt; a mind wearied by exertion, arising from excessive vigor just as laziness arises from insufficient vigor, both equally failing to sustain steady practice; a mind accompanied by illness, where bodily weakness saps the mind's capacity for practice; and a mind followed by hindrance, arising from indulging in idle talk of kings and thieves, delighting in worldly business, excessive sleep, or noisy company. Alongside these six for gathering wholesome teachings, he notes, the precept of benefiting sentient beings calls for examining six further areas — self, other, decline or flourishing of resources, and decline or flourishing of the teaching — checking specifically whether one's actions on behalf of beings cause both self and other to flourish in the teaching, in which case the action should be pursued, or cause both to decline, in which case it must never be undertaken, with the fuller account given in the Decisions section of the Bodhisattva Stage, fascicle 75.

With this fifth requirement, the treatise concludes: these five things are what bodhisattvas should always be doing, closing the fourth category of the third summary verse in full.

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this fifth requirement as the thread tying together everything the third summary verse has covered so far: whatever a bodhisattva does — walking or remaining still, engaged in any of the preceding four constant practices or any other undertaking — the great aspiration for awakening, inseparable from the great compassion of the unabiding great nirvana that neither settles into samsara nor withdraws into personal extinction, must always stand at the very forefront of the mind's application, guiding every action from beginning to end.

He notes that with this closing statement, the fourth category of the third summary verse — the five things always to be done, namely freedom from heedlessness, refuge for the destitute, constant offering to the buddhas, constant self-examination for fault, and holding bodhicitta as constant guide — reaches its own full conclusion, distinct from but closely related to the five things definitely to be done just covered two milestones earlier: the "definitely must do" category names what establishes a bodhisattva's practice at its foundation, while the "always should do" category names what must be sustained without interruption once that foundation is laid. The treatise turns next to the fifth and final category of the third summary verse, establishing ten supreme, culminating states that mark a bodhisattva as foremost among all beings.

60. Milestone: Ten Supreme States Opens — Bodhisattva Lineage, the First Arousal of Bodhicitta, Diligence and Wisdom, and Loving Speech

The treatise turns to the fifth and final category of the third summary verse: ten states every bodhisattva acknowledges as supreme, first among their kind, established at the very summit of excellence. The first is that the bodhisattva lineage is the most excellent among all lineages — surpassing the lineage fixed toward the disciple's path, the lineage fixed toward solitary awakening, the indeterminate lineage, and the lineage of no capacity whatsoever. The second is that the very first arousal of the aspiration for awakening is the most excellent of all correct vows — a vow the Avatamsaka Sutra itself calls too profound to fully describe, know, distinguish, believe and understand, realize, practice, penetrate, contemplate, measure, or enter. The third is that diligence and wisdom are the most excellent among all the perfections — diligence supplies the driving force to accomplish anything at all, while wisdom keeps the mind from clinging to any object, and without wisdom leading the first five perfections, their merit would remain merely worldly good fortune rather than boundless, transcendent virtue. The fourth is that loving speech is the most excellent among the four means of gathering beings — since it is specifically the beneficial variety of loving speech, expounding correct teaching and correct principle, that actually draws beings into the true teaching.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi treats this closing list as a kind of victory lap for the whole chapter — ten superlatives ranking the bodhisattva path's own qualities against every comparable alternative, so that a reader closing the chapter is left with a vivid sense of just how singular the bodhisattva's undertaking actually is. He explains the first item against the classical fivefold division of lineage — fixed toward the disciple's path, fixed toward solitary awakening, the bodhisattva's own lineage, indeterminate lineage capable of any of the three, and beings of no spiritual capacity at all (icchantikas) — with the bodhisattva lineage alone capable of leading to complete, unsurpassed buddhahood rather than a lesser liberation.

For the second item, he cites the Avatamsaka Sutra's seventeenth chapter, where the bodhisattva Dharmamati, asked how much merit the very first arousal of bodhicitta produces, replies that the meaning is too profound to be fully explained, known, distinguished, believed and understood, realized, practiced, penetrated, contemplated, measured, or entered — even a bodhisattva's very first, still-conventional arousal of the aspiration for awakening, difficult as it already is to accomplish, outranks every other kind of vow a being could make. For the third, he explains that diligence supplies the sheer energy needed to accomplish any of the six perfections at all, while wisdom keeps the mind free of attachment to whatever object is engaged — and specifically, unless wisdom leads the first five perfections, their cultivation collapses into ordinary worldly merit rather than truly boundless, transcendent virtue, which is why these two, rather than any other single perfection, are named supreme among all six. For the fourth, he distinguishes loving speech's three forms — comforting speech, joyfully encouraging speech, and beneficial speech — with the third, expounding correct teaching and principle, doing the actual work of drawing beings into the true teaching and toward eventual awakening, more effectively than giving, beneficial conduct, or working alongside beings, the other three means of gathering, can accomplish on their own.

61. Milestone: The Tathagata Supreme Among All Beings, Compassion Supreme Among the Immeasurables, and the Fourth Dhyana Supreme Among Meditative Absorptions

The fifth supreme state is that the tathagata, the world-honored one, is the most excellent among all sentient beings — perfect in merit and wisdom, endowed with the ten powers, four fearlessnesses, and eighteen unshared qualities, surpassing not only ordinary beings and followers of the lesser vehicle but every bodhisattva as well. The sixth is that compassion for beings is the most excellent among the four immeasurables — since it is specifically the wish to remove beings' suffering that most directly addresses what beings actually need, among loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity. The seventh is that the fourth dhyana is the most excellent among all the meditative absorptions — free of the three calamities and eight disturbances afflicting the lower stages, its concentration and wisdom held in perfect balance, the very basis from which the Buddha himself contemplated dependent origination and attained unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening.

Ru-Yi Translation

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the fifth item simply: the Buddha, having perfected both merit and wisdom and attained qualities shared with no ordinary being or follower of the lesser vehicle whatsoever, stands as the single most excellent being in the world, surpassing every bodhisattva still on the path toward that same attainment — the fruit toward which the entire chapter's account of bodhisattva qualities has, from the beginning, been oriented. The sixth item, he notes, singles out compassion from among the four immeasurables — loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity — because compassion alone directly aims at removing beings' suffering, the most urgent need any suffering being actually has, which is why it ranks as supreme among the four even though all four are cultivated together.

The seventh item, he explains, singles out the fourth dhyana from among the four levels of form-realm meditative absorption because it alone is free of the three calamities of water, fire, and wind that periodically destroy the lower three levels, and free as well of the eight disturbances — sorrow, pain, investigation, examination, joy, pleasure, inhalation, and exhalation — that still trouble those lower levels; its concentration and wisdom are held in perfect, balanced proportion, neither one overwhelming the other, which is precisely why the Buddha relied on the fourth dhyana as the basis from which to contemplate the twelve links of dependent origination and attain unsurpassed, correct, complete awakening. Whether cultivating the noble path or seeking supernormal powers, he notes, this balanced state of concentration and wisdom is the most excellent basis for either, which is why the fourth dhyana ranks supreme among all four levels of meditative absorption.

62. Milestone: Emptiness Supreme Among the Three Concentrations, Cessation Supreme Among Absorptions, and Pure Skillful Means Supreme of All — Closing the Third Summary Verse in Full

The eighth supreme state is that the concentration on emptiness is the most excellent among the three concentrations of emptiness, wishlessness, and markless-ness — since realizing emptiness is the root that makes the other two easy to accomplish, and the great vehicle's own concentration on emptiness penetrates the ultimate emptiness of all phenomena, not merely personal emptiness as in the lesser vehicle. The ninth is that the absorption of cessation is the most excellent among all meditative attainments — an uncontaminated absorption available only to noble ones who have attained the third fruit or beyond, in which the defiled activity of consciousness tied to self-view (and, for a bodhisattva, to both self-view and phenomena-view) comes entirely to rest, resembling nirvana itself in its abiding peace, and constituting a vast field of merit for whoever makes offerings to one who has just emerged from it. The tenth and final supreme state is that the pure skillful means already discussed earlier in the treatise are the most excellent among all skillful means whatsoever — specifically the sixth of the six kinds explained in fascicle 45, the Buddha's own display of the eight aspects of a buddha's life, through which disciples come to trust without doubt that an ordinary being can indeed cultivate the path to buddhahood. With this tenth state, all ten supreme states close in full, and with them, chapter eighteen's entire third summary verse reaches its own complete conclusion.

63. Milestone: The Fourth and Fifth Summary Verses Open Together — Nine Topics Spanning the Remainder of the Chapter

The treatise now takes up the fourth and fifth summary verses of the qualities chapter together as a single combined unit, since their prose explanation proceeds as one continuous treatment. 65. Milestone: Establishing Through Truths Opens — Boundless in Number, Beginning With the Single Truth and the Two Truths

The treatise turns to the second kind of establishing designations, establishing through truths, noting at the outset that the number of ways truth can be classified is genuinely boundless — the Buddha speaks of one truth on some occasions, two through ten on others, and countless further groupings besides. The first classification establishes a single truth: the meaning of non-falsity itself, referring to the suchness of all phenomena, admits of only one truth with no second, since this is a non-conceptually-established truth beyond all naming — the domain of pure, uncontaminated wisdom alone, known only through direct realization, never through language. The second classification establishes two truths: conventional truth, the meaning expressed through language, positing a self, a being, or a life span through mere convention, designation, and speech alone; and ultimate truth, the nature of phenomena beyond all language, the domain in which the wisdom of the noble ones actually operates.

68. Milestone: The Seven Truths — Craving's Savor, Its Danger, Renunciation, the Nature of Phenomena, Resolute Understanding, and the Noble Versus the Ignorant

The seventh classification establishes seven truths, once again reorganizing the same four noble truths from new angles and adding two further truths beyond language. The truth of craving's savor is origination, the defiling condition the Buddha designates to show how contaminated, conditioned phenomena stir up attachment and delusion in the mind. The truth of danger is suffering, the corresponding condition the Buddha designates to show how uncontaminated wisdom, seeing all phenomena as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, can perceive and surpass every fault and affliction. The truth of renunciation is cessation, the purification of conditioned phenomena achieved once the mind, joined with uncontaminated wisdom through calm-abiding and insight, escapes defiled suffering entirely. The truth of the nature of phenomena is the non-conceptually-established truth, the genuine realization of every phenomenon's own nature that non-discriminating wisdom attains once calm-abiding and insight are perfected. The truth of resolute understanding is the path, the unshakeable wisdom born from contemplating the true nature of phenomena within calm-abiding and insight, belonging specifically to the domain of the noble ones. The truth of the noble is cessation and the path together, held by those who have already seen the truth and whose contemplation of every phenomenon accords with reality without contradiction. And the truth of the ignorant is suffering and origination together, the domain of ordinary beings who take the provisionally-named phenomena of the world to be genuinely, unquestionably real.

Ignorant worldly people, whose awareness of these phenomena likewise shows no contradiction to their own view, are called the truth of the ignorant.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first three of the seven as simply origination, suffering, and cessation viewed from new angles: craving's savor names origination, since contaminated, conditioned phenomena are the defiling conditions that stir up a practitioner's attachment and delusion; danger names suffering, since uncontaminated, transcendent wisdom, contemplating all phenomena as impermanent, suffering, empty, and without self, can clearly see through and rise above every fault these conditions produce; and renunciation names cessation, since a mind joined with uncontaminated wisdom through sustained cultivation of calm-abiding and insight achieves the purification that escapes defiled suffering once and for all — resolute understanding, the fifth, is the cause of this renunciation, since the unshakeable confidence born from directly contemplating the true nature of phenomena within meditative practice is precisely what a noble one relies on to actually accomplish that escape.

The fourth truth, the nature of phenomena, he identifies as the non-conceptually-established truth already introduced twice before in this same sequence — the genuine realization, free of any confusion produced by mere verbal accounts of "nature," that non-discriminating wisdom attains once calm-abiding and insight together reach their full accomplishment. The sixth and seventh truths, he explains, contrast the noble against the ignorant directly: those who have already seen the truth realize the nature of phenomena — both the true nature purified of the afflictive obscuration and the true nature purified of the cognitive obscuration — without any contradiction between their realization and reality itself, while ordinary, unlearned worldly people, never doubting that the provisionally-designated phenomena of everyday language are simply, straightforwardly real, follow along with conventional appearances their entire lives without any such contradiction ever troubling them — not because their view is correct, but because they never examine it closely enough to notice the trouble. He notes that "the ignorant" here comprises what the tradition elsewhere calls worldly-conventional truth and reasoned-conventional truth together, both truths that remain entirely within the domain of language and convention.

69. Milestone: The Eight Truths — Suffering Unfolded Into Its Three Kinds, and the Fourfold Movement of Samsara and Its Purification

The eighth classification unfolds the four noble truths into eight by dividing suffering into its three classical kinds and adding a further fourfold structure of flow and reversal. The truth of the suffering of formations is neutral feeling, since all conditioned phenomena flow ceaselessly through the three times without a single instant of stable rest. The truth of the suffering of change is pleasant feeling, since whatever feels pleasant when it arises inevitably brings distress to body and mind when it passes away. The truth of suffering itself is painful feeling, the suffering beings undergo directly through hunger, illness, wind and rain, cold and heat, beating, and forced labor. The truth of flowing turns these three unended sufferings into ongoing samsaric rebirth across the three realms, belonging to the truth of suffering; the truth of returning cessation is the final, complete escape from the three realms into nirvana's peace, belonging to the truth of cessation; the truth of defilement names the three sufferings continuing to flow through the causal power of affliction, karma, and birth, belonging to suffering and origination together; the truth of purity names the same three sufferings brought to rest through the accomplishment of moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom, belonging to cessation and the path together; and the truth of correct effort names that same cultivation of the four foundations of mindfulness, the eightfold noble path, and the thirty-seven factors of awakening as the actual method by which defilement is removed and purity achieved, belonging to the path.

Based on these three kinds of suffering, flowing through the three realms is called the truth of flowing. Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first three of the eight as the classical three kinds of suffering unfolded directly from the truth of suffering: the suffering of formations, corresponding to neutral feeling, names the simple fact that every conditioned phenomenon flows ceaselessly across past, present, and future without a single moment of genuinely stable rest; the suffering of change, corresponding to pleasant feeling, names how any feeling pleasant in the moment of its arising invariably brings distress to body and mind the instant it passes away and changes into something else; and suffering itself, corresponding to painful feeling, names the direct, unmistakable suffering beings undergo through hunger, sickness, harsh weather, physical punishment, and forced toil. Since these three together already comprise the whole of the first noble truth, he notes, the commentary simply says "the first three may be understood as given" without further elaboration.

The remaining five, he explains, restate the four noble truths through a different structural lens — flow and its reversal. Flowing names the fact that, so long as these three sufferings remain unresolved, beings continue circulating endlessly through birth-and-death in the three realms, belonging to the truth of suffering; returning cessation names the final, complete escape from that same circulation into nirvana's peace, belonging to the truth of cessation. Defilement names these same three sufferings continuing to flow specifically because affliction, karma, and birth keep functioning as their causal conditions, belonging to both suffering and origination together; purity names the same three sufferings brought to rest specifically because moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom have been accomplished, belonging to both cessation and the path together. And correct effort, finally, names the actual cultivation of the four foundations of mindfulness, the eightfold noble path, and the entire thirty-seven factors of awakening as the concrete method by which defilement is removed and purity achieved — belonging to the truth of the path. He notes that this eightfold scheme, unlike several of the schemes already covered, deliberately foregrounds the dynamic, ongoing character of samsaric suffering and its purification rather than treating the four truths as four static categories.

70. Milestone: The Nine Truths — Craving for Existence and Craving for Non-Existence, and the Two Kinds of Nirvana

The ninth classification establishes nine truths by unfolding both suffering and origination further still. Impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self together comprise the four aspects of the truth of suffering, since neither within nor apart from the five aggregates can any fixed, singular, sovereign self ever actually be found. Craving for existence is the view holding body and mind to be permanent and clinging to the wish to keep enjoying pleasant, favorable experience forever, generating attachment that belongs to origination; craving for non-existence is the opposite view, holding that self and world alike dissolve entirely into nothingness at death, and, out of a longing to be rid of suffering once and for all, generating an equally afflicted attachment to that very annihilation, likewise belonging to origination. The method for severing both of these two kinds of craving is applied wisdom, also called the path, cultivating moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom to eliminate the ignorance underlying both extremes and attain the decisively superior path. And the two kinds of nirvana that method leads to are nirvana with remainder, the arhat's own liberation while a karmic body still remains, belonging to cessation, and nirvana without remainder, the final, complete cessation once that karmic body's own continuity finally ends, likewise belonging to cessation.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi identifies the first four of the nine as the classical four aspects of the truth of suffering already familiar from the Stage of Disciples' own account: impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and no-self, with different treatises drawing the line between emptiness and no-self slightly differently — some treating emptiness as the absence of self found even within the aggregates themselves, and no-self as the absence of self found apart from the aggregates, though the underlying point remains the same either way: no fixed, singular, sovereign self can be found anywhere, whether sought inside or outside the five aggregates, since the very definition of "self" as permanent, single, and autonomously governing simply finds nothing corresponding to it in reality.

He then explains craving for existence and craving for non-existence as the two extreme views underlying the truth of origination: craving for existence belongs to those holding the eternalist view that body and mind persist permanently, who perform meritorious deeds specifically to secure future rebirths filled with pleasant, desirable experience; craving for non-existence belongs to those holding the opposite, nihilist view that self and world both vanish into utter nothingness at death, indifferent to the workings of karma, who nonetheless perform meritorious or unshakeable deeds out of a longing to be rid of every suffering once and for all — an attachment to annihilation that is, ironically, still a form of craving, and therefore still belongs to origination rather than genuine liberation, exactly as detailed in the Sutra on the Supreme Approach to Discerning Dependent Origination. The seventh truth, the means of severing these, is applied wisdom — the path itself, cultivating moral discipline, concentration, and wisdom to eliminate the specific ignorance underlying both craving for existence and craving for non-existence and thereby attain what the commentary calls the decisively superior path. The eighth and ninth truths, he concludes, simply distinguish the two familiar stages of nirvana — with the karmic residue of a still-continuing body, and without it, once that body's own continuity has finally ended — both belonging, in different ways, to the truth of cessation.

71. Milestone: The Ten Truths Mapped Onto the Twelve Links of Dependent Origination, Closing Establishing Through Truths — and Establishing Through Principles Briefly Opens

The tenth and final numbered classification establishes ten truths, mapped by the commentary onto the twelve links of dependent origination unfolding across their earlier, middle, and later phases, seven inclining toward continued samsaric flow and three toward its final purification. The truth of compelling suffering names the four sufferings of birth, old age, death, and meeting what is hated, each cutting like a blade; the truth of the suffering of scarce wealth and standing names the suffering of not obtaining what is sought; the truth of the suffering of bodily imbalance names the sickness arising when the four elements fall out of harmony; the truth of the suffering of the beloved's decay names the suffering of parting from what is loved or having what is loved turn unlovely; and the truth of the suffering of coarse, latent tendency names the very seeds of affliction underlying the suffering inherent in the five clung-to aggregates — these first five, all belonging to the path of suffering, are together called the truth of suffering. The truth of karma and the truth of affliction, belonging to origination, name respectively the accumulating karmic causes and the afflictions of craving and views that drive them. The truth of hearing the true teaching and correctly reflecting upon it, belonging to the path, names the wholesome roots gained by drawing near a genuine spiritual friend and reflecting rightly on what is heard. The truth of correct view, also belonging to the path, names the first uncontaminated right view of the eightfold noble path together with the wisdom of personal emptiness that serves as the very basis for entering the noble path. And the truth of the result of correct view, belonging to cessation, names the final, complete fruit that correct view leads to — whether the fourth fruit of arhatship, solitary awakening, or unsurpassed awakening itself — the last and most complete fruition correct view can produce. With this tenth truth, the treatise concludes that these ten kinds of establishing truths do not exhaust the whole; a more thorough analysis would reveal boundlessly many more — closing establishing through truths, the second of the four kinds of establishing designations, in full. The third kind, establishing through principles, is then explained simply by reference back to the four kinds of reasoning already given at length in the Stage of Disciples.

Reading note — after Master Cháng Bǎi

Master Cháng Bǎi explains the commentary's own framing device for this final, tenfold scheme: the twelve links of dependent origination are divided into an earlier phase (ignorance conditioning volitional formations, volitional formations conditioning consciousness), a middle phase presently unfolding (consciousness conditioning name-and-form, name-and-form conditioning the six sense-bases, the six sense-bases conditioning contact, contact conditioning feeling), and a later phase branching in two directions from that middle phase — toward continued samsaric flow, when a being who has not yet encountered the three jewels lets contact condition feeling, feeling condition craving, and craving condition grasping entirely under the sway of ignorance, remaining caught in the same afflicted circulation as before; or toward purification, when a being who has come to trust the three jewels instead lets every contact between the six senses and their objects accord with wisdom rather than ignorance, gradually purifying body and mind and entering the complete, genuine accomplishment of the noble path. The first seven truths, belonging to the flow-ward direction, and the last three, belonging to the purification-ward direction, together generate this tenfold scheme.

He walks through the first five as simply the truth of suffering subdivided by its specific circumstance: compelling suffering as the four sharpest sufferings of birth, aging, death, and meeting what is hated; scarce wealth and standing as the suffering of desired things never obtained; bodily imbalance as the sickness following from the four elements falling out of harmony; the beloved's decay as the suffering of separation from what is cherished or its turning into something no longer cherished; and coarse, latent tendency as the very afflictive seeds underlying the suffering inherent in the five clung-to aggregates themselves — together comprising the truth of suffering in its fullest detail. Karma and affliction, he continues, simply restate origination as, respectively, the accumulated karmic causes and the underlying craving and views that generate them. Hearing the true teaching and correctly reflecting names the wholesome roots gained through approaching a genuine spiritual friend and reflecting properly on what is heard — the wisdom born of hearing and reflecting, both preliminary to formal meditative cultivation. Correct view names the very first uncontaminated right view within the eightfold noble path, joined with the wisdom realizing personal emptiness, serving as the essential foothold for entering the noble path at all. And the result of correct view names whatever final fruition correct view eventually produces — the fourth fruit for a disciple, solitary awakening for one following that path, or unsurpassed awakening itself for a bodhisattva — the last and most complete fruit correct view can yield. He closes by noting that the treatise's own concluding remark, that ten classifications by no means exhaust the whole, should be taken seriously: countless further groupings of truth remain possible beyond the ten just given, and with this remark, establishing through truths — the second of the four kinds of establishing designations — closes in full; establishing through principles, the third kind, is dispatched briefly since the four kinds of reasoning it concerns have already been explained at length in the Stage of Disciples, fascicle 25, with no need for further repetition here.
