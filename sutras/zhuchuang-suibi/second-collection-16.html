Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Second Collection, Part 16

Continuing the Second Collection (二筆), nearing its close. This section moves from Zhuhong's own self-critical comparison to a weeping Song master, through a defense of a superstitious-sounding tree inscription, a friend's telling fear of a haunted meditation cave, a goose's accidental reputation for asceticism, and a Tang emperor's austere refusal to celebrate his own birthday.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

133. 惜寸陰 — Treasuring Every Inch of Shadow

Zhuhong measures his own morning resolve against a weeping Song master's nightly self-reproach, and finds himself falling honestly short.

Chinese Source

古謂：「大禹聖人，乃惜寸陰，至於眾人，當惜分陰。」而佛言人命在於呼吸。夫分陰之中，有多呼吸，則我輩何止當惜分陰，一剎那一彈指之陰，皆不可不惜也。昔伊庵權禪師，至晚必流涕曰：「今日又只恁麼空過，未知來日工夫何如？」其勵精若此。予見晨朝日出，則憶伊庵此語，曰：「今又換一日矣！昨日已成空過，未知今日工夫何如？」然予但歎息，未嘗流涕，以是知為道之心不及古人遠甚，可不愧乎！可不勉乎！

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients said: "Great Yu, a sage, treasured even a single inch of shadow on the sundial; ordinary people ought at least to treasure a tenth of that." And the Buddha said that human life exists only within the space of a single breath. Within even a tenth of an inch of shadow, many breaths pass — so we ought not merely treasure a tenth of an inch, but even a single instant, a single snap of the fingers, must not go untreasured.

Long ago, Chan Master Yi'an Quan would weep every evening, saying: "Today has again passed by empty and wasted — I do not know what tomorrow's effort will bring." Such was the intensity of his own self-discipline. Whenever I see the morning sun rise, I remember Yi'an's words, and say to myself: "Now another day has begun! Yesterday has already passed by empty — I do not know what today's effort will bring." And yet I only sigh, and have never once wept — from this I know that my own resolve for the Way falls far, far short of the ancients'. How can I not feel ashamed! How can I not strive all the harder!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The opening maxim about treasuring even a tenth of Yu the Great's own careful attention to time is traditionally traced to the Jin-dynasty general Tao Kan, who used it to spur his own subordinates to diligence; Yi'an Quan was a genuine Song Chan master whose own decisive awakening — while serving porridge in the meditation hall, so absorbed in his own koan that he forgot to hold out his bowl until a neighboring monk's touch startled him into sudden realization — makes his nightly self-reproach here the record of a man who eventually did break through, adding real weight to Zhuhong's own comparatively modest self-assessment.

134. 萬年寺 — Wannian Monastery

A defense of a seemingly superstitious warning carved into stone at a mountain monastery, arguing that even a law known to be imperfectly enforceable is still worth establishing — a principle Zhuhong traces back to the Buddha's own inability to prevent Devadatta's schism.

Chinese Source

萬年寺當天台萬山之中，殿前古樹十餘，一字橫亙，行列整而枝葉茂，鬱然為山門美觀。有刻石記之者曰：「此上仙所植也，有伐之者，其人立死。」或云：「為此記者其愚乎哉！他時後日，能保有力者不負之而趨乎？則奚以記為？」予以為不然。夫興之必有廢也，古人非不知也，法不得不如是立也。後人信斯記而戢其邪心，與不信而造業，自屬彼人，立法者無心焉，任之而已矣。破和合僧者墮無間，佛記也；佛未入滅，而調達誘祇園之僧若干以去，佛不能制調達之負而趨，然則佛愚乎哉？

Ru-Yi Translation

Wannian Monastery lies deep among the ten thousand peaks of Mount Tiantai; before its main hall stand more than ten ancient trees, arranged in a single line, their ranks orderly and their branches and leaves luxuriant, forming a magnificent, imposing sight at the monastery gate. A stone inscription there records: "These were planted by an immortal from above — whoever cuts one down will die on the spot."

Someone said: "Whoever wrote this inscription must have been a fool! In some future day, could it possibly guarantee that some powerful man would not simply ignore it and cut them down anyway? What, then, was the point of writing it at all?" I would say this criticism is mistaken. That whatever flourishes must eventually decline is something the ancients certainly knew perfectly well — but the law had to be established regardless. Whether later generations, believing this inscription, restrain their own destructive impulses, or disbelieve it and commit the offense anyway, this belongs entirely to each individual's own conduct — the one who established the law has no further control over it, and simply leaves each person to his own fate. That one who destroys the harmony of the sangha falls into the Avīci hell is the Buddha's own teaching — and yet even before the Buddha himself entered nirvana, Devadatta lured away a number of monks from the Jetavana community, and the Buddha could not prevent Devadatta's own disregard for this teaching. Does this mean the Buddha himself was a fool?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wannian Monastery is a genuine, historically documented Tiantai temple with a long history of destruction and rebuilding under successive names; Devadatta's schism, in which he lured five hundred monks away from the Buddha's own community with a stricter ascetic program before eventually being reconciled or condemned depending on the source tradition, was already available to Zhuhong as the standard scriptural precedent for exactly the argument he makes here — that a teacher's authority to state a law does not include the power to guarantee universal compliance with it.

135. 富貴留戀人 — Wealth and Rank Hold People Fast

A talented, devout friend's telling fear of a haunted meditation cave becomes evidence that even genuinely accomplished people remain quietly attached to their own worldly comforts, unwilling to risk "waking" from them.

Chinese Source

僧之高行者，平日自分不以富貴染心，然能持之見生，未必不失之他世。一友人以文章魁海內，直史館，聲名藉甚，偶遊天目，謂予言：「此山中石室有僧坐逝，其故身猶存，予欲禮覲，輒心怖不敢。」予問故。答曰：「昔有人禮石室僧者，纔拜下，即仆地隕絕，而龕內僧方欠伸從定起。予慮或然，是以不敢。」因與予相視大笑。此公弘才碩德，智鑑精朗，又雅意佛乘，尚愛著其一時富貴，守在夢之身，惟恐其醒，他又何言乎？田舍翁五畝之宅，寒令史抱關擊柝之官，窮和尚三二十家信心供養之檀越，已眷眷不能捨，死猶攜之識田，況復掇巍科、居要地、占斷世間榮耀者，亦奚怪其愛著也。富貴之留戀人，雖賢智者未免。吁！可畏哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Even a monk of genuinely lofty conduct, who in ordinary times believes his own mind untouched by wealth and rank, may well hold to this in the present life without necessarily being able to hold to it in some future life. A friend of mine, whose literary talent had made him the foremost writer in the land, holding a senior post at the Historiography Institute with a towering reputation, once happened to visit Mount Tianmu, and said to me: "There is a stone chamber in this mountain where a monk passed away seated in meditation, and his body remains preserved there still — I wished to pay my respects, but felt suddenly afraid, and dared not go." I asked him why. He answered: "I heard that once, someone went to pay homage to the monk in that stone chamber, and the very moment he bowed down, he collapsed and died on the spot — and at that same instant, the monk within the chamber stirred, yawned, and rose from his meditative absorption. I feared this might happen to me too, and so I dared not go." And with that, we looked at each other and burst out laughing together.

This gentleman, of broad talent and profound virtue, of keen and penetrating discernment, and moreover a man who had always taken a genuine interest in the Buddha-dharma, still clung to his own present wealth and rank, guarding this dream-like body of his, fearing nothing so much as waking from that dream — what, then, is there left to say of anyone else? An old farmer with his own five-acre plot, a lowly clerk guarding a gate and striking the night watch, a poor monk with only twenty or thirty devoted households supporting him — all of these already cling so fondly to what little they have that they carry the attachment with them into the very storehouse-consciousness at death; how much more so, then, for those who have achieved the highest examination honors, occupy positions of real power, and possess every worldly glory the world has to offer — is it any wonder they cling so fondly to it all? Wealth and rank hold people fast — even the worthy and wise cannot always escape it. Alas! How truly fearsome this is!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry is one of the few in the collection that turns on Zhuhong's own account of a private, personal exchange with a named-only-by-implication friend, and its emotional turn is unusually gentle — the shared laughter over the ghost story gives way, without any accusation, to Zhuhong's own quiet observation of what that fear actually revealed; the closing catalogue of ordinary people clinging to modest possessions (a farmer's small plot, a night watchman's post, a poor monk's handful of donors) mirrors the accumulating structure Zhuhong uses elsewhere in the collection, such as the "King-and-Retinue Flower" entry's escalating natural comparisons (entry 46 above).

136. 鵝道人 — The Goose, "Practitioner of the Way"

A folk nickname for the goose, earned simply by its vegetarian diet, becomes an occasion for real grief at how casually people slaughter the very animal locals half-jokingly honor as a fellow practitioner.

Chinese Source

山中老氓呼鵝曰「鵝道人」，問之，則曰：「鴨之入田也，蟥螟蟊蚓等吞噉無孑遺，故鴨所遊行號大軍過。雞之在地也，蜈蚣之毒惡，蟋蟀之跳梁，無能逃其喙者。而鵝惟噬生草與糠秕耳，齋食不腥，是名道人。」予聞而汪然大戚焉！夫雞鴨戕物，人戕雞鴨，報施似適其平，曷為乎烹鵝而食其肉也？鵝受道人之稱，人甘猛虎之行，吁乎傷哉！雖然，鵝不食腥，類騶虞之不殺，非師友訓之，其性然也。性也者，宿習之使也。故學道人不可不慎其習。

Ru-Yi Translation

The old villagers in the mountains call the goose "the Goose Practitioner of the Way." I asked them why, and they explained: "When ducks enter a rice paddy, every leech, rice-borer, root-worm, and earthworm within it is devoured entirely, leaving nothing behind — so wherever a flock of ducks passes, it is called 'the great army passing through.' When chickens walk the ground, no venomous centipede or leaping cricket can escape their beaks. But the goose eats only fresh grass and grain husks — its diet contains no meat at all, and so it is called a 'practitioner of the Way.'"

Hearing this, I wept freely, overcome with sorrow! Chickens and ducks kill small creatures, and people in turn kill chickens and ducks — this retribution seems, in its own way, evenly balanced. But why, then, do people also slaughter and eat the flesh of the goose? The goose has earned the title of "practitioner of the Way," and yet people behave like ravenous tigers toward every living creature regardless — alas, how truly heartbreaking! And yet, that the goose eats no meat, resembling the legendary zouyu beast that harms nothing living, is not something taught to it by any master or friend — it is simply its own nature. And nature itself is shaped by long habit accumulated over time. This is exactly why a student of the Way must be careful indeed about what habits he cultivates.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The zouyu is a legendary righteous beast from the Classic of Poetry, described since antiquity as refusing to kill any living creature and appearing only in an age of true virtue — Zhuhong's comparison elevates the goose's simple dietary fact into the register of a genuine moral exemplar, while his closing point (that even an animal's virtue is the product of accumulated habit, not innate moral choice) redirects the whole anecdote back toward his own recurring theme of habituation shaping character, already explored at length in entry 89's survey of passing fads above.

137. 生日 — Birthday

Zhuhong contrasts an austere Tang emperor's refusal to celebrate his own birthday with a deeper form of birthday observance still — not mere merit-making, but direct contemplation of one's own original face from before birth.

Chinese Source

世人生日，設宴會，張音樂，繪圖畫，競辭賦，以之為樂，唐文皇獨不為，可謂超越常情矣。或曰：「是日也，不為樂而誦經禮懺，修諸福事，則何如？」曰：「誠善矣！欲報父母劬勞生育之恩，及滅己躬平生所作之業，於此宜盡心焉。然末也，非本也。先德有言：『父母未生前，誰是汝本來面目？』是日也，有能不為樂而正念觀察未生前之面目者乎？若於此廓爾洞明，則不但報此身之父母，而累劫之親恩無不報；不但滅現生之業，而多生之夙障罔弗滅矣。罷人世之樂，得涅槃之樂，孝矣哉若人乎！偉矣哉若人乎！」

Ru-Yi Translation

When ordinary people celebrate their own birthday, they hold banquets, play music, commission paintings, and compete in composing verse, taking all this as a form of celebration. Emperor Wenzong of Tang alone refused to do so — a truly extraordinary departure from common custom.

Someone asked: "On this day, instead of celebrating with pleasure, would it not be better to recite sutras, perform repentance, and cultivate various forms of merit?" I answered: "This is certainly good! To repay one's parents' toil in giving one birth, and to dissolve the karma one has accumulated over one's own lifetime — these are things one ought to devote real effort to on this day. And yet this remains only a secondary matter, not the root of the thing itself. A teacher of old once asked: 'Before your parents ever gave birth to you, who, then, was your own original face?' On one's birthday, is there anyone who can set aside celebration entirely, and instead turn right mindfulness toward contemplating that original face from before birth? If one could achieve total, penetrating clarity on this very point, one would not merely repay the debt owed to one's parents in this single life, but repay the debt owed to one's parents across countless past lives as well; one would not merely dissolve the karma of this present life, but dissolve the accumulated hindrances of many lifetimes besides. To set aside the false pleasures of this world, and attain instead the true bliss of nirvana — such a person is a truly filial child indeed! Such a person is truly a great figure in this world!"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Emperor Wenzong of Tang genuinely refused elaborate birthday celebrations, restricting his own official birthday observance to modest vegetarian fare and forbidding animal slaughter — a documented act of imperial austerity, though the historical record also remembers his reign for the catastrophic "Sweet Dew Incident," in which his own plot to eliminate eunuch power collapsed into the mass execution of over a thousand court officials, leaving him effectively a prisoner of the eunuchs for the rest of his life; Zhuhong cites only the birthday austerity, not the larger and more troubled reign, in keeping with this collection's consistent practice of drawing a single, precise virtue from a historical figure without needing to endorse his entire record.

138. 因病食肉 — Eating Meat Because of Illness

A practical, unsentimental argument against abandoning a vegetarian diet during illness under pressure from well-meaning relatives, while still allowing a narrow, precisely bounded exception for those under a superior's direct authority.

Chinese Source

有受佛戒，斷肉食，而忽罹病緣，為親友所強勸，已而遇俗醫又慫恿之，至有久茹齋者，一旦破毀。不思肉之力僅能肥身，不能延命，智者已必不為。又況膏粱子弟，或臞瘠如餒人；而蔾藿田夫，或充腴若富賈，則肥身且未，保如命何？菜食而病，教以食肉，肉食而病，復令何食？在病者以理自持而已。若其位處卑幼，上有尊人，勢分所臨，不可違逆者，食三淨肉可也；殺生而食，不可也。

Ru-Yi Translation

There are those who, having received the Buddhist precepts and given up meat entirely, suddenly fall ill, and are then strongly urged by relatives and friends to eat meat again — and further encouraged by some ordinary physician besides — until even those who have kept a vegetarian diet for many years abandon it all in a single day. Have they never considered that meat's only real effect is to fatten the body, not to extend one's life — something a genuinely wise person would never do regardless? And consider further: even among the children of the wealthiest, most indulgent households, some remain gaunt and undernourished as though starving; while among farmers who eat nothing but the plainest food, some grow as plump and healthy as any rich merchant. If meat cannot even reliably fatten the body, how could it possibly preserve one's life?

If a vegetarian falls ill, should he then be told to eat meat — and if a meat-eater falls ill, what, then, should he be told to eat instead? This matter rests entirely on the patient's own firm adherence to principle. But if one occupies a lowly or junior position, with elders and superiors above who insist and whose authority cannot be defied, then eating the "three kinds of pure meat" may be permitted — but killing an animal specifically in order to eat it is never permitted.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The "three kinds of pure meat" (三淨肉) is a genuine, specific category from the early Vinaya rules, permitting meat only when the animal was not seen being killed, not heard being killed, and not killed specifically for the eater — a Hīnayāna-era accommodation that Mahāyāna scriptures like the Nirvana Sutra explicitly tightened, treating it as an intermediate concession on the way to full abstention rather than a permanent allowance; Zhuhong's own narrow exception here (permitted only under direct familial authority, never through personal preference) reflects that same Mahāyāna tightening.
