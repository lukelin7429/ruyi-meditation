Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 8

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). This section resolves a rivalry between Chan and Pure Land practitioners, defends the Platform Sutra against a shallow misreading used to dismiss the Pure Land, weighs mountain retreat against active seeking, distinguishes ultimate buddha-nature from present differences between species, and closes with a gentle but pointed correction of the martyr-statesman Wen Tianxiang's own line on reincarnation.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

32. 禪宗淨土遲速 — The Relative Speed of Chan and Pure Land

A resolution to a real rivalry between two monks — one claiming Chan investigation always outpaces Buddha-recitation toward awakening — arguing that speed depends on individual faculty and diligence, not on the method chosen.

Chinese Source

一僧專修念佛法門，一僧以禪自負，謂念佛者曰：「汝念佛必待生西方已，見阿彌陀佛，然後得悟；我參禪者，見生便得悟去。遲速較然矣，汝罷念而參可也。」僧莫能決，舉以問予。予曰：「根有利鈍，力有勤惰，存乎其人，則彼此互為遲速，未可是此而非彼也。喻如二人同趨寶所，一人乘馬，一人乘船，同日起程，而到之遲速，未可定也；則利鈍勤惰之說也，參禪念佛亦復如是。語其遲，念佛人有累劫蓮花始開，參禪人亦有多生勤苦不能見性者矣！語其速，參禪人有當下了悟，不歷僧祇獲法身；念佛人亦有見生打徹，臨終上上品生者矣！古云：『如人涉遠，以到為期，不取途中強分難易。』」

Ru-Yi Translation

One monk devoted himself exclusively to Buddha-recitation; another, priding himself on Chan practice, said to the Buddha-reciting monk: "You must wait until you are reborn in the Western Pure Land and behold Amitābha in person before you can attain awakening; but we who practice Chan attain awakening within this very life. The difference in speed is obvious enough — you should abandon recitation and take up Chan investigation instead."

The monk could not resolve this himself, and brought the question to me. I said: "Faculties differ in sharpness, and effort differs in diligence, depending entirely on the individual — each path has its own faster and slower cases, and one cannot simply declare one method right and the other wrong. It is like two people setting out on the same day toward a place of treasure, one by horse and one by boat — which will arrive first cannot be determined in advance; it depends on sharpness and diligence, and the same holds true for both Chan investigation and Buddha-recitation. Speaking of slowness: there are those who recite the Buddha's name for countless kalpas before their lotus finally opens, and there are those who investigate Chan for many lifetimes of hard effort without ever seeing their own nature. Speaking of speed: there are those who investigate Chan and awaken instantly, attaining the Dharma-body without passing through countless kalpas at all; and there are those who recite the Buddha's name and achieve complete, thorough realization within this very life, attaining the highest of the highest grade of rebirth at the moment of death. The ancients said: 'As with a person traveling a great distance, arrival itself is the only measure — one should not insist on ranking the difficulty of the road along the way.'"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry pairs naturally with the collection's own earlier entry on the Chan-versus-Pure-Land priority question (entry 20 of the Second Collection, "得悟人正宜往生淨土") — there, Zhuhong argued that genuine Chan awakening actually strengthens rather than diminishes the desire for rebirth in the Pure Land; here, facing the reverse claim that Chan is simply faster, he declines to rank the paths at all, treating individual capacity rather than method as the real variable.

33. 六祖壇經 — The Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch

A defense of the Pure Land teaching against a shallow reading of the Platform Sutra's own line about the ten wholesome deeds, arguing that those deeds alone lead only to heavenly rebirth, not to seeing the Buddha — so the Sixth Patriarch cannot have meant to reject the Pure Land teaching by it.

Chinese Source

六祖示不識字，一生靡事筆研，《壇經》皆他人記錄，故多訛誤。其十萬八千、東方西方等說久已辯明。中又云：「但修十善，何須更願往生？」夫十善，生天之因也，無佛出世，輪王乃以十善化度眾生；六祖不教人生西方見佛，而但使生天可乎？其不足信，明矣！故知執《壇經》而非淨土者，謬之甚者也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The Sixth Patriarch himself showed that he could not read or write, and never once composed anything with his own brush his entire life — the Platform Sutra itself was entirely recorded by others, and so naturally contains many errors. Its claims about "a hundred and eight thousand" and "east" and "west" have already long been clarified elsewhere. But it also says: "Simply cultivate the ten wholesome deeds — what need is there to further vow rebirth in the Pure Land?" Yet the ten wholesome deeds are only the cause for rebirth in the heavens; even before any buddha had appeared in the world, a wheel-turning king could already guide sentient beings through the ten wholesome deeds alone. Could the Sixth Patriarch really have meant to teach people not to seek rebirth in the West to behold the Buddha, but only to seek rebirth in heaven instead? This claim is clearly unreliable! From this we know that whoever clings to the Platform Sutra's own words to reject the Pure Land has grievously misunderstood it.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The Platform Sutra's own transmission history is genuinely complicated — recorded by the disciple Fahai rather than written by Huineng himself, it circulated in several distinct versions across the centuries, with the currently standard edition dating only from Yuan-dynasty editorial work by Zongbao; Zhuhong's willingness to treat specific lines within it as transcription errors, rather than defending every word as literally the Patriarch's own, reflects the same textually careful, non-dogmatic approach he brings to other classical sources throughout this collection.

34. 居山 — Dwelling in the Mountains

A reconciliation of two seemingly opposed pieces of traditional wisdom about retreat — one praising mountain seclusion, another warning it can make a student forget the Way — by placing active seeking before, and seclusion only after, genuine realization.

Chinese Source

古云：「大隱居廛，小隱居山。」遂有甘心汨沒於塵俗者。不知居廛者，混俗和光，鬧中得靜，有道之士則然，非初心所宜也。或曰：「永嘉謂：『未得道而先居山，但見其山，必忘其道。』是不許居山也。」此各有說。予讚居山，為汨沒於塵俗者誡也；而永嘉所言自是正理。出家兒大事未明，千里萬里尋師訪道，親近知識，朝參暮請，豈得蒙昧無知作守山鬼乎？故知行腳在前，居山在後可也；則亦不悖乎永嘉之言也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The ancients said: "The great recluse dwells in the marketplace; the lesser recluse dwells in the mountains." And so there have been those willing to bury themselves contentedly amid worldly dust. But they fail to realize: dwelling in the marketplace while blending harmoniously with the crowd, finding stillness amid the noise, is something only a genuinely accomplished practitioner can do — it is not suitable for a beginner at all.

Someone might say: "Yongjia held that 'to dwell in the mountains before attaining the Way means seeing only the mountain itself, and inevitably forgetting the Way' — does this not mean he opposed mountain retreat entirely?" Each view has its own point. My own praise for mountain dwelling is meant as a warning to those already sunk in worldly dust and desire; and Yongjia's own statement is likewise entirely sound reasoning. A monk whose own great matter remains unclear must travel a thousand, ten thousand li to seek out a teacher and the Way, drawing close to a genuine spiritual guide, questioning him morning and night — how could he possibly remain ignorant and unaware, playing the role of a mere mountain-guarding ghost instead? This is why traveling to seek instruction must come first, and dwelling in the mountains only afterward — and this, too, does not contradict Yongjia's own words at all.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry reprises the same argument already developed at length in this collection's entry 11 above ("年少閉關"), which likewise insisted that retreat belongs properly after, not before, genuine realization; here Zhuhong extends that same structure to reconcile two authoritative but seemingly conflicting sayings, rather than simply repeating the point against premature retreat on its own.

35. 佛性 — Buddha-Nature

A careful reconciliation of the Buddhist teaching that all beings share buddha-nature with Mencius's own insistence that human and animal natures differ — arguing the two statements operate on entirely different levels and do not actually conflict.

Chinese Source

經言：「蠢動含靈皆有佛性。」孟子之闢告子也，曰：「然則犬之性猶牛之性，牛之性猶人之性歟？」有執經言而非孟子，予以為不然。皆有佛性者，出世盡理之言；人畜不同者，世間見在之論，兩不相礙。是故極本窮源，則螻蟻蠛蠓，直下與三世諸佛平等不二；據今見在，則人通萬變，畜惟一知，何容並視。豈惟人與畜殊，彼犬以司夜，有警則吠；若夫牛，即發扃鑽穴、踰牆斬關，且安然如不聞見矣。犬牛之性果不齊也，而況於人乎？萬材同一木也，而梧、檟、枳、棘自殊；百川同一水也，而江、湖、溝、渠各別。同而未嘗不異，異而未嘗不同者也。如執而不通，則世尊成正覺時，普見一切眾生成正覺，今日何以尚有眾生？

Ru-Yi Translation

A sutra says: "Even the crawling and the sentient all possess buddha-nature." When Mencius refuted Gaozi, he asked: "Would this mean the nature of a dog is the same as the nature of an ox, and the nature of an ox the same as the nature of a man?" Some have cited the sutra's own words to criticize Mencius for this — I believe this is mistaken.

"All possess buddha-nature" is a statement of ultimate truth transcending this world; that man and beast differ is a statement about worldly, present reality — the two do not actually conflict with one another at all. If one traces things back to their ultimate root, then even the smallest ant or gnat is, at that very level, equal and undivided from the buddhas of all three times; but observed within present, worldly reality, human beings comprehend the ten thousand transformations of things while beasts possess only the barest sensory awareness — how could these possibly be regarded as equivalent?

And it is not only that humans and beasts differ: a dog set to guard the night will bark the instant it senses any disturbance; but a cow, even if someone were to force open the gate, drill through the wall, climb over it, or break down the door, would remain perfectly undisturbed, as though seeing and hearing nothing at all. The natures of dog and cow are clearly not identical — how much less so, then, between different human beings! All timber comes from a single source, trees, and yet the parasol tree, the catalpa, the trifoliate orange, and the thorn bush each differ in their own quality; the hundred rivers are all made of the same water, and yet rivers, lakes, ditches, and canals each differ in their own character. What is the same is never without its own difference, and what differs is never entirely without some underlying sameness. If one clings rigidly to one side without allowing for the other, then at the very moment the World-Honored One attained perfect enlightenment, he would have seen every sentient being attain enlightenment together with him at that same instant — why, then, would there still be sentient beings left in this world today at all?

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Mencius's own exchange with Gaozi on the nature of dogs, oxen, and men is a genuine and famous passage from the Mencius itself, part of his broader argument that human nature is distinctively good rather than a blank slate shared equally with animals — Zhuhong's resolution here, distinguishing ultimate ontological equality from present functional difference, mirrors the same "two levels, not one contradiction" structure he uses elsewhere in this collection to reconcile Confucian cosmology with Buddhist doctrine (compare the Second Collection's entry 118 on yin, yang, and the Supreme Ultimate).

36. 僧畜僮僕 — Monks Keeping Servants

A pointed complaint against monks who purchase household servants despite already having disciples for support, contrasted with Confucius's own disciples and Tao Yuanming's own students performing exactly this kind of service out of genuine devotion.

Chinese Source

僧有畜僮僕供使令者。夫出家人有弟子可服役，奚以僮僕為？或曰：「弟子為求道而來，非執役人也。」噫！夫子之適國也，一則曰冉有僕，一則曰樊遲御；淵明之赴友人召也，一門生二子舁其籃輿。後世圖而繪之，以為高致。今出家為僧，乃寵愛其弟子如富貴家兒，而另以錢買僮僕供爨負薪、張傘執刺，末法之弊一至是乎！

Ru-Yi Translation

There are monks who keep servants to command at will. But one who has left the household life already has disciples who can perform such service — why, then, would he need servants at all? Someone might say: "Disciples come seeking the Way, not to serve as laborers." Ah! When Confucius traveled from state to state, at one point his disciple Ran You drove his carriage, and at another his disciple Fan Chi drove it as well; when Tao Yuanming set out to answer a friend's invitation, a single student together with his own two sons carried his palanquin. Later generations even painted these very scenes as images of lofty, refined character. And yet today, having become a monk, one dotes on one's own disciples as though they were the children of a wealthy household, and instead spends money to purchase servants to cook meals, carry firewood, hold umbrellas, and deliver calling cards — that the corruption of this degenerate age should reach even this extreme!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Ran You and Fan Chi are both genuine disciples of Confucius named in the Analects itself as having driven his carriage on separate journeys; Tao Yuanming's own reliance on a single student and his two sons to carry his palanquin to a friend's invitation is likewise documented in his official biography — Zhuhong's point is precise: these were freely given acts of devotion from genuine students, not the purchased labor of hired servants, which is exactly the distinction today's monks have collapsed.

37. 文文山 — Wen Wenshan

A gentle but pointed correction of the martyred loyalist statesman Wen Tianxiang's own line about reincarnated family bonds, arguing his sentiment — however moving — reflects a lack of real familiarity with Buddhist teaching on karma and impermanence.

Chinese Source

文山六歌，有「來生業緣在，骨肉當如故」之句，是信有三世矣。特不知：宿業因緣之至也，則聚為一家；宿業因緣之畢也，則散為歧路。如鳥宿林，天明而為東西南北鳥矣，安保其如故也？文山節義才學表表百世，而此言乃似「七月七日長生殿」語，則未嘗留心內典之故也。惜哉！

Ru-Yi Translation

Wen Shan's own "Six Songs" contain the line: "In the next life, when the karmic bond remains, flesh and blood shall be as they once were" — showing he genuinely believed in the three periods of time. And yet he did not seem to realize: when the causes and conditions of past karma converge, people gather together as a single family; and once those same causes and conditions are exhausted, they scatter again along separate roads — like birds roosting together in the same forest for the night, who at daybreak fly off in every direction, east, west, south, and north — how, then, could one ever be assured that things will remain "as they once were" at all? Wen Shan's own moral integrity and literary talent stand out across the generations, and yet this particular line of his sounds almost like something out of an old tale of lovers' vows exchanged in a palace hall — proof that he never gave real attention to Buddhist scripture. What a pity!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wen Tianxiang (1236–1283) is one of the most celebrated loyalist martyrs in Chinese history, a Song chancellor who refused to submit even after three years of Mongol captivity and was executed still declaring his allegiance to the fallen dynasty — his own "Song of Righteous Spirit," composed while awaiting death, remains a canonical statement of Confucian integrity under duress. Zhuhong's critique here is specific and limited: he does not question Wen's moral character at all, only this one line's naive assumption that reincarnated family bonds simply persist unchanged, which he corrects with the same impermanence already at the center of his own understanding of karma throughout this collection.
