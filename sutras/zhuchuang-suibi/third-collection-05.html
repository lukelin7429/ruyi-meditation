竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 18–21

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 5

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). This section opens with a wry comparison between pilgrimage to famous mountains and rebirth in the Pure Land, then moves into a genuine institutional debate at Zhuhong's own Yunqi Monastery over two specific rules he enforced — banning improper solicitation of donations and reckless citation of Chan masters' recorded exchanges — recorded here as real back-and-forth exchanges with a resistant monk.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: work in progress, entries 18–21 of 三筆 Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

18. 遊名山不願西方 — Visiting Famous Mountains, Yet Not Wishing for the Western Pure Land

A brief, pointed comparison between the popular pilgrimage circuit of China's three great bodhisattva mountains and the far more direct — and far faster — path of rebirth in Amitābha's own Pure Land.

Chinese Source

遊五臺者曰文殊在，遊峨嵋者曰普賢在，遊普陀者曰觀世音在；獨不曰西方極樂世界有彌陀在乎？又不曰三大士者徒仰嘉名；阿彌陀佛見在說法，親炙休光之為愈乎？又不曰跋涉三山，累年月而後到；信心念佛，一彈指而往生乎？大可歎也。

Ru-Yi Translation

Those who visit Mount Wutai say Mañjuśrī is there; those who visit Mount Emei say Samantabhadra is there; those who visit Mount Putuo say Avalokiteśvara is there — why does no one ever say that Amitābha himself is present in the Western Pure Land of Ultimate Bliss? And why does no one ever say: to merely admire the illustrious names of these three great bodhisattvas from a distance is one thing, but would it not be far better to receive Amitābha's own radiant instruction directly, while he is even now, at this very moment, expounding the Dharma in person? And why does no one ever say: journeying to these three mountains requires trudging on for years — while sincere Buddha-recitation can bring one to rebirth in the West in the space of a single snap of the fingers? This is truly something to lament.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wutai, Emei, and Putuo remain, along with Jiuhua, the "four great sacred mountains" of Chinese Buddhism, each traditionally the earthly manifestation-site of its own bodhisattva; Zhuhong's argument here is characteristically economical — rather than disputing the mountains' sanctity, he simply points out the asymmetry between traveling for years to admire a bodhisattva's reputed presence and reaching Amitābha's own direct instruction, by the Pure Land teaching's own account, in a single instant.

19. 非理募化 — Improper Solicitation of Donations

A real institutional exchange at Yunqi Monastery: a monk objects to Zhuhong's rule against improper fundraising, arguing it deprives donors of merit-making opportunities, and Zhuhong answers with a documented precedent from the Buddha's own lifetime.

Chinese Source

雲棲僧約：「非理募化者出院」。一僧曰：「此不足禁，禁之則缺眾生福田。非理募化，雖其人自負過愆，而眾生獲破慳捨財之益；世僧假佛為名以營生，佛何曾為此輩出一禁約乎？」予曰：「子言則誠善矣，然知其一，未知其二。非理募化者，瞞因昧果，施者知之，因而退心，後遂不施，安在其能破慳也？佛世有諸弟子自遠遊歸，所過聚落，望而閉戶；問故，則畏僧之募化也。因以白佛，佛乃種種呵責，何言其不禁約也？慎之哉！」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Yunqi monastic rule states: "One who solicits donations improperly shall be expelled from the monastery." A monk objected: "This need not be forbidden — forbidding it deprives sentient beings of a field for cultivating merit. Even if the one soliciting improperly bears his own fault for it, sentient beings still gain the benefit of breaking their own stinginess and giving away their wealth; and besides, there are worldly monks who make their living falsely invoking the Buddha's name, and the Buddha never once established any prohibition against people like that."

I answered: "What you say is well-intentioned, but you know only one side of the matter, not the other. Whoever solicits donations improperly conceals cause and effect from the donor — once the donor eventually learns the truth, his own heart withdraws, and he never gives again afterward. How, then, could this possibly break anyone's stinginess at all? And besides, in the Buddha's own time, some of his disciples returning from distant travels found that every village they passed through shut its doors the moment they saw them coming; when asked why, the villagers explained they feared the monks would come soliciting donations. The disciples reported this to the Buddha, and the Buddha rebuked them severely for it in every way — how, then, can you say the Buddha never established any prohibition on this at all? Be careful indeed!"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This entry offers real, documentary evidence of how Yunqi Monastery was actually governed — the "Yunqi monastic rule" (雲棲僧約) was a genuine written code Zhuhong drafted for his own community, and this exchange, recorded as a direct back-and-forth with a specific objecting monk, gives a rare glimpse of him defending one of its provisions in real argumentative give-and-take rather than as settled abstract doctrine.

20. 妄拈古德機緣一 — Recklessly Citing the Ancient Masters' Own Exchanges, First Note

A second real exchange from the same monastic rulebook: a monk argues that even reckless citation of Chan gōng'àn plants some wholesome connection, and Zhuhong answers with a sharp warning about the difference between citation grounded in genuine realization and mere glib performance.

Chinese Source

雲棲僧約：「妄拈古德機緣者出院」。一僧云：「此不必禁，禁之則斷般若緣。彼謗《法華》者，地獄罪畢，還以謗故植緣《法華》，況妄拈者非謗乎？」予曰：「子言則誠善矣，然知其一，未知其二。謗《法華》者，出地獄而植善緣；孰若信敬《法華》者，不入地獄而即植善緣乎？又謂妄拈非謗，而不思無知臆談皆名謗《大般若》。是故漫述師言者，被點簡云：『先師無此語，莫謗先師好。』彼尊師也，非謗也。錯答一轉語者，墮野狐身，彼錯也，非謗也。何二人皆成罪戾？古人一問一答，皆從真實了悟中來；今人馳騁口頭三昧，明眼人前，似藥汞之入紅爐，妖邪之遇白澤耳。若不禁止，東豎一拳，西下一喝，此作一偈，彼說一頌，如風如狂，如戲如謔，虛頭熾而實踐亡，子以為宗門復興，吾以為佛法大壞也。」

Ru-Yi Translation

The Yunqi monastic rule states: "One who recklessly cites the ancient masters' own recorded exchanges shall be expelled from the monastery." A monk objected: "This need not be forbidden — forbidding it would cut off people's own connection to prajñā-wisdom. Even one who slanders the Lotus Sutra, after finishing his term of suffering in hell, still plants a karmic connection to the Lotus Sutra precisely because of that very slander — how much more so, then, for someone who merely cites these exchanges recklessly, which is not even slander at all?"

I answered: "What you say is well-intentioned, but you know only one side of the matter, not the other. One who slanders the Lotus Sutra plants a wholesome connection only after first passing through hell — how could this possibly compare to one who believes and reveres the Lotus Sutra from the very start, planting that same wholesome connection without ever entering hell at all? And besides, you claim that reckless citation is not slander — but have you never considered that ignorant, baseless speculation about the Great Prajñāpāramitā Sutra is itself already a form of slandering that very sutra? This is exactly why, long ago, when someone carelessly repeated his own teacher's words, he was corrected with: 'Our late master never said any such thing — please do not slander our late master.' That person who repeated the words was, in fact, honoring his teacher, not slandering him at all. And consider the one who gave a single wrong answer and fell into the body of a wild fox for five hundred lifetimes — his fault was simply an error, not slander either. Why, then, did both these men still incur real fault?

You must understand: the ancients' every question and answer arose from genuine, thorough realization. People today, by contrast, merely indulge in glib verbal cleverness — and before someone whose eyes are genuinely open, this is like mercury dropped into a blazing furnace, or an evil spirit encountering the mythical Baize beast. If this is not strictly forbidden, then one person raises a fist here, another shouts there, this one composes a verse, that one recites a gāthā, as wild and reckless as madness, as frivolous as mere jest — until empty pretense flourishes while genuine practice disappears entirely. You believe this would revive the Chan school; I believe it would utterly destroy the Buddha-dharma instead."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The two cautionary precedents Zhuhong cites are both genuine, well-known gōng'àn: the "our late master never said any such thing" exchange comes from a documented dialogue involving the Fayan-lineage master Wenyi, and "the wild fox's body" refers to the famous Baizhang Huaihai case, in which an elder who once wrongly denied that a great practitioner still falls under cause and effect is freed from five hundred lifetimes as a fox only after Baizhang corrects the answer to "does not obscure cause and effect" — the same gōng'àn already referenced for its lesson on karmic precision in this project's Second Collection.

21. 妄拈古德機緣二 — Second Note

The disgruntled monk presses further, asking whether any assessment of the ancient masters' exchanges is now forbidden — Zhuhong clarifies the rule targets recklessness specifically, then demonstrates the difference with a genuine test case.

Chinese Source

僧不悅曰：「審如是，古德機緣，更不可開口一評量乎？」曰：「止禁妄拈，未嘗言不可拈也。二僧同起捲簾，古德云一得一失；子試評量，得失誰在？」僧無語。予曰：「昔人有言：『十回被師家問，九回答不得。』未為害，但忌無知妄談，則終無升進耳。慎之哉！」

Ru-Yi Translation

The monk, displeased, said: "If that is truly so, then are we forbidden even to open our mouths and offer any assessment at all of the ancient masters' own exchanges?" I answered: "The rule forbids only reckless citation — it never once said that citation itself is forbidden. Consider this: two monks once simultaneously rolled up a bamboo curtain, and an ancient master judged that one gained something while the other lost something — try assessing this for yourself: who, in fact, gained, and who lost?" The monk had no answer.

I said: "The ancients had a saying: 'Ten times questioned by one's own teacher, nine times unable to answer.' This itself is no real harm — what one must truly guard against is ignorant, reckless talk, which leaves one with no hope of ever genuinely advancing at all. Be careful indeed!"

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

The "curtain" test case Zhuhong poses is a genuine gōng'àn from the founder of the Fayan school, Fayan Wenyi — the same lineage whose own patriarch figures elsewhere in this project — and the monk's failure to answer it on the spot is exactly the demonstration Zhuhong needs: not an argument that reckless citation is forbidden in principle, but a live example of how quickly confident speculation collapses when actually tested.
