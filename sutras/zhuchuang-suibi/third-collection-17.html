竹窗隨筆・三筆 · Entries 75–79

Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 17

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). A layman's theatrical retirement into leisure shames busy monks by contrast, a puzzle about the timing of conception draws careful, honest agnosticism rather than false certainty, a detailed three-part warning against well-intentioned but harmful "protection" of the Dharma, a nuanced typology of Confucian anti-Buddhist polemic, and a quiet correction of a widespread but improper local custom.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

75. 今日方閒 — Only Today Am I Finally At Leisure

A layman's theatrical mock funeral, staged while still alive to mark his own retirement into genuine leisure, becomes a pointed contrast with monks who remain busy and distracted despite already being free of ordinary household burdens.

Chinese Source

吾杭有魯姓者，忘其名，人以其面痲也，稱魯痲子。中年謂其子曰：「吾婚嫁事畢，爾曹亦能自立矣，吾將求閒。」於是備棺槨，凡魂轎、明旌、鼓樂皆悉營辦，諸子衰絰執杖引棺，己肩輿隨後，至西湖之別墅，置棺中庭，遣諸子歸。榜其門曰：「今日方閒」，至死不入城墎。嗚呼，亦達矣！夫俗士具有家緣，其忙宜也，脫忙而曰「今日方閒」；出家者本閒也，乃勞形苦志，奔利趨名，終日營營而不知休息者，當榜曰「今日方忙」，可也。

Ru-Yi Translation

There was a man in my own Hangzhou, surnamed Lu — I have forgotten his given name — whose face was pockmarked, so people called him "Pockmarked Lu." In his middle years, he said to his own children: "I have finished arranging your marriages, and you are each now able to stand on your own — I intend, from now on, to seek real leisure." And so he prepared his own coffin, along with the spirit-palanquin, the funeral banner, and the musicians — everything arranged in full — and had his sons don mourning garments and carry staffs to lead the coffin, while he himself followed behind in a sedan chair. Arriving at his own villa by West Lake, he had the coffin placed in the courtyard, and sent his sons home. He then inscribed over his own gate: "Only today am I finally at leisure" — and never once entered the city again until his own death.

Ah, how truly enlightened a man! An ordinary layperson, burdened with household affairs, is naturally busy — for him to cast off that busyness and declare, "Only today am I finally at leisure," is no small thing. But a monk is, by his very nature, already at leisure from the start — and yet there are those who, like ordinary laypeople, exhaust body and mind chasing after fame and profit, busy all day long in utter confusion, never once resting. Such a person ought instead to inscribe over his own gate: "Only today am I finally busy" — that would suit him far better.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Whether or not this specific man's own funeral pageant was witnessed firsthand by Zhuhong, its theatrical staging — a mock funeral procession complete with mourning clothes, spirit-palanquin, and banner, all performed while the man remained very much alive — dramatizes the same principle already developed abstractly in this collection's entry 63's discussion of a bodhisattva-like layman, and directly extends the recurring theme, running through entries 39–40, 65, and 72 above, that time itself is the resource most easily and most dangerously squandered.

76. 入胎 — Entering the Womb

A careful, intellectually honest attempt to reconcile scriptural teaching about conception with popular reports of spirits entering the womb at the very moment of birth — resolved by statistical rarity rather than by dismissing either account.

Chinese Source

經言入胎皆在十月之先，而世間傳聞者，皆臨產之時，死彼生此。有供僧山中者，忽見僧直入內室，俄報坐草生子，急往山中探之，則僧已入滅矣。與經言不合，何也？蓋入胎於十月之先者其常，而臨產入胎者千萬中之一二也；世人惟見一二，而不見千萬故也。然早入胎不見現形者，何也？或臨產入者能現，而早入不能現也，經無明文，不敢妄為之說。眾生入胎不可思議，以俟夫天眼聖人決焉。

Ru-Yi Translation

The sutras teach that entry into the womb always occurs before the tenth month of pregnancy, and yet popular reports invariably describe someone dying in one place at the very moment of birth in another. There was a man who supported a monk in the mountains, and one day suddenly saw the monk walk directly into his own inner chamber; a moment later, word came that a child had just been born within — he rushed to the mountain to check on the monk, and found he had already passed away. This does not accord with the sutras' own teaching — why is that?

Entry into the womb before the tenth month is the ordinary case; entry at the very moment of birth occurs perhaps once in a million cases. Ordinary people witness only this rare one or two instances, and never see the far more common million.

But why, then, does an early entry into the womb never manifest visibly, as this late entry apparently did? Perhaps only entry at the moment of birth can manifest visibly, while early entry cannot — the sutras offer no explicit statement on this, and I dare not speculate further. The entry of sentient beings into the womb remains genuinely inconceivable, a matter to be settled only by a sage possessing the heavenly eye.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

This is one of the more methodologically careful entries in the entire collection — rather than forcing the popular anecdote to fit scripture or dismissing scripture in favor of the anecdote, Zhuhong proposes a statistical resolution (the rare case is simply the only kind anyone notices) and then explicitly declines to speculate further where the sutras themselves offer no clear answer, a restraint that stands out against his more confident argumentative style elsewhere in this collection.

77. 護法 — Protecting the Dharma

A detailed, three-part warning that well-intentioned efforts to "protect" the Dharma — reclaiming monastery property, promoting written works, and supporting fellow monks — can each cause real harm when pursued carelessly, without proper discernment.

Chinese Source

人知佛法外護付與王臣，而未知僧之當其護者，不可以不慎也。護法有三：一曰興崇梵剎，二曰流通大教，三曰獎掖緇流。曷言乎慎也？護剎者，梵剎果爾原屬寺產，豪強占焉，奪而復之，理也。有如考諸圖籍，則疑似不明，傳之久遠，則張王互易，以勢取之，可乎？喜捨名為吉祥地，力不敵而與者謂之冤業藪。若僧惟勸化有力大人，以恢復舊剎為大功德主，而不思佛固等視眾生如羅睺羅。殃民建剎，即廣踰千頃，高淩九霄，栴檀為材，珠玉為飾，佛所悲憐而不喜者也。有過無功，不可不慎，一也。護教者，其所著述，果爾遠合佛心，近得經旨，贊歎而傳揚之，理也。有如外道迂談，胸臆偏見，過為稱譽，可乎？若僧惟乞諸名公作序、作跋，而不思疑誤後學，有過無功，不可不慎，二也。護僧者，其僧果爾真參真悟，具大知見者，尊而禮之，實心實行，操持敦確者，信而近之，理也。有如虛頭禪客，下劣庸流，亦尊之信之，可乎？若僧惟親附貴門，冀其覆庇，而綿纊錦繡，以裹癰疽，祇益其毒，有過無功，不可不慎，三也。是則王臣護法，而僧壞法也，悲夫！

Ru-Yi Translation

People know that the Buddha-dharma's outer protection is entrusted to kings and ministers, but do not realize that monks themselves also bear a responsibility to protect it — and this cannot be undertaken carelessly. Protecting the Dharma involves three things: first, restoring and honoring monasteries; second, circulating the great teaching; third, encouraging and supporting junior monks.

Why must one be careful in each of these? As for protecting monasteries: if a monastery's property has genuinely been seized by the powerful, reclaiming it through proper legal means is entirely justified. But suppose the land records themselves are ambiguous, and ownership has changed hands repeatedly over a long span of time — is it right to reclaim such property through sheer force? Property given willingly is called an auspicious site; property taken by force from those unable to resist is called a den of karmic grievance. Monks often think only of persuading some powerful patron to become the great benefactor who restores an old monastery, without considering that the Buddha regards all sentient beings with the same impartial love he showed his own son Rāhula. To build a monastery at the cost of harming the common people — however vast its grounds, however towering its halls, however fine its sandalwood timber or jeweled ornaments — is something the Buddha can only pity, never rejoice in. This brings fault without merit, and cannot be undertaken carelessly — this is the first point.

As for protecting the teaching: if a written work genuinely accords with the Buddha's own mind and captures the sutras' own true meaning, then praising and circulating it is entirely justified. But suppose the work is full of heterodox rambling or purely subjective bias — is it right to praise it excessively regardless? Monks who merely seek prefaces and postscripts from famous men, without considering whether the work might mislead later students, likewise bring fault without merit, and cannot proceed carelessly — this is the second point.

As for protecting monks: if a monk has genuinely investigated and genuinely awakened, possessing real right views, then honoring and revering him is entirely justified; if another monk practices with real sincerity and steady integrity, trusting and drawing close to him is likewise justified. But suppose one honors and trusts an empty, boastful pretender, or a mediocre and inferior character all the same — is that right? Monks who merely attach themselves to the powerful, hoping for their protection, are like wrapping silk floss and fine brocade around a festering sore — only worsening the poison within. This too brings fault without merit, and cannot proceed carelessly — this is the third point.

From these three points, it becomes clear: it is kings and ministers who genuinely protect the Dharma, while monks themselves are the ones undermining it. How sorrowful!

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Rāhula, cited here as the standard of the Buddha's own impartial love, was the Buddha's actual biological son before his own renunciation, and became one of his ten principal disciples, renowned for his meticulous, "hidden practice" — Zhuhong's invocation of this specific relationship sharpens his broader point: that harming ordinary people to fund a grand monastery violates the very impartiality the Buddha extended even to his own child, a rare instance in this collection of institutional critique grounded directly in the Buddha's own biography.

78. 儒者闢佛 — Confucian Scholars Who Reject Buddhism

A careful, non-reductive typology distinguishing three genuinely different kinds of Confucian anti-Buddhist polemic — sincere, biased, and transcendent — refusing to lump honest philosophical disagreement together with genuine bigotry.

Chinese Source

儒者闢佛，有跡相似而實不同者，不可概論也。儒有三：有誠實之儒，有偏僻之儒，有超脫之儒。誠實儒者，於佛原無惡心，但其學以綱常倫理為主，所務在於格致、誠正、修齊、治平，是世間正道也，即佛談出世法自不相合。不相合勢必爭，爭則或至於謗者，無怪其然也，伊川、晦菴之類是也。偏僻儒者，稟狂高之性，主先入之言，逞訛謬之談，窮毀極詆，而不知其為非，張無盡所謂「聞佛似寇仇，見僧如蛇蠍」者是也。超脫儒者，識精而理明，不惟不闢，而且深信；不惟深信，而且力行，是之謂真儒也。雖然，又有遊戲法門，而實無歸敬；外為歸敬，而中懷異心者，非真儒也。具眼者辨之。

Ru-Yi Translation

Confucian scholars who reject Buddhism appear outwardly similar, but are, in substance, not all the same — they cannot be lumped together indiscriminately. There are three kinds of Confucians: the sincere and honest Confucian, the narrow and biased Confucian, and the transcendent Confucian.

The sincere Confucian bears no genuine ill will toward Buddhism at all — his own scholarship simply centers on the fundamental bonds and constant relationships of society, devoted to investigating things, extending knowledge, sincerity, rectifying the mind, cultivating the self, ordering the family, governing the state, and bringing peace to the realm — a genuinely correct worldly path, which naturally does not align with Buddhism's own teaching of transcending the world. Where alignment fails, disagreement inevitably follows, and disagreement may sometimes turn into outright criticism — this is hardly surprising, and figures like Yichuan and Huian belong to this category.

The narrow and biased Confucian, by nature arrogant and self-important, clings to whatever view he first adopted, indulges in reckless, mistaken claims, and heaps every possible insult upon Buddhism without ever recognizing his own error — exactly as Zhang Wujin himself once described: "hearing of the Buddha as though hearing of a mortal enemy, seeing a monk as though seeing a viper or scorpion."

The transcendent Confucian, by contrast, possesses genuine discernment and clear understanding of principle — not only does he not reject Buddhism, he believes in it deeply; and not only does he believe in it deeply, he puts it into genuine practice — this is what may truly be called a genuine Confucian. And yet, even here, there are those who treat Buddhism as a mere plaything, without any real reverence at all — outwardly appearing devout, while secretly harboring an entirely different intention — such a person is not a genuine Confucian either. Let those with genuine discernment judge for themselves.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Yichuan (Cheng Yi) and Huian (Zhu Xi) were both genuine, major Song Neo-Confucian philosophers whose criticism of Buddhism was substantial and well-documented — Zhu Xi in particular questioned the authenticity of most Buddhist scriptures beyond the Sutra of Forty-Two Sections, already discussed in this collection's own entries 56–57 above; Zhang Wujin is Zhang Shangying, a genuine Song chancellor and devout lay Buddhist whose own Treatise in Defense of the Dharma is the actual source of the vivid line about "hearing of the Buddha as though hearing of a mortal enemy," which Zhuhong quotes here to characterize the narrow, biased type of critic rather than the sincere kind exemplified by Cheng Yi and Zhu Xi.

79. 居士搭衣 — Laypeople Wearing Monastic Robes

A quiet correction of a widespread but improper local custom — laypeople receiving precepts while wearing the full monastic robe set — traced not to lay ignorance but to monks' own failure to explain proper practice.

Chinese Source

圓頂方袍，則知三衣，僧服也；髮其首而僧其衣，非制矣。古人謂反有罪愆，而著為成訓。世人不察，僧亦不言，可歎也。予少時見昭慶戒壇受優婆塞、優婆夷戒者，咸著三衣，蓋沿習為風，而不知其非也。此非在家者之過，出家僧不以明告，而惟順人情以致此也，故表而出之。

Ru-Yi Translation

A shaved head and square-cut robe mark the three monastic robes as belonging properly to monks alone; for someone with a full head of hair to wear a monk's robe regardless is contrary to the Buddha's own institution. The ancients held that doing so actually incurs real fault, and this teaching was set down as established doctrine. And yet ordinary people fail to realize this, and monks themselves fail to say anything about it — how truly regrettable!

When I was young, I saw that at the precept-ordination platform of Zhaoqing Temple, all the laypeople receiving the five precepts, both men and women, wore the full three-robe set — this had simply become an established custom over time, with no one realizing it was actually contrary to proper practice. This is not the fault of the laypeople themselves at all — it is because monks failed to clearly instruct them, instead simply going along with popular custom, that this situation came about. I set it down here specifically to bring this to light.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhaoqing Temple, already mentioned in this project's translation of the Second Collection as one of Hangzhou's genuine vinaya monasteries, was the actual site of a real, recurring ordination platform where laypeople received the five precepts — Zhuhong's memory of this specific location and custom, and his careful assignment of blame to monastic negligence rather than lay ignorance, closely parallels the same practical, non-punitive approach he took to the five-strip robe's own proper form in entry 58 above.
