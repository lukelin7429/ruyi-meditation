Notes Beside the Bamboo Window — Third Collection, Part 11

Continuing the Third Collection (三筆). Zhuhong defends a devout Song layman's genuine rebirth against scholarly criticism of his editorial errors, recounts his own real dispute with a lecturer over the proper sequence of receiving precepts, and closes with a documented ghost story rebuking cruel punishment of prisoners and servants.

Translator: Ru-Yi Meditation Center Status: complete — all 398 entries translated Commentary guide: 華藏講記組's vernacular commentary (Huazang Study Group)

48. 龍舒往生 — Longshu's Rebirth

A defense of the devout Song layman Wang Rixiu's genuine, witnessed rebirth against scholarly criticism of real editorial flaws in his own compiled Pure Land scripture, arguing his lifelong sincerity outweighs these technical faults.

Chinese Source

或問：「居士臨終立化，其往生之祥，昭灼如是，而所輯大彌陀經，不免抄前著後、抄後著前，此一失也。又宋景濂謂居士於金剛經不用昭明三十二分，無論矣，亦不依天親、無著所定，而另為品第，此二失也。似於觀經讀誦大乘往生正因未協，而立化者何？」答：「此雖有過，然其平日念佛求生至真至切、至誠至篤，自利利他，功德非細，小疵不足掩其大善。尚有帶業往生者，何疑於龍舒？或其品位不能與上上流，則未可知矣。」

Ru-Yi Translation

Someone asked: "Layman Longshu died standing upright at the moment of his passing, and the auspicious signs of his rebirth were so clear and manifest — and yet the Great Amitābha Sutra he compiled is not free of errors, mixing up passages from earlier and later sections; this is one fault. And Song Jinglian further criticized him for not following Prince Zhaoming's own traditional thirty-two divisions in his treatment of the Diamond Sutra — setting that aside — and also for not following the divisions established by Vasubandhu and Asaṅga, instead creating his own arrangement entirely; this is a second fault. This seems to conflict with the Contemplation Sutra's own teaching that 'reciting Mahāyāna scripture' is a genuine cause for rebirth — so how, then, could he have died standing upright like that?"

I answered: "Though what you describe are indeed faults, Longshu's own devotion to Buddha-recitation and seeking rebirth was genuinely sincere and earnest in the extreme, benefiting both himself and others — his merit was no small thing, and these minor blemishes are not enough to obscure his greater virtue. Given that even those who carry unresolved karma can still attain rebirth, why should we doubt Longshu at all? Whether his own grade of rebirth falls short of the very highest, that we cannot say for certain."

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Wang Rixiu (Longshu) was a genuine Song scholar who abandoned a promising civil career to compile his own harmonized edition of the Immeasurable Life Sutra from four earlier translations and to write the widely circulated Pure Land Writings of Longshu — his death, standing upright while reciting the Buddha's name, is recorded in traditional rebirth accounts; Song Lian (Jinglian), the Ming scholar who criticized his editorial choices, was himself a genuinely devout Buddhist and close associate of the Ming founding emperor, making this a case of one sincere practitioner critiquing another's scholarship without either doubting the other's genuine devotion.

49. 直受菩薩戒 — Receiving the Bodhisattva Precepts Directly

A real, documented dispute in which Zhuhong's own teaching on the proper sequence of monastic precepts was denounced by a rival lecturer as demonic corruption — resolved, after the man's death, by a simple citation from the very sutra he himself had once quoted.

Chinese Source

予著《戒疏發隱》中，言必先受五戒、十戒、二百五十戒，然後受菩薩十重四十八輕戒。有講師憤然不平曰：「何以不教人直受菩薩戒，而迂曲如是？佛記末法中，有魔王混入吾法而壞吾法，今其人矣！」予不答。講師卒，其徒理前語，欲集諸僧、諸宰官居士等，設大會而作辯難。予亦不答。有代予答者曰：「無以為也。不觀彼所引菩薩善戒經乎？經云：『譬如重樓四級，自下而上，次第歷然，不可躐等；受戒亦然。』經語也，無以為也。」其人乃止。

Ru-Yi Translation

In my own Hidden Meanings of the Commentary on the Precepts, I stated that one must first receive the five precepts, then the ten precepts, then the two hundred and fifty precepts, before receiving the bodhisattva's ten major and forty-eight minor precepts. A certain lecturing master, indignant and displeased, said: "Why not simply teach people to receive the bodhisattva precepts directly, rather than through such a roundabout process? The Buddha foretold that in the degenerate age, a demon king would infiltrate our own teaching and corrupt it — surely this man is exactly that demon!" I did not respond.

After this lecturing master died, his own disciples raised the same complaint again, wishing to gather monks, officials, and laypeople together for a great assembly to debate the matter with me. I still did not respond. Someone answered on my behalf instead, saying: "There is no need for this at all. Have you not read the very Sutra on the Bodhisattva's Good Precepts that your own teacher cited? The sutra says: 'It is like ascending a four-story tower — one must proceed from the bottom upward, level by level in strict order, never skipping a stage; receiving the precepts follows exactly the same principle.' These are the sutra's own words — there is nothing left to debate." The man then fell silent.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Zhuhong's own Hidden Meanings of the Commentary on the Brahma Net Precepts is a genuine, surviving five-fascicle work of his; this entry is one of the collection's clearest examples of his characteristic restraint under attack — refusing to answer a hostile accusation directly, twice, and allowing the matter to resolve itself through simple citation of shared scripture rather than personal argument, a pattern already visible in his handling of institutional disputes recorded in this collection's entries 19–22 above.

50. 刑戒 — A Warning Against Cruel Punishment

A vivid, documented ghost story rebuking a cruel official's casual brutality toward prisoners, in which a mysterious Daoist priest's intervention lets the official's own son feel exactly the pain he had been inflicting on others.

Chinese Source

大長者呂叔簡作《刑戒》，鄒南皋先生梓之，予跋之。茲傳聞一事甚奇：某官者，素酷暴，動輒行笞數十下，酸楚之聲震地，若罔聞者。有道人排闥入，直立廳事，瞋目而指之。某官大怒，呼左右極力笞之。忽後堂大叫公子為鬼擊，幾斃。某官張皇退堂入內，則其子自言：「若有鬼神巨箠箠我，皮破肉爛，血漬雙股，痛不可忍。」急遣人至廳，被笞人已失所在。乃號咷大哭，舉身自擲，頭面皆損。噫！彼道人者，其天神乎！人皆有父母，人之子，己之子，均子也，奈何己子如珍，他子如草，於心安乎？又一尊官愛幼子，每日令屠者進一豬胃，胃瘦則大怒，笞責屠，傷重，調治兩月乃愈。有居家嚴刑以待婢僕，亦復如是。愚謂《刑戒》一書，當布之四方、傳之百世可也。

Ru-Yi Translation

The venerable elder Lü Shujian composed a work called Warnings Against Cruel Punishment, which Mr. Zou Nangao had printed, and for which I wrote a postscript. It records one especially strange and remarkable story: a certain official, notoriously cruel and violent by nature, would order dozens of strokes of the cane at the slightest provocation, and though the sound of the victims' agonized screaming shook the very ground, he remained as though he had heard nothing at all.

One day, while he was carrying out just such a punishment, a Daoist priest suddenly pushed open the door and entered, standing bolt upright in the hall, glaring at him and pointing directly at him. The official flew into a rage, and ordered his attendants to beat the prisoner even more severely. Suddenly, a great cry erupted from the inner chambers: his own son was being struck by some unseen spirit, and was nearly dead. The official rushed, alarmed, from the hall into the inner rooms, where his son cried out: "Some spirit is beating me with a great cudgel — my skin is torn, my flesh mangled, blood soaking both my legs, the pain unbearable!" The official hastily sent men to the hall to detain the priest — but both the priest and the beaten prisoner had already vanished without a trace. He then broke into loud wailing, throwing himself violently to the ground, injuring his own head and face.

Ah! That Daoist priest must surely have been a heavenly spirit! Everyone has been, or will one day be, a parent — another man's son and one's own son are equally sons — why, then, treat one's own child as a treasure and another's as mere weeds? How can one's own heart possibly rest easy with that?

The book records another case as well: a certain high official doted on his young son, and had a butcher deliver a fresh pig's stomach to him every single day; when once the stomach delivered was somewhat small, he flew into a rage and had the butcher beaten so severely that it took two full months of treatment before he recovered. There are households, too, that inflict this same kind of severe punishment on their own servants and maids. I would say that this book, Warnings Against Cruel Punishment, ought to be spread throughout the land and handed down for a hundred generations to come.

Reading note — after the Huazang Study Group's commentary

Lü Kun (Shujian) was a genuine Ming-dynasty official who rose to Vice Minister of Justice, and his Warnings Against Cruel Punishment drew directly on his own extensive judicial experience to argue against excessive corporal punishment — a real, influential text among Ming and Qing officials; Zhuhong's own postscript to it is independently documented, confirming this was not merely a book he happened to read but one he actively helped bring into wider circulation.
